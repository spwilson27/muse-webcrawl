Arcadia (Greek: Ἀρκαδία, Arkadía) is a region in the central Peloponnese of Greece. It takes its name from the mythological figure Arcas, and it was a land of mountains, shepherds, and the god Pan. In the European Renaissance and later, Arcadia became a byword for an idyllic, unspoiled pastoral wilderness — a poetic ideal of shepherd life in harmony with nature — and the name has been given to places, works of art, and concepts far beyond the Greek region itself. The modern regional unit of Arcadia has its capital at Tripoli and is one of the largest regional units of Greece by area, with a relatively small population.

Geography

Arcadia is a high, largely landlocked plateau ringed by mountains, including Mount Mainalo, Parnon, and the Taygetus range to the south. Its terrain of closed basins and gorges isolated its communities in antiquity. The region has a narrow outlet to the Argolic Gulf in the far east, where the coastal district of Kynouria lies. Winters are cold by Greek standards, with snow on the mountains, and the economy is based on agriculture, livestock, and increasingly tourism.

History

Ancient Arcadia was a tribal region whose inhabitants claimed to be autochthonous — "older than the moon". Its cities, including Mantineia, Tegea, Orchomenus, and Megalopolis (founded in 371 BC as the capital of the Arcadian League), were frequently divided between Spartan and Theban alliances. The region supplied mercenaries throughout the Greek world. Under Rome, Byzantium, the Franks, and the Ottomans it remained a rural backwater; its mountain villages played notable roles in the Greek War of Independence of 1821, in which the region's chieftains were prominent.

The Arcadian ideal

Through Virgil's Eclogues, which relocated the pastoral idyll to Arcadia, and Renaissance works such as Sannazaro's Arcadia (1504) and Sidney's Arcadia, the name became shorthand for a golden-age countryside. The Latin phrase Et in Arcadia ego — "even in Arcadia, there am I [Death]" — in paintings by Guercino and Poussin, turned the idyll into a memento mori. "Arcadian" remains a literary adjective for rustic innocence, and the name survives in places such as Arcadia, California, and the French Acadia (Akadie) of Canada.
