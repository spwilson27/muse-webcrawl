Melian Dialogue

The Melian Dialogue is a passage in Book 5 of Thucydides' History of the Peloponnesian War, recounting a negotiation in 416 BC between envoys of Athens and the leaders of Melos, a small island in the Aegean that had remained neutral in the war. It is one of the most famous texts in the study of international relations and political realism.

Background

Melos, a Spartan colony, had refused to join the Athenian-led Delian League and had maintained neutrality. In the summer of 416 BC Athens sent an expedition of some 30 ships and over 1,500 hoplites, demanding that Melos submit and pay tribute. Before attacking, the Athenian generals sent representatives to negotiate.

The dialogue

Unusually, Thucydides presents the exchange as a dramatic dialogue rather than set speeches. The Athenians argue that justice applies only between powers of equal strength: "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must." They dismiss appeals to fairness, the gods and Spartan aid as self-deception. The Melians appeal to justice, divine favour toward the righteous, and the hope of Lacedaemonian help. The Athenians reply that hope is a comfort only to those with resources to back it.

Outcome

The Melians refused to submit. Athens besieged the city, which surrendered unconditionally during the winter after internal treachery. The Athenians executed the adult men of Melos, sold the women and children into slavery, and settled 500 Athenian colonists on the island.

Interpretation

The dialogue is a foundational text of the realist school of international relations, often read as Thucydides' analysis of power politics and imperial expediency — though scholars debate whether he endorses the Athenian argument or presents it as a symptom of the corruption of language and morality in wartime.
