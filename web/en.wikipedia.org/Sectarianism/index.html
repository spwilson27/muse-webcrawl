Sectarianism

Sectarianism is a form of bigotry, discrimination, or hatred arising from attaching importance to perceived differences between subdivisions within a group, such as different denominations of a religion, factions of a political movement, or branches of a philosophy. It involves allegiance to one's own sect or party above others and can produce social division, prejudice, and violence between groups that share most of their beliefs but differ on secondary points.

Religious sectarianism

The term is most often applied to divisions within religions: between Sunni and Shia Muslims, between Catholics and Protestants in Northern Ireland and Scotland, and among rival Buddhist, Hindu, Jewish, and Sikh communities. In Northern Ireland, sectarian rivalry between largely Catholic nationalists and largely Protestant unionists fuelled the Troubles (c. 1968–1998), and marks areas with murals, flags, and segregated housing. In the Middle East, Sunni–Shia tensions have shaped conflicts in Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Bahrain, and Yemen.

Political sectarianism

Sectarianism also describes rigid factional loyalty within political movements, where party or ideological sub-groups treat rivals within the same broad cause as enemies — a usage common in critiques of far-left factionalism and of partisan polarization generally. Scholars distinguish sectarianism from ordinary partisanship by its moral absolutism, social segregation, and willingness to use violence.

Causes and scholarship

Analysts note that sectarian identities are often mobilized by elites competing for power and resources, and that "sectarian" conflicts typically overlay older contests over land, state power, and foreign patronage. Peace processes in Northern Ireland and Lebanon have used power-sharing institutions to manage sectarian division rather than eliminate it.
