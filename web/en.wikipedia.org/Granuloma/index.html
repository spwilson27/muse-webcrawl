Granuloma

Histology of a granuloma showing epithelioid cells and a multinucleated giant cell

A granuloma is an organized collection of macrophages that forms in response to chronic inflammation, typically when the immune system attempts to wall off substances it perceives as foreign but is unable to eliminate. Granulomatous inflammation is a distinctive pattern of chronic inflammation found in a limited number of infectious and non-infectious conditions.

Formation

Granulomas develop when macrophages fail to digest a persistent stimulus — such as intracellular bacteria, fungi, or inert foreign material — and undergo activation driven by T-cell cytokines, especially interferon-gamma. The macrophages transform into epithelioid cells with abundant pink cytoplasm and may fuse into multinucleated giant cells. A rim of lymphocytes and fibroblasts typically surrounds the aggregate. In tuberculosis, granulomas often show central caseous necrosis and are called tubercles.

Causes

Infectious causes include tuberculosis, leprosy, histoplasmosis, and schistosomiasis. Non-infectious causes include sarcoidosis, Crohn's disease, berylliosis, and foreign-body reactions to materials such as suture material or talc. Pathologists distinguish caseating (necrotizing) from non-caseating granulomas as an important diagnostic clue, although the distinction is not absolute.

Clinical significance

Granulomas themselves may be harmless incidental findings or may damage organs through mass effect and fibrosis, as in pulmonary sarcoidosis or tuberculous destruction of lung tissue. Diagnosis usually requires biopsy with special stains and cultures to identify an underlying cause, since treatment — antimicrobials, immunosuppression, or removal of a foreign body — depends entirely on the aetiology.
