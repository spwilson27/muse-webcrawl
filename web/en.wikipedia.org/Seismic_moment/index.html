Seismic moment

The seismic moment of an earthquake is a measure of its total size based on the physics of fault rupture. It is defined as M0 = μAD̄, where μ is the shear modulus (rigidity) of the rock, A is the area of the fault that ruptured, and D̄ is the average slip across that area. Its units are those of torque or energy — newton metres in SI units, or dyne centimetres in cgs.

Significance

Introduced into seismology by Keiiti Aki in the 1960s, seismic moment can be estimated from the long-period (low-frequency) level of seismograms and, for modern events, from geodetic data such as GPS and InSAR measurements of ground deformation. Unlike amplitude-based magnitude scales, it does not saturate for very large earthquakes, because it measures the actual faulting rather than the shaking recorded in a particular frequency band.

Moment magnitude

Seismic moment underlies the moment magnitude scale (Mw), developed by Thomas Hanks and Hiroo Kanamori in 1979: Mw = (2/3)(log10 M0 − 9.1) for M0 in newton metres. Moment magnitude is now the standard measure reported for significant earthquakes. The largest recorded event, the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile, had a seismic moment of roughly 2–3 × 1023 N·m, corresponding to Mw 9.5.
