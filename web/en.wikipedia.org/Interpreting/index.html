Interpreting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Interpreting is a translational activity in which a person produces, in real time or near real time, a first and final rendition in another language of a source-language discourse, which the interpreter has heard or seen once — or, in the case of some signed-language and community settings, in short segments. It differs from translation, which conventionally concerns written texts that can be revised. An interpreter renders spoken or signed language, mediating communication between parties who do not share a language.

Modes

Simultaneous interpreting

In simultaneous interpreting, the interpreter speaks (or signs) the target-language message at nearly the same time as the source message is delivered, typically from a soundproof booth using headphones and a microphone. The mode became prominent at the Nuremberg trials after the Second World War and is standard at the United Nations and other large multilingual conferences. Because of the cognitive load, simultaneous interpreters usually work in pairs, alternating roughly every 20–30 minutes.

Consecutive interpreting

In consecutive interpreting, the speaker pauses after a segment — from a few sentences to several minutes — while the interpreter renders it in the target language, often with the aid of a specialised note-taking system. Consecutive mode is common in bilateral meetings, courtrooms, medical consultations and press conferences.

Other modes

Whispered interpreting (chuchotage) involves the interpreter whispering the rendition to a small number of listeners. Sight translation is the oral rendering of a written document. Relay interpreting passes a message through a pivot language. Remote and telephone interpreting expanded rapidly with videoconferencing technology.

Settings

Interpreters work in conference, court and legal, medical, community and public-service, military, media and signed-language settings. Signed-language interpreting provides communication between deaf and hearing people and is governed in many jurisdictions by certification and codes of ethics.

Cognitive process

Research in interpreting studies, including Daniel Gile's "Effort Models", describes simultaneous interpreting as the concurrent management of listening and analysis, short-term memory, speech production and coordination efforts. Training programmes teach techniques such as anticipation, reformulation and note-taking, and professional associations — including AIIC, the International Association of Conference Interpreters — set working-condition and ethics standards.

History

Interpreters have existed since ancient times wherever peoples speaking different languages traded, negotiated or made war; Egyptian, Greek and Roman sources all record interpreters, and figures such as La Malinche in the Spanish conquest of Mexico illustrate the political weight of the role. Professional conference interpreting emerged after the First World War, when multilingual diplomacy at the League of Nations created demand for simultaneous and consecutive services.
