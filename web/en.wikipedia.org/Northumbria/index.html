Northumbria (Old English: Norþanhymbra) was an early medieval Anglo-Saxon kingdom in what is now Northern England and south-east Scotland, one of the seven traditional kingdoms of the Heptarchy. It was formed by the union of the two provinces of Bernicia in the north and Deira in the south, united under Æthelfrith around 604 and permanently from the reign of Oswald in the 630s.

Golden age

During the 7th and early 8th centuries Northumbria was the most powerful kingdom in Britain. King Edwin of Deira converted to Christianity in 627, and Oswald invited Aidan from Iona to found the monastery of Lindisfarne in 635, from which Celtic Christianity spread through the kingdom. The rivalry between Roman and Celtic practice was settled at the Synod of Whitby in 664. Northumbria produced the historian Bede, whose Ecclesiastical History was completed at Jarrow in 731, and the illuminated Lindisfarne Gospels.

Decline and fall

Viking raiders sacked Lindisfarne in 793, and in 867 the Great Heathen Army captured York, ending the Northumbrian royal line in Deira. The north survived under Anglo-Saxon earls at Bamburgh while the south became the Scandinavian kingdom of York. The last king, Eric Bloodaxe, was expelled and killed in 954, after which Northumbria became an earldom within a unified England.

Legacy

The name survives in the modern county of Northumberland and in regional identity across the North East. The Northumbrian dialect of Old English is an ancestor of modern Scots and the northern English dialects.
