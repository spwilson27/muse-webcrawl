Mansa Musa

Mansa Musa (reigned c. 1312 – c. 1337) was the ninth mansa (emperor) of the Mali Empire in West Africa. During his reign Mali may have been the largest producer of gold in the world, and Musa has been called one of the wealthiest people in history. He is best known for his spectacular pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324–25, which introduced Mali to the wider Islamic world and to European mapmakers, and for developing Timbuktu and Gao into major centres of trade, scholarship and Islamic learning.

Accession and empire

Musa came to power after his predecessor, Mansa Abu Bakr II, abdicated to lead a fleet of ships exploring the Atlantic Ocean and never returned — an account preserved by the Arab historian al-Umari. Musa was a grandson (or grand-nephew) of Sundiata Keita, founder of the empire. At its height under Musa, Mali controlled the great trading cities of the Niger Bend, including Timbuktu, Gao and Djenné, and dominated the trans-Saharan trade in gold bound for North Africa and salt from the desert mines of Taghaza.

The pilgrimage of 1324

Musa's hajj is among the most famous journeys of the medieval world. His caravan reportedly numbered tens of thousands of followers, including 12,000 slaves each dressed in fine silk, and a baggage train of camels carrying gold. In Cairo, Musa spent and gave away so much gold that its price in Egypt was depressed for years afterwards — an episode recorded by al-Umari from interviews with the emir who hosted him. On the journey Musa hired scholars and, according to tradition, the Andalusian poet-architect Abu Ishaq al-Sahili, who designed mosques including the Djinguereber mosque in Timbuktu, built of mudbrick with timber beams.

Legacy

Musa's patronage turned Timbuktu into a renowned centre of Islamic scholarship, centred on the Sankore mosque-university, whose libraries and scholars attracted students from across the Muslim world. The fame of his wealth reached Europe: the Catalan Atlas of 1375 depicts him enthroned, holding a nugget of gold, with a caption describing him as the richest and noblest king in the land. He was succeeded by his son Maghan I and then by his brother Suleiman. Although the empire declined after the 14th century, Mansa Musa remains a symbol of African wealth and learning, frequently cited in popular accounts as the richest man who ever lived — a claim historians regard as impossible to verify or quantify precisely.
