Pharyngitis

An inflamed, reddened pharynx typical of viral pharyngitis.

Pharyngitis is inflammation of the back of the throat, known as the pharynx. It typically results in a sore throat and fever. Other symptoms may include a runny nose, cough, headache, difficulty swallowing, swollen lymph nodes, and a hoarse voice. Symptoms usually last 3–5 days, but may be longer depending on the cause.

Causes

Most cases are caused by viral infections, such as the common cold (rhinovirus), influenza, mononucleosis (Epstein–Barr virus), adenovirus, measles, and herpes simplex. Bacterial causes account for a minority of cases, the most important being group A streptococcus (GAS), which causes "strep throat" in roughly 15–30% of cases in children and 5–15% in adults. Other bacteria include Chlamydia pneumoniae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and Neisseria gonorrhoeae. Irritants such as smoke, dry air, acid reflux, and allergies can also inflame the pharynx.

Diagnosis

Because treatment differs, clinicians try to distinguish streptococcal from viral pharyngitis. Scoring systems such as the Centor criteria (fever, tonsillar exudates, tender anterior cervical lymph nodes, and absence of cough) estimate the probability of GAS. A rapid antigen detection test or throat culture confirms the diagnosis.

Management

Viral pharyngitis is managed supportively, with analgesics such as ibuprofen or acetaminophen, warm fluids, and rest. Antibiotics, most often penicillin or amoxicillin, are indicated only for confirmed or strongly suspected group A streptococcal infection, where they shorten symptoms modestly and prevent complications such as rheumatic fever and peritonsillar abscess. Antibiotics are ineffective and discouraged for viral cases.

Epidemiology

Sore throat is one of the most common reasons for primary-care visits; in the United States it accounts for millions of outpatient visits each year, with peak incidence in children aged 4–7 years and during colder months for respiratory viruses.
