Mithridates VI of Pontus

Mithridates VI Eupator (135–63 BC) was the ruler of the Kingdom of Pontus in northern Anatolia from 120 to 63 BC, and one of the Roman Republic's most formidable and determined opponents. Expanding his kingdom into a great Black Sea empire, he fought three Mithridatic Wars against Rome over nearly three decades, defying Sulla, Lucullus and finally Pompey.

Rise

According to legend, Mithridates spent seven years after his father's assassination hiding in the wilderness, hardening himself and — fearing poison — dosing himself with sub-lethal amounts of toxins to build immunity; the practice of acquiring tolerance this way is still called mithridatism, and a universal antidote, mithridatium, bore his name. As king he conquered Colchis, the Bosporan Kingdom and much of the Black Sea coast.

Wars with Rome

In 88 BC Mithridates invaded the Roman province of Asia and ordered the massacre of the Roman and Italian residents — the Asiatic Vespers, in which tens of thousands were killed. Sulla defeated him in Greece and forced the Peace of Dardanus (85 BC). War resumed twice more; Lucullus drove him from Pontus, but final defeat came at the hands of Pompey in 66 BC. Fleeing to his Bosporan kingdom, Mithridates planned an invasion of Italy, but his son Pharnaces rebelled. Unable to poison himself because of his acquired immunity, he ordered a bodyguard to kill him, in 63 BC.

Legacy

A polyglot said to speak the languages of all 22 nations under his rule, Mithridates was celebrated in Racine's tragedy Mithridate and Mozart's opera Mitridate, re di Ponto.
