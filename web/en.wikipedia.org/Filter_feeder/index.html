Filter feeder

A whale shark, the largest fish, is a filter feeder.

Filter feeders are animals that feed by straining suspended matter and food particles from water, typically by passing the water over a specialized filtering structure. Some animals that use this method of feeding are clams, krill, sponges, baleen whales, and many fish (including some sharks). Some birds, such as flamingos and certain species of duck, are also filter feeders. Filter feeders can play an important role in clarifying water, and are therefore considered ecosystem engineers. They are also important in bioaccumulation and, as a result, as indicator organisms.

Feeding strategies

Filter feeders range in size from a few millimetres (such as rotifers and some copepods) to the largest animals on Earth, the baleen whales. Many filter-feeding animals are sedentary or even sessile, relying on water currents to bring food particles to them. Others, such as ram-feeding whales and whale sharks, actively swim through concentrations of plankton with their mouths open.

Two broad strategies are recognised: suspension feeding, in which particles suspended in the water column are captured, and deposit feeding, in which particles are extracted from sediments. Suspension feeders use a variety of structures to trap particles, including mucous nets, setae (bristle-like structures), cilia, and baleen plates.

Examples

Bivalve molluscs such as oysters, mussels, and clams pump water across their gills, trapping phytoplankton and detritus in mucus that is then transported to the mouth. A single oyster can filter many litres of water per day. Sponges draw water through pores called ostia and capture bacteria and organic particles with choanocyte cells.

Among vertebrates, the baleen whales (Mysticeti) use plates of keratin hanging from the upper jaw to strain krill and small fish from the water. The whale shark, basking shark, and megamouth shark independently evolved filter feeding among fishes. Flamingos filter feed with their heads upside down, using lamellae in their beaks to trap algae and small crustaceans.

Ecological role

Filter feeders are important in nutrient cycling and water clarification. Dense beds of bivalves can remove substantial amounts of phytoplankton and suspended sediment from estuaries, improving water clarity and light penetration for seagrasses. Because they accumulate particles from large volumes of water, filter feeders also concentrate pollutants and pathogens, making them useful indicator organisms for environmental monitoring and giving them a role in the transfer of toxins through food webs.
