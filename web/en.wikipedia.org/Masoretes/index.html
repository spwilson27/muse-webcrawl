The Masoretes (Hebrew: בעלי המסורה, Ba'alei ha-Masora) were groups of Jewish scribe-scholars who worked between the 6th and 10th centuries CE, based primarily in the Jewish communities of Tiberias in ancient Galilee (modern Israel), Jerusalem, and Babylonia (modern Iraq). Their work fixed the precise text, pronunciation, and liturgical recitation of the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), whose consonants alone had previously been transmitted.

Work

The Masoretes devised a system of diacritics — the niqqud (vowel points) and cantillation marks (te'amim) — written above, below, and within the consonantal letters to preserve the traditional reading. They also compiled the Masorah: thousands of marginal notes counting verses, words, and letters, noting unusual spellings and rare forms, all designed to safeguard the text against copying errors. The Tiberian system of the Ben Asher family became authoritative; Aaron ben Moses ben Asher produced a model codex in the 10th century, and Maimonides later endorsed the Ben Asher tradition.

Manuscripts

The most famous Masoretic manuscripts are the Aleppo Codex (c. 920 CE), long regarded as the most authoritative copy of the Hebrew Bible, and the Leningrad Codex (1008 CE), the oldest complete manuscript of the Hebrew Bible, which underlies modern printed editions such as the Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia.
