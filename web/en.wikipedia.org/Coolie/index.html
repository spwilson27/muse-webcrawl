Coolie

Coolie (also spelled cooly, kuli, quli, koelie, etc.) is a term for a low-wage labourer, typically of Indian or Chinese descent. The word originated in the 17th century in India and spread to the rest of Asia, South Africa, the Caribbean and the Americas. The term carries pejorative connotations in modern usage.

Etymology

The term is believed to derive from the Hindi and Urdu word qulī (meaning a hired labourer), itself possibly from a Turkic word for slave. In South India, the Tamil word kuḷi means wages or hire. The Gujarati Koli people, an ethnic group in western India, have also been proposed as a source.

History

Following the abolition of slavery in the British Empire in the 1830s, colonial planters turned to indentured labour from India and China to work plantations in the Caribbean, Mauritius, Fiji, South Africa, Malaya, and elsewhere. Workers signed contracts (often under deceptive conditions) binding them for fixed terms of years in exchange for passage, wages, and sometimes land at the end of the term. Conditions were frequently harsh and the system was widely criticized as a "new system of slavery".

Chinese labourers, similarly recruited, worked on plantations in Cuba and Peru, on railroads in North America, and in mines in various colonies. The indenture system was gradually abolished in the early 20th century; Indian indenture ended in 1917–1920.

Modern usage

In modern South Asia, the word is still used neutrally in some places to mean a porter or day labourer (for example, railway porters in India), but in many contexts it is regarded as an ethnic slur against people of Asian descent and is generally avoided.
