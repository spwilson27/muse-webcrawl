Acculturation

Field: Anthropology, sociology, cross-cultural psychology

Related concepts: Assimilation, enculturation, transculturation, cultural diffusion

Key theorists: John W. Berry, Robert Redfield, Ralph Linton, Melville J. Herskovits

Acculturation is a process of social, psychological, and cultural change that stems from the balancing of two cultures while adapting to the prevailing culture of the society. Acculturation is a process in which an individual adopts, acquires and adjusts to a new cultural environment as a result of being placed into a new culture, or when another culture is brought to them. Individuals of a differing culture try to incorporate themselves into the new more prevalent culture by participating in aspects of the more prevalent culture, such as their traditions, but still hold onto their original cultural values and traditions. The effects of acculturation can be seen at multiple levels in both the devotee of the prevailing culture and those who are assimilating into the culture.

At a group level, acculturation often results in changes to culture, religious practices, health care, and other social institutions. There are also significant ramifications on the food, clothing, and language of those becoming introduced to the overarching culture. At the individual level, the process of acculturation may involve socialization and can cause effects in an individual's psychological well-being and mental health.

The concept of acculturation has been studied since antiquity by philosophers, historians and social scientists, but the term itself entered the anthropological vocabulary in the late nineteenth century. Over time, distinct theoretical models have been developed to describe how individuals and groups negotiate contact between cultures, most notably the fourfold model of psychologist John W. Berry, which distinguishes integration, assimilation, separation, and marginalization as strategies of acculturation.

Definitions and history

Acculturation is generally defined as the process of cultural and psychological change that results following meeting between cultures. The concept was first systematically used by American anthropologists in the 1880s in connection with studies of Native American peoples. J. W. Powell, writing for the United States Bureau of Ethnology, used the term to describe the changes that followed when two cultures came into contact. Through the following decades the concept was elaborated in ethnographic studies of contact situations in North America, the Pacific, and colonial Africa.

A classic definition was produced in 1935 in the famous memorandum prepared by Robert Redfield, Ralph Linton, and Melville J. Herskovits for the Social Science Research Council. According to the memorandum, acculturation "comprehends those phenomena which result when groups of individuals having different cultures come into continuous first-hand contact, with subsequent changes in the original cultural patterns of either or both groups." This definition emphasized that acculturation is a two-way process: although in practice one culture is often dominant, change can occur in both groups.

Acculturation is distinct from several related concepts. Assimilation describes a process in which an individual or group is absorbed into the dominant culture, typically losing its original cultural identity; acculturation, by contrast, does not necessarily imply the loss of the first culture. Enculturation (or socialization) is the process by which a person learns the requirements of the culture surrounding them in the first place, whereas acculturation concerns the acquisition of a second culture. Cultural diffusion is the spread of cultural items between groups, which may occur without the sustained first-hand contact implied by acculturation. Transculturation, a term coined by the Cuban anthropologist Fernando Ortiz, emphasizes the creation of entirely new cultural phenomena out of contact, loss, and acquisition.

Historical approaches

Early acculturation studies focused on colonized and indigenous peoples and often assumed a unidirectional flow of traits from a "donor" culture to a "recipient" one. Ethnographers documented changes in material culture, religion, kinship, and language under colonial rule, missionization, and trade. By the mid-twentieth century, scholars increasingly criticized the assumption that cultures were bounded, unchanging wholes and began to attend to the selectivity of borrowing: groups accept some traits, reject others, and reinterpret still others in terms of their existing values.

Sociologists and historians applied the concept to immigration, particularly to the United States, where successive waves of newcomers adapted to American institutions while transforming them. In the mid-twentieth century, Milton Gordon's influential account of assimilation in American life described stages through which immigrant groups entered the host society, of which cultural assimilation — essentially acculturation — was only the first. Later scholarship on transnationalism stressed that migrants may sustain ties to their countries of origin, producing hybrid identities that do not fit a simple movement from one culture to another.

Psychological acculturation

Cross-cultural psychology treats acculturation as a process experienced by individuals. Psychologist John W. Berry proposed that acculturating individuals face two central issues: the degree to which they wish to maintain their heritage culture and identity, and the degree to which they wish to engage with the larger society. The crossing of these two dimensions yields four acculturation strategies:

  • Integration — maintaining the heritage culture while also participating in the larger society (often associated with biculturalism).
  • Assimilation — relinquishing the heritage culture in favour of full participation in the new society.
  • Separation — maintaining the heritage culture while avoiding involvement with the larger society.
  • Marginalization — losing contact with both the heritage culture and the larger society.

Research associated with Berry and with large comparative projects such as the International Comparative Study of Ethnocultural Youth (ICSEY) has generally found that integration is associated with the most favourable psychological and sociocultural outcomes, and marginalization with the least favourable, although outcomes depend heavily on the receptiveness of the host society and on public policies such as multiculturalism.

The psychological experience of acculturation can involve "acculturative stress" — stress reactions such as anxiety and depression that arise from the challenges of adapting to a new culture, including language barriers, discrimination, and conflicts between generations within families. Scholars such as Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman's stress-and-coping framework have been applied to explain why similar circumstances produce very different outcomes for different people: appraisal of the situation and available coping resources mediate the relationship between cultural change and well-being.

Models and outcomes

Beyond Berry's fourfold model, researchers have proposed domain-specific and relative models. The Relative Acculturation Extended Model (RAEM), developed by Marisa Navas and colleagues in Spain, notes that immigrants may prefer different strategies in different domains of life: for example, favouring assimilation in the workplace while preferring separation in family and religious life. Such domain specificity complicates any simple classification of individuals.

At the societal level, outcomes of acculturation include the formation of plural societies, in which distinct communities coexist; melting-pot patterns, in which groups blend into a new composite culture; and situations of conflict or segregation. Government policies — ranging from forced assimilation, such as the removal of Indigenous children to residential schools in Canada and Australia, to official multiculturalism — profoundly shape which outcomes occur.

Language and acculturation

Language is one of the most visible arenas of acculturation. Immigrants and their children typically shift toward the dominant language over generations, a process described by the three-generation model: the first generation may remain dominant in its heritage language, the second becomes bilingual, and the third is primarily monolingual in the new language. Language shift has major consequences for identity, education, and access to services, and heritage-language maintenance is often a deliberate strategy of cultural preservation within families and diaspora communities.

Effects

Acculturation affects diet and health, as immigrant groups adopt the eating patterns of host countries — a phenomenon associated in some studies with rising rates of obesity and cardiovascular disease among newcomers, sometimes called the "immigrant health paradox" when initially healthier immigrants converge toward host-country health levels. It also affects education, intergenerational relations, and religious practice. For receiving societies, acculturation brings cultural enrichment, new cuisines, music, and economic dynamism, alongside political debates about integration, identity, and the limits of pluralism.
