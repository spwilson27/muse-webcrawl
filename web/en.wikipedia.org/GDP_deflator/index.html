GDP deflator

The GDP deflator measures the price level of all domestically produced goods and services

In economics, the GDP deflator (implicit price deflator) is a measure of the money price of all new, domestically produced, final goods and services in an economy in a year relative to the real value of those goods and services. It can be used to convert nominal GDP into real GDP, removing the effect of inflation.

Formula

GDP deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100

In the base year, nominal GDP and real GDP are equal, so the deflator equals 100. A deflator of 110 means that the overall price level has risen by about 10 percent since the base year.

Properties

Unlike the consumer price index (CPI), which is based on a fixed basket of consumer goods and services, the GDP deflator covers the prices of everything produced in the economy — consumption, investment, government spending, and exports — while excluding imports. Because its "basket" is the changing composition of current output, the deflator is a Paasche-type price index and is not subject to the same fixed-basket substitution bias as the CPI. Its coverage also means it reflects prices faced by businesses and government, not just households.

Use

National statistical agencies, such as the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, publish the GDP price index alongside GDP releases. Economists use the deflator to express economic aggregates in constant prices, compare living standards over time, and study economy-wide inflation trends.
