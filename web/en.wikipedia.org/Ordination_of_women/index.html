Ordination of women

The ordination of women to ministerial or priestly office is a contested practice within several religious traditions. In Christianity it has been debated since the nineteenth century: many mainline Protestant churches now ordain women on equal terms with men, while the Roman Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church reserve priestly ordination to men, as do Orthodox Judaism for rabbis and, in traditional practice, Islam for imams leading mixed congregations.

History

Antoinette Brown Blackwell was ordained by a Congregational church in 1853, a first in the United States; Olympia Brown followed in 1863 in the Universalist Church. Florence Li Tim-Oi became the first woman priest in the Anglican Communion in Hong Kong in 1944. The Church of England voted in 1992 to ordain women as priests (first ordinations 1994) and in 2014 to consecrate women as bishops, with Libby Lane consecrated in 2015. The irregular ordination of the "Philadelphia Eleven" in the U.S. Episcopal Church in 1974 preceded official approval in 1976.

Arguments

Supporters cite principles of equality, the gifts of women for ministry, and passages such as Galatians 3:28; opponents cite scriptural passages on male headship and the unbroken tradition of the early church. The Catholic position was restated in the 1976 declaration Inter Insigniores and the 1994 apostolic letter Ordinatio Sacerdotalis. In Judaism, Regina Jonas was privately ordained as the first woman rabbi in Germany in 1935; the Reform, Conservative, and Reconstructionist movements now ordain women, while Orthodoxy remains divided, with some ordaining women under titles such as maharat. Buddhist traditions have revived the bhikkhuni (nuns') ordination in Theravada countries amid continuing controversy.
