Extravehicular activity

An astronaut performing a spacewalk.

Extravehicular activity (EVA) is any activity done by an astronaut outside a spacecraft beyond Earth's appreciable atmosphere. In the United States it is usually called a spacewalk; Russian activities are often termed VKD. EVAs may be tethered, with the astronaut connected to the spacecraft by a safety line and umbilical, or untethered, using a propulsion unit such as the Manned Maneuvering Unit or the SAFER backpack.

History

The first EVA was performed by Soviet cosmonaut Alexei Leonov on March 18, 1965, from Voskhod 2; his suit ballooned in vacuum and he barely re-entered the airlock. The first American spacewalk followed on June 3, 1965, when Ed White floated from Gemini 4. During Apollo, astronauts conducted EVAs on the lunar surface and, on the return journeys of the final missions, in deep space to retrieve film canisters. The first untethered spacewalk was made by Bruce McCandless II in 1984.

Purposes and hazards

EVAs are performed for satellite servicing (famously the Hubble Space Telescope repair missions), space station assembly and maintenance, scientific experiments, and testing equipment. Hazards include micrometeoroids, radiation, extreme temperature swings, suit puncture, and—in 2013—water intrusion into a helmet that nearly drowned astronaut Luca Parmitano. Modern EVAs on the International Space Station typically last five to eight hours and require extensive preparation, including pre-breathing oxygen to avoid decompression sickness.

Records

Russian cosmonaut Anatoly Solovyev holds the record for the most EVAs and greatest cumulative time in spacewalks, with 16 outings totaling over 82 hours.
