Bureau of Land Management

The Bureau of Land Management (BLM) is an agency within the United States Department of the Interior responsible for administering federal lands. Headquartered in Washington, D.C., it manages more than 247 million acres (about one-tenth of the land area of the United States) and some 700 million acres of subsurface mineral estate, mostly in the western states and Alaska.

History

The BLM was created in 1946 by merging the General Land Office (established 1812) with the U.S. Grazing Service. Its lands are largely the remnant of the public domain not claimed by homesteaders or reserved for other purposes. For its first decades the bureau's work centred on grazing and mineral leasing; the Federal Land Policy and Management Act of 1976 (FLPMA) ended homesteading, declared a policy of retaining public land in federal ownership, and gave the BLM its modern multiple-use mandate.

Mandate and activities

Under the multiple-use and sustained-yield principles, the BLM balances energy development, livestock grazing, timber, recreation, conservation and rights-of-way. It issues grazing permits under the Taylor Grazing Act, administers oil, gas and coal leasing, manages wild horses and burros, and oversees the National Conservation Lands system, including national monuments, wilderness areas and wild and scenic rivers. More than 60 million visitors a year use BLM land for recreation, from hiking to off-road driving.

Controversies

The agency has been at the centre of disputes over grazing fees, sage-grouse protection, hydraulic fracturing rules and national monument boundaries, including the 2014 Bundy standoff in Nevada and the reduction and restoration of the Bears Ears and Grand Staircase-Escalante monuments.
