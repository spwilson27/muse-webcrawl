Embargo

An embargo is a government order that restricts commerce with a specified country or the exchange of specific goods, usually imposed for political, security or moral reasons. Embargoes are a form of economic sanction and may be partial — covering, for example, arms only — or comprehensive, covering nearly all trade.

How embargoes work

An embargo prohibits specified exports to, imports from, or both with the target state, and may be enforced through customs controls, financial restrictions and penalties against violators, including third-country firms. Unlike a naval blockade, an embargo is a legal measure rather than an act of war. Effectiveness varies: embargoes can raise the cost of objectionable policies, but may also harm civilian populations, encourage smuggling and import substitution, or fail if other states do not cooperate.

Notable examples

The United States embargo against Cuba, begun in 1960 and codified in later legislation, is among the longest-running modern embargoes. The 1973 oil embargo by Arab members of OPEC against states supporting Israel triggered the 1970s energy crisis. United Nations Security Council embargoes have targeted arms transfers to states and non-state actors in numerous conflicts, while the League of Nations' partial embargo on Italy in 1935 is a famous case of limited effect.

Related uses of the term

"Embargo" is also used for a prohibition on publication before a set date, as in a press embargo, and historically for orders detaining ships in port.
