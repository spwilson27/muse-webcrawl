In biology, detritus is dead particulate organic material, as distinguished from dissolved organic material. It typically includes the bodies or fragments of dead organisms, as well as fecal material. Detritus hosts communities of microorganisms that colonize and decompose it, and it forms the base of detrital food webs in many ecosystems.

Detritus in ecosystems

On land, detritus accumulates as leaf litter and other dead plant material on the soil surface, where detritivores and decomposers break it down and return its nutrients to the soil. In aquatic environments, detritus sinks through the water column—as the "marine snow" of the open ocean—and accumulates in sediments, supporting benthic communities far from the sunlit surface where the organic matter was produced.

Detrital food webs

Many organisms derive most of their energy from detritus rather than from living tissue. In estuaries, mangrove forests, and the deep sea, detritus-based food chains involving bacteria, detritivores, and their predators can exceed grazing food chains in importance. The processing of detritus is therefore a key control on nutrient cycling and carbon storage in soils and sediments.

Other uses of the term

In geology, detritus refers to rock fragments and mineral grains derived from the mechanical weathering and erosion of pre-existing rock; clastic sedimentary rocks such as sandstone are composed of such detrital material.
