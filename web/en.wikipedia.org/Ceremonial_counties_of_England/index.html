Ceremonial counties of England

The ceremonial counties of England, also known as the lieutenancy areas, are areas of England to which lord-lieutenants, the monarch's personal representatives, are appointed. Legally, the areas in England, Wales and Scotland known as "counties for the purposes of the lieutenancies" are defined by the Lieutenancies Act 1997. There are currently 48 ceremonial counties in England.

Role

Ceremonial counties serve principally as the basis for the lieutenancy and for the appointment of high sheriffs; they also provide a convenient general-purpose county geography used, for example, in the naming of some police forces and in sporting and cultural contexts. They are distinct from the metropolitan and non-metropolitan counties used for local government, from the historic counties, and from the registration and postal counties of earlier periods, although they are frequently aligned with them.

History

The lieutenancies trace back to the mid-16th century, when lord-lieutenants began to be appointed to the historic counties. The arrangements were substantially recast by the Local Government Act 1888, aligned with the new administrative counties by the Local Government Act 1972 (effective 1974), and adjusted again in the 1990s during the creation of unitary authorities. The Lieutenancies Act 1997 consolidated the boundaries into the present list, which includes counties such as Rutland that had temporarily lost separate status, and large conurbation areas such as Greater London, Greater Manchester, the West Midlands, Tyne and Wear, Merseyside and South and West Yorkshire.

Relation to other geographies

Some ceremonial counties correspond exactly to a single top-tier local authority area; others, such as Kent, combine the county council area with a unitary authority (Medway). The ceremonial county of the City of London is exceptional: the sovereign's lieutenant there is a commission headed by the Lord Mayor.
