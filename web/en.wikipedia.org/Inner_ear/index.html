The inner ear (internal ear, auris interna) is the innermost part of the vertebrate ear. Situated in the temporal bone, it consists of a system of fluid-filled cavities — the bony labyrinth — containing structures responsible for hearing and for the sense of balance.

Anatomy

The bony labyrinth comprises three parts: the cochlea, a spiral-shaped cavity dedicated to hearing; the vestibule, containing the utricle and saccule, which detect linear acceleration; and the three semicircular canals, which detect rotational movements of the head. Within the bony labyrinth lies the membranous labyrinth, filled with endolymph and surrounded by perilymph.

Function

In the cochlea, sound vibrations set the basilar membrane in motion, bending hair cells in the organ of Corti. These sensory cells convert mechanical energy into electrical signals carried by the cochlear nerve to the brain. In the vestibular apparatus, hair cells respond to head movements and gravity, providing the brain with information used to maintain balance and stabilise gaze.

Clinical significance

Disorders of the inner ear include sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus, Ménière's disease, labyrinthitis, and benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV). Cochlear implants can restore a sense of hearing to people with severe cochlear damage by directly stimulating the auditory nerve.
