Amyloid

Amyloid deposits show apple-green birefringence under polarized light when stained with Congo red.

Amyloids are aggregates of proteins characterized by a fibrillar morphology of typically 7–13 nm diameter, a β-sheet secondary structure (known as cross-β), and the ability to be identified by characteristic staining with dyes such as Congo red. In the human body, amyloids have been linked to the development of various diseases; pathologically, an amyloid is identified by its extracellular deposition in tissues.

Structure

The defining structural feature of amyloid is the cross-β fold, in which β-strands run perpendicular to the long axis of the fibril and hydrogen-bond along it, producing an exceptionally stable, insoluble filament. More than 30 different human proteins are now known to form amyloid deposits in disease, including amyloid beta in Alzheimer's disease, transthyretin in cardiac amyloidosis, and immunoglobulin light chains in AL amyloidosis.

History of the term

The name amyloid comes from the early mistaken identification by botanist Matthias Schleiden in 1838 of the waxy amyloid substance in plants; the German pathologist Rudolf Virchow applied the term to human tissue deposits in the 1850s, believing them to be starch-like (Latin amylum). Functional amyloids also exist in healthy organisms — for example in bacterial curli fibres and in the storage of peptide hormones — and some fungi and insects exploit the amyloid fold for normal physiological purposes.
