Danube Delta

The Danube Delta is the second-largest river delta in Europe, after the Volga Delta, and is the best preserved on the continent. It lies where the Danube river flows into the Black Sea, mostly in Dobruja, Romania, with a smaller northern part in Ukraine. Covering about 5,165 km² including the Razim–Sinoe lagoon complex, it was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1991 and is protected as a biosphere reserve.

Geography

Near Tulcea the Danube splits into three main branches — Chilia in the north (carrying roughly 60% of the flow), Sulina in the middle (dredged and straightened for navigation, linking the sea to the port of Tulcea) and Sfântu Gheorghe in the south. Between them lie a maze of channels, lakes, floating reed islands (plaur), marshes and oak forests on levees, such as Letea Forest, where wild horses roam among lianas. The delta grows seaward by up to tens of metres a year in places, as the river deposits some 40 million tonnes of sediment annually — though upstream dams have reduced this load.

Wildlife

The delta hosts over 300 species of birds, including the largest colony of great white pelicans in Europe, pygmy cormorants, red-breasted geese and white-tailed eagles, and 45 freshwater fish species, including sturgeons that produce prized caviar. Mammals include otters, wildcats and the introduced muskrat and raccoon dog. Some 15,000 people live in the delta's scattered villages, including the Lipovans — descendants of Russian Old Believers — who traditionally fish from wooden boats.

Conservation

Under Ceaușescu, large areas were drained for agriculture; after 1990 the Romanian Danube Delta Biosphere Reserve was established and some polders were re-flooded. Threats include poaching, overfishing, shipping along the Ukrainian Bystroye canal, and nutrient pollution carried down the Danube.
