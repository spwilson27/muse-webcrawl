Elasticity (economics)

In economics, elasticity measures the responsiveness of one economic variable to a change in another. If the price elasticity of demand is high, a small change in price causes a large change in the quantity demanded; if it is low, quantity demanded changes little. The concept was developed by Alfred Marshall in his Principles of Economics (1890).

Price elasticity of demand

Ed = (% change in quantity demanded) / (% change in price)

Demand is described as elastic when the absolute value of the ratio exceeds 1, unit elastic when it equals 1, and inelastic when it is below 1. Goods with close substitutes, luxuries and goods taking a large share of income tend to be elastic; necessities such as staple foods and fuels tend to be inelastic.

Other elasticities

Economists also use income elasticity of demand, cross-price elasticity between two goods, and price elasticity of supply. Elasticities are crucial to tax incidence analysis: the side of the market that is less elastic bears more of a tax, whether or not the tax is levied on it.

Applications

Governments and firms estimate elasticities to forecast revenue, set prices and anticipate the effects of taxes and subsidies, for example on tobacco, alcohol and sugary drinks.
