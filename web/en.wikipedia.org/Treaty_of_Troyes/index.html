The Treaty of Troyes was an agreement signed on 21 May 1420 at Troyes, France, under which King Henry V of England and his heirs would inherit the throne of France on the death of King Charles VI, in place of Charles's own son, the Dauphin Charles. It is one of the most remarkable settlements of the Hundred Years' War.

Background

France was weakened by Charles VI's recurrent mental illness and by civil war between the Armagnac and Burgundian factions. The assassination of John the Fearless, Duke of Burgundy, by the Dauphin's followers at Montereau in 1419 drove his son Philip the Good into alliance with England. After Henry V's conquest of Normandy (following the victory at Agincourt in 1415), Queen Isabeau of Bavaria and the Burgundians negotiated terms under which the Dauphin was disinherited — the treaty text blamed his "horrible crimes", a reference to the Montereau murder. The treaty was ratified by the Estates-General in Paris in December 1420.

Terms

Henry V was recognised as regent of France during Charles VI's lifetime and as heir to the French crown. He married Charles's daughter Catherine of Valois on 2 June 1420, receiving a large dowry, and undertook to conquer the lands still held by the "so-called Dauphin" — while formally preserving French laws, institutions and the separate identity of the two kingdoms. The settlement created the so-called dual monarchy of England and France.

Collapse

The plan collapsed because Henry V died first, on 31 August 1422, followed weeks later by Charles VI (21 October 1422). The crowns of both kingdoms passed to Henry's infant son, Henry VI, proclaimed in Paris and London, while the Dauphin, crowned Charles VII at Reims in 1429 with the aid of Joan of Arc, fought on. Under John, Duke of Bedford's regency, Anglo-Burgundian France held the north, but after the Burgundians defected by the Treaty of Arras (1435) and French military reforms took effect, the English position disintegrated; by 1453 England retained only Calais. The legal claim embodied at Troyes nonetheless persisted: English and later British monarchs continued to style themselves kings or queens of France until 1801.
