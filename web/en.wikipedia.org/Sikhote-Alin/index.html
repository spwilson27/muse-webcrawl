Sikhote-Alin

Sikhote-Alin
Location: Primorsky and Khabarovsk Krais, Russia
Length: about 900 km (560 mi)
Highest point: Mount Tardoki Yani, 2,090 m (6,857 ft)
Designation: UNESCO World Heritage Site (2001, extended 2018)

The Sikhote-Alin (Russian: Сихотэ́-Али́нь) is a mountain range in Primorsky and Khabarovsk Krais, Russia, extending about 900 kilometres (560 mi) to the northeast of the Russian Pacific seaport of Vladivostok. The highest summits are Mount Tardoki Yani (2,090 m), Mount Ko (2,003 m) and Mount Anik (1,933 m).

Geography and ecology

The range drains eastward to the Sea of Japan; its western slopes feed the Ussuri River and ultimately the Amur. The Sikhote-Alin supports a remarkable mixture of taiga and temperate forest, where northern species such as reindeer and brown bear live alongside southern species — most famously the Amur tiger (Siberian tiger) and the endangered Amur leopard. Because of this biodiversity, the Central Sikhote-Alin was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2001, with the Bikin River valley added in 2018.

Meteorite

The range gave its name to the Sikhote-Alin meteorite, one of the largest observed meteorite falls in recorded history, which fell in the mountains on 12 February 1947; fragments of the iron meteorite are preserved in museums worldwide.

Nature reserves

Protection began early: the Sikhote-Alin Nature Reserve (Zapovednik), founded in 1935 and named after the range, is one of Russia's oldest and largest strict nature reserves and a leading centre for tiger research. The explorer Vladimir Arsenyev travelled through the range in the early 1900s with his guide Dersu Uzala, later celebrated in Arsenyev's books and Akira Kurosawa's film.
