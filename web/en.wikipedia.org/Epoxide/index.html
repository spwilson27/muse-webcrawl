Epoxide

An epoxide is a cyclic ether with a three-membered ring consisting of one oxygen atom and two carbon atoms. The simplest epoxide is ethylene oxide (oxirane). Because the ring is highly strained, epoxides are far more reactive than ordinary ethers, making them valuable intermediates in organic synthesis and industry.

The epoxide functional group: a three-membered ring of two carbons and one oxygen

Synthesis

Epoxides are commonly prepared by the oxidation of alkenes with peroxy acids (the Prilezhaev reaction, often using mCPBA), by base-induced ring closure of halohydrins, and by catalytic asymmetric methods such as the Sharpless epoxidation of allylic alcohols and the Jacobsen epoxidation. Industrially, ethylene oxide is produced by direct silver-catalyzed oxidation of ethylene, and propylene oxide is made by the chlorohydrin or hydroperoxide processes.

Reactions

Nucleophiles open the epoxide ring under acidic or basic conditions, typically attacking the less substituted carbon under basic conditions and the more substituted carbon under acidic conditions. Ring opening with water gives vicinal diols; with amines, alcohols, and other nucleophiles it gives a wide range of bifunctional products. Polymerization of epoxides yields polyethers, and epoxides cured with amines form epoxy resins, widely used as adhesives and coatings.
