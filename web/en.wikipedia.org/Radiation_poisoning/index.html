Radiation poisoning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. This article is about acute radiation syndrome. For chronic effects, see Radiation sickness (disambiguation).

Radiation poisoning, formally acute radiation syndrome (ARS), is a collection of health effects that are caused by being exposed to high amounts of ionizing radiation in a short period of time. Symptoms can start within an hour of exposure and can last for several months. Early symptoms are usually nausea, vomiting, and loss of appetite; in the following hours or weeks, complications arise from damage to rapidly dividing tissues, particularly bone marrow, the gastrointestinal tract, and, at the highest doses, the neurovascular system.

Causes and mechanism

ARS results from a dose of penetrating radiation to a large part of the body, typically more than about 0.7 gray (Gy) delivered over minutes to hours. Sources include nuclear weapons, reactor accidents (such as Chernobyl in 1986), criticality accidents, and mishandled industrial or medical radiation sources. Ionizing radiation damages DNA and kills cells, hitting hardest the stem cells of the blood-forming and digestive systems.

Stages and subsyndromes

The course of ARS is classically divided into a prodromal phase (immediate nausea and vomiting), a latent phase of apparent recovery, a manifest illness phase, and then either recovery or death. Depending on dose, the dominant subsyndromes are hematopoietic (roughly 0.7–10 Gy), gastrointestinal (roughly 10–50 Gy), and neurovascular (above roughly 50 Gy). The dose lethal to 50% of exposed people within 60 days (LD50/60) without treatment is around 4–5 Gy; with supportive care it is higher.

Treatment

There is no cure that reverses radiation damage; treatment is supportive and includes decontamination, infection prevention, blood transfusions, and colony-stimulating factors such as filgrastim to aid marrow recovery. Specific decorporation agents exist for internal contamination by particular radionuclides — for example, potassium iodide for radioiodine and Prussian blue for caesium. Bone marrow transplantation has been used in a few severe cases with limited success.

History

Cases accumulated among early radiologists, the victims of the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, workers in criticality accidents (such as Louis Slotin in 1946), the Chernobyl liquidators and firefighters, and victims of lost-source accidents such as the 1987 Goiânia accident.
