Wardian case

The Wardian case was an early type of terrarium, a sealed protective container for plants. It was invented by Dr. Nathaniel Bagshaw Ward (1791–1868) in about 1829, after he accidentally discovered that plants could thrive in a sealed glass container without additional watering.

History

Ward, a London physician with a passion for botany, found that a fern and a grass moth pupa sealed in a bottle survived because moisture evaporated from the soil, condensed on the glass, and returned to the soil, while photosynthesis kept the air fresh. He published his findings in On the Growth of Plants in Closely Glazed Cases (1842).

Impact

The Wardian case revolutionised the transport of live plants around the world. Plants that previously died on long sea voyages could now survive for months. It enabled the transfer of tea plants from China to India, rubber trees from Brazil to Malaya, and quinine-producing cinchona from South America to Asia, profoundly shaping colonial economies and global agriculture.

Design

A typical Wardian case resembled a miniature greenhouse: a wooden base holding soil, glazed sides and a sloped glass roof, often with handles for lifting. Plants were watered before sealing and then left undisturbed for the voyage.
