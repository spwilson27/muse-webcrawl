Plantation

A sugar plantation in the tropics

A plantation is an agricultural estate, generally centred on a plantation house, meant to be a self-sufficient settlement for the planting of cash crops, usually grown for export. The crops grown include cotton, cannabis, coffee, tea, cocoa, sugar cane, opium, sisal, oil seeds, oil palms, fruits, rubber trees and forest trees. In modern usage the term also covers large-scale commercial forestry estates.

History

The plantation system expanded dramatically with European colonisation. Sugar plantations on Atlantic islands — Madeira, the Canaries and São Tomé — provided the model that was transferred to Brazil and the Caribbean from the sixteenth century, based on the labour of enslaved Africans transported across the Atlantic. In British North America and the United States, tobacco, indigo, rice and above all cotton plantations in the South rested on slavery until emancipation in 1865, after which sharecropping and tenant farming succeeded it. Plantation agriculture elsewhere employed indentured labourers, notably Indian workers carried to Fiji, Mauritius, Malaya and the Caribbean.

Modern plantations

In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, plantations have been associated with multinational companies producing palm oil, rubber, tea, bananas and timber, particularly in Southeast Asia, West Africa and Latin America. They are praised for export earnings and economies of scale but criticised for land conflicts, poor labour conditions, deforestation and biodiversity loss — palm-oil estates in Indonesia and Malaysia being a prominent focus of environmental campaigns. In forestry, plantation pine and eucalyptus stands supply much of the world's industrial wood.

In the American South, preserved plantation houses such as those along the River Road in Louisiana are now museums interpreting both planter life and the experiences of the enslaved people who worked the estates near cities such as Pittsburgh's trading partners down the Ohio and Mississippi rivers.
