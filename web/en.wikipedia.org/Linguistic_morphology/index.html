Linguistic morphology

Morphology
"Unbreakable" analysed as un- + break + -able

In linguistics, morphology is the study of words, how they are formed, and their relationship to other words in the same language. It analyses the structure of words and parts of words, such as stems, roots, prefixes and suffixes. The smallest unit of meaning in a language is the morpheme: for example, the English word unbreakable consists of three morphemes, un-, break and -able.

Basic concepts

  • Morpheme: the smallest meaningful unit; free morphemes can stand alone (break), while bound morphemes must attach to others (un-, -able).
  • Allomorphs: variant forms of a morpheme, such as the English plural pronounced /s/, /z/ or /ɪz/.
  • Lexeme and word form: a lexeme (e.g. go) is realised by word forms (go, goes, went, gone).

Processes

Inflection

Inflection modifies a word to express grammatical categories — tense, number, case, person, gender — without changing its basic meaning or word class: walk/walked, dog/dogs, Latin noun declensions and verb conjugations.

Derivation and compounding

Derivation creates new lexemes, often changing word class: happy → unhappy → happiness. Compounding combines two free forms: blackbird, houseboat. Other processes include reduplication, blending, clipping and conversion (zero derivation).

Morphological typology

Languages are classically classified by how they build words: isolating languages (e.g. Vietnamese, Mandarin) use few bound morphemes; agglutinative languages (Turkish, Finnish, Swahili) string together clearly separable affixes; fusional languages (Latin, Russian) fuse several meanings into single endings; and polysynthetic languages (Inuktitut, Mohawk) pack sentence-like content into single words.
