Dysarthria

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dysarthria is a condition of weakness or difficulty controlling the muscles used for speech. It is often characterized by slurred or slow speech, which can be difficult to understand. Common causes of dysarthria include nervous system disorders and conditions that cause facial paralysis or tongue and throat muscle weakness. Certain medications can also produce dysarthria.

Causes

Damage to the central or peripheral nervous system — from stroke, traumatic brain injury, brain tumors, Parkinson's disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), multiple sclerosis, or cerebral palsy — can weaken or discoordinate the muscles of the lips, tongue, jaw, vocal cords, and respiratory system used in speech.

Classification

Clinicians classify dysarthria by the site of the lesion and the resulting speech pattern: flaccid, spastic, ataxic, hypokinetic, hyperkinetic, and mixed types. The perceptual qualities of speech help localize the neurological damage.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is made by a speech-language pathologist, who assesses intelligibility, articulation, voice, resonance, and prosody. Treatment focuses on improving articulation, rate, breath support, and communication strategies; in severe cases, augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) devices may be used. Dysarthria differs from aphasia, which is a disorder of language formulation rather than speech execution.
