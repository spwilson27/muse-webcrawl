In psychoanalysis, psychosexual development is a central element of Sigmund Freud's theory of personality. Freud proposed that children pass through five stages in which the libido (psychic sexual energy) is focused on different erogenous zones; how each stage is negotiated shapes adult character. Unresolved conflict can produce fixation at a stage, expressed in adulthood as characteristic traits or neuroses. The theory was set out in the Three Essays on the Theory of Sexuality (1905) and remains historically influential, though scientifically controversial.

The stages

Oral stage

From birth to about one year, gratification centres on the mouth—feeding, sucking, and biting. Freud associated excessive or frustrated oral gratification with later "oral" traits such as dependence, smoking, or overeating.

Anal stage

From about one to three years, the focus is on bowel control and toilet training. Freud linked strict or lenient training to "anal-retentive" (orderly, stubborn) or "anal-expulsive" (messy, defiant) personalities.

Phallic stage

From about three to six years, the focus shifts to the genitals. Freud placed the Oedipus complex here—the child's desire for the opposite-sex parent and rivalry with the same-sex parent—resolved through identification; he also posited castration anxiety in boys and, controversially, "penis envy" in girls. Carl Jung proposed an analogous "Electra complex".

Latency stage

From about six years to puberty, sexual impulses are repressed and energy is channelled into school, friendships, and skills.

Genital stage

From puberty onward, mature sexual interests emerge, directed toward partners outside the family; successful passage of the earlier stages leads, in Freud's account, to a balanced, caring adult personality.

Criticism

The theory has been criticised for its lack of empirical falsifiability, its reliance on retrospective clinical case studies (such as "Little Hans"), and its phallocentric assumptions—criticised early on by Karen Horney and later by feminist scholars. Developmental psychologists favour empirically grounded stage theories (e.g., Erikson's psychosocial stages, which rework Freud's sequence in social terms). Nevertheless, psychosexual development remains a landmark in the history of psychology and a staple of psychoanalytic training.
