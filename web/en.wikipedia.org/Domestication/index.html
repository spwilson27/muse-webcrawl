Domestication is a multi-generational mutualistic relationship in which an animal species, such as humans, assumes significant long-term influence over the care and reproduction of another organism to secure a more predictable supply of resources. Through domestication, humans have produced domestic animals, crops and fungi adapted to human environments and needs, a process central to the rise of agriculture and settled societies.

Process and pathways

Archaeologists describe three broad pathways for animals: the commensal pathway, in which wild animals such as wolves and cats habituated themselves to human settlements; the prey pathway, in which hunted species such as sheep, goats and cattle were brought under herd management; and the directed pathway, a deliberate later effort exemplified by the horse and donkey. Plants were domesticated through selection — often unconscious — for traits such as non-shattering seeds, larger fruits and loss of toxins, beginning in the Neolithic Revolution about 12,000 years ago.

Domestication syndrome

Domesticated mammals share a suite of traits — docility, floppy ears, shorter snouts, smaller teeth, piebald coloration, prolonged juvenile behaviour — called the domestication syndrome. Dmitry Belyayev's farm-fox experiment, begun in Siberia in 1959, showed that selecting silver foxes purely for tameness produced these traits within a few dozen generations, suggesting they arise as linked by-products of selection on behaviour, possibly via mild deficits in neural crest cell development.

Major domesticates

The earliest domesticate was the dog, descended from wolves well before agriculture. The Fertile Crescent yielded sheep, goats, cattle and pigs, and crops including wheat, barley, lentils and peas; East Asia gave rice, millet, pigs and chickens; the Americas gave maize, beans, squash, potatoes, llamas and turkeys. Relatively few large mammal species have ever been domesticated — a fact Jared Diamond attributed to the "Anna Karenina principle" that candidate species must simultaneously satisfy requirements of diet, growth rate, temperament and social structure.
