Television network

A television network or television broadcaster is a telecommunications network for distribution of television program content, where a central operation provides programming to many television stations or pay television providers. Until the mid-1980s, television programming in most countries of the world was dominated by a small number of terrestrial networks; the subsequent growth of cable and satellite television greatly expanded the number of networks.

Structure

In countries such as the United States, broadcast networks supply programming to local affiliated stations, which broadcast it in their markets on schedules set or approved by the network. Affiliates are typically owned by separate station groups rather than by the network itself, although networks also own some flagship stations ("owned-and-operated" stations). Networks sell national advertising within their programs, while affiliates sell local advertising in designated breaks.

History

Television networks grew from radio networks in the 1940s. In the United States, NBC, CBS, and ABC—the "Big Three"—dominated for decades, joined later by Fox, The CW, and others. In many other countries, public broadcasters such as the BBC, NHK, and ARD were established as national networks, sometimes with monopolies that lasted until the late 20th century.

Types

Networks may be commercial or public, national or regional, and distributed by terrestrial broadcasting, cable, satellite, or the internet. Cable networks (channels) do not use local affiliates but are delivered directly by multichannel providers. Streaming services have disrupted the network model by distributing programming on demand without a broadcast schedule.
