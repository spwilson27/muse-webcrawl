Shani

Shani (Sanskrit: शनि, Śani), also known as Shanaishchara ("the slow-moving one"), is a Hindu deity associated with the planet Saturn and one of the nine heavenly bodies (Navagraha) in Hindu astrology. Shani is regarded as the god of karma, justice and retribution: he is believed to deliver the results of a person's deeds — good and bad — during their lifetime, and is both feared for the hardships he brings and revered as an impartial dispenser of justice.

Mythology

Shani is the son of Surya, the sun god, and Chhaya ("shadow"), the shadow-wife of Surya. His siblings include Yama, the god of death, and the river goddess Yamuna. According to legend, Shani's gaze is so powerful that even as an infant his first glance caused an eclipse or lamed his father's chariot horse; his look is said to bring misfortune on whomever it falls, which is why even the gods are portrayed as wary of him. Stories in the Puranas describe Shani's austerity and devotion to Shiva, who granted him the role of karmic judge.

Astrological significance

In Vedic astrology (Jyotisha), Shani is a malefic planet governing longevity, discipline, delay, labour and suffering. The period of Sade Sati — the roughly seven-and-a-half years when Saturn transits the sign before, of, and after one's natal Moon — is popularly dreaded as a time of trials, though astrologers also describe it as a period of maturation and karmic settling. Saturn's major period (mahadasha) in the Vimshottari system lasts 19 years.

Worship

Shani is worshipped especially on Saturdays. Devotees offer black sesame seeds, mustard oil, and iron, light oil lamps beneath peepal trees, and recite the Hanuman Chalisa or Shani mantras, Hanuman being traditionally believed immune to Shani's influence. Major temples include the Shani Shingnapur temple in Maharashtra, famous for its open-air black stone idol and the village custom of houses without doors, and temples at Thirunallar (associated with the legend of King Nala) and Kuchanur.

Iconography

Shani is depicted dark-complexioned, dressed in black or dark blue, riding a crow or vulture (or a chariot drawn by them), and holding a sword, arrows, an axe or a trident. In some depictions he is lame, reflecting legends of an injury to his leg.
