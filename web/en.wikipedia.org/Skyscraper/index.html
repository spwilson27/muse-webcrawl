A skyscraper is a tall, continuously habitable building, usually of more than about 40 floors, designed for residential or commercial use. Skyscrapers became possible through the invention of the safety elevator (1852, Elisha Otis) and steel-frame construction, which freed walls from bearing the building's weight.

History

The Home Insurance Building in Chicago (1885), ten storeys with a steel skeleton, is generally considered the first skyscraper. The Chicago and New York schools raced upward: the Woolworth Building (1913), the Chrysler Building and Empire State Building (both completed 1930–31) in New York. After mid-century, the International Style's glass boxes spread worldwide; the World Trade Center's Twin Towers (1971–73) and Chicago's Sears (Willis) Tower (1973) held the world height record in turn. Since the late 1990s the tallest buildings have risen in Asia and the Middle East — Taipei 101 (2004), then Dubai's Burj Khalifa (2010), at 828 m still the tallest human-made structure.

Design and construction

Modern supertall towers use a stiff core (often concrete) with outriggers, bundled tubes or diagrids to resist wind loads, which dominate design over gravity at great heights. Curtain walls of glass and metal hang from the frame. High-speed elevators, sky lobbies, tuned mass dampers and deep pile or raft foundations are standard features. Fire safety, evacuation and — since 2001 — progressive-collapse resistance are major design concerns.

Definitions and records

The Council on Tall Buildings and Urban Habitat (CTBUH) certifies records: "supertall" means over 300 m and "megatall" over 600 m. Height is measured to the architectural top, including spires but not antennas.
