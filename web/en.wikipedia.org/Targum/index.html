Targum

A targum (Aramaic: תרגום, plural targumim) is an Aramaic translation or paraphrase of the Hebrew Bible. Targumim arose when Aramaic became the everyday language of many Jewish communities and public readings of the Hebrew scriptures in synagogue were accompanied by oral Aramaic renderings provided by a translator, the meturgeman. Over time these translations were written down and became important works of biblical interpretation in their own right.

Major targumim

Targum Onkelos, on the Torah (Pentateuch), is the most authoritative targum; tradition attributes it to the proselyte Onkelos, and it is largely a careful, literal translation, printed in most editions of the Hebrew Bible alongside the text. Targum Jonathan, on the Prophets, is attributed to Jonathan ben Uzziel and includes more interpretive expansion. For the Writings there are later targumim of varying character; Targum Pseudo-Jonathan, on the Torah, is a highly expansive paraphrase incorporating much legendary material. The Palestinian targum tradition is represented by the Neofiti manuscript and fragmentary texts, and a separate Targum Sheni supplements the book of Esther.

Character

Targumim range from near-literal renderings to free paraphrases filled with midrashic interpretation. They characteristically soften anthropomorphic descriptions of God, using reverential circumlocutions such as Memra ("Word") and Shekhinta (divine presence). They are valuable witnesses to how scripture was understood in late antiquity, to the Aramaic language, and sometimes to older readings of the Hebrew text.

Use

Study of Targum Onkelos remains part of traditional weekly Torah study — the rabbinic requirement to review the weekly portion "twice in Hebrew and once in targum" — and Yemenite Jewish communities preserve the practice of public targum reading in synagogue.
