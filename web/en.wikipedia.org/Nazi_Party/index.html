The National Socialist German Workers' Party (German: Nationalsozialistische Deutsche Arbeiterpartei, NSDAP), commonly known in English as the Nazi Party, was a far-right political party in Germany active between 1920 and 1945 that created and supported the ideology of Nazism. Its precursor, the German Workers' Party (DAP), existed from 1919 to 1920.

History

Adolf Hitler joined the DAP in 1919, became its leader in 1921, and gave the party its 25-point programme and its swastika emblem. After the failed Beer Hall Putsch of November 1923 the party was banned and Hitler imprisoned, during which he dictated Mein Kampf. Re-founded in 1925, the party exploited the economic collapse of the Great Depression, rising from 2.6% of the vote in 1928 to become the largest Reichstag party in July 1932. Hitler was appointed Chancellor on 30 January 1933; within months the party had suppressed all opposition, and after President Hindenburg's death in August 1934 Hitler combined the offices of Chancellor and President as Führer.

Ideology

Nazism combined extreme German nationalism and the idea of Lebensraum (living space) with a racial hierarchy that cast "Aryans" as a master race and Jews, Roma, Slavs and others as subhuman. The party's antisemitism culminated in the Holocaust, the genocide of approximately six million Jews, alongside the persecution and murder of millions of other victims. Its anti-communism, Führer cult and rejection of democracy and liberalism were central tenets.

Rule and downfall

Under Nazi rule Germany rearmed, annexed Austria, destroyed Czechoslovakia and invaded Poland in September 1939, beginning the Second World War in Europe. After Germany's total defeat in 1945, the Allied Control Council formally dissolved the NSDAP in October 1945; at the Nuremberg trials its leadership corps and organisations such as the SS and Gestapo were declared criminal. The party and its symbols remain banned in Germany and several other countries.
