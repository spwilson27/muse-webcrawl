Nebhepetre Mentuhotep II (reigned c. 2061–2010 BCE) was an ancient Egyptian pharaoh of the Eleventh Dynasty who reunified Egypt after the fragmentation of the First Intermediate Period, thereby founding the Middle Kingdom. Egyptians of later ages honoured him almost as a second founder of the state, on a par with Narmer and Ahmose I. His name Mentuhotep means "Montu is content," invoking the Theban war god.

Reunification

Mentuhotep inherited the Theban kingdom of Upper Egypt from his father Intef III, while rival kings of the Ninth/Tenth Dynasty ruled the north from Herakleopolis. After a long struggle — commemorated by the tomb of his fallen warriors at Deir el-Bahari — he conquered the north around his 39th regnal year, took the Horus name Sematawy ("He who unifies the two lands"), and brought the whole country under one rule for the first time in more than a century. Further campaigns subdued Nubia and the oases and reopened trade with the Levant and Punt.

Building and administration

The reunified king reorganized the provincial administration, reducing the power of the nomarchs over time, and undertook building works throughout Egypt. His masterpiece is his mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari in western Thebes: a terraced, colonnaded complex combining a pyramid-like central structure with rock-cut tombs for his queens and court, revolutionary in design and later the model — on a grander scale — for Hatshepsut's famous temple beside it. His painted sandstone statue, found in the Bab el-Hosan cache, depicts him with dark skin, arms crossed in Osiride pose, wearing the Red Crown of Lower Egypt, symbolizing his rule over the reunified land.

Legacy

Mentuhotep II reigned about 51 years and was succeeded by his son Mentuhotep III. His reunification inaugurated the Middle Kingdom, regarded by Egyptians as a classical age of literature, art, and strong kingship.
