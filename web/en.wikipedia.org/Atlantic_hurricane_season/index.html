Atlantic hurricane season


A major hurricane in the Atlantic basin

The Atlantic hurricane season is the portion of the year when tropical cyclones — tropical storms and hurricanes — are most likely to form in the Atlantic Ocean basin, which includes the North Atlantic, the Caribbean Sea, and the Gulf of Mexico. The season officially runs from June 1 to November 30, a period chosen because it encompasses the great majority of Atlantic tropical cyclone formation, though storms occasionally form outside these dates.

Climatology

Activity peaks around September 10, when sea surface temperatures are warmest and atmospheric conditions — low vertical wind shear and moist air — are most favourable. On average, based on the 1991–2020 climatology, an Atlantic season produces about 14 named storms, of which roughly seven become hurricanes and three reach major hurricane strength (Category 3 or higher on the Saffir–Simpson scale). Seasons vary greatly depending on factors such as El Niño–Southern Oscillation, Atlantic sea-surface temperature patterns, and the position of the Bermuda High.

Forecasting and naming

Seasonal outlooks are issued by agencies and research groups including NOAA and Colorado State University. The National Hurricane Center in Miami monitors the basin and issues advisories. Storms are named from rotating six-year lists maintained by the World Meteorological Organization; names of especially destructive storms, such as Katrina (2005) and Maria (2017), are retired. The most active season on record was 2020, with 30 named storms, while the costliest was 2017.

Notable seasons

Historically destructive seasons include 2005 (which produced Katrina, Rita and Wilma), 2017 (Harvey, Irma and Maria), and 2024. Rising sea levels and warming oceans are expected to increase the rainfall and storm-surge impacts of Atlantic hurricanes even where overall storm frequency changes little.
