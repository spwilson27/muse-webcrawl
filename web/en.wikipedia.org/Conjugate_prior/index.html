Conjugate prior

Conjugate prior
Field: Bayesian statistics
Property: Posterior in same family as prior
Examples: Beta–binomial, Dirichlet–multinomial, Gamma–Poisson

In Bayesian statistics, a conjugate prior is a prior distribution that, when combined with a given likelihood function, yields a posterior distribution belonging to the same probability distribution family as the prior. Conjugacy makes Bayesian updating analytically tractable: the posterior can be computed by simply updating the prior's hyperparameters with summary statistics of the data.

Examples

The beta distribution is conjugate to the binomial likelihood: a Beta(α, β) prior with observed successes and failures yields a beta posterior whose parameters are the old ones plus the counts. The Dirichlet distribution is conjugate to the multinomial; the gamma distribution is conjugate to the Poisson; and the normal distribution is conjugate to itself for inference about a normal mean with known variance.

Interpretation

Hyperparameters of conjugate priors can often be interpreted as pseudo-observations — imaginary data points representing prior experience. This makes it easy to express how strongly the prior should influence conclusions.

Limitations

Conjugate priors exist only for likelihoods in the exponential family in the standard cases, and convenience may come at the cost of realism: the true prior beliefs may not match any conjugate form. Modern computational methods such as Markov chain Monte Carlo have reduced the need for conjugacy, though it remains useful for closed-form updates, online learning, and as building blocks in larger models.
