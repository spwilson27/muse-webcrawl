The Battle of Crécy took place on 26 August 1346 in northern France and was one of the most important battles of the Hundred Years' War. The combined impact of new weapons and tactics — massed English longbowmen, dismounted men-at-arms, and possibly some early cannon — led a small English army under Edward III to a decisive victory over a much larger French host led by Philip VI.

Background

Edward III landed in Normandy in July 1346 and marched north on a great chevauchée, sacking Caen and coming within sight of Paris before crossing the Somme at the ford of Blanchetaque. Choosing ground near Crécy-en-Ponthieu, the English dismounted and prepared a defensive position on a ridge.

Battle

The French arrived piecemeal late in the day and attacked against advice. Genoese crossbowmen opened the action but were outshot by the longbowmen and hampered by a sudden downpour that slackened their bowstrings; as they recoiled, French cavalry charged through them. In the gathering dusk the French launched repeated charges — tradition says as many as fifteen — against the English line, where dismounted men-at-arms fought supported by archers. All failed. French dead included King John of Bohemia (who famously charged while blind, with his horse's reins tied to those of his companions), the Duke of Lorraine, and thousands of knights and men-at-arms — perhaps a third of the chivalry of France. English losses were remarkably light.

Consequences

Edward marched on to besiege and capture Calais, which remained English for over 200 years. Crécy demonstrated the growing power of disciplined infantry and missile weapons over feudal cavalry, a lesson repeated at Poitiers (1356) and Agincourt (1415). The emblem of the Prince of Wales — three ostrich feathers — is traditionally linked to the arms of the fallen King John of Bohemia and his motto Ich dien ("I serve").
