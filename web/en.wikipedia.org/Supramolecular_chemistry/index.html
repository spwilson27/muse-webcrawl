Supramolecular chemistry

A self-assembled supramolecular structure held together by hydrogen bonds.

Supramolecular chemistry is the branch of chemistry concerning chemical systems composed of discrete molecules held together by non-covalent interactions, rather than by covalent bonds. The term was popularized by Jean-Marie Lehn, who defined it as "chemistry beyond the molecule". Supramolecular structures arise through hydrogen bonding, metal coordination, hydrophobic effects, van der Waals forces, π–π interactions, and electrostatic effects.

History

The field's foundations include the host–guest chemistry of crown ethers (Charles J. Pedersen), cryptands (Jean-Marie Lehn), and spherands (Donald J. Cram); the three shared the 1987 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing molecules with structure-specific interactions of high selectivity. Earlier conceptual roots lie in Emil Fischer's "lock and key" model of enzyme binding (1894) and in studies of clathrates and inclusion compounds.

Concepts

Molecular self-assembly is the spontaneous association of molecules into ordered aggregates under thermodynamic control. Molecular recognition involves the selective binding of a guest by a complementary host. Mechanically interlocked molecules such as rotaxanes and catenanes, developed notably by Fraser Stoddart and others, extend supramolecular chemistry into molecular machines, recognized by the 2016 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Applications

Supramolecular systems are used in catalysis, drug delivery, sensing, materials science, and nanotechnology. Examples include self-healing polymers, supramolecular gels, molecular capsules that stabilize reactive intermediates, and template-directed synthesis of complex architectures.
