Political convention

A political convention is a meeting of a political party, typically held to select candidates, adopt a party platform, and conduct party business. The term is most closely associated with the national nominating conventions of the United States, where the Democratic and Republican parties formally nominate their candidates for President and Vice President of the United States, but conventions are used by parties in many countries.

United States nominating conventions

National conventions in the United States developed in the 1830s as a replacement for the congressional caucus system of nomination. Delegates chosen through primaries and caucuses gather every four years, usually in the summer before a presidential election, to vote on the party nominee, adopt rules and a platform, and rally support for the general election campaign. Since the reforms following the 1968 Democratic National Convention, the outcome is normally determined in advance by primary elections, making modern conventions largely ceremonial and theatrical events designed for television.

Historical significance

Before the primary era, conventions were genuine decision-making bodies in which balloting could extend through many rounds; the 1924 Democratic convention required 103 ballots. Contested conventions, in which no candidate arrives with a majority of delegates, remain possible in principle. Conventions have also been sites of major political conflict, including the 1968 Democratic convention in Chicago, marked by anti-war protests and clashes with police.

Other countries

Parties in parliamentary systems, such as party conferences in the United Kingdom and party congresses in Germany, use conventions to elect leaders, debate policy, and set manifestos, though candidate selection for head of government is usually intertwined with parliamentary leadership.
