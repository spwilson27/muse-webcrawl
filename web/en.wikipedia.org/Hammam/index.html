Hammam

A hammam, also known as a Turkish bath, is a type of public steam bath associated with Islamic culture and the wider Middle East, North Africa and parts of Europe. It developed from Roman and Byzantine bathing traditions and became a central social institution in Ottoman cities.

Architecture and ritual

A traditional hammam progresses through rooms of increasing heat: a changing and resting hall, a warm room, and a hot room, often domed with small glass openings that admit shafts of light. Bathers wash, sweat on a heated marble platform (the göbektaşı), and may receive scrubbing with a coarse mitt (kese) and massage. Ritual purity in Islam gave bathing an important religious as well as social role.

History

Public baths flourished under the Umayyads and reached elaborate forms in Ottoman Istanbul, where famous examples such as the Çemberlitaş Hamamı were designed by the architect Sinan in the 16th century. Hammams were important social spaces, especially for women. Victorian Turkish baths adapted the idea in Britain and America in the 19th century.

Modern use

Historic hammams still operate in Turkey, Morocco and elsewhere, now serving tourists as well as local bathers, while the word is also used for steam rooms in spas worldwide.
