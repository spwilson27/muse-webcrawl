Jainism is an ancient Indian religion that traces its spiritual ideas through a lineage of twenty-four tirthankaras, the last of whom, Mahavira (6th century BCE), is regarded as its great teacher. Its central tenet is ahimsa — nonviolence toward all living beings.

Beliefs

Jains believe every soul is inherently divine and capable of liberation (moksha) from the cycle of rebirth by shedding karma, conceived as a subtle material that clings to the soul. The three jewels of Jain practice are right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct. Other key doctrines include anekantavada (many-sidedness of truth), non-possession (aparigraha), and the equality of all life forms, which underlies strict vegetarianism.

Sects and practice

The two major sects are the Digambara ("sky-clad"), whose monks renounce clothing, and the Shvetambara ("white-clad"). Lay Jains observe the five vows in modified form, practice fasting (notably during the festival of Paryushana), and have historically been prominent in Indian commerce, scholarship, and temple building — producing celebrated sites such as the Dilwara Temples and the colossal statue of Bahubali at Shravanabelagola.

History

Jainism arose in the Gangetic plain during a period of religious ferment shared with early Buddhism. It spread across India under royal patronage and survives today mainly in western India, with about 4 to 5 million adherents worldwide.
