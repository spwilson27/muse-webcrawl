Great Migration (African American)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Great Migration was the movement of about six million African Americans out of the rural Southern United States to the urban Northeast, Midwest, and West between roughly 1910 and 1970. It was one of the largest internal migrations in American history and profoundly reshaped the nation's cities, culture, and politics.

Causes

Migrants fled the South's Jim Crow segregation, disenfranchisement, lynching and racial violence, and the economic dead ends of sharecropping, worsened by boll weevil infestations and floods. They were drawn north and west by industrial jobs opened by the First and Second World Wars, by labour recruiters, and by the advocacy of Black newspapers such as the Chicago Defender, which urged readers to come north. The mechanisation of Southern agriculture after 1940 pushed a second, larger wave.

Course and destinations

The First Great Migration (1910–1940) carried about 1.6 million people to cities such as New York, Chicago, Detroit, Cleveland, and Pittsburgh. The Second Great Migration (1940–1970) moved some five million more, many to the West Coast — Los Angeles, Oakland, Seattle, Portland — for defence industry work. Migrants travelled established routes: from Mississippi up the Illinois Central to Chicago, from the Carolinas up the East Coast to New York and Philadelphia.

Impact

The migration shifted Black population and political power to the cities, fuelled the Harlem Renaissance and the growth of Black music — blues, jazz, gospel, and later Motown and rock and roll — and created the urban constituencies that sustained the civil rights movement. It also met resistance: restrictive covenants, redlining, and race riots (as in Chicago in 1919 and Detroit in 1943) shaped segregated northern ghettos. Since the 1970s a "New Great Migration" has seen many African Americans return to a changed, metropolitan South. Jacob Lawrence's Migration Series (1940–41) and Isabel Wilkerson's The Warmth of Other Suns (2010) are landmark depictions.
