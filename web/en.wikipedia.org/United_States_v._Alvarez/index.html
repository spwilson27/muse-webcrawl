United States v. Alvarez, 567 U.S. 709 (2012), is a landmark United States Supreme Court case in which the Court struck down the Stolen Valor Act of 2005, which criminalised false claims of having received a military decoration or medal. The Court held, 6–3, that the Act violated the First Amendment's free speech protections.

Background

Xavier Alvarez, a member of a California water district board, introduced himself at a 2007 board meeting by falsely claiming to be a retired United States Marine who had received the Congressional Medal of Honor. He had never served in the military. Charged under the Stolen Valor Act, Alvarez pleaded guilty while reserving the right to challenge the statute's constitutionality. The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals held the Act unconstitutional, reasoning that false statements are not categorically outside the First Amendment.

Decision

Justice Anthony Kennedy, joined by Chief Justice John Roberts and Justices Ruth Bader Ginsburg and Sonia Sotomayor, delivered a plurality opinion holding that content-based restrictions on speech are presumptively invalid and that the government had not shown the Act satisfied strict scrutiny. Justice Stephen Breyer, joined by Justice Elena Kagan, concurred in the judgment, applying a form of intermediate scrutiny and emphasising that the government could pursue its goals through less restrictive means, such as a database of actual medal recipients.

Dissent

Justice Samuel Alito, joined by Justices Antonin Scalia and Clarence Thomas, dissented, arguing that false statements about military honours inflict real harm on the integrity of the awards system and have little or no First Amendment value.

Aftermath

In response to the decision, Congress passed the Stolen Valor Act of 2013, which narrowed the offence to those who falsely claim military honours with the intent to obtain money, property or another tangible benefit. Alvarez's case remains a leading precedent on the constitutional status of false speech.
