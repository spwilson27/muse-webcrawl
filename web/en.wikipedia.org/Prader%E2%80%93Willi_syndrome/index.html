Prader–Willi syndrome

Prader–Willi syndrome (PWS) is a genetic disorder caused by the loss of function of genes on the paternal copy of chromosome 15q11–13. It is the most common syndromic cause of life-threatening obesity in children.

Signs and symptoms

Infants with PWS typically have severe hypotonia and feeding difficulties. From early childhood, hypothalamic dysfunction produces an insatiable appetite (hyperphagia) that leads to morbid obesity unless food access is strictly controlled. Other features include short stature, small hands and feet, intellectual disability, behavioral problems, hypogonadism, and characteristic facial features.

Genetics

The disorder arises through genomic imprinting: the relevant genes are normally expressed only from the father's chromosome. PWS results from a paternal deletion (about 70% of cases), maternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 15, or imprinting defects. The mirror-image condition from the maternal deletion is Angelman syndrome.

Management

There is no cure; treatment centers on strict dietary control, structured environments, and growth hormone therapy, which improves height, body composition, and muscle tone. The syndrome is named for Andrea Prader and Heinrich Willi, who described it in 1956.
