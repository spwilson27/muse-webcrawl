Human rights

Human rights are moral principles and legal norms that set standards of human behaviour and are regularly protected in national and international law. They are commonly understood as inalienable, fundamental rights to which every person is inherently entitled simply for being human, regardless of nationality, sex, ethnicity, religion or other status.

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted by the UN in 1948.

History

Ideas of rights appear in ancient law codes and religious traditions, but the modern concept emerged from natural law philosophy and Enlightenment thought — notably John Locke — and political milestones such as Magna Carta (1215), the English Bill of Rights (1689), the American Declaration of Independence (1776) and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789).

The international framework

After the Second World War and the Holocaust, the United Nations adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in 1948, drafted by a committee chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt. It was followed by binding treaties including the International Covenants on Civil and Political Rights and on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (1966), and conventions against torture and discrimination and on the rights of the child.

Generations and debates

Analysts often group rights into "generations": civil and political rights; economic, social and cultural rights; and collective rights such as development and a healthy environment. Debates continue over universality versus cultural relativism, enforcement, and the duties of states and corporations. Regional systems — the European Court of Human Rights, the Inter-American and African systems — provide judicial enforcement in much of the world.
