Steam devil

A steam devil is a small, weak whirlwind over water or wet land that has drawn fog into the rotating mesovortex, rendering it visible. They form over lakes and oceans during cold air outbreaks, when very cold air passes over comparatively warm water.

Formation

When frigid air is much colder than the water below, the water vapour evaporating from the surface saturates the shallow layer of air above it, producing steam fog. If there is vertical wind shear or a pre-existing area of rotation near the surface, the convective updrafts can concentrate the rotation into a narrow column, visibly lifting the fog in a spiralling tube. Steam devils are usually tens of metres tall and a few metres across, with weak winds, and they last only a few minutes.

Occurrence

They are most often reported over the Great Lakes in winter, and over other lakes, rivers, hot springs, and occasionally above power plant cooling ponds. A notable outbreak was photographed over Lake Michigan during the January 1994 cold wave. Steam devils are far weaker than waterspouts and are not considered dangerous, though they are a striking winter phenomenon related to "sea smoke" (Arctic sea smoke).
