Mid-ocean ridge

A mid-ocean ridge (or mid-oceanic ridge) is an underwater mountain system formed by plate tectonics. It consists of various mountains linked in chains, typically having a valley known as a rift running along its spine. This type of oceanic mountain ridge is characteristic of an oceanic spreading center, responsible for seafloor spreading. The global mid-ocean ridge system is the longest mountain range in the world, extending some 65,000 km through all the major oceans.

Formation

Mid-ocean ridges mark divergent plate boundaries where two tectonic plates move apart. As they separate, magma from the mantle rises to fill the gap, solidifying into new oceanic crust. The ridge crest stands high because young crust is hot and buoyant; as crust ages and cools it subsides, forming the deep abyssal plains. Spreading is classified as slow (e.g., the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, ~25 mm/yr) or fast (e.g., the East Pacific Rise, over 100 mm/yr), which strongly affects ridge morphology: slow ridges have a pronounced central rift valley, while fast ridges form smooth rises.

Discovery

The existence of a mountain range under the Atlantic was first suggested by Matthew Fontaine Maury in 1850 and confirmed by soundings during the laying of transatlantic telegraph cables. Systematic mapping by Marie Tharp and Bruce Heezen in the 1950s revealed the rift valley running along the ridge crest — a key observation supporting seafloor spreading and, ultimately, plate tectonics. Symmetric magnetic anomalies on either side of ridges, documented by Vine, Matthews, and Morley in 1963, provided decisive evidence.

Hydrothermal vents

Seawater circulating through ridge crust is heated and discharged at hydrothermal vents, including "black smoker" chimneys discovered in 1977 near the Galápagos Rift. These vents support chemosynthetic ecosystems independent of sunlight and are sites of metal-rich mineral deposits.
