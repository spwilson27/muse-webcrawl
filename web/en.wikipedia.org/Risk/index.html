Risk is the possibility of something bad happening — the potential for loss, harm or other adverse outcomes arising from uncertainty. In formal usage, risk is often expressed as a combination of the probability of an event and the severity of its consequences.

Definitions

The international standard ISO 31000 defines risk as the "effect of uncertainty on objectives", noting the effect may be positive or negative. Economist Frank Knight's 1921 distinction remains influential: risk applies where outcomes are unknown but their probabilities are measurable, whereas uncertainty (Knightian uncertainty) applies where probabilities cannot be quantified.

Risk assessment and management

Risk management follows a cycle of identifying hazards, assessing likelihood and impact, evaluating controls, and treating the risk — by avoidance, reduction, transfer (for example through insurance), or acceptance. Risk registers and matrices are standard tools in engineering, project management and occupational safety. In public health, relative and absolute risk quantify how exposures change disease incidence.

Risk in finance

In finance, risk is the variability of returns; measures include volatility (standard deviation), beta, and Value at Risk (VaR), the maximum loss expected over a horizon at a given confidence level. The risk–return tradeoff holds that higher expected returns generally require bearing more risk — the basis of portfolio theory and the capital asset pricing model. Financial risk divides into market, credit, liquidity and operational risk.

Perception of risk

Psychologists such as Paul Slovic have shown that lay risk perception is shaped by dread, familiarity and voluntariness as much as by statistics: people overestimate rare, dramatic risks (plane crashes) and underestimate common ones (road traffic). This gap between perceived and statistical risk complicates risk communication.
