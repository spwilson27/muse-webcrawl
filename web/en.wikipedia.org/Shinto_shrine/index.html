Shinto shrine

A Shinto shrine (神社, jinja) is a structure whose main purpose is to house ("enshrine") one or more kami, the spirits or deities of the Shinto religion. There are estimated to be around 80,000 Shinto shrines in Japan. The shrine is a place where the kami are enshrined and worshipped, and where visitors may pray, make offerings, and participate in festivals.

Structure

The most important building of a shrine is the honden (main hall), where the enshrined kami's sacred object, the shintai, is kept. The honden is usually closed to the public. Worshippers pray at the haiden (hall of worship), and offerings are presented at the heiden (hall of offerings). The shrine precinct (keidai) is typically entered through one or more torii gates, which mark the transition from the mundane to the sacred. A purification font (temizuya) allows visitors to rinse their hands and mouth before approaching.

History

In early Shinto, kami were worshipped at sacred natural sites — mountains, waterfalls, and ancient trees — without permanent buildings. Permanent shrine architecture developed under the influence of Buddhism from the 6th century CE onward. Styles such as shinmei-zukuri (seen at Ise Grand Shrine), taisha-zukuri (Izumo Taisha), and sumiyoshi-zukuri preserve archaic features. Ise Grand Shrine is ritually rebuilt every 20 years in the ceremony known as Shikinen Sengu.

Shrine Shinto and festivals

Most shrines belong to the Association of Shinto Shrines (Jinja Honcho), founded in 1946 after the disestablishment of State Shinto. Shrines host festivals (matsuri) throughout the year, in which the kami may be carried through the community in a portable shrine (mikoshi). Kannushi (priests) and miko (shrine maidens) conduct rituals, and visitors commonly draw fortunes (omikuji) and purchase amulets (omamori).
