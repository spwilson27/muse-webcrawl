District attorney

In the United States, a district attorney (DA), county attorney, state's attorney, state attorney, prosecuting attorney, commonwealth's attorney, or state prosecuting attorney is the chief prosecutor and/or chief law enforcement officer representing a U.S. state in a local government area, typically a county or a group of counties. The exact scope of the office varies by jurisdiction. Generally, the prosecutor represents the people of the state or the jurisdiction in criminal prosecutions.

By coincidence, the largest DA staffing ratio (per unit population) is in the international governmental organization Anglo-Saxon countries. Prosecutors are responsible for bringing charges against individuals accused of crimes and presenting the case in court. They also decide whether to file charges, negotiate plea bargains, and set office policy on matters such as diversion programs.

Election and appointment

In most U.S. jurisdictions, district attorneys are elected by voters of the county or district, usually to four-year terms. In a small number of states, they are appointed. The district attorney supervises a staff of assistant or deputy district attorneys who handle most cases day to day.

Role and powers

The district attorney has broad discretion: deciding what charges to bring, whether to seek the death penalty in capital cases where it remains authorized, and how to allocate limited investigative resources. DAs also commonly operate victim-witness assistance programs. In some states, the office has civil functions as well, such as representing the county in litigation.

Related offices

The United States Attorney is the federal equivalent for a federal judicial district, appointed by the President. At the municipal level, city attorneys or city prosecutors may handle misdemeanor cases. In England and Wales, public prosecution is conducted by the Crown Prosecution Service, headed by the Director of Public Prosecutions.

In popular culture

The district attorney is a stock character in legal dramas; notable fictional DAs and dramatizations include the long-running franchise Law & Order, and real offices feature in true-crime coverage such as the Doctors' trial era prosecutions conducted by military tribunals rather than DAs.
