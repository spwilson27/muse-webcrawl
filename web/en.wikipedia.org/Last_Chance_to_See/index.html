Last Chance to See

Last Chance to See is a 1989 BBC radio documentary series and its accompanying book, written and presented by Douglas Adams and zoologist Mark Carwardine. The series follows the pair as they travel the world in search of animals on the verge of extinction, including the aye-aye of Madagascar, the Komodo dragon of Indonesia, the kakapo of New Zealand, the baiji (Yangtze river dolphin) of China, the northern white rhinoceros of Zaire (now the Democratic Republic of the Congo), and the Rodrigues fruit bat. It is regarded as one of Adams's most significant works outside science fiction, and the book he considered among his favorites.

Background

The project began with a 1985 magazine assignment in which Adams traveled to Madagascar for The Observer's colour supplement to write about the aye-aye. Adams and Carwardine subsequently proposed a radio series in which they would seek out some of the world's rarest animals before they vanished. The broadcasts weave Adams's characteristically humorous travel writing with serious discussion of habitat loss, hunting and pollution.

Species featured

  • Aye-aye (Madagascar) — a nocturnal lemur persecuted in local folklore.
  • Komodo dragon (Indonesia) — the world's largest lizard.
  • Kakapo (New Zealand) — a flightless, nocturnal parrot.
  • Baiji (China) — the Yangtze river dolphin, declared functionally extinct in 2006.
  • Northern white rhinoceros (Zaire) — hunted for its horn; only two females survived by 2018.
  • Rodrigues fruit bat (Mauritius) — rescued from near-extinction by captive breeding.
  • Amazonian manatee (Brazil) and the mountain gorilla (Zaire/Rwanda) appear in related segments.

Sequel

In 2009, BBC Two broadcast Last Chance to See as a television series in which Stephen Fry retraced the journeys with Carwardine, twenty years on, revisiting the species Adams had documented. A follow-up book, Last Chance to See: In the Footsteps of Douglas Adams, accompanied the series.

Legacy

Adams described the project as the work of which he was proudest, and it helped crystallize his later conservation advocacy. Several featured species have since declined further — the baiji has been declared functionally extinct and the northern white rhinoceros survives only as two females — giving the work an elegiac status in environmental literature.
