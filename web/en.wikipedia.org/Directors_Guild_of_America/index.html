The Directors Guild of America (DGA) is an entertainment guild that represents the interests of film and television directors in the United States motion picture industry and abroad. Founded in 1936 as the Screen Directors Guild, it merged with the Radio and Television Directors Guild in 1960 to form the modern DGA.

Role

The Guild negotiates and enforces collective bargaining agreements — the Basic Agreement and the Freelance Live and Tape Television Agreement — establishing minimum compensation, working conditions, residual payments, and creative rights for its members, who include directors, unit production managers, assistant directors, associate directors, and stage managers. The DGA is known for rarely striking; its negotiations typically conclude before contract expiration.

Awards

The Guild presents the annual Directors Guild of America Awards, first given for 1948 productions. The DGA Award for Outstanding Directorial Achievement in Feature Film is a strong predictor of the Academy Award for Best Director: only rarely has the DGA winner failed to win the corresponding Oscar.

Notable history

King Vidor was the Guild's first president. In 1950 the Guild was embroiled in controversy when president Joseph L. Mankiewicz was targeted by Cecil B. DeMille over a loyalty-oath dispute during the McCarthy era; Mankiewicz survived the recall attempt. The DGA also historically enforced the rule that a film have only one director credit, a rule that long prevented most directing teams and is associated with the "Alan Smithee" pseudonym used from 1968 to 2000 for directors disowning a film.
