Franchising

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Franchising is a method of distributing products or services involving a franchisor, who establishes the brand's trademark or trade name and a business system, and a franchisee, who pays a royalty and often an initial fee for the right to do business under the franchisor's name and system. The contract binding the parties is called a franchise agreement.

History

Arrangements resembling franchising date to the Middle Ages, but the modern system developed in the United States in the 19th century, when companies such as Singer Sewing Machine and Coca-Cola licensed independent operators to sell and distribute their products. Business-format franchising expanded rapidly after the Second World War, led by restaurant chains such as McDonald's, which combined a trademark license with a complete standardized operating system.

Fees and control

Franchisees typically pay an initial franchise fee, ongoing royalties based on a percentage of gross sales, and contributions to a common advertising fund. In exchange, the franchisor provides training, site selection assistance, supply chains, and marketing. Franchisors exercise substantial control over how outlets operate in order to protect the brand, while franchisees own and manage their individual units and bear most of the local business risk.

Regulation

Many countries regulate franchising to protect prospective franchisees. In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission's Franchise Rule requires franchisors to provide a Franchise Disclosure Document containing information about fees, obligations, litigation history, and financial performance representations before a franchise is sold. Some countries and states also regulate termination and renewal of franchise agreements.
