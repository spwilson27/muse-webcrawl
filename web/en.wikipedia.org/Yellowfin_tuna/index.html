Yellowfin tuna

Yellowfin tuna
Thunnus albacares
Family: Scombridae
Conservation status: Least Concern (IUCN)

The yellowfin tuna (Thunnus albacares) is a species of tuna found in the pelagic waters of tropical and subtropical oceans worldwide. Known as ahi in Hawaii, it is one of the largest tuna species, reaching up to about 2.4 metres (7.9 ft) in length and 200 kg (440 lb) in weight, though it is smaller than the Atlantic and Pacific bluefin tunas.

Description

The yellowfin has a torpedo-shaped, metallic dark-blue body, a silvery belly (often patterned with broken vertical lines), and bright yellow dorsal and anal fins and finlets, from which it takes its name. In large adults the second dorsal and anal fins become very long, sometimes exceeding 20% of the body length. Like other tunas it is warm-bodied (regionally endothermic), a fast, powerful swimmer capable of speeds over 70 km/h.

Ecology

Yellowfin tuna are highly migratory and travel in schools, often mixed with skipjack tuna and associated with dolphins, whale sharks, floating objects and other marine life. They feed on fish, crustaceans and squid, and are preyed upon by sharks, billfish and toothed whales. Spawning occurs year-round in warm equatorial waters, peaking in summer.

Fisheries

Yellowfin is one of the most commercially important tunas, caught mainly by purse seine and longline fleets for canning (as "light" tuna) and for the fresh and sashimi markets, where its ruby lean flesh is prized for ahi dishes. Global catches run to well over a million tonnes annually. Management by regional fisheries bodies has seen one stock — in the Indian Ocean — assessed as overfished, while the species overall is listed by the IUCN as Least Concern following a 2021 reassessment.
