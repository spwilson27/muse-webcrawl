Overjustification effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The overjustification effect is a phenomenon in which offering an expected external incentive for an already intrinsically rewarding activity can reduce a person's intrinsic motivation to perform that activity. When this occurs, people begin to attribute their behaviour to the external reward rather than to their own interest, and when the reward is later withdrawn, engagement in the activity may fall below its original level.

Classic experiments

The effect was demonstrated in experiments by Mark Lepper, David Greene, and Robert Nisbett (1973). Preschool children who enjoyed drawing were divided into groups: one group was promised a certificate for drawing, one received the certificate unexpectedly, and one received nothing. Afterwards, in free play, the children who had been promised the reward spent significantly less time drawing than the other groups. Edward Deci reported a parallel finding with adults solving puzzles for money (1971).

Explanations

Self-perception theory holds that people infer their own motives by observing their behaviour and its circumstances: if an obvious external reward is present, they discount internal interest as the cause. Cognitive evaluation theory, part of self-determination theory, distinguishes the controlling aspect of a reward — which undermines autonomy — from its informational aspect, such as verbal praise conveying competence, which can sustain or enhance intrinsic motivation.

Conditions and debate

The undermining effect is most reliable for expected, tangible rewards offered for simply performing an interesting task. It is weak or absent for boring tasks (where there is little intrinsic motivation to lose) and for unexpected rewards or sincere praise. Meta-analyses, notably by Deci, Koestner, and Ryan (1999), confirmed the effect for tangible expected rewards, while other researchers have argued the practical magnitude is modest. The topic remains debated in education and workplace management, where it informs caution about paying students for reading or tying creative work to controlling incentives.
