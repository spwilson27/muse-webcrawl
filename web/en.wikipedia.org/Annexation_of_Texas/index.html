Annexation of Texas

The annexation of Texas was the incorporation of the Republic of Texas into the United States of America, completed on 29 December 1845, when Texas became the 28th state. The annexation fulfilled the expansionist doctrine of Manifest Destiny but inflamed sectional tensions over slavery and provoked war with Mexico.

Background

Texas had won independence from Mexico in the Texas Revolution of 1835–1836. The republic's government, and most of its Anglo-American settlers, sought annexation by the United States, but Washington hesitated: annexation threatened war with Mexico, which never recognised Texan independence, and would add a large slaveholding territory, upsetting the sectional balance. A treaty of annexation negotiated under President John Tyler was rejected by the Senate in 1844.

The election of 1844 and joint resolution

Democrat James K. Polk campaigned in 1844 on annexation and won. Instead of a treaty requiring a two-thirds Senate majority, Congress passed a joint resolution — the Tyler Texas Annexation Resolution, signed 1 March 1845 — offering statehood on terms allowing Texas to divide into up to five states and retain its public lands while assuming its debts.

Admission

Texas accepted: a convention drafted a state constitution, voters approved annexation in October 1845, and Congress admitted Texas on 29 December 1845. The formal transfer of government took place on 19 February 1846, when President Anson Jones lowered the republic's flag.

Consequences

Mexico severed relations, and disputes over the Texas–Mexico boundary (the Nueces Strip) led to the Mexican–American War (1846–1848), after which Mexico ceded its northern territories. The annexation's terms shaped the Compromise of 1850, which fixed Texas's present boundaries in exchange for federal assumption of its debt.
