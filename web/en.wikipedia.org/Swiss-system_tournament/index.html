Swiss-system tournament

A Swiss-system tournament is a non-eliminating tournament format that features a fixed number of rounds of competition, but considerably fewer than in a round-robin tournament. Competitors meet one-on-one in each round and are paired against opponents with similar running scores, but not the same opponent more than once. The winner is the competitor with the highest aggregate points at the end.

History

The system was first used in a chess tournament in Zürich in 1895, devised by Julius Müller, and it remains the most common format for open chess tournaments worldwide. Variants are used in many other games and sports, including Magic: The Gathering, Scrabble, petanque, and some esports competitions.

Procedure

In the first round, players are usually seeded and paired top-half against bottom-half, or paired randomly. In subsequent rounds, players with equal scores are paired against each other as far as possible, with tie-breaking rules and colour or side balancing applied in chess. Because players of similar strength meet, a clear winner typically emerges within a manageable number of rounds, while every participant plays in every round — unlike single-elimination formats.

Analysis

Criticisms of the system include that late-round pairings can produce strategic incentives (such as agreed draws in chess) and that tiebreak systems, such as the Buchholz score, are needed to separate players on equal points.
