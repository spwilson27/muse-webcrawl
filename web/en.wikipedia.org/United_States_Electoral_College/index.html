United States Electoral College

The United States Electoral College is the group of presidential electors that is formed every four years during the presidential election for the sole purpose of voting for the president and vice president. The process is described in Article II of the U.S. Constitution and the Twelfth Amendment. Each state appoints a number of electors equal to its total representation in Congress (senators plus representatives), and the District of Columbia receives three under the Twenty-third Amendment, for a total of 538 electors; an absolute majority of 270 electoral votes is required to win.

How it works

When voters cast ballots in a presidential election, they are formally choosing their state's slate of electors pledged to a candidate. In 48 states and D.C., the candidate winning the statewide popular vote receives all of that jurisdiction's electoral votes; Maine and Nebraska award two votes to the statewide winner and one per congressional district. The electors meet in their state capitals in December to cast ballots, which are then counted by Congress on January 6. Electors who vote against their pledge are called "faithless electors"; in Chiafalo v. Washington (2020) the Supreme Court held that states may punish them.

History and disputes

The framers designed the College as a compromise between election by Congress and direct popular vote. Five elections — 1824, 1876, 1888, 2000, and 2016 — produced a president who lost the national popular vote. The disputed elections of 1800 and 1824 were decided by the House of Representatives. Each such outcome has renewed debate between defenders, who cite federalism and the need for geographically broad support, and critics, who argue the system overweights small states and swing states while making most voters' ballots irrelevant to the outcome.

Reform efforts

Hundreds of constitutional amendments to abolish or reform the College have been proposed in Congress; none has succeeded. The National Popular Vote Interstate Compact, adopted by a number of states and D.C. totaling over 200 electoral votes, would commit member states to award their electors to the national popular-vote winner once signatories control 270 electoral votes.
