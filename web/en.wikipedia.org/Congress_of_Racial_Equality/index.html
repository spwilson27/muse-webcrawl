Congress of Racial Equality

Congress of Racial Equality (CORE)
Founded: 1942, Chicago, Illinois
Founders: James Farmer, George Houser, James R. Robinson, Bernice Fisher
Focus: Civil rights, nonviolent direct action

The Congress of Racial Equality (CORE) is an African-American civil rights organization founded in Chicago in 1942. One of the "Big Four" civil rights organizations of the 1960s (alongside the NAACP, the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, and the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee), CORE pioneered the use of nonviolent direct action modelled on Gandhian methods against segregation in the United States.

Early years

CORE grew out of the Fellowship of Reconciliation. Its founders — including James Farmer, George Houser, and Bernice Fisher — organized sit-ins against segregated restaurants in Chicago in 1942–43, years before the tactic became famous. In 1947 CORE sponsored the Journey of Reconciliation, an interracial bus ride through the upper South testing enforcement of the Supreme Court's ban on segregation in interstate travel, whose participants included Bayard Rustin.

Freedom Rides and the 1960s

In 1961, under national director James Farmer, CORE organized the Freedom Rides, in which interracial groups rode interstate buses into the Deep South to challenge segregation in terminals. The riders were met with mob violence in Alabama, bringing federal intervention and national attention. CORE also co-organized the 1963 March on Washington and, in 1964, the Freedom Summer voter-registration project in Mississippi; CORE workers James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner were murdered by Ku Klux Klan members that summer.

In the late 1960s, under Floyd McKissick, CORE moved toward Black Power and black economic development. Under Roy Innis, who became national director in 1968, the organization took more conservative positions, and its membership and influence declined, though it continues to operate.
