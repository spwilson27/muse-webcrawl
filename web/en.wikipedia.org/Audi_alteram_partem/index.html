Audi alteram partem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


"Hear the other side"

Audi alteram partem (or audiatur et altera pars) is a Latin phrase meaning "listen to (or hear) the other side", or "let the other side be heard as well". It is the principle that no person should be judged without a fair hearing in which each party is given the opportunity to respond to the evidence against them.

Natural justice

The maxim is one of the two traditional pillars of natural justice in common law systems, the other being nemo iudex in causa sua ("no one should be a judge in their own cause", the rule against bias). A fair hearing generally requires notice of the case to be answered and a reasonable opportunity to present one's side.

History

The principle appears in ancient Greek drama and philosophy — the idea is voiced in Euripides and Seneca — and became embedded in Roman law, canon law, and the English common law. It is famously defended in English case law in cases such as Cooper v Wandsworth Board of Works (1863) and Ridge v Baldwin (1964), which extended fair-hearing requirements to administrative decisions.

Modern application

Today the principle underlies procedural fairness in administrative law, disciplinary proceedings, and international human rights instruments such as Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights, which guarantees a fair trial.
