Siege engine

A siege engine is a device designed to break or circumvent city walls and other fortifications in siege warfare. Some are simple, improvised tools such as the battering ram or the siege ladder, while others are complex mechanical devices such as the ballista, mangonel, and trebuchet. Siege engines were employed from antiquity until the advent of effective gunpowder artillery made most of them obsolete.

History

Siege engines were used in the ancient Near East, Greece, and Rome. Assyrian reliefs depict battering rams and siege towers attacking walled cities. Greek and Roman engineers developed torsion artillery such as the ballista and onager. The Romans systematized siegecraft with circumvallation walls, ramps, and towers, notably at Alesia and Masada.

Medieval engines

In medieval warfare, the counterweight trebuchet — capable of hurling heavy stones against castle walls — became the dominant heavy siege weapon from the 12th century onward. Other devices included the siege tower, the mantlet, the cat (a covered structure for miners), and various forms of catapult. Defenders countered with hoardings, murder-holes, and counter-mining.

Decline

The introduction of cannon in the 14th and 15th centuries gradually rendered torsion and counterweight engines obsolete, as gunpowder artillery could breach medieval curtain walls that had resisted earlier engines. Fortification design responded with the low, thick walls of the star fort.
