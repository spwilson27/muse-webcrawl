Moral luck

Moral luck is a philosophical problem concerning the extent to which moral assessment of an agent depends on factors beyond the agent's control. The term was brought to prominence by Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel in papers first presented in 1976, which exposed a tension between the intuition that people should be judged only for what is under their control — the control principle — and the evident role of luck in ordinary moral judgement.

Nagel's four kinds of luck

  • Resultant luck: luck in how one's actions turn out. Two equally reckless drivers, only one of whom hits a pedestrian, are judged very differently.
  • Circumstantial luck: luck in the situations one faces — whether one ever confronts a moral test at all.
  • Constitutive luck: luck in one's character, temperament, and inclinations.
  • Causal luck: luck in being determined by antecedent circumstances — the problem of free will and determinism.

The problem

Nagel argued that as the area of genuine agency shrinks under reflection, the self that can be praised or blamed seems to dwindle toward nothing — yet abandoning moral judgement is not an option. Williams, through cases such as a Gauguin-like painter abandoning his family for art, argued that rational justification of life decisions can itself depend on luck, challenging the Kantian aspiration to make morality immune to fortune.

Legal and practical echoes

Criminal law institutionalises resultant luck: attempted murder is punished less than completed murder, though the defendant's choices may be identical. Philosophical responses range from denying that true moral luck exists, to accepting luck while revising the control principle, to distinguishing kinds of assessment to which luck is or is not relevant.
