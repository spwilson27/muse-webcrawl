Sorites paradox

Sorites paradox
FieldLogic, philosophy of language
OriginAttributed to Eubulides of Miletus (4th century BC)
Also known asParadox of the heap
Core issueVagueness of predicates such as "heap" or "bald"

The sorites paradox (from Greek sōritēs, "of a heap") is a paradox arising from the vagueness of ordinary language predicates. In its classic form: a million grains of sand form a heap; removing one grain never turns a heap into a non-heap; yet repeated removal eventually leaves a single grain—or none—which is plainly not a heap. The argument generates a contradiction by small, individually plausible steps. (For the Italian coastal town with a similar name, see Sorrento.)

Formulation

The paradox can be cast as a chain of conditional premises: if n grains make a heap, so do n − 1. Applying this by induction from a clear heap down to one grain forces the false conclusion that one grain is a heap. Equivalent versions use "bald", "tall", "rich", and colour terms—one hair or one grain never seems to make the difference, yet differences accumulate.

Proposed solutions

Major responses include: epistemicism (Williamson), holding there is a sharp but unknowable boundary; supervaluationism, treating vague statements as true only if true on all admissible sharpenings; fuzzy logic, assigning degrees of truth; contextualism, where the boundary shifts with conversational context; and nihilism about heaps, denying that such objects exist. The paradox remains a central test case for theories of vagueness.
