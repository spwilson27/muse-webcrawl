Hypoglycemia

Hypoglycemia, also called low blood sugar, is a fall in blood sugar to levels below normal. This may result in a variety of symptoms, including clumsiness, trouble talking, confusion, loss of consciousness, seizures and death. Feelings of hunger, sweating, shakiness and weakness may also be present. Hypoglycemia is conventionally defined in diabetes care as a blood glucose below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L), with a level below 54 mg/dL (3.0 mmol/L) considered seriously low.

Causes

The most common cause is medications used to treat diabetes mellitus, such as insulin and sulfonylureas, especially when combined with missed meals, exercise or alcohol. In people without diabetes, causes include insulinoma, severe liver or kidney disease, sepsis, adrenal insufficiency, certain tumours, and reactive (postprandial) hypoglycemia after meals or gastric surgery.

Symptoms

Autonomic (adrenergic) symptoms — sweating, tremor, palpitations, anxiety and hunger — arise from counter-regulatory hormone release. Neuroglycopenic symptoms from brain glucose deprivation include confusion, abnormal behaviour, blurred vision, seizures and coma. Whipple's triad (symptoms consistent with hypoglycemia, a documented low glucose at the time, and relief when glucose is raised) supports the diagnosis.

Treatment

A conscious patient is treated with fast-acting carbohydrate (the "15-15 rule": 15 g of glucose or juice, recheck in 15 minutes). If the person cannot swallow, glucagon injection or intravenous dextrose is given. Long-term management targets the underlying cause.
