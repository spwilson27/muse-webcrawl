Cousin marriage

Cousin marriage is the marriage between cousins — people with common grandparents or other close ancestors. Attitudes and laws vary widely across cultures and eras. In many parts of the world, especially the Middle East, North Africa, and South Asia, marriage between first cousins is common and preferred: more than 10 percent of marriages worldwide are between first or second cousins, and in some regions the proportion exceeds 50 percent. In most of the Americas and Europe it is uncommon, and in a majority of United States states first-cousin marriage is restricted or prohibited.

Prevalence and reasons

Where it is practised, cousin marriage is often valued for keeping property within the family, strengthening kin ties, ensuring compatibility of background, and making marriage arrangements more secure. Parallel-cousin marriage (to the father's brother's child) is characteristically preferred in Arab cultures; cross-cousin marriage was historically prescribed in parts of India and China. Religious rules differ: Islam permits first-cousin marriage, Roman Catholic canon law requires a dispensation for it, and Hindu law in India prohibits it within prohibited degrees in the north while traditionally allowing cross-cousin and even uncle–niece unions in the south.

Genetic considerations

Children of first cousins have an inbreeding coefficient of 6.25 percent and a modestly elevated risk of congenital disorders and recessive genetic disease — studies generally place the risk of birth defects at roughly 4–6 percent, compared with a background rate of about 3 percent. Risks grow in communities with many generations of repeated cousin marriage. Genetic counselling is often recommended for consanguineous couples.

History

Cousin marriage was unremarkable in much of the historical West: notable examples include Charles Darwin (married to Emma Wedgwood), Albert Einstein, and the intermarried royal houses of Europe. Its decline in Western countries during the 20th century has been linked to urbanization, smaller families, and women's education, while bans in the United States emerged in the mid-19th century influenced by eugenic ideas.
