Tribunal

[Image: The Nuremberg military tribunal courtroom, 1946]

A tribunal, generally, is any person or institution with authority to judge, adjudicate on, or determine claims or disputes — whether or not it is called a tribunal in its title. For example, a single judge in a court may be described as "the tribunal". Many governmental bodies with names containing "tribunal" are not courts in the ordinary sense: the term derives from the tribunes, the magistrates of the Classical Roman Republic who sat on a raised platform (a tribunal) to administer justice.

Types

Tribunals include administrative tribunals that decide disputes between citizens and the state (such as immigration, tax, and employment tribunals in the United Kingdom); military tribunals and courts-martial; religious tribunals; and international tribunals, such as the International Court of Justice, the ad hoc International Criminal Tribunals for the former Yugoslavia (1993) and Rwanda (1994), and their successor, the International Criminal Court. The Nuremberg and Tokyo trials after the Second World War were landmark international military tribunals.

Role and procedure

Tribunals are often created to provide specialised, faster, or cheaper adjudication than ordinary courts, with procedures that may be less formal and members who may include lay or expert panellists as well as judges. Their decisions may be subject to appeal or judicial review in the regular courts. In some legal systems, tribunal systems have been unified and reformed — in the United Kingdom, the Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007 created a single two-tier tribunal structure under a Senior President of Tribunals.
