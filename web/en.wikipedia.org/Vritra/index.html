Vritra (Sanskrit: वृत्र, Vṛtra, "the enveloper" or "obstructor") is a serpent or dragon in Hindu mythology, the personification of drought and adversary of Indra. In the Rigveda, Vritra is the great serpent who held back the waters of the world, causing drought, until Indra slew him with the thunderbolt (vajra) and released the rivers to flow to the sea.

Mythology

The battle between Indra and Vritra is one of the central myths of the Rigveda, celebrated in numerous hymns, most famously Rigveda 1.32. Vritra, also called Ahi ("snake"), coiled around the cosmic waters and withheld them from the world. Indra, strengthened by soma, attacked the serpent with the vajra fashioned by the craftsman god Tvashta, striking Vritra on the back and splitting open his body, whereupon the waters rushed forth like lowing cattle.

The myth is frequently interpreted as a nature allegory: the release of the pent-up waters represents the coming of the monsoon rains or the melting of winter ice in the mountains. Comparative mythologists have noted parallels with other Indo-European dragon-slaying myths, such as Thor's battle with Jörmungandr and Zeus's fight against Typhon.

Later traditions

In later Hindu texts, including the Mahabharata and the Puranas, Vritra is sometimes portrayed as a brahmin — the son of Tvashta — created to avenge the death of Tvashta's son Trishiras, whom Indra had killed. In these versions Indra's slaying of Vritra carries the sin of brahminicide, which Indra must expiate. In some accounts Vritra is even depicted as a devotee of Vishnu whose apparent enmity toward Indra conceals a deeper devotion.
