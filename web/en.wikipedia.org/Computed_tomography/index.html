Computed tomography (CT), also known as computed axial tomography (CAT), is a medical imaging technique that uses computer-processed combinations of many X-ray measurements taken from different angles to produce tomographic (cross-sectional) images of specific areas of a scanned object, allowing the user to see inside the object without cutting.

History

The mathematical principles were developed by Johann Radon in 1917 (the Radon transform). The first commercially viable CT scanner was invented by Sir Godfrey Hounsfield at EMI Laboratories in England, with the first patient scan performed in 1971 at Atkinson Morley Hospital in London. Allan MacLeod Cormack of Tufts University independently developed the underlying mathematics. Hounsfield and Cormack shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Principles

An X-ray source rotates around the patient while detectors opposite it measure attenuation. Computer algorithms — historically filtered back projection, increasingly iterative reconstruction — combine the projections into images. Tissue attenuation is expressed in Hounsfield units, calibrated so that water is 0 HU and air is −1000 HU; bone may exceed +1000 HU.

Generations and technology

Modern scanners use slip-ring technology enabling continuous helical (spiral) acquisition. Multi-detector CT (MDCT) systems with 64 to 320 detector rows can image the whole body in seconds. Dual-energy CT acquires data at two X-ray spectra, improving tissue characterisation.

Clinical uses

CT is widely used for trauma assessment, cancer staging, guidance of biopsies and interventions, cardiac imaging, and diagnosis of internal injuries and disease. Contrast agents, usually iodine-based, may be given intravenously or orally to enhance vessels and organs.

Risks

CT exposes patients to ionising radiation at doses higher than plain radiography, which has prompted dose-reduction efforts and appropriateness criteria. Contrast agents carry small risks of allergic reaction and kidney injury.
