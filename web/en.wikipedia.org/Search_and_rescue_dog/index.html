Search and rescue dog

A search and rescue dog (SAR dog) is a dog trained to find missing people after natural or man-made disasters, or to locate people lost in wilderness areas. Using their powerful sense of smell, such dogs can detect human scent from great distances and work with a human handler as part of organised search and rescue teams.

Types of work

Air-scenting dogs work off-lead and follow human scent carried on the wind, while trailing and tracking dogs follow the specific scent trail of an individual. Disaster dogs, often called urban search and rescue (USAR) dogs, search collapsed buildings and rubble. Other specialisations include cadaver dogs, which locate human remains, avalanche dogs, and water search dogs that detect scent rising from submerged bodies.

Training

Training a search dog typically takes 18 months to two years. Dogs are selected for drive, agility, and sociability and are trained largely through play, learning that finding a person earns a reward. Handlers must also qualify in first aid, navigation, and search management. Teams certify through organisations such as mountain rescue associations and national urban search and rescue task forces.

History

Dogs have been used to find the lost and buried for centuries; the hospice dogs of the Swiss Alps, ancestors of the Saint Bernard, were famous for locating travellers in snow. Organised search dog units developed rapidly after the Second World War and are now standard components of disaster response worldwide.
