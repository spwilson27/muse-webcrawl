Orbital manoeuvre

In spaceflight, an orbital manoeuvre is the use of propulsion systems to change the orbit of a spacecraft. The change in velocity required is called delta-v (Δv), and the total Δv available defines a mission's manoeuvring budget. Orbital mechanics — in particular the Oberth effect and the vis-viva equation — governs how efficiently burns change an orbit.

QuantityDelta-v (Δv)
Common manoeuvresHohmann transfer, plane change, phasing, rendezvous burns
Governing lawVis-viva equation; Tsiolkovsky rocket equation

Types of manoeuvre

A Hohmann transfer uses two burns to move between coplanar circular orbits. A bi-elliptic transfer can be more efficient for large orbit changes. Plane-change (inclination) manoeuvres are among the most expensive in Δv and are best performed at low orbital velocity. Phasing manoeuvres adjust a spacecraft's position within its orbit, a prerequisite for rendezvous and docking.

Impulsive and low-thrust burns

Chemical rockets deliver near-instantaneous "impulsive" burns, simplifying trajectory design. Electric propulsion (ion thrusters) produces very low thrust over months, requiring spiral trajectories and different optimisation methods, but offers far greater total Δv per unit of propellant.

Assists

Gravity assists use a planet's gravity to change velocity without propellant, while aerobraking and aerocapture exploit atmospheric drag. Combining these techniques with propulsive manoeuvres makes ambitious interplanetary missions feasible.
