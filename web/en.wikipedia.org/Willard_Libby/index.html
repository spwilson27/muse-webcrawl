Willard Libby

Willard Frank Libby (December 17, 1908 – September 8, 1980) was an American physical chemist noted for his role in the 1949 development of radiocarbon dating, a process which revolutionized archaeology and geology. For this discovery he received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1960.

Career

Born in Grand Valley, Colorado, Libby studied at the University of California, Berkeley, where he built sensitive Geiger counters and detected the weak natural radioactivity of samarium. During World War II he worked on the Manhattan Project at Columbia University, helping develop gaseous diffusion for uranium enrichment. After the war, at the University of Chicago, he reasoned that cosmic rays produce carbon-14 in the atmosphere, which living organisms absorb; measuring the remaining carbon-14 in dead material would reveal its age. His team verified the method against samples of known age, such as wood from ancient Egyptian tombs.

Later work

Libby served as a commissioner of the U.S. Atomic Energy Commission (1954–1959), advocated of atmospheric nuclear testing fallout research and of the "peaceful atom." He later moved to UCLA, where he directed the Institute of Geophysics and Planetary Physics. He died in Los Angeles in 1980.
