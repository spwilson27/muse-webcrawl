Seven liberal arts

The seven liberal arts personified

The seven liberal arts were the subjects of study in classical and medieval education, considered the essential curriculum for a free person (Latin liber, "free"). They were divided into two groups: the trivium (grammar, rhetoric, and logic — "the three ways") and the quadrivium (arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy — "the four ways").

The trivium

  • Grammar — the study of language, literature, and correct speech.
  • Rhetoric — the art of persuasion and effective composition.
  • Logic (dialectic) — the art of reasoning and argument, based largely on Aristotle and Boethius.

The quadrivium

  • Arithmetic — number in itself.
  • Geometry — number in space.
  • Music — number in time, chiefly the theory of harmony and proportion.
  • Astronomy — number in space and time, the motions of the heavens.

History

The division traces its origins to Plato's Republic and was systematised in late antiquity, notably in Martianus Capella's 5th-century allegory The Marriage of Philology and Mercury, which personified each art as a female figure, and in the works of Boethius and Cassiodorus. In medieval universities the seven arts formed the undergraduate curriculum of the Faculty of Arts, prerequisite to the higher faculties of theology, law, and medicine. A well-known mnemonic verse ran: "Gram loquitur, Dia vera docet, Rhe verba colorat; Mus canit, Ar numerat, Geo ponderat, As docet astra." The scheme underlies the modern concept of the liberal arts college.
