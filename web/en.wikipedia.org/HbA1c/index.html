HbA1c

Full nameGlycated haemoglobin (haemoglobin A1c)
Normal rangeBelow 5.7% (below 39 mmol/mol)
Diabetes threshold6.5% (48 mmol/mol) or above
ReflectsAverage blood glucose over ~2–3 months

HbA1c, or glycated haemoglobin, is a form of haemoglobin that is chemically linked to a sugar. Glucose in the blood attaches to haemoglobin in red blood cells, and because those cells live for about three months, the proportion of glycated haemoglobin reflects the average blood glucose level over the preceding two to three months.

Use in diabetes care

HbA1c is the principal test for monitoring long-term glucose control in people with diabetes and, since recommendations by the American Diabetes Association (2010) and the World Health Organization (2011), is also used to diagnose diabetes: a value of 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) or above indicates diabetes, and 5.7–6.4% (39–47 mmol/mol) indicates prediabetes. Higher long-term values are associated with greater risk of complications such as retinopathy, kidney disease and cardiovascular disease.

Reporting and limitations

Results are reported as a percentage (DCCT/NGSP units) or in mmol/mol (IFCC units) in many countries. The test can be distorted by conditions that change red-cell lifespan or haemoglobin structure, such as anaemia, haemoglobin variants, recent transfusion or pregnancy.
