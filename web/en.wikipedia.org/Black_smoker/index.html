Black smoker

A black smoker is a type of hydrothermal vent found on the seabed, typically in the abyssal and hadal zones. They are formed in fields hundreds of meters wide where superheated water from below Earth's crust comes through the ocean floor. The water is rich in dissolved minerals from the crust, most notably sulfides, which precipitate on contact with the cold ocean water to form black chimney-like structures; the precipitating particles give the venting fluid the appearance of black smoke.

Formation and characteristics

Seawater seeps into the oceanic crust, is heated by magma to temperatures exceeding 400 °C, and rises buoyantly. As the acidic, metal-rich fluid mixes with near-freezing seawater, iron, zinc, copper, and other sulfides precipitate, building chimneys that can grow several meters tall and deposit massive sulfide ore bodies. Black smokers were first discovered in 1977 near the Galápagos Islands by scientists aboard the submersible Alvin.

Ecosystems

Despite darkness, cold ambient water, and toxic fluids, black smokers support dense communities of organisms based on chemosynthesis rather than photosynthesis. Giant tube worms (Riftia pachyptila), clams, mussels, shrimp, and crabs thrive around vents, nourished by symbiotic sulfur-oxidizing bacteria. Hydrothermal vents have been proposed as a possible setting for the origin of life on Earth.
