Sanctuary lamp

Also calledChancel lamp, altar lamp, everlasting light
TraditionsCatholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran, Jewish (ner tamid)
SignifiesPresence of Christ in the reserved Eucharist

A sanctuary lamp, also called a chancel lamp or altar lamp, is a light that burns perpetually before the tabernacle in Roman Catholic, Old Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and some Anglican and Lutheran churches, as a sign of the presence of Christ in the reserved sacrament of the Eucharist.

Practice

The General Instruction of the Roman Missal directs that "a special lamp, which is fuelled by oil or wax, should shine permanently near the tabernacle as an indication and honour of the presence of Christ." The lamp is traditionally held in a glass receptacle, often of red glass, and may hang by chains, stand on the floor, or be fixed to the wall near the tabernacle. Canon law (canon 940 of the 1983 Code) gives the practice legal force in the Latin Church.

Analogous customs

The sanctuary lamp is frequently compared to the Jewish ner tamid ("eternal flame"), the lamp kept burning before the Ark in synagogues, itself derived from the lampstand of the Tabernacle in Exodus. In Eastern Orthodox churches a lampada burns before the iconostasis or a principal icon, and oil lamps are likewise maintained in Hindu temples and before Buddhist shrines.

Symbolism

The unceasing flame signifies Christ as the Light of the World, the vigilance of prayer, and the abiding presence of God in the consecrated place, recalling the lamps commanded for the sanctuary in the Books of Exodus and Leviticus.
