Social credit

Social credit, as an economic theory, is the body of monetary and distributive ideas set out by C. H. Douglas beginning in 1918–1920, from which the Social Credit political movement took its name. In Douglas's usage, "credit" meant the community's real productive capacity — its "real credit" — as distinct from the financial credit created by banks. Social credit theory holds that the financial system should be redesigned so that money, treated as a mere accounting ticket, accurately reflects and distributes society's real credit.

Core propositions

The theory rests on several claims. First, in the modern price system, the incomes distributed during production are systematically less than the prices generated, because prices must also recover overhead and intermediate costs (the A + B theorem). Second, the deficiency is currently made good by bank lending, exports, and public works, producing chronic debt growth. Third, the community collectively owns the technological and organizational heritage — the "unearned increment of association" — that makes modern production possible, and citizens are therefore entitled to share in it through a dividend.

Instruments

Two instruments follow: a National Dividend, a periodic payment to every citizen financed by newly issued credit based on the nation's real productive assets, and a compensated price (Just Price), under which retail prices are discounted at the point of sale and sellers reimbursed, aligning prices with consumers' aggregate income while using the discount rate to regulate the economy. Douglas proposed that a National Credit Authority manage the money supply statistically rather than by the gold standard or bank discretion.

Assessment

Economists such as John Maynard Keynes treated Douglas's intuition about deficient demand sympathetically while rejecting the A + B theorem as an accounting fallacy; others, including Lionel Robbins and Friedrich Hayek, were sharply critical. Social credit theory survives today chiefly within monetary-reform circles and as an ancestor of citizen's-income and sovereign-money proposals.
