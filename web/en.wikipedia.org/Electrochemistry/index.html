Electrochemistry

Electrochemistry is the branch of physical chemistry concerned with the relationship between electrical potential difference and identifiable chemical change. It studies reactions in which electrons move directly between electrodes and reactants in an electrolyte, encompassing both the generation of electricity from chemical reactions and the use of electricity to drive chemical change.

Alessandro Volta's pile (1800) was the first device to produce a steady electric current from chemical reactions.

Electrochemical cells

An electrochemical cell consists of two electrodes — an anode, where oxidation occurs, and a cathode, where reduction occurs — in contact with an electrolyte. In a galvanic (voltaic) cell, a spontaneous reaction produces electrical energy, as in a battery. In an electrolytic cell, an external voltage drives a non-spontaneous reaction, as in electrolysis and electroplating.

Key concepts

Standard electrode potentials quantify the tendency of a half-reaction to occur and allow the voltage of a cell to be predicted; the Nernst equation relates potential to concentration. Faraday's laws of electrolysis connect the quantity of charge passed to the amount of substance transformed.

Applications

Electrochemistry underpins batteries and fuel cells, corrosion science, industrial production of aluminium and chlorine, electroplating, sensors, and much of modern analytical chemistry. Luigi Galvani's experiments in the 1780s and Michael Faraday's work in the 1830s laid the foundations of the field.
