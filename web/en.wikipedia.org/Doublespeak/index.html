Doublespeak


Doublespeak deliberately obscures or reverses meaning.

Doublespeak is language that deliberately obscures, disguises, distorts, or reverses the meaning of words. The term, coined after George Orwell's "doublethink" and "Newspeak" in the novel Nineteen Eighty-Four, is applied to euphemisms, bureaucratese, and political language designed to make unpleasant facts sound acceptable or to avoid responsibility.

Forms

The concept was popularised by William Lutz, who classified doublespeak into euphemisms ("collateral damage" for civilian deaths), jargon ("revenue enhancement" for tax increases), gobbledygook — inflated language meant to overwhelm — and inflated language ("pre-owned" for used). Military and corporate communication are frequent targets of the critique.

Criticism

Linguists note that not all euphemism is deceptive: softened terms can be polite or tactful, and the doublespeak label is itself sometimes used rhetorically. The quarterly Doublespeak Award, given by the National Council of Teachers of English, satirises notable public examples.
