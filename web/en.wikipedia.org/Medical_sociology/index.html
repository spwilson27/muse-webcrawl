Medical sociology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Medical sociology is the sociological analysis of health, illness and medical organisations and institutions, including the social causes and consequences of health and disease, health-care delivery, and the behaviour of patients and health professionals. It is a major subfield of sociology that overlaps with medical anthropology, health psychology and social epidemiology.

Key concepts

Important concepts include the sick role, described by Talcott Parsons, in which the ill person is exempted from normal duties but obliged to seek care and try to recover; medicalisation, the process by which human conditions come to be defined as medical problems; and social determinants of health, the conditions in which people live and work that shape health outcomes and inequalities.

Areas of study

Medical sociologists study topics such as health inequalities by class, race and gender; the doctor–patient relationship; the organisation and financing of health systems; stigma and chronic illness; and the social construction of diagnoses.

History

The subfield expanded rapidly after the Second World War alongside the growth of modern health-care systems and funding for health services research.
