Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a long-term condition that results in inflammation and ulcers of the colon and rectum. The primary symptoms of active disease are abdominal pain and diarrhea mixed with blood. Weight loss, fever, and anemia may also occur. Often, symptoms come on slowly and can range from mild to severe. Symptoms typically occur intermittently with periods of no symptoms between flares. Complications may include abnormal dilation of the colon (megacolon), inflammation of the eye, joints, or liver, and colon cancer.

The cause of UC is unknown. Theories involve immune system dysfunction, genetics, changes in the normal gut bacteria, and environmental factors. Rates tend to be higher in the developed world, with some proposing this to be the result of less exposure to intestinal infections or a Western diet and lifestyle. The disease is more common in people of Ashkenazi Jewish descent. Diagnosis is typically by colonoscopy with tissue biopsies.

Signs and symptoms

The severity of disease varies, with symptoms ranging from mild abdominal cramping to severe bloody diarrhea. The disease typically begins in the rectum and may extend continuously proximally through the colon. Unlike Crohn's disease, which can affect any part of the gastrointestinal tract, ulcerative colitis is limited to the colon and rectum. Extraintestinal manifestations may include arthritis, inflammation of the eye (uveitis and episcleritis), skin conditions such as erythema nodosum and pyoderma gangrenosum, and primary sclerosing cholangitis of the liver.

Cause

No direct cause has been established for ulcerative colitis. It is believed to result from an inappropriate immune response to the gut microbiota in genetically susceptible individuals. Several genes have been associated with the condition, and environmental factors such as diet, smoking status, and prior appendectomy appear to modify risk. Smoking is protective against UC, in contrast to Crohn's disease, where it is harmful.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on a combination of clinical history, endoscopic findings, and histological examination of biopsies. Colonoscopy typically shows continuous inflammation beginning at the rectum, with friable, granular mucosa. Blood tests may show anemia and raised inflammatory markers. Stool studies are performed to exclude infection. Imaging is generally reserved for detecting complications.

Treatment

Treatment aims to induce and maintain remission. Medications include aminosalicylates such as mesalazine, corticosteroids for flares, immunosuppressants such as azathioprine, and biologic agents including anti-TNF antibodies and vedolizumab. Surgery — removal of the colon (colectomy) — is potentially curative and is indicated for medically refractory disease, toxic megacolon, perforation, or cancer. People with long-standing disease require regular colonoscopic surveillance because of an increased risk of colorectal cancer.

Epidemiology

Ulcerative colitis affects roughly 35 to 100 people per 100,000 in Western populations, with the highest rates in North America and northern Europe. Onset is bimodal, most often between ages 15 and 30 and again between 50 and 70. It forms, together with Crohn's disease, the group of conditions known as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).
