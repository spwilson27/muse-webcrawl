Fluid dynamics

Fluid dynamics is a subdiscipline of fluid mechanics that describes the flow of fluids — liquids and gases. It has several subdisciplines, including aerodynamics (the study of air and other gases in motion) and hydrodynamics (the study of liquids in motion). Fluid dynamics has a wide range of applications, including calculating forces and moments on aircraft, determining the mass flow rate of petroleum through pipelines, predicting weather patterns, understanding nebulae in interstellar space and modelling fission weapon detonation.

Fluid dynamics offers a systematic structure — based on practical laws — that underlies these practical disciplines and embraces empirical and semi-empirical laws derived from flow measurement, used to solve practical problems. The solution to a fluid dynamics problem typically involves the calculation of various properties of the fluid, such as flow velocity, pressure, density, and temperature, as functions of space and time.

Equations

The foundational axioms of fluid dynamics are the conservation laws, specifically conservation of mass, conservation of linear momentum, and conservation of energy (also known as the first law of thermodynamics). These are based on classical mechanics and are modified in quantum mechanics and general relativity. They are expressed using the Reynolds transport theorem. In addition to the above, fluids are stipulated to obey the continuum assumption. Fluids are composed of molecules that collide with one another and solid objects; however, the continuum assumption considers fluids to be continuous rather than discrete.

The resulting equations — the Navier–Stokes equations — describe the motion of viscous fluid substances and are among the most important equations in physics. A simplified form, the Euler equations, applies to inviscid flow. For incompressible flow with constant viscosity, the equations can be reduced further and in special cases solved analytically.

Classifications

Flows are commonly classified as laminar or turbulent. In laminar flow, fluid particles move in smooth layers, whereas in turbulent flow the motion is chaotic and characterised by eddies and rapid mixing. Other important distinctions include compressible versus incompressible flow, viscous versus inviscid flow, and steady versus unsteady flow. The Reynolds number is the key dimensionless parameter that predicts the transition between flow regimes.

Applications

Fluid dynamics is used in traffic engineering (where traffic is treated as a continuous fluid), in computing the trajectories of spacecraft, in oceanography and meteorology through geophysical fluid dynamics, and in medicine, for example in modelling blood flow through the circulatory system. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) applies numerical analysis and data structures to solve and analyse problems involving fluid flows.
