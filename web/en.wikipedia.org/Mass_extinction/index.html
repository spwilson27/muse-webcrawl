Extinction event

Extinction event (mass extinction)
Definition: Widespread, rapid decrease in biodiversity on Earth
Major events: "Big Five" mass extinctions of the Phanerozoic
Current: Holocene extinction (proposed sixth)

An extinction event (also known as a mass extinction or biotic crisis) is a widespread and rapid decrease in the biodiversity on Earth. Such an event is identified by a sharp fall in the diversity and abundance of multicellular organisms, occurring over a geologically short period. Mass extinctions are typically defined as losses of roughly three-quarters or more of species within a short interval.

The "Big Five"

Palaeontologists recognize five major mass extinctions in the Phanerozoic eon. The Ordovician–Silurian event (~444 million years ago) was linked to glaciation and falling sea levels. The Late Devonian extinction (~372 Ma) unfolded over millions of years, possibly from ocean anoxia. The Permian–Triassic extinction (~252 Ma), the "Great Dying", was the most severe, killing an estimated 90% or more of marine species, probably driven by Siberian Traps volcanism and runaway climate change. The Triassic–Jurassic event (~201 Ma) cleared the way for dinosaur dominance. Finally, the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction (~66 Ma), caused largely by the Chicxulub asteroid impact, ended the reign of the non-avian dinosaurs.

Causes

Proposed causes include massive volcanism and flood basalt eruptions, asteroid and comet impacts, rapid climate change (warming or cooling), ocean acidification and anoxia, sea-level change, and combinations of these factors. Extinction events reset ecosystems: recovery typically takes millions of years, and the organisms that radiate afterwards differ profoundly from those that were lost.

A sixth extinction?

Many biologists argue that a sixth mass extinction — the Holocene extinction — is under way, driven by human activity including habitat destruction, overexploitation, pollution, invasive species and climate change. Current extinction rates are estimated at tens to hundreds of times the natural background rate.
