Basilar membrane

Basilar membrane
LocationCochlea of the inner ear

The basilar membrane is a stiff structural element within the cochlea of the inner ear that separates two liquid-filled tubes running along the coil of the cochlea — the scala media and the scala tympani. It supports the organ of Corti, which contains the hair cells that transduce sound into neural signals.

Structure

The membrane is narrow and stiff at the basal end of the cochlea, near the oval window, and progressively wider and more compliant toward the apex. It is composed of extracellular matrix fibers embedded in ground substance, and its graded mechanical properties are the physical basis of frequency analysis in hearing.

Function in hearing

Sound vibrations transmitted through the middle ear set up a traveling wave along the basilar membrane, as described by Georg von Békésy, who won the Nobel Prize for this work. Because of the stiffness gradient, each frequency produces maximal displacement at a characteristic place: high frequencies peak near the base and low frequencies near the apex. This tonotopic organization is preserved through the auditory pathway to the brain. Movement of the membrane deflects the stereocilia of the hair cells in the organ of Corti, opening ion channels and generating receptor potentials.
