2011 Super Outbreak

The 2011 Super Outbreak was the largest, costliest, and among the deadliest tornado outbreaks ever recorded, affecting the Southern, Midwestern, and Northeastern United States from 25 to 28 April 2011. Over four days, 360 tornadoes were confirmed, including four rated EF5. Tornadoes and the associated storms killed 324 people — 238 of them in Alabama — and caused roughly US$11 billion in damage. It is the deadliest tornado outbreak since 1936.

27 April: the peak day

The Storm Prediction Center issued a rare "high risk" outlook for 27 April, and two major rounds of supercells swept the Tennessee Valley. 216 tornadoes touched down that day alone, a United States record for a single day. Four EF5 tornadoes struck northern Alabama and Mississippi, devastating Hackleburg, Phil Campbell, Rainsville, and Smithville. A violent EF4 tornado tore through Tuscaloosa and into the Birmingham suburbs, killing 64 people. Another EF4 struck Ringgold, Georgia, and the Chattanooga area. In total, 316 people died on 27 April, making it the deadliest tornado day in the United States since 1925.

Why warnings were not enough

Forecasters judged the event well in advance, and warnings were timely — but the scale of the day overwhelmed the system: long-track violent tornadoes struck populated areas, power and communications failed (including knocked-out NOAA Weather Radio transmitters and radar sites), and many victims in mobile homes or weak structures had no survivable shelter. The outbreak, together with the Joplin tornado three weeks later, prompted research into warning communication, false-alarm tolerance, and the value of community safe rooms.

Comparison

The outbreak surpassed the 1974 Super Outbreak in total tornado count (360 vs 148) and in economic damage, although the 1974 event produced more F5 tornadoes relative to its count and remains the benchmark for intensity concentration.
