Damascus

Damascus (Arabic: Dimashq) is the capital and largest city of Syria. It is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world, with a history spanning at least eleven thousand years of settlement. Located in southwestern Syria at the foot of the Anti-Lebanon mountains, around the oasis fed by the Barada River, Damascus became a major cultural, commercial and religious centre, and served as the capital of the Umayyad Caliphate from 661 to 750.

The Umayyad Mosque, at the heart of the old city.

History

Damascus was an Aramaean city-state in the first millennium BC, then passed through Assyrian, Persian, Hellenistic and Roman hands. Under Rome it prospered on the trade routes linking the Mediterranean with Mesopotamia and Arabia; the Roman-era street plan, including the Via Recta, still shapes the old city. In 635 the city surrendered to Muslim forces, and under the Umayyads it became the capital of an empire stretching from Spain to Central Asia. The Great Umayyad Mosque, built under Caliph al-Walid I on the site of a Roman temple and Byzantine church, preserves some of the finest early Islamic mosaic art.

After the Abbasid revolution in 750 the capital moved to Baghdad, and Damascus passed among Tulunid, Fatimid, Seljuk and Ayyubid rulers; Saladin made it his base and is buried there. The Mamluks developed it as a centre of crafts and learning, celebrated for its damascene steel and damask textiles — both named after the city. Ottoman rule lasted from 1516 to 1918. Following the First World War came a brief Arab kingdom under Faisal, then the French Mandate, and finally Syrian independence in 1946. The city grew rapidly in the twentieth century into a metropolitan area of more than two million people, and endured severe strain during the Syrian civil war that began in 2011, though the old city largely escaped the worst destruction seen in cities such as Aleppo.

Geography and landmarks

The old city, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1979, is enclosed by Roman-era walls and contains the Umayyad Mosque, the Azem Palace, covered souks such as al-Buzuriyah and al-Hamidiyah, and historic churches and synagogues in its Christian and Jewish quarters. Mount Qasioun overlooks the city from the northwest. The surrounding Ghouta oasis, long famed for orchards and apricots, has suffered heavily from drought, urban expansion and conflict.
