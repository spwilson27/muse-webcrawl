Merit (Buddhism)

Merit (Sanskrit: puṇya; Pali: puñña) is a concept in Buddhism referring to the beneficial and protective force accumulated by good deeds, acts of generosity, moral conduct and mental cultivation. Merit is believed to contribute to a person's happiness in this life and to a favourable rebirth in the cycle of saṃsāra, and in Mahāyāna Buddhism it is one of the two accumulations — merit and wisdom — needed for Buddhahood.

Making merit

The Buddha is recorded in the Pali Canon as teaching three bases of meritorious action: giving (dāna), moral discipline (sīla) and mental development or meditation (bhāvanā). Later scholastic lists enumerate ten meritorious deeds, adding reverence, service, hearing and teaching the Dharma, straightening one's views, and the transference and rejoicing in others' merit. In Theravāda countries such as Thailand, Myanmar and Sri Lanka, "merit-making" structures lay religious life: offering food to monks, funding temples and stupas, sponsoring ordinations and releasing captive animals.

Transfer of merit

A distinctive practice is the dedication or transfer of merit (pattidāna) to others, especially to deceased relatives believed to be reborn as hungry ghosts (petas), who can benefit from gifts made in their name. In Mahāyāna, the bodhisattva's dedication of merit to the liberation of all beings (pariṇāmanā) is integral to the path.

Merit and karma

Merit is the wholesome side of karma: intentional actions motivated by generosity, kindness and wisdom that produce pleasant results. Buddhist texts nonetheless rank merit below insight — merit sustains good conditions within saṃsāra, but liberation (nirvāṇa) requires wisdom into impermanence and non-self.
