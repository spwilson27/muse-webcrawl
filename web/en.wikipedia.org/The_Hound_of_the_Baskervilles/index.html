The Hound of the Baskervilles

The Hound of the Baskervilles is the third of the four crime novels by the Scottish writer Arthur Conan Doyle featuring the detective Sherlock Holmes. Originally serialised in The Strand Magazine from August 1901 to April 1902, it is set in Dartmoor in Devon in England's West Country and tells the story of an attempted murder inspired by the legend of a fearsome, diabolical hound of supernatural origin. It is the most famous Sherlock Holmes tale and one of the best-known detective stories of all time.

Plot

Dr James Mortimer visits Holmes and Dr Watson with a legend: the Baskervilles of Baskerville Hall are cursed by a spectral black hound that has haunted Dartmoor since the wicked Hugo Baskerville's death in the seventeenth century. Sir Charles Baskerville has just been found dead near the hall, with the footprints of a gigantic hound nearby. To protect the heir, Sir Henry Baskerville, Watson accompanies him to Devon, reporting to Holmes — who is secretly investigating on the moor himself. Holmes uncovers that the naturalist Jack Stapleton is a hidden Baskerville heir who set his huge, phosphorus-coated hound on Sir Charles, and the villain perishes fleeing into the Grimpen Mire.

Background

Doyle wrote the novel after an eight-year hiatus following Holmes's apparent death in "The Final Problem"; the story is set before that death. It drew on West Country legends of spectral hounds such as the Black Shuck and the Dartmoor "wish hounds", and on Doyle's holiday on Dartmoor with Bertram Fletcher Robinson, who contributed to the plot's genesis.

Adaptations

The novel has been adapted for film and television more often than almost any other Holmes story, including the 1939 Basil Rathbone version and Hammer's 1959 film with Peter Cushing.
