The Upper Silesia plebiscite was a referendum held on 20 March 1921 in the German-Polish border region of Upper Silesia, mandated by the Treaty of Versailles to determine whether the territory would remain with Germany or join the newly re-established Poland. Upper Silesia was one of Europe's richest coal and steel regions, and its population was a mixture of German speakers, Polish speakers, and bilingual Silesians.

Campaign and result

The vote followed two Polish uprisings (1919 and 1920) and was accompanied by intimidation and violence on both sides. Overall, 59.6% voted for Germany and 40.4% for Poland, but results varied sharply by district: the industrial east around Kattowitz (Katowice) voted heavily Polish, while the rural west voted German. Controversially, some 200,000 "outvoters" — people born in Upper Silesia but living elsewhere in Germany — were eligible to vote, and they voted overwhelmingly for Germany.

Partition

Within weeks a third Silesian uprising erupted under Wojciech Korfanty. In October 1921 the League of Nations imposed a compromise partition: Poland received roughly one-third of the territory and half the population, including most of the industrial district and its mines; Germany retained the west. The German–Polish Convention on Upper Silesia (Geneva, 1922) guaranteed minority rights in both parts for fifteen years. The division embittered German–Polish relations throughout the interwar period.
