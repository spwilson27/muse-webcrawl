Gustave Courbet

Courbet, Self-Portrait (The Desperate Man), c. 1843–1845.

Jean Désiré Gustave Courbet (10 June 1819 – 31 December 1877) was a French painter who led the Realism movement in 19th-century French painting. Committed to painting only what he could see, he rejected academic convention and Romantic sentimentality, depicting ordinary people and provincial life on the monumental scale previously reserved for history painting.

Major works

A Burial at Ornans (1849–50), showing his great-uncle's funeral in Courbet's home town, caused a sensation at the Paris Salon. The Stone Breakers (1849, destroyed in Dresden in 1945) portrayed labourers with unsparing directness. When his work was refused at the Exposition Universelle of 1855, Courbet mounted his own Pavilion of Realism, exhibiting The Painter's Studio (1855), a complex allegory of his artistic life. Later works include seascapes, hunting scenes and the erotic L'Origine du monde (1866).

Later life

Courbet was imprisoned for his role in the Paris Commune of 1871, during which the Vendôme Column was toppled — an act for which he was held financially responsible. Facing ruinous fines, he fled to Switzerland in 1873 and died in exile at La Tour-de-Peilz.
