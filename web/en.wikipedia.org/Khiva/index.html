Khiva

Khiva (Uzbek: Xiva; Persian: خیوه, Khīveh) is a district-managed city of about 93,000 people in Xorazm Region, Uzbekistan. It was for centuries the capital of Khwarezmia and of the Khanate of Khiva, a major oasis city on the Silk Road at the edge of the Karakum Desert. The inner town, Itchan Kala, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site — the first place in Uzbekistan to be so inscribed (1990).

History

Legend connects Khiva's founding with Shem, son of Noah, who is said to have dug the Kheivak well here. The oasis of Khorezm, watered by the Amu Darya, was an ancient centre of Iranian civilisation. Khiva town rose to prominence in the 16th century, when in 1511 the Uzbek Shaybanid Ilbars became its first khan and the city became capital of the Khanate of Khiva after the river shifted away from the old capital, Konye-Urgench.

The khanate grew wealthy on caravans and infamous for its slave market, which traded in Persian and Russian captives. The city repelled or absorbed expeditions by Peter the Great (1717) and, in 1839, a Russian column destroyed by winter weather. In 1873 General Konstantin von Kaufmann captured Khiva; the khanate became a Russian protectorate, and in 1920 it was abolished and folded into the Khorezm People's Soviet Republic, then into the Uzbek SSR.

Itchan Kala

The walled inner town preserves more than 50 monuments and some 250 old houses, largely 18th–19th century. Notable are the unfinished turquoise Kalta Minor minaret (1855), the Juma Mosque with its forest of 213 carved wooden columns, the Kuhna Ark citadel, the Tosh-Hovli palace with its tiled harem, and the mausoleum of the wrestler-saint Pahlavan Mahmud, the city's patron. The outer town, Dishan Kala, was walled in 1842.

Sister cities and culture

Khiva celebrated its 2500th anniversary in 1997 under UNESCO auspices. The city is a centre for traditional Uzbek crafts — wood carving, silk, ceramics and karakul fur hats. The mathematician al-Khwarizmi, whose name gives us "algorithm", was a native of the Khorezm region.
