Reaction rate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The reaction rate or rate of reaction is the speed at which a chemical reaction takes place, defined as proportional to the increase in the concentration of a product per unit time and to the decrease in the concentration of a reactant per unit time. For a reaction aA + bB → pP + qQ, the rate is

rate = −(1/a) Δ[A]/Δt = −(1/b) Δ[B]/Δt = (1/p) Δ[P]/Δt = (1/q) Δ[Q]/Δt

dividing by each stoichiometric coefficient so that a single rate describes the whole reaction. Reaction rates are the central subject of chemical kinetics and span an enormous range, from near-instantaneous explosions and ionic reactions in solution to rusting over years and geological transformations over millennia. Rates are usually measured in mol·L⁻¹·s⁻¹.

Rate laws

Experimentally, the rate is usually found to obey a rate law of the form rate = k[A]m[B]n, where k is the rate constant and m and n are the orders of reaction with respect to each reactant — quantities determined by measurement, not (except for elementary steps) by the equation's stoichiometry. The overall order m + n governs the mathematical form of the integrated rate law and the half-life: for first-order reactions (such as radioactive decay) the half-life is constant, ln2/k.

Factors affecting rate

  • Concentration — more reactant particles mean more frequent collisions.
  • Temperature — rates roughly double for each 10 °C rise; the dependence is quantified by the Arrhenius equation, k = A e−Ea/RT, through the activation energy Ea.
  • Physical state and surface area — finely divided solids react faster, as in dust explosions.
  • Catalysts — provide an alternative pathway of lower activation energy, increasing k without being consumed; enzymes are biological catalysts of extraordinary specificity.
  • Pressure (for gases), light (for photochemical reactions), and solvent also influence rates.

Theory

Collision theory explains rates through the frequency and energy of molecular collisions, corrected by an orientation (steric) factor; the more refined transition state theory models the passage of reactants over an energy barrier through an activated complex, linking kinetics to thermodynamics. Measuring and modelling reaction rates is essential in chemical engineering reactor design, atmospheric chemistry, combustion, pharmacology and biochemistry.
