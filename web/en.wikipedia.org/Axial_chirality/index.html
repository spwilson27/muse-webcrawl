Axial chirality

FieldStereochemistry
DescriptorsRa / Sa (or M / P helicity)
ExamplesBiaryls (BINAP, BINOL), allenes, spiranes
Enantiomers when rotation blockedAtropisomers

Axial chirality is a special case of chirality in which a molecule does not possess a stereogenic centre but possesses a stereogenic axis—an axis about which a set of substituents is held in a spatial arrangement that is not superposable on its mirror image. Chirality arises from restricted rotation about a bond or bonds, rather than from a tetrahedral carbon bearing four different groups.

Examples

Classic examples include ortho-substituted biphenyls (biaryls), in which bulky substituents prevent rotation about the aryl–aryl bond, giving rise to separable enantiomers called atropisomers; allenes with even numbers of cumulative double bonds and unlike substituent pairs; and certain spiranes and alkylidenecycloalkanes.

Assignment

Axial stereochemistry is described with the descriptors Ra and Sa under an extension of the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog rules, or with the helicity descriptors P (plus) and M (minus).

Significance

Axially chiral ligands such as BINAP and BINOL are central to asymmetric catalysis (recognised by the 2001 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for work using BINAP–ruthenium complexes), and atropisomerism is increasingly important in pharmaceutical chemistry, where drug molecules may exist as stable atropisomers with different biological activity.
