Phonograph

Edison's phonograph of 1877, the first machine to record and reproduce sound.

A phonograph, later called a gramophone and in its modern form a record player or turntable, is a device for the mechanical recording and reproduction of sound. Invented by Thomas Edison in 1877, it was the first technology able to capture sound and play it back, launching the recording industry.

Invention

Edison's original instrument recorded sound on a cylinder wrapped in tinfoil: a diaphragm-driven stylus indented the foil as the cylinder turned. Playback retraced the indentations. Édouard-Léon Scott de Martinville's earlier phonautograph (1857) had traced sound waves but could not reproduce them, and Charles Cros described a theoretical device in the same year as Edison's patent.

Development

Alexander Graham Bell's laboratory improved the medium to wax cylinders in the graphophone. In 1887 Emile Berliner patented the gramophone, which used flat discs with lateral grooves — easier to mass-produce by stamping, a decisive commercial advantage. Shellac 78 rpm discs dominated until vinyl microgroove LPs (1948) and 45 rpm singles (1949) transformed the market, followed by stereo, cassettes, and CDs. A vinyl revival began in the 2000s.

Mechanism

A stylus rides in the spiral groove of a rotating disc; its vibrations are converted — mechanically in acoustic players, electrically via a cartridge in modern turntables — into an audio signal. Speed, tracking force, and equalization (standardized as the RIAA curve) govern fidelity.
