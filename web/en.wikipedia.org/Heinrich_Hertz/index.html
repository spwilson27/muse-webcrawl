Heinrich Rudolf Hertz (22 February 1857 – 1 January 1894) was a German physicist who first conclusively proved the existence of the electromagnetic waves predicted by James Clerk Maxwell's equations of electromagnetism. The SI unit of frequency, the hertz (Hz), is named in his honour. His laboratory demonstration in 1887–88 of generating and detecting "Hertzian waves" (radio waves) laid the foundation for wireless telegraphy and radio.

Life and education

Hertz was born in Hamburg into a prosperous family; his father Gustav Ferdinand Hertz was a barrister and later senator. Showing early aptitude for both science and languages, he studied engineering at Frankfurt and Munich before turning to physics at the University of Berlin, where he took his doctorate in 1880 under Hermann von Helmholtz. He became a lecturer at Kiel in 1883 and professor of physics at the Technische Hochschule Karlsruhe in 1885; in 1889 he succeeded Rudolf Clausius at the University of Bonn.

Electromagnetic waves

At Karlsruhe, Hertz built an apparatus with an induction-coil oscillator producing sparks across a gap, and a separate resonant loop receiver with a tiny spark gap. In 1887 he showed that signals travelled across his laboratory without wires, and in 1888 he demonstrated that the waves could be reflected, refracted and polarised like light, and measured their velocity, finding it equal to the speed of light — confirming Maxwell's theory that light itself is an electromagnetic wave. During the same period he noticed the photoelectric effect, reporting that ultraviolet light falling on electrodes facilitated spark discharge, a phenomenon later explained by Einstein.

Contact mechanics and later work

Hertz also contributed foundational work on contact mechanics (the Hertzian contact stress, 1882) and, at Bonn, on cathode rays and a treatise on mechanics, Die Prinzipien der Mechanik, published posthumously. He died at Bonn on 1 January 1894, aged 36, of complications following surgery for granulomatosis with polyangiitis (then diagnosed as a bone infection).

Legacy

Asked about the practical use of his waves, Hertz reportedly replied, "It's of no use whatsoever" — yet within a decade Guglielmo Marconi and others had built practical wireless systems upon them. The hertz became the international unit of frequency in 1930 (adopted by the SI in 1960). A lunar crater and the Hertz Foundation bear his name, as do countless streets and schools in Germany.
