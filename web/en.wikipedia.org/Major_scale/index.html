Major scale

The ascending and descending C major scale, which contains no sharps or flats.

The major scale (or Ionian mode) is one of the most commonly used musical scales, widely used in Western music. Like many musical scales, it is made up of seven notes; the eighth duplicates the first at double its frequency so that it is called a higher octave. The seven pitches, ordered by pitch, are commonly named using solfège syllables: Do, Re, Mi, Fa, Sol, La, Ti (or Si), Do.

Structure

The major scale is a diatonic scale. Its sequence of intervals between successive notes is whole step, whole step, half step, whole step, whole step, whole step, half step (often abbreviated W–W–H–W–W–W–H). In semitones, starting from the tonic, the pattern is 0, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11, 12. The half steps occur between the third and fourth degrees and between the seventh and eighth degrees.

A major scale may be built by starting on any note; the key signature of the resulting scale indicates the sharps or flats required. The circle of fifths orders the fifteen major keys from C major (no sharps or flats) through sharp keys such as G, D and A major, and flat keys such as F, B♭ and E♭ major.

Scale degrees

Each note of the major scale has a traditional name and function:

  • 1st – Tonic
  • 2nd – Supertonic
  • 3rd – Mediant
  • 4th – Subdominant
  • 5th – Dominant
  • 6th – Submediant
  • 7th – Leading tone
  • 8th – Tonic (octave)

The tonic, subdominant and dominant degrees anchor the harmony of music in a major key; chords built on these degrees (I, IV and V) are sufficient to harmonise many simple melodies.

Modes

Rotating the major scale so that it begins on each successive degree produces the seven diatonic modes: Ionian (on the 1st degree), Dorian (2nd), Phrygian (3rd), Lydian (4th), Mixolydian (5th), Aeolian (6th, identical to the natural minor scale) and Locrian (7th). Each mode has a distinct character and is used in genres from jazz and rock to folk and liturgical music.

Relation to the minor scale

Every major scale has a relative minor whose tonic lies a minor third (three semitones) below the major tonic and which uses exactly the same notes and key signature. For example, A minor is the relative minor of C major. The parallel minor, by contrast, shares the tonic but has a different key signature.
