Renvoi

Renvoi
FieldConflict of laws (private international law)
Term originFrench renvoi, "send back"
Landmark caseForgo case (France, 1878–1882)
FormsSingle renvoi, double (total) renvoi, no renvoi

Renvoi is a doctrine in the conflict of laws (private international law) that arises when a court's choice-of-law rules point to the law of a foreign country, and that country's own conflict rules point back to the law of the forum (remission) or onward to the law of a third country (transmission). The court must decide whether a reference to "the law" of a country means only its internal (domestic) rules, or its law including its conflict-of-laws rules.

The problem

Suppose a court in country A must decide a succession case governed by the law of country B, the deceased's nationality. If B's conflict rules say succession is governed by the law of the domicile, which is A, then accepting B's conflict rules leads the court in A to apply its own law — a remission. Rejecting renvoi, the court simply applies B's internal law. Accepting it can in principle produce an endless loop ("circulus inextricabilis"), which courts avoid by accepting the first reference back.

Approaches

Legal systems differ. No renvoi: many jurisdictions and modern EU regulations (such as Rome I and Rome II for contract and tort) exclude renvoi entirely, referring only to internal law. Single renvoi: the forum accepts a remission or transmission once and applies the internal law it points to — the approach famously adopted by the French Cour de cassation in the Forgo litigation over a Bavarian national's estate. Double (or total) renvoi: used in some English cases, the forum court decides exactly as the foreign court would, including the foreign court's own renvoi doctrine — as in Re Annesley [1926] and Re Ross [1930].

Scope

Renvoi is generally confined to areas such as succession, marriage validity and title to land, where achieving the same result as the foreign court (decisional harmony) matters most. It is excluded in commercial matters like contract and tort in many codifications, and the doctrine is applied less and less as modern statutes define their references expressly.
