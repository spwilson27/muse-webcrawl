The foundry model is a business model in the semiconductor industry in which a company (a foundry, also called a fab) specializes in manufacturing integrated circuits designed by other companies, rather than designing and marketing chips of its own. Companies that design chips but outsource manufacturing are called fabless companies. The model separates chip design from fabrication, allowing each side to specialize and invest accordingly.

History

The pure-play foundry model is generally credited to Morris Chang, who founded Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC) in 1987 with support from the Taiwanese government. Before the foundry model, most chip companies were integrated device manufacturers (IDMs) that designed and fabricated their own products, an approach requiring ever larger capital expenditure as fabrication plants ("fabs") grew more expensive.

Structure and consequences

Foundries invest tens of billions of dollars in fabs equipped with state-of-the-art process technologies, and sell wafer capacity to many customers. The model enabled the rise of fabless design firms such as Qualcomm, Nvidia, and AMD (which spun off its fabs as GlobalFoundries in 2009), and underlies most modern electronics supply chains. Its concentration in a small number of firms and regions, especially Taiwan, has drawn geopolitical attention.
