Intracluster medium

In astronomy, the intracluster medium (ICM) is the superheated plasma that permeates a galaxy cluster. The gas consists mainly of ionized hydrogen and helium and accounts for the bulk of the baryonic material in galaxy clusters, outweighing the stars in the cluster's galaxies. Heated to temperatures of tens of millions of kelvin (typically 10–100 million K), the ICM radiates brightly in X-rays, primarily by thermal bremsstrahlung.

Composition and mass

The ICM is enriched with heavy elements expelled from galaxies by supernovae and stellar winds, so its metallicity (commonly around a third of the solar value) records the cluster's history of star formation. Although the ICM dominates the baryonic mass, the total mass of a cluster is dominated by dark matter; the hot gas in hydrostatic equilibrium is a key tool for measuring cluster masses.

Cooling flows and feedback

In dense cluster cores the gas should radiatively cool and flow inward within a fraction of the cluster's age — the "cooling flow problem" — yet observations show far less cold gas and star formation than this predicts. Energy input from the central galaxy's active galactic nucleus, visible as jets inflating bubbles in the X-ray gas, is believed to offset most of the cooling.

Observation

Besides X-ray imaging and spectroscopy (used to derive temperature, density and chemical profiles), the ICM is observable through the Sunyaev–Zeldovich effect, in which cosmic microwave background photons are scattered by hot electrons, producing a detectable distortion used to find distant clusters.
