Herringbone pattern

Named forBones of the herring fish
GeometryRectangles laid at 45° or 90° in zigzag rows
UsesFloors, paving, masonry, textiles, tiling

The herringbone pattern is an arrangement of rectangles — or, in weaving, of a broken twill — forming a distinctive zigzag design said to resemble the bones of a herring. It is one of the oldest and most widely used decorative patterns, found in Roman road paving, parquet floors, brickwork, and tweed clothing.

Layout

In flooring and paving, equal rectangular blocks are laid with the short end of each against the long side of the next, producing columns of interlocking "V" shapes. In the classic 90-degree herringbone the blocks meet at right angles; in the 45-degree variant the whole pattern is rotated so the zigzag runs diagonally. Unlike the similar chevron pattern, herringbone uses complete rectangles rather than parallelogram-cut ends.

History

The Romans used the layout, called opus spicatum, in roads and masonry. It became a hallmark of French parquet flooring in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and a staple of brick paving and basketry. In textiles, "herringbone" describes a broken twill weave producing columns of diagonal lines reversing direction, long favored for suits and overcoats, especially in tweed.

Applications

Today herringbone is used in wood and tile flooring, kitchen splashbacks, driveways, and fashion fabrics, valued for the sense of movement and texture it gives a flat surface.
