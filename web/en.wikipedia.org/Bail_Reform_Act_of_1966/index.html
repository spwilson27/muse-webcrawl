The Bail Reform Act of 1966 (Pub. L. 89-465) was a United States federal statute that transformed pretrial release in federal criminal cases. Before the Act, federal bail was set almost exclusively as a money bond, with the result that indigent defendants remained jailed before trial solely because they could not pay. The Act established a statutory presumption in favor of release on personal recognizance for non-capital offenses, requiring judges to release a defendant on their own recognizance — or impose the least restrictive conditions needed — unless the court determined that such release would not reasonably assure the defendant's appearance at trial.

Background

The Act grew out of the bail reform movement of the early 1960s, including Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy's campaign against wealth-based detention and the Manhattan Bail Project (founded 1961 by the Vera Foundation), which demonstrated that defendants released without money bail returned for trial at high rates when their community ties were verified. The 1966 Act was the first major federal overhaul of bail since the Judiciary Act of 1789.

Provisions

The Act directed judicial officers to consider factors such as the nature of the offense, the defendant's family ties, length of residence, employment, financial resources, character, past convictions, and record of appearance. It authorized non-financial conditions of release — including third-party custody, restrictions on travel or associations, and reporting requirements — and provided for review of release conditions and penalties for failure to appear. In capital cases, or after conviction, the presumption did not apply in the same way.

Legacy and replacement

The 1966 Act influenced bail reform statutes in many states. However, it did not permit detention based on a defendant's danger to the community, and the later preventive-detention movement led Congress to replace it with the Bail Reform Act of 1984, which authorized pretrial detention without bail for defendants found to pose a serious flight risk or danger, and whose core provisions were upheld in United States v. Salerno (1987).
