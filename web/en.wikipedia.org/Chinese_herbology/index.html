Chinese herbology


Dried herbs in a traditional pharmacy

Chinese herbology is the theory of traditional Chinese herbal therapy, which accounts for the majority of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) treatments. Herbal medicine in China has a recorded history of more than two thousand years, beginning with texts such as the Shennong Ben Cao Jing (The Divine Farmer's Materia Medica), compiled around the early Han dynasty. In contemporary practice, substances used are predominantly of plant origin, though traditional formulas may also include minerals and some animal products.

Principles

Within TCM theory, each herb is described by its taste (sour, bitter, sweet, pungent, salty), its thermal nature (cold, cool, neutral, warm, hot), its toxicity, and the meridians it is said to enter. Herbs are rarely used singly; instead, practitioners prescribe formulas containing multiple ingredients, traditionally arranged in a hierarchy of "emperor", "minister", "assistant" and "envoy" herbs intended to act synergistically and to mitigate adverse effects. Standard formulas recorded in classical texts such as Zhang Zhongjing's Shang Han Lun remain widely used, and preparations are commonly taken as decoctions, powders, pills or granules.

Evidence and safety

Some Chinese herbs have yielded important pharmaceuticals: artemisinin, derived from Artemisia annua (qinghao) following investigation of classical texts, became the basis of frontline antimalarial drugs and earned Tu Youyou a share of the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. However, the scientific evidence for most traditional herbal formulas is regarded as limited or inconclusive, and safety concerns include adulteration with pharmaceuticals, contamination with heavy metals, inherent toxicities (such as aristolochic acid, linked to kidney failure and cancer), and interactions with conventional medicines. Use of endangered-species products in some traditional preparations is also controversial and increasingly regulated.
