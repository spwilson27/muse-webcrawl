Orientation (vector space)

The orientation of a real vector space, or of a coordinate system, is the choice of which ordered bases are to be considered positively oriented and which negatively oriented. An orientation is a way of consistently distinguishing between clockwise and counterclockwise arrangements, or between left-handed and right-handed coordinate systems. Every real finite-dimensional vector space has exactly two orientations, and specifying one of them makes the space an oriented vector space.

Definition

Two ordered bases of a real vector space are said to have the same orientation if the change-of-basis matrix between them has a positive determinant, and opposite orientations if the determinant is negative. Because the determinant of an invertible real matrix is either positive or negative, this partitions the set of all ordered bases into exactly two equivalence classes; an orientation is a choice of which class to call positive. In three-dimensional space, the familiar instance is the distinction between right-handed coordinate systems (such as the standard x, y, z axes) and left-handed ones, described by the right-hand rule.

Zero-dimensional and infinite-dimensional cases

By convention, the orientation of a zero-dimensional vector space is a sign, either +1 or −1. For infinite-dimensional spaces the notions are more subtle, because the determinant is not generally defined; instead, one considers equivalence classes of bases under transformations that differ from a reference basis by a determinant-class operator, as in the theory of Fredholm operators.

Related notions

Orientation extends from vector spaces to manifolds: a manifold is orientable if its tangent spaces can be oriented consistently, which is impossible for surfaces such as the Möbius strip and the Klein bottle. Orientation underlies signed volume (the determinant as a measure of oriented volume) and the conventions of vector calculus, including the cross product and Stokes' theorem.
