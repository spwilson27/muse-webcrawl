Persistence of vision

Persistence of vision is the optical phenomenon in which an image continues to appear in one's vision after the exposure to the original image has ceased, traditionally said to last for roughly 1/25 to 1/16 of a second. It has historically been invoked to explain the perception of motion in film and animation: a rapid sequence of still images is perceived as continuous movement. Modern vision scientists regard the explanation as incomplete, attributing motion perception chiefly to apparent motion phenomena such as the phi phenomenon and beta movement, while afterimages remain a real but distinct effect.

History

The phenomenon was described by ancient writers, including Lucretius, and was studied experimentally in the nineteenth century by Peter Mark Roget and others. Victorian optical toys such as the thaumatrope, the phenakistoscope and the zoetrope exploited the effect to create simple animations, and it was popularly cited in early accounts of how cinema works.

Afterimages and flicker

A bright stimulus leaves a positive afterimage that fades over a fraction of a second; complementary-colour (negative) afterimages can last longer. Related is the critical flicker fusion threshold, the frequency — typically around 50–90 Hz depending on brightness — at which a flickering light appears steady. These effects concern the visibility of a stimulus, not the perception of smooth motion between distinct frames.

Motion perception

Research since the early twentieth century, beginning with Max Wertheimer's experiments on apparent motion (1912), showed that viewers perceive movement between two stationary stimuli flashed in succession even where persistence of vision cannot account for it. Film projection at 24 frames per second, with shutters that flash each frame two or three times to reduce flicker, relies on these apparent-motion mechanisms to produce the impression of continuous movement.
