Paella is a rice dish originally from the Valencian Community in Spain, regarded as one of the best-known dishes in Spanish cuisine. It takes its name from the wide, shallow traditional pan used to cook it over an open fire — paella in Valencian, from the Latin patella, "pan."

History

The dish originated in the rice-growing country around the Albufera lagoon south of Valencia, where farm labourers cooked rice with whatever was at hand — beans, snails, rabbit, and vegetables — in a communal pan. Saffron, introduced during the Moorish period, gives the rice its golden colour. By the late nineteenth century paella had spread across Spain and acquired seafood and mixed variants along the Mediterranean coast.

Preparation

Traditional paella uses short-grain rice such as Bomba, which absorbs liquid without breaking down. The rice cooks in broth with a sofrito of tomato and olive oil, and it is never stirred once the liquid is added. A prized feature is the socarrat, the thin crust of toasted rice that forms at the bottom of the pan. Authentic paella valenciana contains chicken, rabbit, green beans, lima beans, and sometimes snails; Valencian cooks have long campaigned to protect the recipe from additions such as chorizo, which traditionalists consider anathema.
