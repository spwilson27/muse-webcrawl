The Hoosier National Forest is a property administered by the United States Forest Service in the hills of southern Indiana. Comprising about 203,000 acres (820 km2) of public land in nine counties, it is the only national forest in the state. Its headquarters are in Bedford, with a satellite office in Tell City.

History

Much of the land that became the Hoosier was once farmed, but the thin, rocky soils of the unglaciated hill country proved poor for agriculture, and many farms were abandoned during the Great Depression. The federal government began purchasing cut-over and eroded land in the 1930s, and the forest was formally established in 1951. Civilian Conservation Corps crews planted pines, built roads and constructed fire towers during the 1930s.

Geography and ecology

The forest lies within the Crawford Upland and Mitchell Plain regions, characterized by rolling hills, sinkholes, caves and limestone karst. Deciduous hardwoods such as oak and hickory dominate, intermixed with planted pine stands. The forest includes the Charles C. Deam Wilderness, designated in 1982, which covers roughly 13,000 acres near Lake Monroe.

Recreation

Popular activities include hiking, horseback riding, mountain biking, camping, hunting and fishing. Trails such as the Two Lakes Loop and the German Ridge network serve visitors, and the forest borders the Ohio River to the south.
