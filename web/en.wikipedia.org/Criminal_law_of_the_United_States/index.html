Criminal law of the United States

Criminal law of the United States is the body of law that defines criminal offences and regulates the prosecution and punishment of offenders in the United States. It operates at two levels: the federal government prosecutes offences defined by federal statutes, while each of the fifty states maintains its own criminal code. The system derives largely from the English common law, although today nearly all crimes are defined by statute rather than by judicial decision.

Sources of criminal law

Federal crimes are codified principally in Title 18 of the United States Code and include offences such as mail and wire fraud, racketeering under the RICO Act, drug trafficking, and crimes committed on federal property or across state lines. State criminal codes cover the bulk of ordinary crime, including homicide, assault, robbery, burglary, and theft. The American Law Institute's Model Penal Code, published in 1962, has influenced the criminal codes of many states.

Elements of a crime

Most crimes require both a prohibited act (actus reus) and a culpable mental state (mens rea), such as acting purposely, knowingly, recklessly, or negligently. The prosecution must prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt, a standard articulated by the Supreme Court in In re Winship (1970). Defences include self-defence, insanity, duress, and entrapment.

Classification and punishment

Offences are generally classified as felonies, punishable by more than a year's imprisonment, and misdemeanours, punishable by a year or less. Sentencing may involve fines, probation, imprisonment, or, for certain homicides in some jurisdictions, capital punishment. The Federal Sentencing Guidelines, advisory since United States v. Booker (2005), structure federal sentencing decisions.

Constitutional limits

The United States Constitution constrains criminal law through provisions such as the prohibitions on ex post facto laws and bills of attainder, the Fourth Amendment's limits on searches and seizures, the Fifth Amendment's privilege against self-incrimination, and the Eighth Amendment's ban on cruel and unusual punishment.
