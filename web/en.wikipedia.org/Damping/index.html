Damping

In physical systems, damping is the loss of energy of an oscillating system by dissipation. Damping is an influence within or upon an oscillatory system that has the effect of reducing or preventing its oscillation. Examples include viscous drag in mechanical systems, resistance in electronic oscillators, and absorption and scattering of light in optical oscillators.

Damped harmonic oscillator

The archetypal model is a mass on a spring subject to a resistive force proportional to velocity. Its equation of motion is:

m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x = 0

where m is mass, c is the damping coefficient, and k is the spring constant. The behaviour is characterized by the damping ratio ζ (zeta), the ratio of the actual damping to the critical damping.

Regimes

  • Underdamped (ζ < 1): the system oscillates with an amplitude that decays exponentially over time.
  • Critically damped (ζ = 1): the system returns to equilibrium as quickly as possible without oscillating.
  • Overdamped (ζ > 1): the system returns to equilibrium slowly, without oscillating.
  • Undamped (ζ = 0): the system oscillates at its natural frequency indefinitely (an idealization).

Mechanisms

Damping arises through several mechanisms: viscous damping from fluid resistance; Coulomb (dry) friction between sliding surfaces; hysteretic or material damping from internal friction within solids; and magnetic damping from eddy currents. In electrical circuits, resistance in an RLC circuit produces analogous behaviour, and the quality factor Q is inversely related to damping.

Applications

Damping is engineered into shock absorbers and vehicle suspensions, seismic base isolation and tuned mass dampers in tall buildings (such as Taipei 101), door closers, and analog measuring instruments, where critical damping allows a needle to settle quickly without overshoot. Unwanted damping is minimized in musical instruments, clocks, and resonators.
