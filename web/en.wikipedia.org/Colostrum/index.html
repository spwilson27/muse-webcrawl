Colostrum

Colostrum, sometimes called "first milk" or (in cattle) "beestings", is the form of milk produced by the mammary glands in late pregnancy and the first few days after giving birth. Compared with mature milk it is thicker and more yellow, richer in protein — especially antibodies — and lower in fat and lactose.

Composition and function

Colostrum is rich in immunoglobulins (predominantly secretory IgA in humans), lactoferrin, leukocytes, growth factors and fat-soluble vitamins including vitamin A, which gives it its yellow colour from carotenoids. Its immune components provide passive immunity to the newborn while its own immune system matures, coating the gut and protecting against pathogens. In many farm animals, whose placentas do not transfer antibodies, absorbing colostral immunoglobulins in the first hours of life is essential for survival. Colostrum also has a mild laxative effect that helps the newborn pass meconium.

Human colostrum

Human colostrum is produced in small volumes — typically only a few millilitres per feed on the first day — matched to the small capacity of the newborn stomach. Over roughly three to five days it transitions through transitional milk to mature milk. Health authorities recommend early initiation of breastfeeding so that infants receive colostrum.

Uses and supplements

Bovine colostrum is sold as a dietary supplement and used in some traditional foods, such as the Indian sweet junnu. It is also used in veterinary and neonatal care for orphaned animals. Claims for benefits in adult athletes and immune support have limited clinical evidence.
