Marcellus Formation

Extent of the Marcellus Formation beneath the Appalachian Basin of the eastern United States.

The Marcellus Formation or Marcellus Shale is a Middle Devonian age unit of marine sedimentary rock found in eastern North America. Named for a distinctive outcrop near the village of Marcellus, New York, the formation extends through the Appalachian Basin beneath New York, Pennsylvania, Ohio, West Virginia and parts of neighbouring states and Ontario. Its organic-rich black shale contains enormous quantities of natural gas, whose large-scale extraction by horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing, beginning in the 2000s, transformed the formation into one of the world's most productive gas fields — and a focus of environmental controversy.

Geology

The Marcellus was deposited about 390 million years ago in a deep foreland basin that formed during the Acadian orogeny. Its principal unit, the Union Springs Member, is a black, carbon-rich shale laid down in oxygen-poor bottom waters; organic matter preserved in the sediment generated natural gas as the rock was buried and heated. The formation ranges from a few metres thick at its northern outcrops to more than 60 metres in the basin's depocentre, and lies at depths of roughly 1.2 to 2.6 kilometres across much of the productive area.

Gas development

Although gas was long known in the Marcellus — the first American gas well, drilled at Fredonia, New York in 1821, drew from Devonian shale — commercial production awaited the combination of horizontal drilling and high-volume hydraulic fracturing, adapted from the Barnett Shale of Texas. Range Resources' successful well in Washington County, Pennsylvania in 2004 triggered a leasing and drilling boom. By the 2010s the Marcellus was the largest source of natural gas in the United States, with estimates of technically recoverable gas in the hundreds of trillions of cubic feet. New York State, however, banned high-volume hydraulic fracturing in 2014–2015.

Environmental issues

Development of the Marcellus has been debated over water use and wastewater disposal, methane emissions and climate impact, risks of groundwater contamination, induced seismicity from disposal wells, air pollution, and effects on landscapes and communities. Studies have generally found that properly managed operations pose manageable risks, while documented cases of contamination have usually been attributed to surface spills or faulty well integrity rather than the fracturing process itself; the balance of costs and benefits remains politically contested across the region.
