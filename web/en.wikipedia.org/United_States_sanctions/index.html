United States sanctions

United States sanctions are financial and trade restrictions imposed against individuals, entities, and jurisdictions in the United States, as well as foreign countries, in furtherance of American foreign policy and national security goals. The United States has imposed economic sanctions since the early 19th century, including the Embargo Act of 1807, and the use of sanctions expanded greatly during the 20th and 21st centuries.

Administered byOffice of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC), U.S. Department of the Treasury
Legal basesTrading with the Enemy Act, IEEPA, Magnitsky Act, CAATSA, and others
FormsTrade embargoes, asset freezes, financial restrictions, export controls
OFAC, part of the Treasury Department, administers most U.S. sanctions programs.

Administration

The Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) in the Treasury Department administers and enforces most sanctions programs, maintaining the Specially Designated Nationals (SDN) list of blocked persons. The State Department and Commerce Department administer additional measures, including arms embargoes and export controls on sensitive technology. Presidents typically declare a national emergency under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) of 1977 to impose new sanctions programs.

Programs

Comprehensive embargoes have at various times targeted Cuba (since 1960), Iran, North Korea, Syria, and Russia-occupied regions of Ukraine, while narrower "targeted" or "smart" sanctions focus on named officials, companies, banks, and sectors. The Global Magnitsky Act authorizes sanctions on individuals implicated in human rights abuses or corruption anywhere in the world. Secondary sanctions threaten foreign parties who transact with sanctioned targets, extending U.S. reach through the centrality of the dollar in global finance. Critics argue sanctions often harm ordinary populations and can be ineffective at changing regime behavior, while defenders describe them as a tool between diplomacy and war.
