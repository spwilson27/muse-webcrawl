Removal jurisdiction

In the United States, removal jurisdiction allows a defendant in certain actions filed in state court to "remove" the case to the federal district court for the district and division in which the state action is pending. Removal is governed principally by 28 U.S.C. §§ 1441–1455.

Requirements

Under § 1441(a), a civil action may generally be removed only if the federal court would have had original jurisdiction over it — that is, if it raises a federal question under § 1331 or satisfies diversity jurisdiction under § 1332 (complete diversity of citizenship and an amount in controversy exceeding $75,000). A case may not be removed on diversity grounds if any defendant is a citizen of the state in which the action was brought (the "forum defendant rule"), and defendants generally must act within 30 days of service. Ordinarily all defendants who have been properly joined and served must consent to removal.

Procedure and remand

The removing defendant files a notice of removal in federal court and notifies the state court, which then proceeds no further. The plaintiff may move to remand the case to state court, ordinarily within 30 days for procedural defects; lack of subject-matter jurisdiction can be raised at any time. If the federal court determines at any point that it lacks subject matter jurisdiction, the case must be remanded under § 1447(c). Orders granting remand for lack of jurisdiction are generally unreviewable on appeal under § 1447(d).

Special removal statutes

Separate statutes permit removal of cases involving federal officers (§ 1442), members of the armed forces (§ 1442a), civil rights enforcement (§ 1443), and certain class actions under the Class Action Fairness Act of 2005, which relaxed the traditional diversity requirements for large interstate class actions. Criminal prosecutions in state court may also be removed in limited circumstances.
