Center pivot irrigation (also called water-wheel and circle irrigation) is a method of crop irrigation in which equipment rotates around a central pivot and crops are watered with sprinklers. A circular area around the pivot is irrigated, creating the distinctive pattern of green circles seen from aircraft and satellites in arid agricultural regions.

Design

The system consists of a pipeline supported by wheeled towers (trusses) that move in a circle around a fixed central point where water enters. The outermost towers set the pace, and alignment systems keep the spans in line. Water is applied through sprinklers, spray heads, or low-energy precision application (LEPA) nozzles along the pipe; modern systems add drop hoses to reduce evaporation and wind drift, variable-rate irrigation controlled by GPS, and remote monitoring. A typical pivot covers a quarter-section of land (about 130 acres) with a radius near 400 metres, leaving the corners of the square field unirrigated or served by corner attachments.

History

Frank Zybach, a farmer near Strasburg, Colorado, developed the self-propelled circular system in the late 1940s (patent granted 1952). Commercialized by companies such as Valley (Valmont) and later Lindsay (Zimmatic) and Reinke, center pivots spread rapidly across the U.S. Great Plains from the 1950s–1970s, drawing on the Ogallala Aquifer, and later worldwide, including large developments in Saudi Arabia, Libya, and Egypt using fossil groundwater.

Advantages and concerns

Advantages include high labour efficiency, uniform application, the ability to irrigate uneven terrain, and precise control of water and fertilizer (fertigation). Concerns include the capital and energy costs, evaporation losses with high-pressure impact sprinklers, and — most significantly — depletion of aquifers when pumping exceeds recharge, as in parts of the High Plains.
