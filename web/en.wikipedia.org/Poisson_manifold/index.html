Poisson manifold

In differential geometry, a field in mathematics, a Poisson manifold is a smooth manifold endowed with a Poisson structure. The notion of Poisson manifold generalises that of symplectic manifold, which in turn generalises the phase space of Hamiltonian mechanics.

A Poisson structure on a smooth manifold M is a Lie bracket { , } on the algebra of smooth functions C∞(M) taking values in ℝ, subject to the Leibniz rule. Equivalently, a Poisson structure is given by a bivector field π on M satisfying a certain partial differential equation, namely that its Schouten–Nijenhuis bracket with itself vanishes: [π, π] = 0.

History and motivation

Poisson structures first arose in the work of Siméon Denis Poisson on celestial mechanics, and were developed in the context of classical mechanics throughout the nineteenth century. The modern theory of Poisson manifolds was systematised in the 1970s and 1980s, notably through the work of André Lichnerowicz and Alan Weinstein, who established the fundamental splitting theorem describing the local structure of a Poisson manifold.

Symplectic foliation

A central feature of Poisson geometry is that every Poisson manifold admits a foliation into symplectic leaves: at each point, the image of the Poisson bivector determines a subspace of the tangent space, and these subspaces integrate into immersed symplectic submanifolds. When the Poisson structure is non-degenerate — that is, when there is a single leaf equal to the whole manifold — it corresponds exactly to a symplectic structure.

Examples

  • Any symplectic manifold, with the Poisson bracket defined by its symplectic form.
  • The dual of a Lie algebra, carrying the Lie–Poisson structure.
  • Trivial Poisson structure: the zero bivector, whose symplectic leaves are points.
  • Poisson–Lie groups, which combine a group structure with a compatible Poisson structure.
