Lead poisoning is a type of metal poisoning caused by lead in the body. The brain is the most sensitive organ; in children, even low-level exposure is associated with reduced IQ, attention disorders and behavioural problems, and no safe blood lead level has been identified. Lead interferes with a variety of body processes — it mimics calcium and inhibits enzymes such as δ-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase, disrupting haem synthesis and causing anaemia — and is toxic to the nervous, renal, reproductive and cardiovascular systems.

Sources and history

Lead has been mined and used since antiquity — in Roman plumbing (Latin plumbum, source of the chemical symbol Pb), pewter, coins and sweetening wine — and occupational poisoning of painters, potters and plumbers was described in the ancient world. The great modern exposures were industrial: leaded paint in housing, tetraethyllead in petrol (introduced in the 1920s and phased out worldwide by 2021), lead-acid battery manufacture and recycling, smelting, and contaminated drinking water, as in the Flint, Michigan crisis of 2014–15. Blood lead levels in industrialised countries fell dramatically after the removal of lead from petrol and paint, a public-health success documented by studies such as Clair Patterson's campaign against environmental lead contamination.

Signs, diagnosis and treatment

Symptoms range from fatigue, abdominal colic and constipation to wrist drop, kidney failure and, at very high levels, encephalopathy and death. Chronic exposure leaves "lead lines" at the gums (Burton lines) and in growing bones. Diagnosis is by blood lead measurement; the U.S. CDC reference value for children is 3.5 µg/dL (lowered from 5 in 2021). Management centres on identifying and removing the source; chelation therapy is reserved for high levels or symptoms. Prevention — abating lead paint, replacing lead service lines, regulating battery recycling — is considered far more effective than treatment, since neurological injury in children is largely irreversible.
