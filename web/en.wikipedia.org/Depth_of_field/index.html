Depth of field

Depth of field

The depth of field (DOF) is the distance between the nearest and the farthest objects that are in acceptably sharp focus in an image captured with a camera. Although a lens can precisely focus at only one distance at a time, the decrease in sharpness on either side of the focal plane is gradual, so within the DOF the unsharpness is imperceptible under normal viewing conditions.

Factors

Depth of field is determined by the aperture (a smaller aperture — larger f-number — yields greater DOF), the focal length (shorter lenses give more DOF at the same framing distance), the subject distance (DOF grows roughly with the square of distance), and the size of the acceptable circle of confusion, which depends on sensor or film format and final image size. For a given subject magnification, DOF is approximately independent of focal length — a fact often misunderstood.

Hyperfocal distance and diffraction

Focusing at the hyperfocal distance maximizes DOF, making everything from half that distance to infinity acceptably sharp — a technique favored in landscape photography. Stopping down too far, however, softens the whole image through diffraction, so there is an optimum aperture for overall sharpness.

Creative use

Shallow depth of field isolates subjects from blurred backgrounds (whose quality is termed bokeh) and is characteristic of portraiture and fast telephoto lenses; deep focus keeps foreground and background sharp, as in the cinematography of Citizen Kane. Techniques such as focus stacking combine multiple exposures to extend DOF beyond optical limits in macro photography, while tilt-shift lenses manipulate the focal plane itself via the Scheimpflug principle.
