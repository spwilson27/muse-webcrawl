Spontaneous generation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Pasteur's swan-neck flask, used to refute spontaneous generation

Spontaneous generation is the obsolete scientific theory that living organisms can arise from non-living matter — for example, that maggots arise from rotting meat or mice from grain and rags. Formulated by Aristotle and accepted for nearly two thousand years, it was progressively disproved by experiment.

Disproof

In 1668 Francesco Redi showed that maggots appear on meat only when flies can reach it. Lazzaro Spallanzani's boiled-broth experiments in the 18th century weakened belief in microbial spontaneous generation, though critics argued he had excluded air. The debate was decisively settled by Louis Pasteur in 1859–1861: using swan-neck flasks that admitted air but trapped dust, he showed that sterile broth remained sterile indefinitely, demonstrating that microorganisms come from other microorganisms — the principle of biogenesis.

Distinction from abiogenesis

The refutation of spontaneous generation concerns the rapid appearance of life under present-day conditions; it does not address abiogenesis, the natural origin of life on the early Earth over geological timescales, which remains an active field of research.
