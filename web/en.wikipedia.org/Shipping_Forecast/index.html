Shipping Forecast


The sea areas of the Shipping Forecast.

The Shipping Forecast is a BBC Radio broadcast of weather reports and forecasts for the seas around the British Isles, produced by the Met Office and broadcast on BBC Radio 4 on behalf of the Maritime and Coastguard Agency. The forecast covers some 31 sea areas, whose evocative names — Viking, Forties, Cromarty, Forth, Tyne, Dogger, Fisher, German Bight, Humber, Thames, Dover, Wight, Portland, Plymouth, Biscay, Trafalgar, FitzRoy, Sole, Lundy, Fastnet, Irish Sea, Shannon, Rockall, Malin, Hebrides, Bailey, Fair Isle, Faeroes, and Southeast Iceland — are familiar to generations of listeners.

Format

Each bulletin follows a strict format and word limit. After a general synopsis of pressure systems, the forecast for each area gives wind direction and speed (on the Beaufort scale), precipitation, visibility, and sea state, along with warnings of gales, storms, or poor visibility. Bulletins are broadcast four times daily, with the late-night edition introduced by the music "Sailing By" and traditionally followed by the national anthem in the early hours — a quirk that has made the forecast a beloved piece of British cultural life, far beyond the sailors for whom it remains a safety service.

History

Weather bulletins for mariners began in the 1860s under Admiral Robert FitzRoy, founder of the Met Office, following the wreck of the Royal Charter in 1859. The modern Shipping Forecast took shape in 1924, and its sea areas have been adjusted several times — notably in 1949, when Finisterre was added, and in 2002, when Finisterre was renamed FitzRoy to avoid confusion with a Spanish area of the same name. During the Second World War the forecasts were suspended.

Cultural impact

The forecast's rhythmic, poetic litany of names has inspired poems, songs, and novels; poets including Seamus Heaney and Carol Ann Duffy have written about it, and it is frequently cited as a soothing marker of national continuity. Listeners on land far outnumber mariners, many of whom now receive the same information via Navtex and the internet, yet the broadcast continues as both a working service and a cultural institution.
