Partial equilibrium

In economics, partial equilibrium is a condition of economic equilibrium that analyses a single market or sector in isolation, taking equilibrium prices and conditions in all other markets as given. It is contrasted with general equilibrium, which models all markets simultaneously and allows feedbacks between them. Partial equilibrium analysis is the framework behind the familiar supply-and-demand diagram for one good, and it was systematised by Alfred Marshall in his Principles of Economics (1890).

Assumptions and method

The approach holds constant the prices of other goods, consumer incomes, and conditions in related markets — the ceteris paribus ("other things equal") device. The equilibrium price and quantity in the market under study are then found where its supply and demand curves intersect. Marshall justified the simplification when the good absorbs only a small share of consumers' budgets, so that changes in its price have negligible effects elsewhere in the economy.

Applications

Partial equilibrium is the workhorse of applied microeconomics and policy analysis: the effects of a tax or subsidy on a particular market, the incidence of a minimum wage in a low-wage labour market, trade policy for a single industry, and welfare measures such as consumer and producer surplus are all standardly computed within this framework. Much of cost–benefit analysis is partial-equilibrium in spirit.

Limitations

When the market studied is large relative to the economy, or strongly connected to others, ignoring feedbacks can mislead: a tax on energy, for instance, changes costs throughout the economy, and the resulting price changes can alter the market being taxed. In such cases economists turn to general equilibrium models, which are more complete but require far more data and stronger assumptions about the whole economy.
