Pragmatism is a philosophical tradition that began in the United States in the 1870s. It holds that the meaning of a concept or proposition lies in its practical consequences, and that truth and value should be evaluated by their effects in experience and action. Pragmatism is widely considered the first distinctly American school of philosophy.

Origins

The movement emerged from the Metaphysical Club in Cambridge, Massachusetts, where Charles Sanders Peirce formulated the "pragmatic maxim": to clarify a concept, consider what practical effects its object would conceivably have. Peirce's essays of 1877–78, including "How to Make Our Ideas Clear", laid the foundation.

Classical pragmatists

William James popularised pragmatism in his 1907 lectures Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of Thinking, presenting it as a method of settling metaphysical disputes and a theory of truth. John Dewey developed the approach as "instrumentalism", applying it to logic, education, democracy and aesthetics. George Herbert Mead contributed a social account of mind and self.

Core ideas

Pragmatists reject the idea that thought primarily mirrors an independent reality; instead, concepts are tools for prediction, problem-solving and action. Beliefs are rules for action, and inquiry is a self-correcting process tested by its results. The school is pluralistic, fallibilist and meliorist in outlook.

Influence and revival

Pragmatism influenced law, education, political theory and literary criticism, then declined under the rise of analytic philosophy before a major revival in the later 20th century through thinkers such as Richard Rorty, Hilary Putnam and Robert Brandom, often called neopragmatism.
