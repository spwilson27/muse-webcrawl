Thermal

A thermal column (or simply thermal) is a column of rising air in the lower altitudes of Earth's atmosphere, a form of atmospheric updraft. Thermals are created by the uneven heating of Earth's surface from solar radiation, and are an example of convection, specifically atmospheric convection. The Sun warms the ground, which in turn warms the air directly above it. The warmer air near the surface expands, becoming less dense than the surrounding air mass. The mass of lighter air rises, and as it does, it cools due to its expansion in the lower pressure of the higher altitudes. It stops rising when it has cooled to the same temperature, and thus density, as the surrounding air.

Associated with a thermal is a downward flow surrounding the thermal column. The downward-moving exterior is caused by colder air being displaced at the top of the thermal. The size and strength of thermals are influenced by the properties of the lower atmosphere (the troposphere). Generally, when the air is cold, bubbles of warm air are formed by the ground heating the air above it; the warm air rises like a hot air balloon. The air is then said to be unstable, and thermals continue up, forming cumulus clouds. If there is a warm layer above, an inversion can prevent thermals from rising high, and the air is said to be stable.

Thermals on Earth

Thermals are often indicated by the presence of visible cumulus clouds at the apex of the thermal. When a steady wind is present, thermals and their respective cumulus clouds can align in rows oriented with the wind direction, sometimes referred to as "cloud streets" by soaring and glider pilots.

Pilots of gliders, hang gliders and paragliders exploit thermals to gain altitude and extend their flights, a practice known as soaring. Birds such as raptors and storks also use thermals to soar with minimal energy expenditure. Thermals can also form over water under certain conditions, and over urban areas, where dark surfaces such as asphalt absorb more solar heat.

Thermals beyond Earth

Thermals are also seen elsewhere in the Solar System. On Mars, for example, thermals give rise to dust devils. They have also been inferred on other bodies with atmospheres heated by the Sun.
