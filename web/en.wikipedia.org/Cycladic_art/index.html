Cycladic art

A folded-arm figurine of the Spedos type, Early Cycladic II period.

Cycladic art is the distinctive art of the Neolithic and Early Bronze Age Cycladic civilisation of the Cyclades islands in the Aegean Sea (c. 3300–2000 BC). It is best known for its elegant marble figurines — abstract, minimally modelled human figures, usually female with arms folded across the abdomen — which strongly influenced twentieth-century modernist sculptors such as Brâncuși, Modigliani and Henry Moore.

The figurines

Most figurines were carved from the fine white marble of Paros and Naxos using stone tools and abrasives. The canonical type of the Early Cycladic II period (c. 2800–2300 BC) is the folded-arm figure: a flat, elongated body, a tilted-back head with a nose as the only facial feature, and incised details for toes and the pubic triangle. Sizes range from a few centimetres to nearly life-size examples about 1.5 m tall. Traces of pigment show that eyes, hair and jewellery were originally painted in red, blue and black. Named varieties include the Spedos, Dokathismata and Chalandriani types; earlier periods produced cruder "violin-shaped" figures. A small number of male figures depict seated musicians — harpists and a double-flute player — hunters and cup-bearers.

Other artefacts

Cycladic craftsmen also produced marble vessels, including beakers and the curious shallow "frying pan" objects decorated with spirals, ships and female imagery, whose function remains debated. Pottery of the period includes sauceboats and incised pyxides; metalwork in bronze and silver appears later in the sequence.

Function and context

Most figurines come from graves, suggesting a funerary or religious role — possibly representations of a goddess, ancestors, or companions for the dead — but some have been found in settlements, and scholarly opinion remains divided. Because so many pieces on the art market were looted from unrecorded graves, questions of provenance, forgery and repatriation are central to the modern study of Cycladic art. Major collections are held by the Museum of Cycladic Art and the National Archaeological Museum in Athens.
