Monocotyledon

Monocotyledons, commonly referred to as monocots, are a group of flowering plants (angiosperms) whose seeds typically contain only one embryonic leaf, or cotyledon. The group comprises roughly 60,000 species — about a quarter of all flowering plants — including grasses, lilies, orchids, palms, and many of humanity's most important food crops.

Characteristics

Monocots share a suite of anatomical features that distinguish them from the dicotyledons (now largely subsumed within the eudicots):

  • A single cotyledon in the embryo.
  • Parallel leaf venation in most species.
  • Floral parts arranged in multiples of three.
  • Vascular bundles scattered through the stem rather than arranged in a ring, and hence generally no true secondary growth (no woody cambium) — palms and some others thicken by other means.
  • A fibrous, adventitious root system rather than a persistent taproot.

Classification and evolution

Monocots form a well-supported monophyletic clade in the APG classification systems. Molecular evidence places their origin in the Early Cretaceous, over 130 million years ago. Major orders include Asparagales (orchids, asparagus, irises), Liliales (lilies), Arecales (palms), and Poales — the order containing the grass family (Poaceae), which supplies wheat, rice, maize, sugarcane, and bamboo.

Economic importance

Monocots dominate global agriculture: the cereals alone provide the majority of human calories. Other significant monocot products include bananas, pineapples, dates, onions, garlic, vanilla (from an orchid), and ornamental plants such as tulips and lilies.
