Sentimental novel

The sentimental novel or the novel of sensibility is an 18th-century literary genre which celebrates the emotional and intellectual concepts of sentiment, sentimentalism, and sensibility. The genre developed during the Age of Enlightenment in reaction against the perceived rationalism of earlier Augustan literature, instead emphasising feeling as a guide to moral conduct.

Characteristics

Sentimental novels rely on the emotional response of both characters and readers, featuring scenes of distress and tenderness and plots arranged to advance emotions rather than action. The virtuous suffer, weep, and faint; readers were expected to respond with sympathetic tears, which were taken as evidence of good character.

Major works

Key examples include Samuel Richardson's Pamela (1740) and Clarissa (1748), Laurence Sterne's A Sentimental Journey Through France and Italy (1768), Oliver Goldsmith's The Vicar of Wakefield (1766), and Henry Mackenzie's The Man of Feeling (1771). The movement culminated and was partly satirised in works by Jane Austen, notably Sense and Sensibility (1811).

Influence

Sentimentalism influenced the Romantic movement, the epistolary novel, and later "sensation" and domestic fiction of the 19th century. Critics have also traced its effects on humanitarian reform movements, including abolitionism.
