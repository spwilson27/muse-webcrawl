Trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia

The trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias (TACs) are a group of primary headache disorders characterised by unilateral head pain occurring in association with prominent ipsilateral cranial autonomic features, such as tearing, nasal congestion, conjunctival injection, and eyelid swelling. They are classified together in the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3), reflecting a shared pathophysiology involving activation of the trigeminal nerve and the cranial parasympathetic outflow, with a key role for the posterior hypothalamus.

Types

  • Cluster headache — attacks of excruciating, strictly one-sided pain lasting 15 minutes to 3 hours, often occurring in bouts ("clusters") with striking circadian and seasonal regularity, accompanied by restlessness.
  • Paroxysmal hemicrania — shorter (2–30 minutes), more frequent attacks, absolutely responsive to the drug indomethacin.
  • SUNCT and SUNA — short-lasting unilateral neuralgiform headache attacks (seconds to a few minutes), very frequent, with marked autonomic signs; SUNCT includes both conjunctival injection and tearing, SUNA at least one of them or other autonomic features.
  • Hemicrania continua — a continuous, fluctuating one-sided headache with exacerbations, also absolutely responsive to indomethacin.

Diagnosis and treatment

The disorders are distinguished chiefly by attack duration and frequency, and the absolute response to indomethacin defines paroxysmal hemicrania and hemicrania continua. Acute treatments for cluster headache include high-flow oxygen and subcutaneous or intranasal triptans; preventive therapy commonly uses verapamil. An absolute response, or lack of it, to indomethacin is an important diagnostic test, and secondary causes such as pituitary lesions must be excluded with MRI, particularly in atypical cases.
