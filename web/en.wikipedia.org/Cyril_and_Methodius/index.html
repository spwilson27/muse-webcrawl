Cyril and Methodius

Cyril (born Constantine, c. 826–869) and Methodius (c. 815–885) were Byzantine Greek brothers from Thessalonica who became missionaries to the Slavs in the ninth century. Known as the "Apostles to the Slavs", they created the Glagolitic alphabet, the first script for writing a Slavic language, and translated the Bible and liturgical texts into Old Church Slavonic.

The mission to Moravia

In 862 Prince Rastislav of Great Moravia asked the Byzantine emperor for missionaries who could teach Christianity in the Slavonic language. The brothers arrived in 863 with their translations and trained local disciples. Their use of the vernacular in the liturgy brought them into conflict with German clergy who insisted on Latin, and they travelled to Rome, where Pope Hadrian II approved the Slavonic liturgy. Cyril died in Rome in 869 and was buried there.

After Cyril's death

Methodius returned north as papal legate and archbishop, facing imprisonment and opposition from the Frankish clergy. After his death in 885 his followers were expelled from Moravia and found refuge in Bulgaria, where they developed the script later called Cyrillic in Cyril's honour and spread Slavonic letters to Serbia, Kievan Rus' and beyond.

Legacy

Cyril and Methodius are venerated as saints in both Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic traditions. Their feast day, 24 May, is celebrated as a national holiday of education and culture in Bulgaria and North Macedonia, and they were named co-patrons of Europe by Pope John Paul II in 1980.
