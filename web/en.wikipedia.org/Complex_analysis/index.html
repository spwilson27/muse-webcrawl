Complex analysis, traditionally known as the theory of functions of a complex variable, is the branch of mathematical analysis that investigates functions of complex numbers. It is helpful in many branches of mathematics, including algebraic geometry, number theory, analytic combinatorics, and applied mathematics, as well as in physics, including the branches of hydrodynamics, thermodynamics, quantum mechanics, and twistor theory.

Holomorphic functions

Complex analysis is particularly concerned with the analytic functions of a complex variable, known (more generally) as holomorphic functions. A function is holomorphic on an open set if it is complex differentiable at every point of that set. Remarkably, complex differentiability is a much stronger condition than real differentiability: by the Cauchy–Riemann equations, a holomorphic function is automatically infinitely differentiable and equal to its own Taylor series.

Major results

  • Cauchy's integral theorem: the line integral of a holomorphic function around a closed curve in a simply connected domain is zero.
  • Cauchy's integral formula: values of a holomorphic function inside a disc are determined by its values on the boundary.
  • Residue theorem: integrals around isolated singularities are computed from their residues — the basis of contour integration.
  • Liouville's theorem: every bounded entire function is constant, which yields a short proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra.

History

The theory was placed on firm foundations in the 19th century by Augustin-Louis Cauchy, who developed contour integration and the residue calculus, and by Bernhard Riemann, whose geometric approach introduced Riemann surfaces and the Riemann mapping theorem; Karl Weierstrass contributed the power-series viewpoint.
