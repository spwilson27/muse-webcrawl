Island hopping (strategy)

Island hopping in the Pacific War

Island hopping, also called leapfrogging, was a military strategy employed by the Allies in the Pacific War against the Empire of Japan during World War II. The strategy involved bypassing heavily fortified enemy islands instead of capturing every island in sequence, thereby shortening the distance to Japan and establishing airfields and bases within range of the next targets.

Origins and rationale

The idea had roots in pre-war planning (War Plan Orange) but became practical after the Allied victories of 1942–1943. By selectively attacking weaker positions and isolating strongpoints such as Rabaul and Truk, Allied forces cut off Japanese garrisons from supply, leaving them to "wither on the vine." General Douglas MacArthur and Admiral Chester W. Nimitz advanced along twin axes through the Southwest and Central Pacific.

Major operations

Key applications of the strategy included the Solomon Islands campaign, the Gilbert and Marshall Islands operations (Tarawa, Kwajalein), the Mariana Islands landings (Saipan, Guam, Tinian), the bypassing of the Carolines strongpoints, the Philippines campaign, and the final steps at Iwo Jima and Okinawa, which brought American bombers within range of the Japanese home islands.

Legacy

Island hopping minimized Allied casualties relative to a sequential conquest strategy, though the islands that were assaulted saw extremely heavy fighting. The concept influenced later thinking about bypassing fortified points and concentrating on decisive positions.
