Constantine I (Latin: Flavius Valerius Constantinus; c. 272 – 337), also known as Constantine the Great, was a Roman emperor who reigned from 306 to 337. Proclaimed emperor by his troops at York after the death of his father Constantius Chlorus, he fought his way through the civil wars of the Tetrarchy, defeating Maxentius at the Battle of the Milvian Bridge in 312 and Licinius in 324 to become sole ruler of the Roman Empire.

Religious policy

Constantine is famed for his role in the triumph of Christianity. According to tradition, before the Milvian Bridge he saw a vision of the Christian symbol with the words "in this sign, conquer." In 313 he issued the Edict of Milan with Licinius, granting religious tolerance throughout the empire and restoring confiscated Christian property. He convened the First Council of Nicaea in 325, which produced the Nicene Creed, and was baptised on his deathbed. He is venerated as a saint in Eastern Christianity.

Constantinople and legacy

In 330 Constantine dedicated a new imperial capital, Constantinople, on the site of Byzantium; it remained the capital of the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire for over a millennium. His administrative, military and monetary reforms — including the introduction of the gold solidus — stabilised the empire. After disbanding the Praetorian Guard, he ordered the partial demolition of their camp, the Castra Praetoria.
