Hallstein Doctrine

The Hallstein Doctrine, named after Walter Hallstein, was a key principle in the foreign policy of the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) from 1955 to 1970. It stipulated that the Federal Republic would not establish or maintain diplomatic relations with any state that recognized the German Democratic Republic (East Germany). The doctrine rested on the Federal Republic's claim to be the sole legitimate representative (Alleinvertretungsanspruch) of the entire German people.

Application

Formulated in September 1955 after Chancellor Konrad Adenauer's visit to Moscow — where diplomatic relations with the Soviet Union were established, an exception justified because the USSR was one of the four occupying powers — the doctrine was applied against Yugoslavia in 1957 and Cuba in 1963, with which Bonn broke relations after they recognized East Germany. The doctrine increasingly strained West Germany's diplomacy in the developing world and complicated its relations with Eastern Europe.

Abandonment

The doctrine was quietly softened in the later 1960s — Bonn established relations with Romania in 1967 despite its recognition of the GDR — and was effectively abandoned with Willy Brandt's Ostpolitik after 1969, which led to treaties with the Soviet Union and Poland and, in 1972, the Basic Treaty normalizing relations between the two German states.
