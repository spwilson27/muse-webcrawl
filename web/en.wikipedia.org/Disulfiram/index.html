Disulfiram

Disulfiram

Trade name: Antabuse

Use: Treatment of chronic alcoholism

Mechanism: Inhibits aldehyde dehydrogenase

Discovered: 1881; alcohol effect noted in the 1940s

Disulfiram is a medication used to support the treatment of chronic alcoholism by producing an acute sensitivity to ethanol (drinking alcohol). It is sold under the trade name Antabuse, among others. Disulfiram works by inhibiting the enzyme aldehyde dehydrogenase, causing the accumulation of acetaldehyde when alcohol is consumed and producing highly unpleasant symptoms that deter drinking.

Mechanism and effects

Normally, ethanol is metabolized to acetaldehyde and then rapidly to harmless acetate. By blocking the second step, disulfiram causes acetaldehyde concentrations five to ten times higher than usual after drinking. The resulting disulfiram–alcohol reaction includes flushing, throbbing headache, nausea, vomiting, sweating, chest pain, and palpitations, beginning within minutes and lasting from 30 minutes to several hours. Because the enzyme inhibition is effectively irreversible, sensitivity to alcohol can persist for up to two weeks after the last dose.

Medical use

Disulfiram is an adjunct in managing selected patients with chronic alcohol dependence who want to remain abstinent. Its effectiveness depends heavily on adherence and patient motivation; supervised administration improves outcomes. It does not reduce alcohol cravings. Patients must avoid all alcohol, including in sauces, medications, and toiletries, and treatment requires informed consent because of the severity of the potential reaction.

Side effects and risks

Without alcohol, disulfiram is relatively well tolerated, though it can cause drowsiness, headache, and a metallic taste. Rare but serious adverse effects include hepatitis and liver failure, peripheral neuropathy, and psychosis. The disulfiram–alcohol reaction can be dangerous in patients with cardiovascular disease.

History

The compound was first described in 1881 and used in the rubber industry. In the 1940s, Danish researchers Erik Jacobsen and Jens Hald, investigating it as an antiparasitic, discovered its interaction with alcohol — famously after experiencing the effect themselves — leading to its introduction as a treatment for alcoholism.
