Endoscopic third ventriculostomy

Endoscopic third ventriculostomy
SpecialtyNeurosurgery
IndicationObstructive (non-communicating) hydrocephalus
AlternativeVentriculoperitoneal shunt
Key anatomyThird ventricle, foramen of Monro, tuber cinereum

Endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV) is a neurosurgical procedure used to treat obstructive hydrocephalus by creating an opening in the floor of the third ventricle of the brain, allowing cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) to flow directly into the basal cisterns and bypass an obstruction in the normal CSF pathway, most often at the cerebral aqueduct. It offers an internal drainage route that avoids implanting a shunt, with its attendant risks of infection and mechanical failure.

Procedure

Through a small burr hole in the skull, typically on the right side, the surgeon introduces a rigid or flexible endoscope into the lateral ventricle and advances it through the foramen of Monro into the third ventricle. Under direct vision, the thin floor of the ventricle (the tuber cinereum, between the infundibular recess and the mammillary bodies) is perforated and the opening enlarged, often with a balloon catheter. Pulsatile CSF flow through the new stoma confirms success. In infants and selected cases, ETV is combined with cauterization of the choroid plexus (ETV/CPC), a technique championed by Benjamin Warf for treating hydrocephalus in sub-Saharan Africa.

Indications and outcomes

ETV works best for obstructive hydrocephalus caused by aqueductal stenosis, tectal or pineal tumours, and posterior fossa lesions; outcomes in communicating hydrocephalus are poorer. Success depends strongly on age and cause: the ETV Success Score predicts outcomes from these variables, with older children and adults with aqueductal stenosis achieving success rates around 80 per cent, while young infants fare worse. Failures may occur early (technical problems, bleeding) or late, from closure of the stoma or new arachnoid scarring.

Complications

The procedure risks injury to the basilar artery, which lies just beneath the ventricle floor, as well as hypothalamic damage, memory disturbance from forniceal injury, CSF leak, meningitis, and intraventricular haemorrhage; serious complications occur in a few per cent of cases. Unlike shunts, a successful ETV usually requires no hardware maintenance, but patients still need follow-up because delayed failure, though uncommon, can be abrupt and dangerous.
