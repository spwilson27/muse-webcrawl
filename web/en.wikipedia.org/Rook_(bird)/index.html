Rook (bird)

Rook
Scientific name: Corvus frugilegus
Family: Corvidae
Range: Europe and Asia; introduced to New Zealand
Conservation status: Least Concern

The rook (Corvus frugilegus) is a member of the crow family (Corvidae) in the genus Corvus, native to Eurasia. It is a large, gregarious, black bird distinguished from the similar carrion crow by its pale grey-white face, bare of feathers in adults, and by its peaked crown and shaggy "trousered" thighs. Two subspecies are recognised: the western C. f. frugilegus and the eastern C. f. pastinator.

Behaviour

Rooks are highly social, feeding in flocks on farmland and nesting colonially in rookeries — noisy clusters of large twig nests built high in trees, often near human settlements. Their diet is omnivorous, dominated by earthworms, insect larvae (such as leatherjackets), and grain; they probe soft ground with their strong pointed bills, which differ from the heavier bills of other crows. Rooks are intelligent problem-solvers: captive birds have been shown to drop stones into water to raise its level, as in Aesop's fable of the crow and the pitcher, and to use and modify simple tools.

Distribution and status

The rook breeds across Europe and Asia from Ireland to the Pacific; northern populations migrate south and west in winter. It was introduced to New Zealand in the 19th century, where it later became a declared pest and was largely eradicated. In Britain its numbers declined with agricultural change but remain large, and the species is classed as Least Concern overall. In folk tradition the rook's name survives in expressions such as a "parliament of rooks" and in the rookery as a symbol of communal life.
