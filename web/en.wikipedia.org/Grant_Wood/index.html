Grant DeVolson Wood (February 13, 1891 – February 12, 1942) was an American painter and representative of the Regionalist movement, best known for his paintings depicting the rural American Midwest. His 1930 painting American Gothic — showing a farmer and a woman (modelled by Wood's sister Nan and his dentist, Dr Byron McKeeby) standing before a house with a Gothic window in Eldon, Iowa — became one of the most famous and widely parodied images in American art.

Life

Wood was born near Anamosa, Iowa, and moved with his family to Cedar Rapids after his father's death in 1901. He studied at the Minneapolis School of Design, the University of Iowa and the School of the Art Institute of Chicago, and made several trips to Europe in the 1920s, where he was influenced by the precise realism of early Netherlandish and German painters such as Jan van Eyck and Hans Memling. From 1935 he taught painting at the University of Iowa, where he directed Works Progress Administration murals and mentored younger artists. He died of pancreatic cancer in 1942, the day before his 51st birthday.

Work and Regionalism

Along with John Steuart Curry and Thomas Hart Benton, Wood was a leading figure of Regionalism, which celebrated rural American life during the Great Depression. His style combined meticulous detail, stylised rounded forms and satirical undercurrents: works such as Daughters of Revolution (1932), Parson Weems' Fable (1939) and Dinner for Threshers (1934) alternately celebrate and gently mock Midwestern pieties. His Stone City art colony, founded in 1932, promoted the idea that artists should draw on their own regions.

Legacy

American Gothic is held by the Art Institute of Chicago and remains an enduring icon of American culture. Wood's reputation has been reassessed by later scholars, who have examined the irony in his work and debated aspects of his private life; the Grant Wood Art Colony continues at the University of Iowa.
