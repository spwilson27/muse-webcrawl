Occipital lobe

The occipital lobe is one of the four major lobes of the cerebral cortex in the brain of mammals. It is the visual processing centre of the mammalian brain, containing most of the anatomical region of the visual cortex. The occipital lobe is the smallest of the four paired lobes in the human brain and is located at the back of the skull.

Structure

The occipital lobe lies beneath the occipital bone and behind the parietal and temporal lobes, from which it is separated on the medial surface by the parieto-occipital sulcus. Its most prominent landmark is the calcarine sulcus, along whose banks lies the primary visual cortex (V1), also known as the striate cortex because of the visible stripe of white matter, the stria of Gennari.

Function

Retinal ganglion cells relay visual information through the optic nerve, optic tract and lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus to V1. From there, processing divides broadly into a ventral stream concerned with object recognition and a dorsal stream concerned with spatial location and motion. The lobe is also involved in colour processing, face recognition and visuospatial processing.

Clinical significance

Damage to one occipital lobe can produce homonymous hemianopia, a loss of the opposite half of the visual field in both eyes. Cortical blindness, visual agnosias and syndromes such as Anton syndrome and Balint syndrome may follow bilateral injury. Epileptic seizures arising in the occipital lobe often begin with elementary visual hallucinations such as flashes and coloured spots.
