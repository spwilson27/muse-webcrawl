The Kingdom of Jerusalem, officially the Latin Kingdom of Jerusalem, was a Crusader state established in the Southern Levant by Godfrey of Bouillon in 1099 after the First Crusade captured Jerusalem from the Fatimid Caliphate. It was the most prestigious of the four Crusader states (the others being the County of Edessa, the Principality of Antioch, and the County of Tripoli) and survived, in reduced form, for nearly two centuries.

History

Godfrey's brother Baldwin I, crowned king in 1100, expanded the kingdom along the coast with Genoese and Venetian naval help. Under Baldwin II and the joint rule of Melisende and Fulk of Anjou, the kingdom reached its greatest extent, protected by great castles such as Krak des Chevaliers and defended by the military orders — the Knights Templar and Knights Hospitaller, founded in Jerusalem itself.

The fall of Edessa in 1144 provoked the Second Crusade, which failed to relieve the kingdom. Under the ailing Baldwin IV, "the Leper King," internal faction deepened, and in 1187 Saladin destroyed the kingdom's army at the Battle of Hattin and retook Jerusalem. The Third Crusade under Richard the Lionheart recovered the coast but not the city; the rump kingdom, centred on Acre, endured through the 13th century — briefly regaining Jerusalem by treaty under Frederick II (1229–1244) — until the Mamluks under al-Ashraf Khalil captured Acre in 1291, ending the Crusader presence on the mainland.

Society

The kingdom was ruled by a Frankish Catholic landholding elite over a population mostly of local Christians, Muslims, and Jews. Its law code, the Assizes of Jerusalem, and its court, the Haute Cour, became famous in medieval Europe. Italian maritime communes dominated its trade through privileged quarters in the port cities.
