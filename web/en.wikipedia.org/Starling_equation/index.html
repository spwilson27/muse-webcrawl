Starling equation

The Starling equation describes the net fluid movement between blood and tissue due to fluid balance. It is named after Ernest Starling, the British physiologist who formulated it in 1896. The equation describes the forces that determine the movement of fluid through capillary membranes, and it plays a central role in the physiology of microcirculation and in the formation of edema.

The equation

Jv = Lp S [ (Pc − Pi) − σ (πc − πi) ]

where Jv is the net fluid movement between compartments, Lp is the hydraulic conductivity of the vessel wall, S is the surface area available for exchange, Pc is the capillary hydrostatic pressure, Pi is the interstitial hydrostatic pressure, πc is the capillary oncotic (colloid osmotic) pressure, πi is the interstitial oncotic pressure, and σ is the reflection coefficient for proteins.

Starling forces

The equation balances hydrostatic pressures, which tend to push fluid out of the capillary, against oncotic pressures generated primarily by plasma proteins such as albumin, which tend to draw fluid in. At the arterial end of a capillary, hydrostatic pressure typically dominates and fluid filters out; at the venous end, oncotic pressure predominates and fluid is reabsorbed, with the remainder drained by the lymphatic system.

Revised Starling principle

Work by Michel, Woodcock, Curry and others led to a revised understanding of the equation, emphasizing the role of the endothelial glycocalyx layer and showing that in many tissues there is little or no reabsorption along the venous capillary, with lymph drainage playing a larger role than the classical model predicted.

Clinical significance

Disturbances of Starling forces underlie edema in conditions such as heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, and burns, where altered hydrostatic pressure, reduced plasma oncotic pressure, or increased capillary permeability shifts fluid into the interstitium.
