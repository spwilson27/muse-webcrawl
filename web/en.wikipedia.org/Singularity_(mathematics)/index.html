Singularity (mathematics)

In mathematics, a singularity is a point at which a given mathematical object is not defined or not well-behaved — for example, where a function tends to infinity, fails to be differentiable, or where a curve crosses itself. The term is used in several branches of mathematics with related but distinct shades of meaning, and the study of singularities is a major theme in analysis, geometry and algebraic geometry.

Real analysis

For a real-valued function, a singularity is a point where the function is not defined or behaves exceptionally. The function f(x) = 1/x has a singularity at x = 0, where it is undefined and grows without bound on either side. Some singularities are "removable": the function sin x / x is undefined at 0, but equals 1 in the limit and can be smoothly extended.

Complex analysis

In complex analysis, an isolated singularity of a holomorphic function is classified as removable, a pole, or an essential singularity. The classification is captured by the behaviour of the function's Laurent series: a pole has finitely many negative-power terms, while near an essential singularity the function comes arbitrarily close to every complex value (the Casorati–Weierstrass theorem), and Picard's great theorem strengthens this dramatically. Singularities inside a contour determine integrals through the residue theorem.

Geometry and other uses

In algebraic geometry, a singular point of a curve or variety is one where the tangent space is not well-defined — for instance the cusp of y² = x³ or the crossing point of a nodal curve. Singularity theory, developed by René Thom, Vladimir Arnold and others, classifies such degeneracies and their unfoldings, with applications from catastrophe theory to optics (caustics) and gravitational physics, where spacetime singularities such as those inside black holes mark the breakdown of general relativity's predictions.
