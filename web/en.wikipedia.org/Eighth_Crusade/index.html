Eighth Crusade

The Eighth Crusade was the last major crusade launched from Western Europe to the Holy Land and the second led by King Louis IX of France.

The Eighth Crusade of 1270 was launched by Louis IX of France against the city of Tunis, in present-day Tunisia, then ruled by the Hafsid sultan Muhammad I al-Mustansir. Louis had already led the Seventh Crusade (1248–1254), which ended in disaster in Egypt. Historians debate whether Louis intended Tunis as a stepping stone to Egypt and the Holy Land, or genuinely expected the sultan to convert to Christianity, as rumours reaching France suggested he might.

Course of the crusade

The French fleet landed near the ruins of ancient Carthage in July 1270 and established a camp. Rather than assaulting Tunis immediately, Louis waited for the arrival of his brother Charles I of Anjou, king of Sicily. Meanwhile disease — probably dysentery or typhus — swept through the crusader camp in the summer heat. Louis himself died on 25 August 1270, reputedly with the word "Jerusalem" on his lips; his son-in-law Theobald II of Navarre and many nobles also perished.

When Charles arrived with reinforcements, the two sides negotiated. The resulting Treaty of Tunis (30 October 1270) granted trade concessions, protection for Christians and an indemnity to the crusaders, who then withdrew. Louis's son, Philip III, succeeded him in France. Louis IX was canonised in 1297, the only French king to be made a saint. A final, smaller expedition — sometimes called the Ninth Crusade, led by Prince Edward of England in 1271–72 — was the last crusade of the period to reach the Holy Land in force.
