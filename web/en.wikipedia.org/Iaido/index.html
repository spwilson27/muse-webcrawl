Iaido

Iaidō (居合道) is a Japanese martial art that emphasises being aware of, and capable of quickly responding to, a sudden attack. It consists of the smooth, controlled movements of drawing the sword (katana) from its scabbard (saya), striking or cutting an opponent, removing blood from the blade, and then replacing the sword in the scabbard. The art is practised predominantly through solo forms (kata) against one or more imagined opponents.

Principles and practice

Every iaido kata begins and ends with the sword sheathed. The four characteristic phases are usually summarised as nukitsuke (drawing and initial cut), kiritsuke (the decisive downward cut), chiburi (shaking or flicking blood from the blade) and nōtō (returning the sword to the scabbard). Training emphasises posture, breathing, concentration and etiquette (reishiki), and practitioners typically begin with a wooden sword (bokutō) or a blunt metal training sword (iaitō), progressing to a sharp blade (shinken) only at advanced levels.

Beginners under the All Japan Kendo Federation (Zen Nippon Kendō Renmei) learn the twelve standardised Seitei Iai forms, created in 1968 and later expanded, before studying the older schools.

History and schools

Iaido descends from the sword-drawing techniques (iaijutsu and battōjutsu) of feudal Japan. Hayashizaki Jinsuke Shigenobu (c. 1542–1621), founder of the Shin Musō Hayashizaki-ryū, is traditionally credited as the originator of the art. The two most widely practised classical schools today are Musō Jikiden Eishin-ryū and Musō Shinden-ryū, both deriving from the teachings transmitted through Hasegawa Eishin and the Tosa region. The suffix -dō ("way") reflects the art's modern emphasis on personal development as much as combat technique.

Organisation

Iaido is practised worldwide through national federations affiliated with the International Kendo Federation (FIK). Grades run from kyū ranks up through dan grades (1st to 8th dan in the ZNKR system, with honorary titles such as renshi, kyōshi and hanshi).
