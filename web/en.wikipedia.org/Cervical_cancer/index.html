Cervical cancer is a cancer arising from the cervix, the lower part of the uterus connecting to the vagina. It is caused, in virtually all cases, by persistent infection with high-risk types of the human papillomavirus (HPV), a sexually transmitted virus. Abnormal cells grow slowly over years, usually without symptoms in early stages, which makes screening effective.

Signs and causes

Early cervical cancer typically causes no symptoms. Later signs include abnormal vaginal bleeding, pelvic pain, and pain during intercourse. Harald zur Hausen's demonstration in the 1980s that HPV causes cervical cancer — recognised by the 2008 Nobel Prize — transformed prevention. Other risk factors include smoking, immunosuppression (including HIV), early sexual activity, and long-term oral contraceptive use.

Screening and diagnosis

The Pap smear (Papanicolaou test), introduced in the 1940s, detects precancerous changes (cervical intraepithelial neoplasia) and has dramatically reduced mortality where organised screening exists. Primary HPV DNA testing is increasingly used. Abnormal results are followed by colposcopy and biopsy; staging uses FIGO criteria with imaging.

Prevention and treatment

Prophylactic HPV vaccines (Gardasil, Cervarix) have been available since 2006; the WHO's 2020 strategy targets elimination of cervical cancer as a public-health problem through 90–70–90 goals for vaccination, screening, and treatment by 2030. Treatment depends on stage: surgery (including fertility-sparing trachelectomy) for early disease, combined chemotherapy and radiation for locally advanced disease, and systemic therapy including bevacizumab and immunotherapy for metastatic disease. Five-year survival exceeds 90% for stage I but falls below 20% for stage IV.

Epidemiology

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer in women worldwide, with the great majority of cases and deaths in low- and middle-income countries, especially sub-Saharan Africa, where screening and vaccine coverage remain limited.
