Volcanic crater

A volcanic crater is an approximately circular depression in the ground caused by volcanic activity. It is typically a bowl-shaped feature, commonly at the summit of a volcano, within which one or more vents may be present. During eruptions, molten rock and volcanic gases are ejected from the crater, and lava flows may issue from vents on its floor or flanks.

Formation

Craters form by several processes. Explosive eruptions blast rock and magma fragments outward, excavating a depression and building a raised rim of ejected material. Alternatively, a crater may form by the collapse of the ground surface when the underlying magma chamber is partially emptied during an eruption; large collapse depressions of this kind are called calderas when they exceed about one kilometre in diameter. Erosion and repeated eruptions can widen a crater over time.

Types and related features

  • Summit craters — depressions at the top of a volcanic cone, often containing the main vent.
  • Flank (parasitic) craters — openings on the sides of a volcano where lava erupts away from the summit.
  • Maar craters — broad, low-relief craters formed by phreatomagmatic explosions when magma meets groundwater.
  • Tuff rings and tuff cones — crater rims built of consolidated volcanic ash.
  • Crater lakes — water-filled craters, such as Crater Lake in Oregon and the acidic lake of Kawah Ijen in Indonesia.

Notable examples

The main crater of Mount Vesuvius, reshaped by the eruption of 1944, measures about 450 metres across. Halemaʻumaʻu crater within Kīlauea's summit caldera hosted an active lava lake for much of the decade after 2008. The crater of Mount St. Helens, formed in 1980, contains a growing lava dome. Diamond Head in Hawaii is a tuff cone with a broad crater about 1.2 kilometres in diameter.
