Eau de Cologne

Eau de Cologne (German: Kölnisch Wasser) is a spirit-citrus perfume launched in Cologne in 1709 by Giovanni Maria Farina, an Italian perfumer who had settled in the city. Its name means "water from Cologne," and the original scent, a mixture of citrus oils including lemon, bergamot, and neroli in dilute alcohol, became famous across Europe.

History

Farina described his creation as reminiscent of "an Italian spring morning, of mountain daffodils and orange blossoms after the rain." The fragrance was supplied to royal courts throughout Europe. A rival and later competitor, the 4711 brand produced by Wilhelm Muelhens, became equally famous from its address at Glockengasse 4711 in Cologne. Because the name was never effectively protected, "eau de Cologne" became a generic term.

Modern usage

Today, "cologne" generally denotes a fragrance concentration of roughly 2–5 percent perfume oils in alcohol, lighter than eau de toilette or eau de parfum, and the word is used generically for men's fragrances in some countries regardless of concentration.
