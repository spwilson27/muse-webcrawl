Lilavati

The Līlāvatī is a treatise on arithmetic and geometry written by the Indian mathematician and astronomer Bhāskara II in 1150 CE, forming the first volume of his Siddhānta Shiromani. Written in Sanskrit verse and traditionally said to be addressed to Bhāskara's daughter Līlāvatī, it remained a standard mathematics textbook in South Asia for centuries.

Contents

The work treats arithmetic operations, fractions, the rule of three, mixtures, series, plane geometry, solid geometry, the shadow of a gnomon, and combinatorial problems. Its problems are posed as elegant verse riddles involving bees, lotus flowers, pearls, and peacocks. Bhāskara presents systematic rules for working with zero and negative numbers, methods for indeterminate equations related to the kuṭṭaka (pulveriser) method, and an approximation of π as 3927/1250.

Legacy

The Līlāvatī was translated into Persian at Akbar's court in 1587 and later into many European languages; it became one of the principal channels through which Indian arithmetic became known abroad. A popular legend claims Bhāskara wrote it to console his daughter after an astrological prediction about her marriage; historians regard this as folklore, but the name — meaning "the playful/beautiful one" — endures as an icon of India's mathematical heritage.
