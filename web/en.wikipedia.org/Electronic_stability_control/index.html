Electronic stability control (ESC), also called electronic stability program (ESP) or dynamic stability control (DSC), is a computerized technology that improves a vehicle's stability by detecting and reducing loss of traction (skidding). When ESC detects a loss of steering control, it automatically applies the brakes to individual wheels to help steer the vehicle where the driver intends to go, and may also reduce engine power.

Operation

ESC continuously compares the driver's intended direction — from the steering-wheel angle sensor — with the vehicle's actual motion measured by yaw-rate, lateral-acceleration, and wheel-speed sensors. If the car begins to understeer (plow straight) or oversteer (spin), the control unit brakes a single wheel — for example the outer front wheel in oversteer — generating a corrective yaw moment within fractions of a second. ESC builds on the hardware of anti-lock brakes (ABS) and traction control.

History and regulation

Bosch and Mercedes-Benz introduced the first production system in 1995 on the Mercedes-Benz S600 coupé; BMW and Toyota offered rival systems soon after. Following studies showing large safety benefits, the US National Highway Traffic Safety Administration required ESC on all new passenger vehicles under 10,000 lb from model year 2012, and the European Union mandated it for new models from 2011 and all new cars from 2014. Canada, Australia, and other markets adopted similar rules.

Effectiveness

Insurance and government studies consistently rank ESC among the most effective safety technologies since the seat belt: it reduces fatal single-vehicle crashes by roughly a third to a half for cars, and by more for SUVs, which are prone to rollovers. ESC cannot overcome the laws of physics or compensate for grossly excessive speed, and systems can usually be partially disabled for deep snow or track use.
