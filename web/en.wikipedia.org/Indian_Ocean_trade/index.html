Indian Ocean trade

Indian Ocean trade refers to the maritime trading networks that have linked East Africa, the Middle East, South Asia, Southeast Asia, and China for more than two thousand years. Long among the world's richest trading systems, it was governed by the predictable monsoon winds, which allowed sailing vessels — dhows, junks, and other craft — to make seasonal round trips across the ocean.

Early networks

Trade between Mesopotamia and the Indus Valley dates to the third millennium BCE. In the first centuries CE, Greek and Roman commerce with India flourished, described in the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea; Roman coins flowed to South India for spices, especially pepper. Austronesian sailors settled Madagascar, and Malay and Indonesian mariners connected the ocean's eastern reaches.

Medieval era

From the 7th century, Muslim merchants spread Islam along the coasts of East Africa and Southeast Asia, founding port cities such as Kilwa on the Swahili Coast. Gujarati, Tamil, Arab, Persian, and Chinese merchants traded textiles, spices, porcelain, gold, ivory, and enslaved people. The treasure voyages of Zheng He in the early 15th century displayed Ming China's reach, reaching as far as Africa. Major emporia included Aden, Hormuz, Calicut, Malacca, and Mogadishu.

European arrival

Vasco da Gama's arrival at Calicut in 1498 opened the sea route from Europe. The Portuguese Estado da Índia attempted to control the spice trade by force, followed by the Dutch and English East India Companies in the 17th century, which redirected much trade into global circuits. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 reshaped routes again, and the Indian Ocean remains a critical conduit for world energy and container shipping.
