Aqueduct (water supply)

An aqueduct is a watercourse constructed to carry water from a source to a distribution point far away. In modern engineering, aqueducts may consist of a combination of pipelines, canals, tunnels, and navigable canals, whereas in antiquity they were often open channels running on a very slight gradient, supported in places by elevated arcades. The word derives from the Latin aqua ("water") and ducere ("to lead").

Ancient aqueducts

Early aqueducts were built by Assyrian, Egyptian, Indian, and Persian engineers — the qanat systems of Persia carried groundwater through gently sloping tunnels. The Romans perfected the aqueduct: eleven aqueducts supplied the city of Rome by the third century AD, and celebrated remains include the Pont du Gard in Gaul, the aqueduct of Segovia in Spain, and the Valens Aqueduct in Constantinople.

Modern aqueducts

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century aqueducts supply large cities at great distances; examples include the Croton and Catskill-Delaware systems for New York City, the Los Angeles Aqueduct, the California Aqueduct, and the Colorado River Aqueduct. Modern works generally use pressurized pipelines, pumping stations, and lined canals rather than gravity arcades.

Uses

Beyond drinking water, aqueducts supply irrigation, hydroelectric projects, and industrial users, and some serve as navigable waterways — though navigable aqueducts carrying canals over valleys are treated as a distinct class of structure.
