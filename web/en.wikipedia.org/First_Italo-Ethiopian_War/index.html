The First Italo-Ethiopian War (Italian: Guerra d'Abissinia) was fought between Italy and Ethiopia from 1895 to 1896. It originated from the disputed Treaty of Wuchale, which Italy claimed made Ethiopia an Italian protectorate. The war ended in a decisive Ethiopian victory at the Battle of Adwa, which secured Ethiopian independence and made Ethiopia a symbol of African resistance to European colonialism.

Background

Italy, a latecomer to the Scramble for Africa, had established the colony of Eritrea on the Red Sea coast. In 1889 Emperor Menelik II signed the Treaty of Wuchale with Italy; the Amharic and Italian texts differed crucially, the Italian version stating that Ethiopia must conduct its foreign affairs through Italy — effectively a protectorate — which Menelik repudiated. Italy invaded the northern Ethiopian region of Tigray in 1895.

Course of the war

Early fighting included Italian successes at Coatit and Senafe and the Ethiopian siege of the Italian garrison at Mekelle. Menelik mobilised an army of around 100,000 men, many armed with modern rifles purchased from France and Russia. On 1 March 1896, General Oreste Baratieri, under pressure from Prime Minister Francesco Crispi to attack, advanced against the Ethiopian position at Adwa. The Italian columns became separated in difficult terrain and were defeated in detail; Italian and askari losses were catastrophic, with several thousand killed and thousands taken prisoner. It was one of the worst defeats inflicted on a European colonial power in the nineteenth century.

Aftermath

The defeat brought down Crispi's government and provoked riots in Italy. By the Treaty of Addis Ababa (October 1896), Italy recognised Ethiopia's absolute independence, retaining only Eritrea. Ethiopia remained independent until Italy's second invasion under Mussolini in 1935–36. Adwa Day, 2 March, is a public holiday in Ethiopia, and the victory remains a foundational event in Ethiopian and pan-African memory.
