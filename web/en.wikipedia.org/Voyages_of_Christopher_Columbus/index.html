Between 1492 and 1504, the Genoese explorer Christopher Columbus led four Spanish transatlantic voyages of exploration to the Americas. These voyages led to the widespread knowledge of the New World in Europe and inaugurated the Columbian exchange and the Spanish colonisation of the Americas, with devastating consequences for the Indigenous peoples of the continent.

First voyage (1492–1493)

Sailing from Palos de la Frontera on 3 August 1492 with the caravels Niña and Pinta and the flagship Santa María, Columbus — seeking a westward sea route to Asia — made landfall on 12 October 1492 on an island the Taíno called Guanahani, which he named San Salvador. He went on to Cuba and Hispaniola, where the Santa María wrecked on Christmas Day; he left a garrison at La Navidad and returned to Spain in March 1493 to acclaim.

Second voyage (1493–1496)

With 17 ships and about 1,200 men, Columbus returned as viceroy and governor, found La Navidad destroyed, founded La Isabela on Hispaniola, and explored the Lesser Antilles, Puerto Rico, Jamaica and Cuba.

Third voyage (1498–1500)

Columbus reached the South American mainland at the Orinoco delta (Venezuela) in August 1498. Reports of misgovernment and settler grievances led the Crown to send the judge Francisco de Bobadilla, who arrested Columbus and sent him to Spain in chains; he was freed but lost the governorship.

Fourth voyage (1502–1504)

On his last voyage Columbus explored the coasts of Central America — Honduras, Nicaragua, Costa Rica and Panama — still seeking a passage to the Indian Ocean, survived a hurricane he had predicted, and was marooned for a year in Jamaica before rescue. He returned to Spain in November 1504 and died in Valladolid in 1506, still maintaining he had reached the outskirts of Asia.
