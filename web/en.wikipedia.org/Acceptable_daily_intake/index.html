Acceptable daily intake

Acceptable daily intake or ADI is a measure of the amount of a specific substance (originally applied for a food additive, later also for residues of veterinary drugs or pesticides) in food or drinking water that can be ingested daily over a lifetime without an appreciable health risk. ADIs are expressed in milligrams of the substance per kilogram of body weight per day (mg/kg bw/day).

Derivation

The concept was introduced in 1961 by the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA). Regulators identify the no-observed-adverse-effect level (NOAEL) from animal toxicology studies and divide it by an uncertainty factor — conventionally 100, combining a factor of 10 for differences between animals and humans and 10 for variation among humans — to obtain the ADI. Additional factors may be applied when data are limited.

Use

ADIs underpin food safety law: they guide maximum permitted levels of additives in food, maximum residue limits for pesticides, and drinking-water guideline values. Actual exposure estimates are compared against the ADI; average consumer exposure to approved additives is normally well below it. A related value, the acute reference dose (ARfD), covers intake within a single day or meal.

Limitations

The ADI applies to healthy adults and assumed lifetime average exposure; brief excursions above the ADI do not necessarily imply harm because of the large safety margins built in. Critics note that the framework was designed for single substances and handles mixtures and endocrine effects imperfectly, issues that remain active areas of regulatory science.
