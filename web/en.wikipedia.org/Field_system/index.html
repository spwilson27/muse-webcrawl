Field system

The term field system refers to the way agricultural land is divided, organised, and rotated for cultivation in a given society. In agrarian history and landscape archaeology, field systems are a primary source of evidence for past land use: their patterns of strips, furlongs, terraces, and enclosures survive in the landscape long after the societies that made them.

The open-field system

The best-known example is the medieval European open-field system, in which the arable land of a village was divided into two or three great fields, each subdivided into strips farmed by individual households but subject to common grazing after harvest and to communal decisions about cropping. Under a two-field system, half the land lay fallow each year; the later three-field system left only a third fallow while adding a spring-sown crop of oats, barley, or legumes, raising yields and spreading labour through the year.

Other systems

Other field systems include the Celtic fields of prehistoric northwestern Europe (small rectangular plots bounded by banks or walls), the ridge-and-furrow landscapes of medieval England, infield–outfield systems in Scotland and Ireland (with rundale), and terraced systems in mountainous and arid regions. From the early modern period, enclosure consolidated strips into hedged closes, ending the open-field regime in much of England.

Study

Archaeologists reconstruct field systems from earthworks, aerial photography, LiDAR, and documents such as tithe maps. (For the unrelated algebraic notion of function fields, see Field of rational functions.)
