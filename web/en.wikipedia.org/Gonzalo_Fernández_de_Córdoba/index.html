Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba (1453–1515), known as El Gran Capitán ("The Great Captain"), was a Spanish general and statesman who led successful military expeditions in southern Italy during the Italian Wars and served as viceroy of Naples. He is considered one of the founders of modern warfare, reorganising Spanish infantry into the formations that evolved into the famed tercios.

Early career

Born into a noble Andalusian family, Córdoba distinguished himself in the Granada War against the last Muslim emirate in Spain, culminating in its surrender in 1492. He was then sent to Italy to support Naples against French invasion.

Italian campaigns

After an initial defeat at Seminara in 1495 taught him the weakness of Spanish sword-and-buckler infantry against Swiss pikes and French heavy cavalry, Córdoba rebuilt his army around pikemen and arquebusiers fighting in flexible combined-arms formations, using fieldworks and terrain. In the second expedition he won the Battle of Cerignola (1503) — often cited as the first battle won primarily by firearms — and the Battle of Garigliano, securing Naples for Ferdinand II of Aragon.

Later life

As viceroy of Naples (1504–1507), Córdoba administered the kingdom ably, but royal suspicion of his popularity led Ferdinand to recall him to Spain, where he lived in semi-retirement until his death in 1515. His reforms laid the groundwork for Spanish military dominance in Europe for the next century.
