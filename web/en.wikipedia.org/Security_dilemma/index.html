Security dilemma

The security dilemma, also known as the spiral model, is a concept in international relations theory describing a situation in which actions by a state intended to increase its own security — such as increasing military strength or forming alliances — cause reactions by other states, which in turn lead to a decrease rather than an increase in the original state's security.

Origins

The term is usually attributed to John H. Herz, who introduced it in his 1951 book Political Realism and Political Idealism. The underlying idea was later developed by Robert Jervis, who distinguished the security dilemma from the related "prisoner's dilemma" dynamics in his 1978 article "Cooperation Under the Security Dilemma".

Mechanism

Under anarchy, states cannot be certain of one another's intentions. Defensive weapons are often indistinguishable from offensive ones, so a state's defensive buildup may be read as aggressive by its neighbors, prompting countermeasures and an arms race even when no party seeks conflict.

Mitigation

Analysts argue the dilemma is less severe when defensive weapons and strategies are clearly distinguishable from offensive ones, and when defensive advantage is high. Arms control agreements, transparency measures, and security regimes can also dampen spirals.
