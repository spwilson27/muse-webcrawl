Vasodilation

Vasodilation, also known as vasorelaxation, is the widening of blood vessels. It results from relaxation of smooth muscle cells within the vessel walls, in particular in the large veins, large arteries, and smaller arterioles. The process is the opposite of vasoconstriction, the narrowing of blood vessels.

Function

Vasodilation increases blood flow to tissues that need it and lowers systemic vascular resistance and blood pressure. In the skin it dissipates heat during exercise or hot weather, producing flushing. Local metabolic signals — low oxygen, high carbon dioxide, adenosine — and endothelial factors such as nitric oxide and prostacyclin cause arterioles to dilate; inflammation mediators such as histamine also produce vasodilation, contributing to redness and warmth.

Clinical relevance

Drugs called vasodilators — nitrates, calcium channel blockers, ACE inhibitors and hydralazine among them — are used to treat hypertension, heart failure and angina. Excessive vasodilation contributes to the circulatory collapse of septic and anaphylactic shock.
