Shamisen

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The shamisen (三味線), also known as sangen or samisen, is a three-stringed traditional Japanese musical instrument derived from the Chinese sanxian. It entered Japan through the Ryūkyū Islands in the 16th century, descending from the Okinawan jabisen, and became the central instrument of Edo-period popular and theatrical music. It is played with a large plectrum called a bachi.

Construction

The shamisen has a slender, fretless neck of hardwood passing through a square wooden body covered front and back with skin — traditionally cat or dog skin, now often synthetic. Three silk (now often nylon or tetron) strings of graduated thickness run over a bridge (koma); sizes range from the thin-necked hosozao used in nagauta to the thick-necked futozao of gidayū and the virtuosic Tsugaru style. A distinctive feature is sawari, a deliberate buzzing resonance of the lowest string engineered into the neck.

Styles

Genres include nagauta, the lyrical music of kabuki; gidayū-bushi, the dramatic narrative chanting of the bunraku puppet theatre, in which the tayū narrator and shamisen player (katari and hiki) drive the drama; jiuta chamber music for voice, shamisen, and sometimes koto and shakuhachi; folk min'yō; and Tsugaru-jamisen, the flamboyant solo style from Aomori popularised by players such as Takahashi Chikuzan and, in modern fusion contexts, the Yoshida Brothers.
