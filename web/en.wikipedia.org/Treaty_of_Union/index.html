The Treaty of Union is the name given to the agreement, concluded in 1706, that led to the creation of the Kingdom of Great Britain, uniting the Kingdom of England (which included Wales) and the Kingdom of Scotland into a single state. Its 25 articles were negotiated by commissioners of Queen Anne and were given legal effect by the Acts of Union passed by both parliaments, with the union taking effect on 1 May 1707.

Background

England and Scotland had shared a monarch since the Union of the Crowns in 1603 but remained separate states with separate parliaments, laws and churches. Regal union exposed dangerous divergences: Scotland's disastrous Darien scheme (1698–1700) had ruined many investors, Westminster passed the Alien Act 1705 threatening to treat Scots as foreigners and embargo Scottish trade unless union or a Hanoverian succession was accepted, and Scotland's Act of Security 1704 asserted the right to choose a different successor to Queen Anne than England's Protestant Hanoverian line. England feared a Jacobite or French-aligned Scotland on its northern border during the War of the Spanish Succession; Scotland's political nation wanted access to English markets and colonial trade.

Terms

The Articles of Union created one kingdom, "Great Britain", with a single Parliament of Great Britain at Westminster and a common flag. The Hanoverian succession was secured. Scotland retained its distinct legal system (Scots law and the Court of Session), its established Presbyterian Church of Scotland — protected by separate acts — and its burghs and universities. Free trade was established between the two countries and with England's colonies. A payment known as the Equivalent (£398,085 10s) compensated Scotland for taking a share of England's national debt and was used in part to reimburse Darien investors — a provision denounced by opponents, and by Robert Burns a century later, as Scots being "bought and sold for English gold". Scotland sent 45 members to the House of Commons and 16 elected peers to the Lords.

Opposition and legacy

The treaty was unpopular in Scotland: petitions against it poured in from parishes and burghs, anti-union riots occurred in Edinburgh and Glasgow, and Jacobites and many Presbyterians opposed it. The Scottish Parliament ratified the articles on 16 January 1707 by 110 votes to 69, amid persistent allegations of bribery and pressure, including payments of arrears to Scottish office-holders. Despite its contested birth, the Union endured, became the constitutional foundation of the United Kingdom (extended to Ireland in 1801) and remains the basis on which Scottish devolution since 1999 and the 2014 independence referendum debate rest.
