Smart contract

A smart contract is a computer program or a transaction protocol that is intended to automatically execute, control or document events and actions according to the terms of a contract or an agreement. The objectives of smart contracts are the reduction of need for trusted intermediaries, arbitration costs, and fraud losses, along with the reduction of malicious and accidental exceptions. The term was introduced by computer scientist Nick Szabo in the 1990s, who compared the concept to a vending machine that automatically enforces a simple sale.

Blockchain implementations

Smart contracts became widely known through blockchain platforms. Ethereum, launched in 2015, provides a Turing-complete virtual machine (the EVM) on which contracts written in languages such as Solidity are executed by all network nodes, with the results recorded immutably. Bitcoin supports a more limited scripting language used for conditions on payments. Because code on a public blockchain cannot easily be altered, bugs can be costly: the 2016 exploit of The DAO, an investment fund implemented as a smart contract, led to the loss of tens of millions of dollars' worth of ether and a controversial hard fork of Ethereum.

Applications and limitations

Applications include decentralized finance (DeFi) lending and exchanges, token issuance, supply-chain tracking, and automated royalty payments. Smart contracts cannot directly access data outside their blockchain; they rely on "oracles" to feed in external information. Their legal status varies by jurisdiction, and they are not necessarily legally binding contracts in themselves.
