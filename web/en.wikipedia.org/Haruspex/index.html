Haruspex

In the religion of ancient Rome, a haruspex (plural haruspices) was a person trained to practise a form of divination called haruspicy, the inspection of the entrails (exta) — hence also called an extispicy — of sacrificed animals, especially the livers of sacrificed sheep and poultry. The reading of omens from the liver is among the oldest forms of divination, originating in Mesopotamia and transmitted to Rome largely through the Etruscans, whose religious discipline, the disciplina Etrusca, treated haruspicy as its central art.

Practice

At a Roman sacrifice, the haruspex examined the victim's entrails — primarily the liver, but also the gall bladder, heart and lungs — for abnormalities of shape, colour, texture and marking. The liver was divided into regions associated with particular deities and with friendly or hostile significance (pars familiaris and pars hostilis). The bronze Piacenza liver, an Etruscan teaching model divided into some forty compartments inscribed with divine names, gives insight into this system. Haruspices were consulted by magistrates before battles and other state undertakings, and they also interpreted prodigies such as lightning strikes and monstrous births, prescribing expiatory rituals. Unlike the augurs, who formed a priestly college of Roman citizens, the haruspices were traditionally Etruscan specialists summoned by the Senate; Emperor Claudius attempted to formalize their organization, and the practice persisted until it was suppressed along with other pagan rites in the late 4th century CE under Theodosius I.

In literature

Famously, the haruspex Spurinna is said to have warned Julius Caesar to beware the Ides of March. Cicero's treatise De divinatione critically examines haruspicy and quotes Cato the Elder's quip that he wondered how one haruspex could look at another without laughing. Nonetheless, haruspicy retained official standing in Rome for nearly a millennium.
