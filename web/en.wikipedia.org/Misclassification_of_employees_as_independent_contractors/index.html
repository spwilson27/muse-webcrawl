Misclassification of employees as independent contractors

Misclassification of employees as independent contractors is the practice of an employer treating workers as independent contractors when, under applicable law, they are in fact employees. The distinction matters because employees are entitled to legal protections that contractors are not—including minimum wage and overtime under laws such as the U.S. Fair Labor Standards Act, unemployment insurance, workers' compensation, employer payroll tax contributions, and often eligibility for benefits—while independent contractors are treated as self-employed businesses responsible for their own taxes and insurance.

Tests for classification

Different laws use different tests. The U.S. Internal Revenue Service traditionally examines behavioral control, financial control, and the relationship of the parties. The Department of Labor applies an "economic reality" test weighing multiple factors, such as the worker's opportunity for profit or loss, investments, permanence, the degree of control, and whether the work is integral to the employer's business. Some states apply the stricter "ABC test," under which a worker is an employee unless the business proves the worker is free from control, performs work outside the usual course of the business, and has an independently established trade.

Prevalence and policy debates

Misclassification—whether deliberate or inadvertent—has been a major enforcement and legislative issue in construction, trucking, janitorial services, and the app-based "gig economy." California's Assembly Bill 5 (2019) codified the ABC test for most occupations, while the ballot measure Proposition 22 (2020) exempted app-based drivers and classified them as contractors with certain benefits. Supporters of stricter rules argue misclassification deprives workers of protections and governments of tax revenue; critics argue rigid tests destroy flexible work arrangements and independent businesses.

Consequences

Employers found to have misclassified workers may owe back wages, unpaid payroll taxes, penalties, and benefit contributions, and workers can bring claims individually or as class actions. High-profile cases have involved delivery drivers, ride-hail platforms, cable installers, and strippers—the latter an unlikely but recurrent source of appellate decisions on classification.
