A hunger strike is a method of nonviolent resistance in which participants fast — refusing food, and sometimes fluids — to pressure an authority to change a policy or grant demands. The striker's willingness to suffer or die is intended to shame or politically embarrass the target.

History

Fasting as protest has ancient roots, including in pre-Christian Ireland and India. In the modern era, British and American suffragettes hunger-struck in prison from 1909, prompting forcible feeding and the 1913 "Cat and Mouse Act". Irish republican Terence MacSwiney died after 74 days in 1920. Mahatma Gandhi used fasts repeatedly in the Indian independence movement. In 1981, ten Irish republican prisoners, beginning with Bobby Sands — elected an MP during his strike — died in the Maze Prison strike over political status. Hunger strikes have since been used by prisoners at Guantánamo Bay, Turkish and Kurdish prisoners, and dissidents worldwide.

Medical and ethical issues

Prolonged fasting causes muscle wasting, organ failure and, on refeeding, the potentially fatal refeeding syndrome. The World Medical Association's Declaration of Malta holds that competent hunger strikers should not be force-fed, a position that conflicts with some states' prison policies.
