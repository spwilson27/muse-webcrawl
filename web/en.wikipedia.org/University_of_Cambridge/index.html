The University of Cambridge is a public collegiate research university in Cambridge, England, United Kingdom. Founded in 1209, it is the world's third-oldest university in continuous operation and one of the world's most prestigious academic institutions.

History

Cambridge was founded by scholars who left the University of Oxford after a dispute with local townspeople. The rivalry between the two ancient universities — collectively "Oxbridge" — persists in traditions such as The Boat Race. Cambridge received a royal charter from Henry III in 1231 and a papal bull in 1318.

Structure

The university is a federation of 31 constituent colleges, which admit students, provide accommodation, and deliver small-group teaching called supervisions, alongside university-run lectures, laboratories, and examinations. Its six schools span Arts and Humanities, Biological Sciences, Clinical Medicine, Humanities and Social Sciences, Physical Sciences, and Technology.

Scientific legacy

Cambridge has an extraordinary record of scientific achievement. Isaac Newton developed his work on calculus, optics, and gravitation there; Charles Darwin studied at Christ's College; the Cavendish Laboratory produced breakthroughs including the discovery of the electron, the splitting of the atom, and the structure of DNA by Crick and Watson. As of recent counts, more than 120 Nobel laureates have been affiliated with the university, the highest number of any institution.
