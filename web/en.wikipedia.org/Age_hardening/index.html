Age hardening

Age hardening, also called precipitation hardening or particle hardening, is a heat treatment technique used to increase the yield strength of malleable materials, including most structural alloys of aluminium, magnesium, nickel, titanium, and some steels and stainless steels. It relies on the controlled formation of finely dispersed precipitate particles that impede the motion of dislocations through the crystal lattice.

Process

The treatment has three stages. First, solution treatment: the alloy is heated so that the alloying elements dissolve into a supersaturated solid solution. Second, quenching: rapid cooling traps that supersaturated state at room temperature. Third, ageing: the material is held at a moderately elevated temperature (artificial ageing) or at room temperature (natural ageing), allowing fine precipitates to nucleate and grow. Strength rises to a peak and then falls with over-ageing, as precipitates coarsen.

Mechanism

The precipitate particles act as obstacles to dislocation motion. Small, coherent particles are sheared by dislocations, while larger, incoherent ones force dislocations to bow around them (the Orowan mechanism). Maximum strength is obtained at the precipitate size that makes both mechanisms equally difficult.

Discovery and applications

Precipitation hardening was discovered by Alfred Wilm in 1901–1906 while working on aluminium–copper alloys, leading to Duralumin, the alloy that made metal aircraft structures practical. Today age-hardened alloys are essential in aerospace (2xxx and 7xxx aluminium), nickel superalloys for turbine blades, maraging steels, and beryllium copper springs and tools.
