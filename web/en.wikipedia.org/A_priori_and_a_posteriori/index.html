A priori and a posteriori

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A priori ("from the earlier") and a posteriori ("from the later") are Latin phrases used in philosophy to distinguish types of knowledge, justification, and argument. A priori knowledge is independent from experience, while a posteriori knowledge depends on empirical evidence.

Distinction

Examples of a priori knowledge include truths of mathematics and logic, such as "7 + 5 = 12" or "all bachelors are unmarried", which can be justified without observation. A posteriori knowledge includes most truths of the natural sciences and everyday life — for example, that water boils at 100 °C at sea level — which require experience to establish. Albert of Saxony and other late medieval and early modern writers used the terms for reasoning from causes to effects (a priori) and from effects to causes (a posteriori); Immanuel Kant gave the distinction its modern epistemological sense in the Critique of Pure Reason (1781).

Related distinctions

The a priori / a posteriori contrast is related to but distinct from the analytic / synthetic and necessary / contingent distinctions. Kant's claim that some synthetic statements — such as those of geometry and arithmetic — are knowable a priori was central to his philosophy. In the 20th century, thinkers such as Saul Kripke argued for necessary a posteriori truths (e.g. "water is H2O") and contingent a priori truths, complicating the classical alignment of these categories.
