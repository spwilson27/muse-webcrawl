The noble gases (historically also called the inert gases or rare gases) are the chemical elements in group 18 of the periodic table: helium (He), neon (Ne), argon (Ar), krypton (Kr), xenon (Xe), the radioactive radon (Rn), and the synthetic oganesson (Og). They are odorless, colorless, monatomic gases with very low chemical reactivity, owing to their complete valence electron shells, which make them exceptionally stable. Argon is the third-most abundant gas in Earth's atmosphere, at about 0.93%.

History

Helium was first detected in the Sun's spectrum in 1868 by Pierre Janssen and Norman Lockyer. In 1894–1898, William Ramsay and collaborators isolated argon, neon, krypton, and xenon from air, and Ramsay later identified helium in terrestrial sources, earning the 1904 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Radon was identified as a radioactive emanation by Friedrich Ernst Dorn in 1900. For decades the gases were assumed wholly inert, until Neil Bartlett synthesized xenon hexafluoroplatinate in 1962, the first noble gas compound.

Properties

Noble gases have weak interatomic forces, hence very low melting and boiling points; all are gases at room temperature. Their ionization energies are the highest in each period, and their electron affinities are near zero. Reactivity increases down the group: xenon forms numerous fluorides and oxides, krypton forms KrF₂, and argon forms HArF under extreme conditions; a helium compound, Na₂He, exists only at very high pressures.

Applications

Helium is used as a lifting gas, in cryogenics, as a shielding gas in welding, and in breathing mixtures for deep diving. Neon and other noble gases fill discharge lamps and signage. Argon provides inert atmospheres for welding, metallurgy, and preserving historical documents. Xenon is used in ion thrusters, arc lamps, and anesthesia; krypton in high-performance lighting and insulating windows.
