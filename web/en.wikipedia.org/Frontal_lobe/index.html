The frontal lobe is the largest of the four major lobes of the cerebral cortex in mammals, situated at the front of each cerebral hemisphere, in front of the parietal lobe and above and in front of the temporal lobe. It contains most of the brain's dopamine-sensitive neurons and is associated with voluntary movement, expressive language, and the executive functions — planning, decision-making, working memory, impulse control, and social behaviour. In humans the frontal lobe reaches full maturity only in the mid-twenties.

Structure

The frontal lobe is separated from the parietal lobe by the central sulcus and from the temporal lobe by the lateral sulcus. Its major regions include the primary motor cortex on the precentral gyrus, which controls voluntary movements of the opposite side of the body; the premotor and supplementary motor areas; the frontal eye fields; Broca's area (in the dominant hemisphere) for speech production; and the prefrontal cortex, the large anterior portion devoted to higher cognition and personality.

Function

The prefrontal cortex supports executive function: setting goals, weighing consequences, suppressing inappropriate behaviour, and holding information in working memory. The frontal lobes also play central roles in motivation, attention, and the sense of self. Because of their extensive connections with the rest of the brain, frontal functions are best characterised as coordinating and regulating other neural systems.

Clinical significance

Damage to the frontal lobe can produce weakness or paralysis (from motor cortex lesions), expressive aphasia (Broca's area), and striking personality change. The classic case is Phineas Gage, who survived an iron rod passing through his skull in 1848 but was reported thereafter as impulsive and irresponsible. Frontal lobe dysfunction is implicated in schizophrenia, ADHD, and frontotemporal dementia. In the mid-twentieth century, prefrontal lobotomy was widely performed for mental illness before being abandoned as ineffective and harmful.
