Batak

Batak is a collective term used to identify a number of closely related Austronesian ethnic groups predominantly found in North Sumatra, Indonesia, centred around Lake Toba. The term usually designates the Toba, Karo, Pakpak, Simalungun, Angkola and Mandailing peoples. The Batak are among the largest ethnic groups in Indonesia, numbering over eight million, and are noted for their distinctive clan (marga) system, traditional houses, music, weaving (ulos cloth) and bark books (pustaha).

Society and culture

Batak society is organised around patrilineal clans called marga, and social relations are governed by the philosophy of Dalihan Na Tolu, the "three-legged hearth": mutual obligations among wife-givers, clan-mates and wife-takers. Traditional architecture features the rumah bolon longhouse with a dramatically upswept saddle roof. Village law, feasting with gondang music, and epic oral traditions remain important.

Religion and history

Traditional Batak religion centred on the high god Debata Mula Jadi Na Bolon and ancestor veneration, recorded in pustaha written in the Batak script. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Rhenish missionaries, most famously Ludwig Ingwer Nommensen from 1862, converted most Toba and Karo Batak to Christianity; the HKBP (Huria Kristen Batak Protestan) became one of Asia's largest Lutheran churches. Many Mandailing and Angkola Batak adopted Islam. The Batak priest-king Sisingamangaraja XII resisted Dutch colonisation until his death in 1907 and is a national hero of Indonesia.
