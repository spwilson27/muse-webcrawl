Leir of Britain

Leir was a legendary king of the Britons whose story was recounted by Geoffrey of Monmouth in his 12th-century Historia Regum Britanniae. He is best known as the source of William Shakespeare's tragedy King Lear. According to the tradition, Leir reigned in the 8th century BC, founded Leicester (Kaerleir, "Leir's city"), and was buried in an underground temple to the god Janus beneath the River Soar.

The legend

Geoffrey relates that Leir, son of Bladud, ruled for sixty years. Having no sons, he resolved to divide his kingdom among his three daughters—Goneril, Regan, and Cordelia—according to how much each professed to love him. The elder daughters flattered him extravagantly, but Cordelia answered plainly that she loved him as a daughter should, neither more nor less. Enraged, Leir gave her no share and married her to Aganippus, king of the Franks, without a dowry, while the elder daughters and their husbands received the kingdom.

Once in power, Goneril and Regan progressively stripped their father of his retinue and dignity. Leir fled to Gaul, where Cordelia received him with honour; the Franks raised an army, restored him to his throne, and he ruled three more years. In Geoffrey's version, unlike Shakespeare's, Cordelia succeeds her father and rules Britain for five years before being deposed and imprisoned by her nephews, whereupon she takes her own life.

Adaptations

The story passed through Holinshed's Chronicles and the anonymous play King Leir (c. 1590), which ends happily, before Shakespeare transformed it into one of his greatest tragedies around 1605–06, adding the Gloucester subplot and the catastrophic ending. Nahum Tate's happy-ending adaptation held the stage from 1681 until the 19th century.
