Ashkenazi Jews

Ashkenazi Jews (Hebrew: יְהוּדֵי אַשְׁכְּנַז), also known as Ashkenazic Jews or simply Ashkenazim, are a Jewish diaspora population that coalesced in the Holy Roman Empire around the end of the first millennium CE. They traditionally spoke Yiddish, a Germanic language with Hebrew, Aramaic and Slavic components. Their religious rite, liturgy, and many customs differ in particulars from those of Sephardic Jews, who developed in Iberia.

History

The term "Ashkenaz" in medieval Hebrew referred to Germany, especially the Rhineland. Jewish communities flourished in the Rhineland cities of Speyer, Worms and Mainz (the "ShUM" cities) by the 10th–11th centuries, producing scholars such as Rashi of Troyes. The Crusades brought massacres in 1096 that devastated these communities. Over subsequent centuries, expulsions from western Europe and better conditions in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth shifted the centre of Ashkenazi life eastward, where by the 17th century the majority of world Jewry lived.

In the 19th and early 20th centuries, pogroms and poverty drove mass emigration, chiefly to the United States. The Holocaust murdered approximately six million Jews, the great majority of them Ashkenazim of eastern Europe, destroying the Yiddish-speaking heartland. After 1948 many survivors emigrated to Israel and North America.

Culture

Ashkenazi culture includes distinctive foods (such as gefilte fish, kugel, bagels and challah), klezmer music, Yiddish literature and theatre, and religious customs codified in part by the Rema (Moses Isserles) in his glosses to the Shulchan Aruch. Genetic studies show Ashkenazi populations descend substantially from Levantine and southern European ancestors, with a well-documented medieval population bottleneck. Ashkenazim today form a majority of Jews in the United States and roughly half of the Jewish population of Israel.
