In situ hybridization

In situ hybridization (ISH) is a type of hybridization that uses a labeled, complementary DNA, RNA or modified nucleic acid strand (a probe) to localize a specific DNA or RNA sequence in a portion or section of tissue (in situ). It allows researchers to see where a particular gene is transcribed, or where a specific DNA sequence resides on chromosomes, preserving the spatial context that homogenized samples lose.

Technique

Tissue is fixed and permeabilized, then incubated with a probe labeled with a radioactive isotope, a fluorophore, or a hapten such as digoxigenin that can be detected with antibodies and a chromogenic enzyme reaction. After washing away unbound probe, the labeled sites are visualized by autoradiography, fluorescence microscopy, or bright-field microscopy. RNA ISH (sometimes called RNAscope for a commercial signal-amplified version) maps gene expression in embryos and tissues; DNA ISH on chromosome spreads locates genes and structural rearrangements.

Types

  • Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) — uses fluorescent probes; widely used in cytogenetics and cancer diagnostics, e.g., detecting HER2 amplification or BCR-ABL fusion.
  • Chromogenic in situ hybridization (CISH) — uses enzyme-driven color reactions readable with a standard microscope.
  • Whole-mount in situ hybridization (WISH) — applied to whole embryos to chart developmental gene expression.

Applications

ISH is used in developmental biology to map expression patterns, in pathology to detect viral genomes (such as HPV and EBV) in tissue, and in clinical genetics for diagnosing chromosomal abnormalities in prenatal and cancer samples.
