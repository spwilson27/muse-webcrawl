Direct cinema

Direct cinema is a documentary genre that originated in the United States in the late 1950s and early 1960s, made possible by new lightweight, portable synchronous sound film equipment. It is characterised by a desire to capture reality as unobtrusively as possible, with filmmakers acting as detached observers — a style often summarised as "fly on the wall".

History

The movement is associated with the Drew Associates group, including Robert Drew, Richard Leacock, D. A. Pennebaker, and the brothers Albert and David Maysles. Landmark works include Primary (1960), following presidential candidates John F. Kennedy and Hubert Humphrey; Crisis (1963); Happy Mother's Day (1963); and the Maysles brothers' Salesman (1969) and Gimme Shelter (1970).

Relation to cinéma vérité

Direct cinema is often contrasted with the French cinéma vérité, exemplified by Jean Rouch's Chronique d'un été (1961). Where vérité filmmakers acknowledged and sometimes provoked the events before the camera, direct cinema practitioners sought to minimise their intervention and influence.

Techniques

Direct cinema films typically avoid voice-over commentary, staged scenes, on-camera interviews, and non-diegetic music, relying instead on handheld camerawork, synchronous sound, and long observational takes assembled in editing.
