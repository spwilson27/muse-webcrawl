Grimoire

TypeTextbook of magic
ContentsSpells, rituals, lists of spirits, instructions for talismans and divination
PeriodMedieval and early modern (with ancient antecedents)
Notable examplesKey of Solomon, Lesser Key of Solomon, Grand Grimoire

A grimoire (/ɡrɪmˈwɑːr/) is a book of magic, typically including instructions on how to create magical objects like talismans and amulets, how to perform magical spells, charms and divination, and how to summon or invoke supernatural entities such as angels, spirits, deities and demons. The word derives from the Old French gramaire ("grammar"), originally meaning any book written in Latin, and by extension a book too learned — or too forbidden — for lay readers to understand.

History

Magical handbooks have ancient roots in the Greek Magical Papyri of Egypt and in Mesopotamian and Jewish traditions. Medieval grimoires, circulating in Latin manuscript, include the Sworn Book of Honorius, the Picatrix (a work of Arabic astrological magic), and the Solomonic cycle attributed to King Solomon — the Key of Solomon (Clavicula Salomonis) and its descendant the Lesser Key of Solomon, whose first section, the Ars Goetia, catalogues 72 demons with their seals and ranks. Church authorities condemned and sometimes burned such books, but clergy and learned magicians were among their chief copyists and users.

Early modern and popular grimoires

With printing, cheap grimoires spread widely in the 18th and 19th centuries, especially in France — the Grand Grimoire (the "Red Dragon"), the Grimorium Verum, and the folk-magical Petit Albert — and influenced magical practice in the Caribbean and the Americas, including the Pennsylvania Dutch pow-wow tradition via The Long Lost Friend (1820). Nineteenth- and twentieth-century occultists of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn and Aleister Crowley's circle drew heavily on grimoire material, and modern scholarly editions and translations have made the corpus widely accessible.

In popular culture

Grimoires are a staple of fantasy fiction, games and film, often depicted as powerful, dangerous spellbooks — a usage that has made "grimoire" familiar far beyond occult circles.
