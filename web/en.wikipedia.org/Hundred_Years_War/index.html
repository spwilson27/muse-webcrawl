Hundred Years' War

Hundred Years' War
Date: 1337–1453 (116 years)
Location: France, Low Countries, Iberia, Britain
Result: French victory; England loses most continental possessions
Key battles: Crécy (1346), Poitiers (1356), Agincourt (1415), Orléans (1429)

The Hundred Years' War was a series of armed conflicts fought between the kingdoms of England and France during the Late Middle Ages from 1337 to 1453. The war originated from disputed claims to the French throne by the kings of England, who also held territory in France, and from conflicts over Flanders and control of the sea.

Causes

When Charles IV of France died without a male heir in 1328, Edward III of England — grandson of Philip IV through his mother Isabella — claimed the crown, but French nobles chose Philip VI of the Valois line. Tensions over the English-held duchy of Gascony led Philip to confiscate it in 1337, and Edward formally asserted his claim to the French throne in 1340.

Course

The war fell into phases separated by truces. Edwardian successes included the naval victory at Sluys (1340) and land victories at Crécy and Poitiers, where King John II was captured; the Treaty of Brétigny (1360) gave England a large enlarged Aquitaine. Under Charles V, France recovered most lost ground. After a lull, Henry V invaded in 1415, won at Agincourt, and by the Treaty of Troyes (1420) was recognised as heir to the French throne. The final phase turned with Joan of Arc's relief of Orléans in 1429; French armies using professional companies and artillery won at Formigny (1450) and Castillon (1453), leaving England only Calais.

Effects

The war accelerated the growth of national identity in both kingdoms, the decline of chivalric warfare in favour of longbowmen, infantry and gunpowder artillery, and the development of standing armies and state taxation. France emerged more centralised; England descended into the Wars of the Roses.
