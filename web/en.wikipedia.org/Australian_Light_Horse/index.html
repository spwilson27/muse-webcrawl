Australian Light Horse

Light horsemen were distinguished by the emu plume worn on the slouch hat.

The Australian Light Horse was a mounted infantry force of the Australian Army, famous for its service in the First World War. Light horsemen rode to battle but normally dismounted to fight, one soldier in four holding the horses of his section. Regiments were recruited largely from rural districts, where men grew up riding and shooting, and rode Waler horses bred in New South Wales.

Origins

Mounted citizen forces had existed in the Australian colonies since the nineteenth century, and Australian mounted units served in the Second Boer War (1899–1902). With Federation in 1901 the light horse regiments were incorporated into the new Commonwealth Military Forces, organised with the assistance of Lord Kitchener's 1910 report on Australian defence.

First World War

During the First World War, light horse regiments served in the Australian Imperial Force. Diverted to Egypt, they fought dismounted at Gallipoli in 1915, including in the costly charge at the Nek. In the Sinai and Palestine campaign of 1916–1918 they operated as mounted infantry on a grand scale, fighting at Romani, Magdhaba, Rafa and Gaza. On 31 October 1917 the 4th and 12th Light Horse Regiments made the famous mounted charge at Beersheba, capturing the town and its vital wells. The light horse later advanced through Palestine and into Syria, reaching Damascus in October 1918.

Between and after the wars

Between the wars the light horse was reorganised as cavalry and partially mechanised. In the Second World War most regiments converted to armour or commando roles, though mounted patrols were briefly used in Australia. Modern armoured reconnaissance regiments of the Royal Australian Armoured Corps preserve light horse titles, traditions and battle honours, and light horse troops remain a ceremonial fixture of Anzac Day commemorations.
