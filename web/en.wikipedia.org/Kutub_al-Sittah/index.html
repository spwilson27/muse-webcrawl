Kutub al-Sittah (Arabic: الكتب الستة, "The Six Books") are the six canonical collections of hadith — reports of the sayings and actions of the Prophet Muhammad — recognized by Sunni Islam. Compiled in the 9th century CE, they form, after the Quran, the most important source of Islamic law and practice.

The six collections

The six books are: Sahih al-Bukhari (compiled by Muhammad al-Bukhari, ~7,500 hadiths with repetitions), Sahih Muslim (Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj), Sunan Abi Dawud (Abu Dawud), Jami' al-Tirmidhi (al-Tirmidhi), Sunan al-Nasa'i (al-Nasa'i), and Sunan Ibn Majah (Ibn Majah). The two Sahih collections, prized for their strict criteria of authenticity, are together called the Sahihayn.

Hadith criticism

Each hadith consists of a text (matn) and a chain of transmission (isnad). The compilers applied elaborate criteria to grade narrators' reliability and memory, classifying reports as sound (sahih), good (hasan) or weak (da'if). This science of hadith became a major discipline of classical Islamic scholarship, though modern historians continue to debate the historical reliability of the attributions.
