Myocarditis

SpecialtyCardiology
SymptomsChest pain, shortness of breath, fatigue, palpitations; sometimes none
ComplicationsHeart failure, arrhythmia, sudden cardiac death, dilated cardiomyopathy
Usual causesViral infections (coxsackievirus B, adenovirus, parvovirus B19, SARS-CoV-2); also autoimmune disease, toxins, drug reactions
PrognosisMost cases mild and fully resolving; a minority progress to chronic heart failure

Myocarditis, also known as inflammatory cardiomyopathy, is inflammation of the heart muscle (the myocardium). The inflammation weakens and enlarges the heart, reducing its ability to pump blood, and can disturb its electrical conduction, causing abnormal rhythms. Myocarditis most often follows viral infection and is usually mild, but fulminant cases can cause rapid heart failure and sudden death; it is a leading cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes.

Microscopy of myocardial tissue in viral myocarditis, showing inflammatory infiltrates and muscle damage.

Causes

Viruses are the most common identified cause in developed countries, including coxsackievirus B, adenoviruses, parvovirus B19, human herpesvirus 6, influenza, and SARS-CoV-2. The bacterium of Lyme disease, the parasite Trypanosoma cruzi (Chagas disease — a major cause in Latin America), and other infections can also inflame the heart. Non-infectious causes include autoimmune disorders such as lupus and giant cell myocarditis, hypersensitivity reactions to drugs, the cancer immunotherapy class of checkpoint inhibitors, alcohol, and cocaine. Rare cases have been reported following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination, predominantly in adolescent and young adult males, typically mild and short-lived — while COVID-19 infection itself carries a substantially higher risk of myocarditis.

Signs, symptoms, and diagnosis

Presentation ranges from no symptoms, through flu-like illness with chest pain and breathlessness mimicking a heart attack, to cardiogenic shock. Blood tests often show elevated troponin; ECGs may be abnormal; cardiac MRI showing oedema and late gadolinium enhancement is the main non-invasive diagnostic tool, with endomyocardial biopsy as the definitive (though not always sensitive) test.

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment is mainly supportive: rest, avoidance of strenuous exercise for several months, and standard heart-failure therapy and arrhythmia management as needed. Immunosuppression helps in specific types such as giant cell and eosinophilic myocarditis. Most patients recover fully, but some develop chronic dilated cardiomyopathy; severe cases may require mechanical circulatory support or transplantation.
