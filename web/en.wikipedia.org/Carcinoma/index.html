A carcinoma is a type of cancer that develops from epithelial cells — the cells that line the surfaces of organs, glands and the body, including the skin. Specifically, a carcinoma is a malignant neoplasm whose cells tend to infiltrate surrounding tissues and give rise to metastases. Carcinomas are by far the most common form of cancer in adults, accounting for roughly 85–90% of cancers in Western populations; cancers of the breast, lung, prostate, colon and pancreas are almost all carcinomas.

By contrast, cancers of connective tissue are sarcomas, of blood-forming cells are leukaemias, and of lymphocytes are lymphomas.

Classification

Carcinomas are classified by the type of epithelial cell they resemble and their growth pattern. Adenocarcinoma arises from glandular epithelium (breast, prostate, colon, pancreas, oesophagus and most lung cancers). Squamous cell carcinoma arises from squamous epithelium (skin, cervix, head and neck, oesophagus, lung). Basal cell carcinoma is the commonest skin cancer, slow-growing and rarely metastasising. Transitional cell (urothelial) carcinoma arises in the bladder and urinary tract. Small cell carcinoma and large cell carcinoma are poorly differentiated subtypes important in the lung. A carcinoma in situ is a pre-invasive lesion in which malignant cells have not yet breached the basement membrane.

Causes and development

Carcinomas develop through accumulated genetic mutations affecting oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes — such as TP53, KRAS and APC — usually over many years. Established causes include tobacco smoke (lung, bladder, pancreas), ultraviolet radiation (skin), human papillomavirus infection (cervix, oropharynx, anus), chronic inflammation and reflux (Barrett's oesophagus), alcohol, asbestos, and inherited syndromes such as Lynch syndrome and BRCA1/2 mutations.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by biopsy and histopathological examination, supplemented by immunohistochemistry to determine the tissue of origin, especially in metastatic carcinoma of unknown primary. Cancers are staged with the TNM system (tumour size/extent, nodal spread, metastasis), which guides treatment and prognosis. Localised carcinoma is often curable by surgical excision, with radiotherapy and systemic therapies — chemotherapy, hormonal therapy, targeted agents and immunotherapy — used according to type and stage. Survival varies enormously by site and stage, from near 100% five-year survival for localised basal cell carcinoma to poor outcomes in metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma.
