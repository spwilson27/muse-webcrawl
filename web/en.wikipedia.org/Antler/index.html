Antler

Antlers are extensions of an animal's skull found in members of the deer family (Cervidae). Antlers are a single structure composed of bone, cartilage, fibrous tissue, skin, nerves, and blood vessels, and are shed and regrown each year. In most species only the males grow antlers; female reindeer (caribou) also grow them.

Growth and cycle

Antlers grow from attachment points on the skull called pedicles. While growing, they are covered in a soft, blood-rich skin called velvet, which nourishes the rapidly growing bone — antlers are among the fastest-growing tissues in mammals. When growth is complete, the velvet dries and is rubbed off, leaving bare bone used for display and combat during the mating season (the rut). After the season, a weakening of the attachment leads to shedding; a new set begins growing the following spring.

Function

Antlers serve chiefly as weapons in contests between males and as visual signals of health and dominance. Their size and symmetry reflect nutrition and condition, making them honest indicators in mate choice. Unlike horns, which are permanent keratin sheaths over bone cores, antlers are entirely bone and are replaced annually.

Human uses

Shed antlers have been used since prehistory for tools, weapons, and carving; velvet antler is used in some traditional medicines, and antler is still worked into knife handles, buttons, and chandeliers.
