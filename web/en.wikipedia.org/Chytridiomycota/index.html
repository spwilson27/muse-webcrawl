Chytridiomycota

DivisionChytridiomycota

Chytridiomycota are a division of fungi, commonly called chytrids, characterised by producing motile spores (zoospores) that swim by means of a single posterior whiplash flagellum — a feature lost in almost all other fungi and evidence of the group's early divergence in fungal evolution. The name derives from the Greek chytridion, "little pot," describing the sporangium that contains the zoospores.

Chytrids are mostly microscopic and live in water and wet soil, where many are saprotrophs decomposing resistant materials such as chitin, keratin and pollen. Others are parasites of algae, other fungi, plants and animals. Some, such as the anaerobic Neocallimastigomycetes of herbivore guts (sometimes placed in their own phylum), aid digestion by breaking down plant fibre.

Chytridiomycosis

The chytrid Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (Bd) causes the disease chytridiomycosis in amphibians, infecting their keratinised skin and disrupting electrolyte balance. Since its identification in the late 1990s, Bd — and the more recently described B. salamandrivorans (Bsal) — has been implicated in declines and extinctions of frog and salamander species worldwide, making chytrids among the most consequential fungal pathogens of wildlife.

Body plan

Many chytrids are unicellular or consist of a simple thallus: a spherical sporangium anchored by branching rhizoids. Asexual reproduction releases zoospores through an operculum or discharge pore; sexual reproduction is known in some groups and may produce thick-walled resting spores.
