Karl Theodor Wilhelm Weierstrass (German: [ˈvaɪɐʃtʁaːs]; 31 October 1815 – 19 February 1897) was a German mathematician often cited as the "father of modern analysis". Despite leaving university without a degree, he studied mathematics and trained as a school teacher, eventually rising to a professorship at the University of Berlin, where he reshaped the foundations of calculus.

Contributions

Weierstrass gave the first rigorous arithmetical definitions of limit and continuity in terms of ε (epsilon) and δ (delta), replacing intuitive appeals to infinitesimals and motion. He proved the intermediate value and Bolzano–Weierstrass theorems, developed the theory of analytic functions through power series, and stunned contemporaries with the Weierstrass function (1872) — a function continuous everywhere but differentiable nowhere, disproving the assumption that continuity implied smoothness.

He also proved the Stone–Weierstrass approximation theorem and contributed to elliptic functions, bilinear and quadratic forms, and the calculus of variations (the Weierstrass–Erdmann corner conditions). His Berlin lectures trained a generation of analysts, among them Sofia Kovalevskaya, whom he tutored privately when women were barred from the university.

Life

After years of obscurity teaching in provincial gymnasia while publishing little, a celebrated 1854 paper on Abelian functions brought him sudden fame and honorary degrees; he spent the rest of his career in Berlin.
