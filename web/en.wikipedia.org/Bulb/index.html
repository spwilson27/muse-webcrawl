Bulb

Bulb
StructureModified stem with fleshy scale leaves
FunctionFood storage and perennation; vegetative reproduction
ExamplesOnion, tulip, daffodil, lily, garlic

In botany, a bulb is a short, modified underground stem surrounded by fleshy, modified leaves (scales) that store food reserves, enabling the plant to survive an unfavourable season and flower rapidly when conditions improve. Bulbs are a form of geophyte storage organ, functionally similar to corms, tubers and rhizomes, from which they are distinguished by their structure of concentric leaf scales around a basal plate.

Structure

A true bulb consists of a compressed stem base (the basal plate) from which roots grow downward, and layers of fleshy scales that are modified leaves storing starches, proteins and water. The growing point at the centre produces leaves and a flowering stem. Some bulbs, such as the onion and tulip, are tunicate, wrapped in a dry papery outer skin (tunic); others, such as lilies, are imbricate, with loose overlapping scales and no protective covering.

Reproduction and cultivation

Many bulbous plants reproduce vegetatively by forming offsets — small daughter bulbs (bulbils or bulblets) around the basal plate — which can be separated and grown on. Bulbs are widely cultivated as ornamentals (tulips, daffodils, hyacinths, crocuses) and as food crops (onions, garlic, shallots). Gardeners typically plant bulbs in autumn for spring flowering, choosing a depth of roughly two to three times the bulb's height.

Bulb-like structures

Plants such as the crocus and gladiolus grow from corms (solid stem tissue rather than leaf scales), while iris and lily of the valley spread by rhizomes. In everyday language, all such underground storage organs are often loosely called "bulbs".
