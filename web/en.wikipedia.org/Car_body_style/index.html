Car body style

A car body style is the shape and configuration of an automobile's body: the number of doors and seats, the arrangement of the roof and pillars, and the way passenger and cargo spaces are divided. Body styles have multiplied over the history of the automobile, and their terminology differs somewhat between North America, Britain and other markets, but a common vocabulary covers the main types in production today.

Principal styles

Sedan (saloon): a three-box design with separate compartments for engine, passengers and luggage, usually four doors and a fixed roof over two rows of seats. The hatchback merges passenger and cargo space in a two-box design with a large upward-opening rear door. An estate car (station wagon) extends the hatchback profile with a lengthened cargo area and near-vertical tailgate.

Coupé: traditionally a two-door car with a sloping rear roofline and a single row or a small second row of seats. Convertible (cabriolet): a car whose roof can be retracted or removed, in soft-top or retractable hardtop form; a roadster is an open two-seater, and a targa top has a removable roof section over the front seats with a fixed roll bar behind.

Utility and crossover styles

A pickup truck has an enclosed cab and an open cargo bed. Sport utility vehicles (SUVs) combine a tall, wagon-like body with raised ground clearance and often all-wheel drive, while crossovers (CUVs) apply the same silhouette to unibody car platforms. Minivans (MPVs) maximise interior volume in a one-box shape with sliding rear doors. A fastback descends in one continuous slope from roof to tail; a notchback has a distinct short deck. The limousine is a luxury car lengthened with a partition between driver and passengers.

History

Early cars borrowed carriage terminology — phaeton, landau, brougham, tonneau — and coachbuilders supplied bespoke bodies on manufacturers' chassis. Enclosed steel bodies became standard in the 1920s and 1930s, and after the Second World War the three-box sedan dominated. From the 1970s hatchbacks spread from Europe; from the 1990s SUVs and then crossovers grew until they overtook sedans in most major markets in the 2010s, prompting manufacturers to market coupé-like "four-door coupés" and SUV-coupés that deliberately blur the traditional categories.

Classification

Regulators and industry bodies classify cars for taxation, safety and emissions purposes by size class and footprint rather than by styling, so marketing terms such as "crossover" have no single legal definition and the same model may be categorised differently in different jurisdictions.
