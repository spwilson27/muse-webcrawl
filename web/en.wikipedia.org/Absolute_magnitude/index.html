Absolute magnitude

In astronomy, absolute magnitude (symbol M) is a measure of the luminosity of a celestial object on an inverse logarithmic astronomical magnitude scale. An object's absolute magnitude is defined to be equal to the apparent magnitude that the object would have if it were viewed from a distance of exactly 10 parsecs (about 32.6 light-years), without extinction (dimming) of its light due to absorption by interstellar matter and cosmic dust.

Definition and distance modulus

The relation between absolute magnitude M, apparent magnitude m and distance d in parsecs is the distance modulus: m − M = 5 log10(d) − 5. If apparent magnitude and distance are known, absolute magnitude follows directly, which allows astronomers to compare the true luminosities of stars. The Sun, for example, has an absolute visual magnitude of about +4.83, although its apparent magnitude from Earth is −26.74. Highly luminous supergiants can reach absolute magnitudes near −10, while faint red dwarfs exceed +15.

Bolometric and band magnitudes

Absolute magnitudes can be given for a specific wavelength band (such as V, the visual band) or as a bolometric magnitude, which accounts for the object's total radiation across all wavelengths and requires a bolometric correction relative to the measured band. For bodies in the Solar System, a different convention (absolute magnitude H) is used, defined as the apparent magnitude the body would have at 1 AU from both the Sun and the observer, at zero phase angle.

Use

Combined with spectral classification, absolute magnitude is the vertical axis of the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram, the fundamental classification scheme for stars. Standard candles — objects of known absolute magnitude, such as Cepheid variables and Type Ia supernovae — allow distances to galaxies to be estimated from their apparent magnitudes, underpinning the cosmic distance ladder.
