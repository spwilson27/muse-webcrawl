Tarsier

Tarsiers are small primates of the family Tarsiidae, found only on islands of Southeast Asia — the Philippines, Borneo, Sumatra, Sulawesi, and nearby islands. Named for their elongated tarsus (ankle) bones, they are the only entirely carnivorous primates, feeding on insects and small vertebrates. Fewer than 20 species are recognized in three genera: Carlito (Philippine tarsiers), Cephalopachus (western tarsier), and Tarsius (eastern tarsiers).

Anatomy

Tarsiers are tiny — 9–16 cm in body length — but have enormous eyes, each roughly the size of their brain and fixed in their sockets; to compensate, they can rotate their heads nearly 180° in each direction, like owls. Their elongated hind limbs and long, adhesive-padded fingers suit vertical clinging and leaping: they jump many times their body length between trunks, catching prey mid-leap. They communicate and hunt partly by ultrasound, hearing frequencies far above the human range.

Behaviour and ecology

Nocturnal and arboreal, tarsiers sleep by day clinging to vertical stems and hunt by night, using sight and hearing to ambush insects, lizards, snakes, and even small birds. Most live in small family groups or solitarily, scent-marking territories. Infants are born furred and open-eyed and can cling within a day.

Evolution and conservation

Tarsiers are haplorhine primates, more closely related to monkeys and apes than to lemurs, though they retain many primitive features; their lineage is ancient, with fossils from the Eocene of Asia and even North America. Habitat loss and capture for the pet and tourism trades threaten several species; the Philippine tarsier is a prominent conservation emblem in Bohol.
