Pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP)
FieldImmunology
DefinitionConserved molecular structures produced by microbes but not by the host, recognised by the innate immune system
Detected byPattern recognition receptors (PRRs), e.g. Toll-like receptors, NOD-like receptors, RIG-I-like receptors
ExamplesLipopolysaccharide (LPS), flagellin, peptidoglycan, lipoteichoic acid, unmethylated CpG DNA, double-stranded RNA
Concept introducedCharles Janeway, 1989

Pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) are small molecular motifs conserved within classes of microbes that are recognised by the innate immune system. Because PAMPs are essential to the microbe and absent from host cells, they allow the immune system to distinguish "infectious non-self" from self. They are detected by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on immune and other cells, triggering inflammation and helping to activate the adaptive immune response.

Examples

Classic PAMPs include lipopolysaccharide (LPS, or endotoxin) of Gram-negative bacterial outer membranes, recognised by TLR4; flagellin, the protein of bacterial flagella, recognised by TLR5 and the NAIP/NLRC4 inflammasome; peptidoglycan and lipoteichoic acid of Gram-positive bacteria; mannans and β-glucans of fungal cell walls; and nucleic-acid patterns such as unmethylated CpG DNA (TLR9) and viral double-stranded RNA (TLR3, RIG-I and MDA5).

Related concepts

The immunologist Charles Janeway proposed the PAMP/PRR framework in 1989; the subsequent discovery of Toll-like receptors (recognised by the 2011 Nobel Prize to Jules Hoffmann, Bruce Beutler and Ralph Steinman) confirmed it experimentally. Related categories include DAMPs — damage-associated molecular patterns released by injured host cells — and MAMPs (microbe-associated molecular patterns), a term some prefer because the structures also occur in harmless commensal microbes. Excessive PAMP signalling, as in Gram-negative sepsis, can drive a harmful systemic inflammatory response.
