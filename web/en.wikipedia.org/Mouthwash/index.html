Mouthwash

Mouthwash, also called mouth rinse, is a liquid held in the mouth — usually swished or gargled for about thirty seconds and then spat out — to reduce oral bacteria, freshen breath and, in therapeutic formulations, to help control plaque, gingivitis and dental caries. It is an adjunct to, not a substitute for, brushing and flossing.

Types and ingredients

Cosmetic rinses chiefly mask bad breath temporarily. Therapeutic mouthwashes contain active ingredients such as fluoride (against decay), chlorhexidine (a powerful antiseptic used for limited periods after surgery or for gum disease), cetylpyridinium chloride, or essential oils such as thymol, eucalyptol and menthol — the classic formula marketed as Listerine since the 1870s. Many preparations traditionally contained substantial alcohol, both as solvent and antiseptic; alcohol-free versions are now common and are recommended for children and people avoiding alcohol.

Evidence and safety

Clinical studies support modest benefits of antiseptic rinses in reducing plaque and gingivitis beyond brushing alone, and fluoride rinses reduce caries in high-risk patients. Chlorhexidine can stain teeth and alter taste with prolonged use. Because of their alcohol content, some mouthwashes have been misused as a cheap intoxicant, and ingestion by children is a recognised poisoning risk; any association with oral cancer remains unproven.

History

Rinsing with urine, wine, salt water and herbal tinctures is recorded from ancient Chinese, Egyptian, Greek and Roman practice; Ayurvedic oil pulling is a related tradition. The modern antiseptic mouthwash followed Lister's antiseptic surgery, with Listerine — formulated in 1879 and named for Joseph Lister — first sold as a surgical antiseptic and floor cleaner before being marketed for "halitosis" in the 1920s, a campaign that made mouthwash a mass consumer product.
