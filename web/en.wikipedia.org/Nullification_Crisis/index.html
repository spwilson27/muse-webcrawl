Nullification Crisis

The Nullification Crisis was a sectional political crisis in the United States in 1832–33, during the presidency of Andrew Jackson, in which South Carolina, led by John C. Calhoun, declared that the federal tariffs of 1828 and 1832 were unconstitutional and therefore null and void within the sovereign boundaries of the state. The confrontation raised the question of whether a state could nullify federal law and, implicitly, secede.

Background

The Tariff of 1828 — dubbed the "Tariff of Abominations" — protected Northern manufacturers while raising costs in the agricultural South. Calhoun, then Vice President, anonymously authored the South Carolina Exposition and Protest (1828), articulating the doctrine of nullification based on the compact theory of the Union. The dispute produced the famous Webster–Hayne debate of 1830 in the Senate.

Crisis and resolution

In November 1832 a South Carolina convention adopted an Ordinance of Nullification. Jackson responded with a Proclamation to the People of South Carolina denying the doctrine, and Congress passed the Force Bill authorising military enforcement. Henry Clay brokered a compromise tariff in 1833 that gradually reduced duties; South Carolina rescinded the ordinance, though it symbolically nullified the Force Bill. Calhoun resigned the vice presidency and took a Senate seat during the crisis.

Legacy

The crisis was the first serious test of federal authority over a defiant state and foreshadowed the secession crisis of 1860–61, though in 1833 South Carolina stood alone and backed down.
