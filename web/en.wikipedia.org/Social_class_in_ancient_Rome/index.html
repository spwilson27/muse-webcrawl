Social class in ancient Rome

For the social structure of the Roman military, see Roman army.

Social class in ancient Rome was hierarchical, with multiple and overlapping social hierarchies. An individual's relative position in one might be higher or lower than in another, which complicated the social composition of Rome.

The status of freeborn Romans during the Republic was established by ancestry (patrician or plebeian, the two gentes-based orders), census rank based on wealth and political privilege, and citizenship. Under the Empire, these divisions were supplemented by the growth of the equestrian order and the development of a more complex system of patronage.

Patricians and plebeians

The patricians were the original aristocratic families of Rome, claiming descent from the earliest senators appointed under the monarchy. The plebeians comprised everyone else. The long Conflict of the Orders (roughly 494–287 BC) gradually won plebeians admission to political offices, the priesthoods, and legal equality, culminating in the Lex Hortensia of 287 BC, which made plebiscites binding on all citizens.

The senatorial and equestrian orders

By the late Republic, the operative distinctions were less patrician versus plebeian than membership in the senatorial order (ordo senatorius) or the equestrian order (ordo equester). Senators were required to possess property worth at least 1,000,000 sesterces under Augustus; equestrians needed 400,000 sesterces. Equestrians staffed the imperial administration, the officer corps, and financial posts.

Citizens, Latins, and peregrini

Roman citizenship carried legal privileges including the right to vote, to sue in Roman courts, and protection from certain punishments. Below full citizens stood holders of Latin rights and the peregrini, free non-citizens. In AD 212 the emperor Caracalla's Constitutio Antoniniana extended citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the empire.

Freedmen and slaves

Slaves were legally property, though they could hold a peculium and sometimes purchase freedom. Freedmen (liberti) became citizens but bore social stigma and were barred from certain offices; their freeborn sons faced no such bar. Wealthy freedmen, such as the imperial freedmen who ran parts of the emperor's household bureaucracy, could nonetheless wield enormous influence.

Patronage

Binding the orders together was the system of patronage: clients (clientes) attended their patron's morning reception (salutatio), supported him politically, and received protection, food, or money in return. Patronage cut across class lines and structured much of Roman public life.
