The 1966 Tashkent earthquake struck the capital of the Uzbek Soviet Socialist Republic in the early morning of 26 April 1966, at 05:23 local time. Although its magnitude was only moderate — about 5.1 on the surface-wave magnitude scale — the earthquake caused widespread destruction because its focus was very shallow and located almost directly beneath the city centre.

Unlike many destructive earthquakes of high magnitude, the Tashkent event demonstrated how a shallow-focus shock under a densely built urban area of vulnerable mud-brick and adobe housing could produce devastation out of proportion to its seismic energy.

Damage

More than 300,000 people were left homeless. Large parts of the old city, built largely of adobe and unreinforced masonry, collapsed or were damaged beyond repair; Soviet-era estimates put the number of destroyed homes at around 78,000. Official figures listed between eight and fifteen deaths, a remarkably low toll attributed to the slow, rumbling onset of the shaking, which woke residents in time for many to escape before buildings collapsed. Over 200 people were injured.

Response and reconstruction

The disaster prompted one of the largest reconstruction efforts in Soviet history. Work brigades and building materials arrived from every republic of the Soviet Union, and the city was substantially rebuilt within a few years. The rebuilt Tashkent featured wide boulevards, large prefabricated panel housing estates, extensive green space and a layout designed with seismic resistance in mind. The reconstruction is commemorated by the Courage Monument (Monument of Courage), unveiled in 1970 on the fourth anniversary of the earthquake, whose split-earth centrepiece has become one of the city's best-known landmarks.

Aftermath

Strong aftershocks continued for months, keeping many residents in tents and temporary shelters. The earthquake spurred major advances in Soviet seismology and earthquake engineering, including the establishment of new seismic zoning rules for Central Asia. Much of Tashkent's modern appearance — broad avenues, fountains, and the first metro system in Central Asia, opened in 1977 — dates from the post-earthquake reconstruction era.
