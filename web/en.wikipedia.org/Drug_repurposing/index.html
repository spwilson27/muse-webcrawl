Drug repurposing

Drug repurposing (also called drug repositioning or reprofiling) is the investigation of existing, approved drugs for new therapeutic indications beyond their original medical use. Because repurposed compounds have already passed safety testing in humans, their development can be faster and cheaper than that of entirely new drugs.

Rationale

Conventional drug development takes more than a decade and has a high failure rate, frequently at the stage of demonstrating safety. A drug already approved for one condition has known pharmacokinetics, dosing and side-effect profiles, so trials for a new indication can often begin at phase II. Repurposing is especially attractive for rare diseases, neglected diseases and emergency responses to new pathogens.

Examples

Famous cases include sildenafil, developed for angina and repurposed for erectile dysfunction and later pulmonary hypertension; thalidomide, withdrawn as a sedative because of birth defects and later approved for leprosy complications and multiple myeloma; aspirin, an analgesic now widely used for cardiovascular protection; and minoxidil, an antihypertensive reformulated to treat hair loss. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the corticosteroid dexamethasone — a long-approved generic — was shown to reduce mortality in seriously ill patients.

Approaches

Candidates are identified by serendipitous clinical observation, retrospective analysis of health records, and computational methods that match drug and disease signatures in gene-expression or molecular-interaction data.
