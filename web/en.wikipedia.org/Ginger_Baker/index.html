Peter Edward "Ginger" Baker (19 August 1939 – 6 October 2019) was an English drummer, widely regarded as one of the most influential drummers in rock music. He was a founder of the rock supergroup Cream, whose recordings in the late 1960s established a new standard for improvisational power-trio playing. A flamboyant and combative personality, Baker pioneered the use of double bass drums in rock and brought jazz and African rhythms into popular music.

Career

Baker emerged from the London blues and modern jazz scenes of the early 1960s, playing with Alexis Korner's Blues Incorporated and the Graham Bond Organisation, where he first worked with bassist Jack Bruce — the start of a famously volatile partnership. In 1966 he formed Cream with Bruce and guitarist Eric Clapton; the band produced hits such as "Sunshine of Your Love" and "White Room" and sold millions of records before breaking up in 1968. Baker and Clapton then formed the short-lived Blind Faith with Steve Winwood.

In the 1970s Baker led the big band Ginger Baker's Air Force, moved to Nigeria, where he built a studio in Lagos and collaborated extensively with Afrobeat pioneer Fela Kuti, and later explored jazz fusion and world music. He reunited with Clapton and Bruce for Cream's 2005 concerts at the Royal Albert Hall. Baker was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame as a member of Cream in 1993. The 2012 documentary Beware of Mr. Baker chronicled his turbulent life. He died in Canterbury in 2019, aged 80.

Style

Baker's playing drew on British jazz drummers such as Phil Seamen and on African master drummers; he favoured a melodic, tom-tom-heavy approach, rudimental technique and jazz timekeeping rather than a heavy backbeat. Rolling Stone ranked him among the greatest drummers of all time, and he influenced generations of rock and metal drummers.
