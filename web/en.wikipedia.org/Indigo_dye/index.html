Indigo dye

Indigo dye is an organic compound with a distinctive blue colour (chemical formula C16H10N2O2). Indigo is a natural dye extracted from the leaves of some plants of the genus Indigofera, in particular Indigofera tinctoria, as well as from woad (Isatis tinctoria). It is one of the oldest dyes used for textile dyeing and printing: indigo-dyed fabrics have been found in Egypt dating to around 4000 years ago, and Indigofera was cultivated throughout Asia, Egypt, Greece and Rome in antiquity.

Production and use

The dye precursor indican in the plant leaves is hydrolysed to indoxyl and oxidised in air to indigotin. Indigo is a vat dye: insoluble in water, it must be reduced to a soluble leuco form ("indigo white") in an alkaline vat; cloth dipped in the vat turns blue as the dye re-oxidises in air. Colonial plantations in India, the Caribbean and the American South made indigo a major cash crop in the 17th–18th centuries, worked largely by enslaved labour.

Chemical synthesis

Adolf von Baeyer determined indigo's structure in the 19th century, and BASF brought synthetic indigo to market in 1897, collapsing the natural indigo trade within fifteen years. Today roughly 50,000 tonnes of synthetic indigo are produced annually, most of it used to dye the blue thread of denim jeans and other workwear.
