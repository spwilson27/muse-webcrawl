In number theory, given a prime number p, the p-adic numbers form an extension of the rational numbers that is distinct from the real numbers, though with some similar properties. They were first described by Kurt Hensel in 1897, though the earlier work of Ernst Kummer can be interpreted as implicitly using p-adic numbers. The p-adic numbers were motivated primarily by attempts to apply the ideas and techniques of power series analysis to number theory.

Introduction

The p-adic numbers may be understood through the p-adic absolute value, which measures the "size" of a rational number by the power of p dividing it: numbers divisible by high powers of p are considered small. Completing the rational numbers with respect to this absolute value yields the field ℚp, just as completing with respect to the usual absolute value yields the real numbers. By Ostrowski's theorem, these are essentially the only completions of the rationals.

Construction

Several equivalent constructions exist. One approach defines the ring of p-adic integers ℤp as the inverse limit of the rings ℤ/pnℤ of integers modulo powers of p; the field ℚp is then its field of fractions. Every p-adic number can be written as a Laurent series in p with digits in {0, 1, …, p−1}, extending infinitely to the left but only finitely to the right of the radix point.

Properties

The p-adic numbers form a field containing the rationals. The topology induced by the p-adic absolute value is totally disconnected, and the ring of p-adic integers is compact in this topology. Unlike the real numbers, ℚp cannot be made into an ordered field. Concepts such as Hensel's lemma — a p-adic analogue of Newton's method — make the p-adic numbers a powerful setting for solving polynomial equations.

Applications

p-adic numbers are central to modern number theory. They appear in the statement and proof of the Hasse principle (local–global principle), in the theory of elliptic curves, in p-adic analysis and representation theory, and in Andrew Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, which used p-adic methods extensively. They also arise in physics in p-adic string theory and in certain models of hierarchical systems.
