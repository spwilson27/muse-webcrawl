Antiretroviral therapy

[Image placeholder]

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) is the use of HIV medicines to treat HIV infection. People on ART take a combination of HIV medicines (called an HIV treatment regimen) every day; because HIV rapidly develops resistance to single agents, standard therapy combines drugs from at least two classes — historically called highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART). ART does not cure HIV, but it suppresses viral replication, restores and preserves immune function, reduces morbidity and mortality, and prevents sexual transmission when viral load is durably undetectable ("Undetectable = Untransmittable," U=U).

Drug classes

Antiretrovirals are grouped by their mechanism: nucleoside/nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs, the backbone of most regimens, e.g. tenofovir and emtricitabine), non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs, e.g. efavirenz, doravirine), protease inhibitors (boosted with ritonavir or cobicistat), integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs, e.g. dolutegravir, bictegravir — now the preferred anchor class), entry and fusion inhibitors, CCR5 antagonists, capsid inhibitors (lenacapavir), and post-attachment inhibitors. Long-acting injectable regimens combining cabotegravir and rilpivirine are an option for some patients.

History and guidelines

The first antiretroviral, zidovudine (AZT), was approved in 1987; combination therapy from 1996 transformed HIV into a manageable chronic condition in high-income settings, and treatment access expanded globally through PEPFAR, the Global Fund and generic manufacture. The START trial (2015) confirmed benefit from starting treatment immediately after diagnosis regardless of CD4 count, and the World Health Organization now recommends ART for all people living with HIV. Antiretrovirals are also used as pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP).
