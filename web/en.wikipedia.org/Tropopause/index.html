Tropopause

Altitude~9 km at poles; ~17 km at equator
BelowTroposphere
AboveStratosphere
TemperatureAbout −50 °C to −80 °C

The tropopause is the atmospheric boundary layer between the troposphere and the stratosphere. It marks the altitude at which the environmental lapse rate — the decrease of temperature with height characteristic of the troposphere — changes: above the tropopause, temperature first remains nearly constant and then increases with height in the stratosphere, largely because of ultraviolet absorption by ozone.

Definition and height

The World Meteorological Organization defines the tropopause as the lowest level at which the lapse rate falls to 2 °C per kilometre or less, and stays below that average for at least 2 km. Its height varies with latitude and season: roughly 17–18 km over the equator, where strong convection pushes it up, and as low as 8–9 km over the winter poles. Commercial jet aircraft typically cruise at or just above the tropopause in mid-latitudes.

Significance

The tropopause acts as a lid limiting exchange between the troposphere and stratosphere. This exchange nevertheless matters greatly: water vapour entering the stratosphere, volcanic aerosols, and long-lived pollutants cross the boundary, and stratospheric ozone can descend into the troposphere in tropopause folds. Tropical tropopause temperatures control stratospheric humidity, and changes in tropopause height are a monitored indicator of climate change — the tropopause has risen as the troposphere warms and the stratosphere cools.
