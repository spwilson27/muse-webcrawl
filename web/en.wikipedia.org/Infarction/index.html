Infarction


Necrotic tissue following loss of its blood supply.

Infarction is tissue death (necrosis) caused by an inadequate blood supply to the affected area. It most commonly results from obstruction of an artery, typically by a thrombus (blood clot) or embolus, but may also arise from mechanical compression, torsion of a vessel, or severe vasospasm. The dead tissue is called an infarct. Infarction must be distinguished from inflammation, a protective immune response of vascularised tissue to injury, although the two processes often occur together and infarcted tissue typically provokes an inflammatory reaction around its margins.

Causes and types

Arterial occlusion by thrombosis on a ruptured atherosclerotic plaque is the usual mechanism in the heart and brain. Venous obstruction can also cause infarction, particularly in organs with a single venous outflow, such as the ovary or testis in cases of torsion. Pathologists distinguish pale (anaemic) infarcts, found in solid organs such as the heart, kidney and spleen where the tissue is dense, from red (haemorrhagic) infarcts, found in loose tissues such as the lung or in organs with a dual blood supply, where blood seeps into the necrotic area.

By site

  • Heart: myocardial infarction ("heart attack"), death of heart muscle from coronary artery occlusion.
  • Brain: cerebral infarction, the tissue death underlying most ischaemic strokes.
  • Lung: pulmonary infarction, usually secondary to pulmonary embolism.
  • Kidney, spleen and limbs: infarction from emboli, vasculitis or vascular occlusion, potentially causing gangrene in limbs.

Outcome

Infarcted tissue is irreversibly injured; it is removed by macrophages and replaced by scar tissue, except in the brain, where it undergoes liquefactive necrosis. The clinical consequences depend on the organ, the size of the infarct and the availability of collateral circulation.
