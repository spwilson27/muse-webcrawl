Constitution of the Spartans

The constitution of Sparta, traditionally attributed to the legendary lawgiver Lycurgus, was a mixed constitution combining monarchy, oligarchy and democratic elements — a structure much admired by ancient political thinkers. Its institutions governed the city-state of Sparta in the Peloponnese for centuries, shaping its famously disciplined, militarised society.

Institutions

Sparta had two kings (the diarchy), drawn from the Agiad and Eurypontid families, who served as military commanders and held religious and judicial functions. The Gerousia, a council of 28 men over sixty elected for life plus the two kings, prepared business for the assembly and acted as a high court. Five ephors, elected annually from the citizen body, wielded sweeping executive and supervisory power — they could even prosecute kings — and are often regarded as the democratic element. The Apella, the assembly of full citizens (Spartiates), voted on proposals by shouting but could not debate or amend them.

Society

Full citizenship depended on completing the state upbringing (the agoge), contributing to a common mess (syssitia), and holding an allotment of land worked by helots, the state-owned serf population whose labour freed Spartiates for military life. The perioeci — free but non-citizen inhabitants of surrounding towns — handled trade and crafts. The Great Rhetra, an archaic constitutional pronouncement preserved by Plutarch, was held to set out the basic order of the state.

Influence

Xenophon and Plato praised the stability of the Lycurgan system, and the Spartan constitution influenced later ideas of mixed government from Polybius through early-modern political theory, though critics note its rigidity contributed to Sparta's long decline in citizen numbers (oliganthropia).
