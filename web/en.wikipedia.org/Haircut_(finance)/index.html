Haircut (finance)

In finance, a haircut is the difference between the market value of an asset used as collateral and the amount of credit extended against it, expressed as a percentage of the market value. For example, an asset worth $100 subject to a 20% haircut supports a loan of $80. The haircut protects the lender against a decline in the collateral's value and against liquidation costs if the borrower defaults.

Use in repo markets

Haircuts are a central feature of repurchase agreements (repos) and securities lending. Short-term Treasury collateral typically carries a haircut of about 2%, while riskier or less liquid collateral — equities, lower-rated corporate bonds, structured products — carries much larger haircuts. Haircuts are also applied by central banks when lending against collateral, and by clearing houses in setting margin requirements.

Determinants

The size of a haircut reflects the collateral's price volatility, liquidity, maturity, credit quality and the length of the liquidation period. Assets whose prices are stable and markets deep require small haircuts; illiquid or volatile assets require large ones.

Role in the 2008 financial crisis

Before the 2007–2008 financial crisis, haircuts on many structured securities in the shadow banking system were near zero. As doubts grew about mortgage-related collateral, lenders sharply raised haircuts, effectively withdrawing funding and forcing asset sales that drove prices down further — a self-reinforcing spiral sometimes described as a "run on repo". Economists Gary Gorton and Andrew Metrick documented average haircuts on non-agency structured products rising from roughly 25% to about 45% at the peak of the crisis.

Regulation

Post-crisis reforms introduced minimum haircut floors for certain securities financing transactions under the Basel framework and the Financial Stability Board's policy framework, intended to limit leverage and procyclicality in collateral markets.
