Tone (linguistics)

Tone is the use of pitch in language to distinguish lexical or grammatical meaning — that is, to distinguish or to inflect words. All verbal languages use pitch to express emotional and other paralinguistic information and to convey emphasis, contrast and other such features in what is called intonation, but not all languages use tones to distinguish words or their inflections, analogously to consonants and vowels. Languages that have this feature are called tonal languages; the distinctive patterns of such a language are sometimes called tonemes, by analogy with phonemes.

Distribution

Tonal languages are common throughout much of the world, including most languages of sub-Saharan Africa, many languages of East and Southeast Asia (including the Sino-Tibetan, Tai–Kadai, Hmong–Mien and Vietic families), and numerous Indigenous languages of the Americas, as well as some Indo-European languages such as Swedish, Norwegian and Punjabi, which have tonal word accents. Estimates suggest that between 60 and 70 percent of the world's languages are tonal.

Types of tone systems

Tone systems are commonly divided into register systems, which distinguish relatively level pitch heights (high, mid, low), and contour systems, which distinguish pitch movements such as rising, falling and dipping. Mandarin Chinese has four main contour tones plus a neutral tone; Cantonese has six or more tones; Vietnamese has six; Yoruba has three level tones. Some languages, such as many Bantu languages, use tone largely for grammatical distinctions, marking tense, mood or case.

Phonetics and representation

The primary acoustic correlate of tone is fundamental frequency, although duration, amplitude and voice quality may contribute. Tones are described in transcription using diacritics (e.g. á, à, ā, ǎ), tone letters of the International Phonetic Alphabet, or numbers (e.g. 55 for a high level tone) in the Chao tone-number system. Processes affecting tone include tone sandhi, in which a tone changes in the environment of neighbouring tones, and downdrift, the gradual lowering of pitch across an utterance.

Tonogenesis

Historically, tone systems often arise through tonogenesis, when a contrast in consonants or voicing is reanalysed as a contrast in pitch — as happened in the history of Chinese, Vietnamese and Punjabi — leaving tone as the primary bearer of the distinction once the original consonantal contrast is lost.
