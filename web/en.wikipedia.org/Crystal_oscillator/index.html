A crystal oscillator is an electronic oscillator circuit that uses a piezoelectric crystal as a frequency-selective element. The oscillator frequency is often used to keep track of time, as in quartz wristwatches, to provide a stable clock signal for digital integrated circuits, and to stabilize frequencies for radio transmitters and receivers. The most common type of piezoelectric resonator used is a quartz crystal, so oscillator circuits incorporating them became known as crystal oscillators.

Principle of operation

A crystal mechanically resonates at a specific frequency because of the piezoelectric effect: an applied voltage deforms the crystal slightly, and mechanical deformation produces a voltage. When the crystal is properly cut and mounted, it vibrates with very little loss, giving the oscillator a high quality factor (Q) — often tens of thousands — and hence excellent frequency stability. In an equivalent-circuit model, the crystal appears as a series RLC circuit with a very small resistance and precisely determined resonance.

History

Piezoelectricity was discovered by Jacques and Pierre Curie in 1880. The first crystal-controlled oscillator, using a crystal of Rochelle salt, was built in 1917 by Alexander M. Nicholson at Bell Telephone Laboratories. Walter Guyton Cady built the first quartz crystal oscillator in 1921 and patented fundamental crystal-control methods. In 1927, Warren Marrison at Bell Labs developed the first quartz clock, exploiting the stability of crystal resonance for timekeeping.

Crystal cuts and modes

Quartz blanks are cut at specific orientations to the crystal axes depending on the desired temperature behavior and mode of vibration. The AT cut, introduced in the 1930s, is the most common for the MHz range because its frequency changes little near room temperature. Low-frequency tuning-fork crystals, cut in the XY orientation, vibrate at 32,768 Hz (215 Hz), a binary-friendly frequency used in nearly all quartz watches and real-time clocks.

Applications

Crystal oscillators are ubiquitous: microcontrollers and microprocessors require a clock; radios use crystal-controlled local oscillators and, historically, banks of selectable crystals; GPS receivers rely on temperature-compensated (TCXO) and oven-controlled (OCXO) crystal oscillators for precision. Where even greater stability is needed — in telecommunications networks and navigation — crystals may be disciplined by atomic clocks.
