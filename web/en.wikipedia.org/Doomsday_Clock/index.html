Doomsday Clock
Maintained by: Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists
Created: 1947
Original setting: 7 minutes to midnight
Factors: Nuclear risk, climate change, disruptive technology

The Doomsday Clock is a symbol maintained by the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists representing how close humanity is to self-destruction, with "midnight" representing catastrophe. The clock's time is set annually by the Bulletin's Science and Security Board in consultation with experts and Nobel laureates.

History

The clock debuted in 1947 at seven minutes to midnight, designed by artist Martyl Langsdorf for the Bulletin's magazine cover. Its setting has changed more than twenty times: it reached two minutes in 1953 after hydrogen bomb tests, moved back to seventeen minutes in 1991 after the Cold War, and in the 2020s was set at 90 seconds — the closest ever — citing the war in Ukraine, climate change, and emerging technological risks. In its recent settings the clock has been expressed in seconds rather than minutes.

Criticism

Critics describe the clock as an imprecise metaphor rather than a measurement; supporters argue its value lies in provoking public discussion about global threats.
