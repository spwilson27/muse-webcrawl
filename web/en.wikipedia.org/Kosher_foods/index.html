Kosher foods are foods that conform to kashrut, the Jewish dietary laws derived from the Torah and rabbinic tradition. The word "kosher" means "fit" or "proper" in Hebrew. Kashrut governs which animals may be eaten, how they must be slaughtered and prepared, and which foods may be combined.

Permitted and forbidden animals

Land mammals must both chew the cud and have fully cloven hooves — cattle, sheep, goats and deer are permitted; pigs, camels and hares are forbidden. Fish must have fins and scales, excluding shellfish and other seafood. Most birds of prey are prohibited; domesticated poultry such as chicken, turkey and duck are permitted. All insects (with narrow exceptions in some traditions) and reptiles are forbidden.

Slaughter and preparation

Meat must come from animals slaughtered by shechita, ritual slaughter performed by a trained shochet using a swift, single cut. Blood must be drained and removed by salting and soaking (melicha), because consuming blood is prohibited. Meat with certain defects found on inspection is declared treif (unfit).

Meat and dairy separation

Based on the biblical prohibition against cooking a kid in its mother's milk, meat (fleishig) and dairy (milchig) may not be cooked or eaten together. Observant households keep separate dishes, utensils and sometimes sinks for each category, and wait a set time between eating meat and dairy. Foods that are neither are pareve (neutral) and may be eaten with either.

Certification

Commercial kosher certification is provided by agencies whose symbols (hechsherim) appear on packaging. Standards vary among agencies and communities, particularly regarding Passover production (kosher l'Pesach), when leavened grain products (chametz) are excluded.
