Potsherd

Also called: Sherd, shard (ceramic)

Study: Ceramic analysis, typology

Uses: Dating, trade networks, diet, technology

A potsherd (often simply sherd) is a broken fragment of pottery. Because fired clay is nearly indestructible in most burial environments, potsherds are among the commonest finds on archaeological sites from the Neolithic onward, and their study is a cornerstone of archaeological dating and interpretation.

Analysis

Archaeologists classify sherds by fabric, form, surface treatment and decoration. "Diagnostic" pieces — rims, bases, handles and decorated body sherds — can be matched to known vessel types whose dates are established from stratified sequences, allowing the layers in which they occur to be dated. Petrographic and chemical analysis of the clay can identify where a vessel was made, revealing trade and exchange networks.

Significance

Residues absorbed into the fabric can preserve evidence of ancient diets, while coarse counts and densities of sherds help archaeologists map the extent and intensity of past occupation. In classical Athens, sherds (ostraka) were used as ballots in votes of ostracism, and inscribed examples preserve the names and politics of the fifth century BC.

Terminology

Specialists usually prefer "sherd" for pottery fragments, reserving "shard" for glass and other sharp-edged materials, though the words overlap in everyday usage.
