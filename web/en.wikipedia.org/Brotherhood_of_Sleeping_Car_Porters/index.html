Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters

The Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters (BSCP) was, in 1925, the first labor union led by African Americans to receive a charter in the American Federation of Labor (AFL). The BSCP gathered Pullman porters — attendants on the Pullman Company's luxury sleeping cars — to fight the company's exploitative policies: the porters, almost all Black men, worked long hours for low wages and depended on tips, while being subjected to demeaning treatment on the job.

Founding and struggle

The union was founded in Harlem, New York, on 25 August 1925 by A. Philip Randolph, a socialist labour organiser and editor, together with porters Ashley Totten and Milton Webster. The Pullman Company fought the union fiercely for a decade, firing organisers and sponsoring a company union. Randolph persisted, using the porters' national network to distribute the Black newspaper The Messenger and build support across the country. The BSCP helped secure passage of amendments to the Railway Labor Act that strengthened railway unions, and in 1935 it won a representation election supervised by the National Mediation Board.

Contract and significance

In 1937 the BSCP signed the first collective bargaining agreement between a major American corporation and a Black union, winning wage increases, a shorter work month, and overtime pay. The victory made Randolph the pre-eminent Black labour leader in America and laid organisational foundations for the civil rights movement: BSCP networks and Randolph's threatened March on Washington in 1941 pressured President Franklin D. Roosevelt into issuing Executive Order 8802, banning discrimination in defence industries. Randolph later helped organise the 1963 March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom. E. D. Nixon, a BSCP organiser in Montgomery, Alabama, went on to organise the Montgomery bus boycott of 1955–56.

Decline

As railroad passenger travel declined after the Second World War, membership fell. In 1978 the BSCP merged into the Brotherhood of Railway and Airline Clerks (now part of the Transportation Communications International Union). The Pullman porters themselves left a lasting legacy in Black American life: the relatively steady, travelling jobs helped build a Black middle class and spread news, music, and political ideas across the country.
