North Atlantic Oscillation

The North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) is a weather phenomenon over the North Atlantic Ocean of fluctuations in the difference of atmospheric pressure at sea level between the Icelandic Low and the Azores High. Through its influence on the strength and direction of westerly winds and storm tracks, the NAO is one of the most important drivers of year-to-year climate variability in the North Atlantic region, especially in winter.

Phases

When the NAO index is positive, both pressure systems are stronger than average: the enhanced pressure gradient drives stronger westerlies and storms across the Atlantic toward northern Europe, bringing mild, wet winters there, while eastern North America and southern Europe tend to be cooler and drier. In the negative phase the gradient weakens, storms track farther south, northern Europe becomes colder and drier, and the Mediterranean receives more rain.

Discovery and measurement

The oscillation was identified in the 1920s by Sir Gilbert Walker. It is usually quantified by an index based on the normalized sea-level pressure difference between stations in Iceland and in the Azores, Gibraltar, or Lisbon. Hurrell's station-based index is widely used for the winter months.

Effects

NAO phases influence temperatures, precipitation, sea-ice extent, ocean currents, and even the productivity of fisheries and agriculture around the North Atlantic basin. The persistently positive NAO of the late 1980s and early 1990s coincided with strong warming over Eurasia, while the record-low winter index of 2009–10 brought severe cold to northern Europe and the eastern United States.
