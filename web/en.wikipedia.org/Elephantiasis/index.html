Elephantiasis

Elephantiasis is the enlargement and hardening of limbs or body parts due to tissue swelling. It is characterised by edema, hypertrophy, and fibrosis of skin and subcutaneous tissues, due to obstruction of lymphatic vessels. It may affect the genitalia. The term elephantiasis is often used in reference to symptoms caused by parasitic worm infections, but may refer to a variety of diseases in which parts of a person's body swell to massive proportions.

Causes

Some conditions that can cause elephantiasis are: lymphatic filariasis, caused by the worms Wuchereria bancrofti, Brugia malayi, and Brugia timori, all transmitted by mosquitoes; podoconiosis, also known as barefoot elephantiasis, caused by sustained contact with irritant soils; and lymphogranuloma venereum. Others include protozoan diseases such as leishmaniasis, recurrent streptococcal cellulitis, leprosy, and chromoblastomycosis.

Treatment

Treatment of lymphatic filariasis depends on the geographic location of the disease. In sub-Saharan Africa, albendazole is used with ivermectin to treat the disease, whereas in other regions albendazole is used with diethylcarbamazine. Elevation of the affected limb, compression, and care of the skin can help manage symptoms, and surgery is sometimes used in severe cases.

History

Elephantiasis caused by lymphatic filariasis is one of the most common causes of disability in the world. The condition has been described since ancient times and remains a major public-health challenge in tropical regions, where mass drug administration programmes aim to eliminate transmission.
