Sir Henry Morgan (c. 1635 – 25 August 1688) was a Welsh privateer, plantation owner, and, later, Lieutenant Governor of Jamaica. Operating with letters of marque from the English authorities in Jamaica, he became one of the most famous and successful buccaneers of the Caribbean, plundering Spanish settlements and shipping during the later seventeenth century.

Early life

Morgan was born near Cardiff in Wales, the son of a prosperous farmer. How he reached the Caribbean is uncertain — legend holds he was kidnapped and sold into service in Barbados, though this probably derives from the biography of another buccaneer. He likely arrived with the English expedition that seized Jamaica from Spain in 1655 and joined the privateers operating from Port Royal.

Privateering career

Morgan rose rapidly, leading audacious raids deep into Spanish America: Puerto Príncipe in Cuba (1668), Portobelo in Panama (1668), Maracaibo on the Venezuelan coast (1669) — where he escaped a superior Spanish squadron by sending in a fireship — and, most famously, the crossing of the Isthmus of Panama and the sack of Panama City in 1671. The Panama raid occurred after England and Spain had signed the Treaty of Madrid, and Morgan was arrested and sent to London. Unpunished amid renewed tensions with France, he was instead knighted by Charles II and sent back to Jamaica as its lieutenant governor.

Later life

As a wealthy planter and official, Morgan spent his later years trying to suppress the very piracy he had practised, though enemies accused him of leniency toward old comrades. He died at Port Royal in 1688, probably of dropsy aggravated by years of heavy drinking, and received a state funeral; his grave was lost when much of Port Royal sank in the earthquake of 1692. His exploits, romanticised by Alexandre Exquemelin's Buccaneers of America, made him the archetypal pirate captain in popular culture.
