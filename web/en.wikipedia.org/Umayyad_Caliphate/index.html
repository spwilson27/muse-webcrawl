The Umayyad Caliphate (661–750 CE) was the second of the four major caliphates established after Muhammad's death and the first hereditary dynasty in Islamic history. Ruled by the Umayyad family from Damascus, at its height it stretched from Iberia and North Africa to Central Asia and the Indus, making it one of the largest empires in history.

Origins

Mu'awiya ibn Abi Sufyan, governor of Syria, contested the caliphate of Ali after the assassination of Uthman. After Ali's death in 661 and Hasan's abdication, Mu'awiya became sole caliph. His designation of his son Yazid as heir introduced dynastic succession and provoked resistance culminating in the death of Husayn at the Battle of Karbala (680) and the Second Fitna.

Expansion and administration

Under Abd al-Malik and al-Walid I, the empire conquered the Maghreb, Hispania (711), Transoxiana and Sindh. Abd al-Malik made Arabic the administrative language, introduced an Islamic coinage, and built the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem. The Great Mosque of Damascus became a masterpiece of early Islamic architecture. Arab Muslim elites governed a majority non-Muslim, non-Arab population; converts (mawali) resented fiscal and social discrimination.

Decline and fall

Tribal factionalism (Qays–Yaman), overextension, and revolts — including by Kharijites, Shia movements and the Abbasid da'wa in Khurasan — weakened the dynasty. The Abbasids defeated Marwan II at the Battle of the Zab in 750 and massacred most Umayyads; Abd al-Rahman I escaped to found an Umayyad emirate in Córdoba, Spain. Umayyad rule framed later debates in theology, including positions associated with the Mu'tazila and their opponents.
