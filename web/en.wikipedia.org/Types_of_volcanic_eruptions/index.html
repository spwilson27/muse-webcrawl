Types of volcanic eruptions

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Several types of volcanic eruptions are distinguished by volcanologists. The styles are conventionally named after volcanoes where that behaviour was first characterised, and range from gentle lava effusion to the most violent explosions known. A single volcano may pass through several styles during one eruptive episode. Explosivity is quantified with the Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI), a logarithmic scale from 0 to 8 based mainly on erupted volume.

Principal types

  • Hawaiian: fluid basaltic lava flows and lava fountains with little ash; builds broad shield volcanoes (Kīlauea, Mauna Loa). VEI typically 0–1.
  • Strombolian: rhythmic mild explosions ejecting incandescent cinders and bombs, named for Stromboli, Italy.
  • Vulcanian: short, violent bursts of viscous magma producing dense ash clouds and ballistic blocks, named for Vulcano in the Aeolian Islands.
  • Peléan: associated with lava domes whose collapse generates pyroclastic flows (nuées ardentes), named for Mount Pelée, Martinique, whose 1902 eruption destroyed Saint-Pierre.
  • Plinian: sustained, enormous convection columns tens of kilometres high, named for Pliny the Younger's account of the AD 79 eruption of Vesuvius; the largest are called ultra-Plinian (e.g., Tambora 1815, VEI 7).
  • Phreatic and phreatomagmatic: steam-driven explosions when magma or lava meets water; includes Surtseyan eruptions in shallow water and subglacial eruptions such as Eyjafjallajökull 2010.
  • Submarine eruptions: the most common on Earth, occurring along mid-ocean ridges, producing pillow lavas.

Controls

Eruption style is governed chiefly by magma silica content and viscosity, dissolved gas content, and the rate of ascent: runny, gas-poor basalt tends to effuse, while viscous, gas-rich rhyolite tends to fragment explosively.
