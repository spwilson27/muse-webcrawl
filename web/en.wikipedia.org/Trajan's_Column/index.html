Trajan's Column (Italian: Colonna Traiana) is a triumphal column in Rome, Italy, completed in AD 113 as part of Trajan's Forum. It commemorates the emperor Trajan's victory in the Dacian Wars with a continuous spiral relief, about 190 metres long, that winds 23 times around the shaft and depicts some 155 scenes with roughly 2,500 carved figures — an unrivalled visual source for the Roman army, its equipment and logistics.

Description

The column stands on a tall pedestal decorated with captured arms and armour. Its shaft, about 30 metres high, is built of 20 huge drums of Carrara marble, each weighing about 32 tonnes, and contains a spiral staircase of 185 steps lit by narrow slits. The inscription on the pedestal famously declares that the column's height shows how much earth was excavated to build the forum. Originally the relief was painted and studded with metal details, and a bronze statue of Trajan crowned the column; in 1587 Pope Sixtus V replaced it with the present statue of St Peter. Trajan's ashes were placed in the pedestal — an unusual honour within the city.

The relief

Read from bottom to top, the frieze narrates the two Dacian Wars as a kind of stone film: legions building forts and bridges (including Apollodorus's great bridge over the Danube), marching, fighting set battles and sieges, receiving embassies, and finally the deportation of the Dacians and the suicide of their king Decebalus, whose head is presented to Trajan. The emperor appears 58 times, calm and central amid the activity, the visual embodiment of imperial virtue.

Influence

The column's narrative spiral was imitated by the Column of Marcus Aurelius in Rome, by Napoleon's Vendôme Column in Paris, and by many later monuments; its lettering — the elegant capitals of the dedicatory inscription — became the model for the typeface Trajan and countless book and film titles. Plaster casts in the Museo della Civiltà Romana and the Victoria and Albert Museum allow the whole frieze to be studied at eye level.
