San Lorenzo Tenochtitlán

LocationVeracruz, Mexico
CultureOlmec
PeriodEarly to Middle Formative
Peak occupationc. 1200–900 BCE

San Lorenzo Tenochtitlán (or simply San Lorenzo) is a large archaeological site in the state of Veracruz, Mexico, encompassing the related sites of San Lorenzo, Tenochtitlán, and Potrero Nuevo on the banks of the Coatzacoalcos River. San Lorenzo was the earliest major Olmec centre, flourishing from about 1400 to 900 BCE, with its peak between roughly 1200 and 900 BCE. It is regarded as one of the earliest complex societies in Mesoamerica.

The site

The main settlement sits on a large artificial plateau rising about 50 metres above the surrounding floodplain, covering some 700 hectares together with its satellite communities. Its inhabitants modified the plateau with terraces, drains, and ponds, and imported enormous quantities of basalt from the Tuxtla Mountains some 60 kilometres away — an indication of organised labour on a large scale.

Monumental sculpture

San Lorenzo is famous for its colossal heads: ten were found at the site, carved from basalt boulders and weighing between about 6 and 28 tonnes. The heads, believed to represent individual rulers, are masterpieces of Olmec art. The site also yielded thrones (formerly called "altars"), monumental stone monuments, and workshop areas where basalt was recycled, apparently after deliberate mutilation of earlier sculptures around 900 BCE.

Decline

Around 900 BCE San Lorenzo declined sharply and the centre of Olmec culture shifted east to La Venta. Causes proposed include river course changes undermining its trade position, internal unrest, and environmental stress. Excavations by Matthew Stirling in the 1940s and by Michael Coe and the Yale project in the 1960s, and later Mexican projects, have revealed much of the site's layout and chronology.
