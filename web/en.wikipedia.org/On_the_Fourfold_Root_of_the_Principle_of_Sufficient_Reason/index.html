On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason

AuthorArthur Schopenhauer
Original titleUeber die vierfache Wurzel des Satzes vom zureichenden Grunde
Published1813 (revised 1847)
SubjectEpistemology, metaphysics

On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason is a philosophical treatise by Arthur Schopenhauer, first published in 1813 as his doctoral dissertation at the University of Jena and substantially revised in 1847. It examines the principle of sufficient reason — the claim that nothing is without a reason why it is so, rather than otherwise — which had been central to rationalist philosophy since Leibniz.

Argument

Schopenhauer argues that the principle is not one rule but takes four distinct forms, according to four classes of objects of representation: (1) the reason of becoming, physical causality governing changes in material objects; (2) the reason of knowing, the grounds that justify judgments and conclusions; (3) the reason of being, the relations of space and time that ground mathematics; and (4) the reason of acting, motivation, whereby the will is moved by motives. Confusing these forms, he argued, leads to philosophical error, including spurious proofs of the existence of God.

Relation to his system

The essay lays the epistemological foundation for Schopenhauer's main work, The World as Will and Representation (1818), in which the principle of sufficient reason governs the world as representation, while the thing-in-itself — the will — lies outside its reach. Schopenhauer considered the treatise essential reading for understanding his philosophy.
