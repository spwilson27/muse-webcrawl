Future tense

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Timeline diagram contrasting past, present and future tenses]

In grammar, a future tense (abbreviated FUT) is a verb form that generally marks the event described by the verb as not having happened yet, but expected to happen in the relative future. An example of a future tense form is the French verbal form aimera, meaning "will love", generally derived from the Latin infinitive plus a conjugated form of the verb "to have" (habere).

Whether a language has a true future tense — as opposed to expressing futurity with modal verbs, adverbs or aspect — is debated. Many languages, including English, lack an inflected future tense and instead use constructions with auxiliary verbs such as will and shall, or the "going to" future, which also carry modal shades of prediction, intention, promise and resolve.

Formation in various languages

  • Romance languages: synthetic futures formed historically from the infinitive + habere (Spanish cantaré, French je chanterai, Italian canterò), alongside periphrastic "go" futures.
  • Germanic languages: auxiliaries such as English will, German werden + infinitive, Dutch zullen.
  • Slavic languages: future marking interacts strongly with aspect; imperfective verbs take an auxiliary (e.g. Russian budu), while perfective verbs use present-tense endings with future meaning.
  • Greek: the particle tha + verb form marks the future.

Semantics

Futurity is inherently modal: statements about the future cannot be known with the certainty possible about the present or past, so future forms commonly also express prediction, probability, command ("You will report at six"), or scheduled imminence. Linguists such as Joan Bybee have shown that future markers typically develop from verbs of volition, obligation or motion ("will", "shall", "go"). Typologically, some languages, such as Mandarin Chinese, have no grammatical tense at all and rely on context and adverbials.

Related forms

The future perfect ("will have done") locates an event before a future reference point, and the future-in-the-past ("would do") reports futurity from a past viewpoint, as in indirect speech and conditional sentences.
