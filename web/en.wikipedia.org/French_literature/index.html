French literature

French literature generally speaking, is literature written in the French language, in particular by citizens of France; it may also refer to literature written by people living in France who speak traditional languages of France other than French. It has been an influence upon the world for many centuries and remains one of the most influential literatures.

Middle Ages and Renaissance

The earliest French texts include the Strasbourg Oaths (842) and the Chanson de Roland (c. 1100), the most famous of the chansons de geste. Medieval literature flourished with the courtly romances of Chrétien de Troyes, the poetry of François Villon, and the theatre of the mystery plays. The Renaissance brought the humanism of Rabelais, whose Gargantua and Pantagruel combined satire and erudition, and Montaigne, inventor of the essay.

The classical age and Enlightenment

The seventeenth century produced the great dramatists Corneille, Racine, and Molière, the moralist La Rochefoucauld, and the fables of La Fontaine. The eighteenth-century Enlightenment was dominated by Voltaire, Montesquieu, Diderot and the Encyclopédie, and Rousseau, whose works prepared the intellectual ground for the French Revolution.

Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Romanticism (Hugo, Lamartine, Musset) gave way to the realism of Balzac and Flaubert, the naturalism of Zola, and the poetry of Baudelaire, Rimbaud, and Mallarmé. The twentieth century produced Proust's À la recherche du temps perdu, the existentialism of Sartre and Camus, the nouveau roman, and writers from the wider Francophone world. French authors have won the Nobel Prize in Literature more often than those of any other country.
