Gene–culture coevolution

Gene–culture coevolution, also known as dual inheritance theory, is a branch of evolutionary theory that proposes humans have two interacting channels of inheritance: genetic inheritance and cultural inheritance (information transmitted by teaching, imitation and other forms of social learning). Cultural practices create new selection pressures that alter gene frequencies, while genetically influenced learning predispositions shape which cultural variants spread. The field was developed from the 1970s by scholars including Luigi Luca Cavalli-Sforza, Marcus Feldman, Robert Boyd and Peter Richerson.

Lactase persistence

The canonical example is lactase persistence in adults. Populations with a long history of cattle dairying — in Europe, the Middle East and parts of Africa — independently evolved alleles that keep the lactase enzyme switched on after weaning, allowing adults to digest fresh milk. The cultural invention of dairying changed the fitness landscape, strongly selecting for these mutations; in Europe the −13910*T allele rose rapidly within the last several thousand years.

Other examples and models

Other proposed cases include yam cultivation in West Africa increasing standing water for malaria-carrying mosquitoes and thereby selection for the sickle-cell allele; cultural norms of food preparation reducing toxin exposure; and the influence of cooking on gut and dentition evolution. Mathematical models in the field track how learned traits (such as conformist transmission, which stabilises group norms) interact with natural selection, and provide the theoretical core of modern cultural evolution research.
