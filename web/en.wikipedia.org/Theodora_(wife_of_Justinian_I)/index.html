Theodora (Greek: Θεοδώρα; c. 500 – 28 June 548) was a Byzantine empress and wife of the emperor Justinian I. She was one of the most influential and powerful figures of the Byzantine era. Coming from humble origins — the historian Procopius says she worked as an actress and courtesan — she married Justinian before he became emperor, was crowned Augusta alongside him in 527, and exercised power as his effective co-ruler. She is a saint in the Oriental Orthodox Church and in the Eastern Orthodox Church.

Early life

Theodora's father Acacius was a bear trainer attached to the Green faction at the Hippodrome of Constantinople. After his death, according to Procopius's scandalous Secret History, Theodora and her sisters performed on stage; she became a celebrated actress and courtesan before converting to Miaphysite Christianity in Alexandria and returning to Constantinople as a wool-spinner. Justinian, then heir to the throne, fell in love with her; a law barring senators from marrying actresses was repealed to permit the marriage (c. 525).

Reign

During the Nika riots of 532, Theodora famously persuaded Justinian not to flee the capital — declaring, in Procopius's account, that "royal purple is the noblest shroud" — enabling Belisarius and Mundus to crush the revolt in the Hippodrome. Her legislation improved women's rights: laws against trafficking in girls, expanded divorce and property rights for women, and death penalty for rape. She founded a convent for former prostitutes and sheltered Miaphysite clergy in the Hormisdas Palace, protecting the Miaphysite church despite her husband's Chalcedonian orthodoxy.

Death and legacy

Theodora died in 548, probably of cancer. Her mosaic portrait in the Basilica of San Vitale in Ravenna, facing Justinian's across the apse, is among the most famous images of the Byzantine world. Historians treat Procopius's Secret History — a venomous invective against her and Justinian — with caution, but her partnership in power is well attested by laws issued in her name and her reception of foreign embassies.
