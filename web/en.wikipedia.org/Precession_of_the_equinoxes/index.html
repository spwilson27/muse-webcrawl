Precession of the equinoxes

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

PhenomenonSlow rotation of Earth's axis
Full cycle~25,772 years
Rate~50.3 arcseconds per year
Discovered byHipparchus (2nd century BC)

The precession of the equinoxes is the slow, continuous change in the orientation of Earth's rotational axis, which causes the positions of the equinoxes to drift westward along the ecliptic. One full cycle takes approximately 25,772 years, corresponding to a shift of about one degree every 72 years.

Discovery

The phenomenon was discovered by the Greek astronomer Hipparchus around 130 BC, who compared his star observations with records from earlier astronomers and found that the stars' longitudes had shifted. Isaac Newton later explained precession physically as the effect of gravitational torques from the Sun and Moon acting on Earth's equatorial bulge.

Effects

Precession changes which star serves as the pole star over time: Polaris holds that role today, while Thuban was the pole star around 3000 BC and Vega will be in roughly 12,000 years. It also causes the tropical year (season to season) to differ from the sidereal year. Combined with other orbital variations, axial precession is one of the Milankovitch cycles influencing long-term climate.

Astrological ages

Because the March equinox point moves slowly through the constellations of the zodiac, it gives rise to the concept of astrological ages, such as the "Age of Aquarius". Astronomical constellation boundaries mean the equinox currently lies in Pisces and will enter Aquarius around the 26th century.
