Congregationalist polity

Congregationalist polity, or congregational polity, is a system of ecclesiastical polity in which every local church congregation is independent and ecclesiastically sovereign, governed by its own members under the headship of Christ. It is practised by Congregational, Baptist, many Pentecostal and numerous non-denominational churches, as well as by some other religious communities.

Principles

The system rests on two convictions: the autonomy of the local congregation, which recognises no earthly authority above itself, and the priesthood of all believers, by which members collectively discern and decide the church's affairs. Congregations typically own their property, call and dismiss their ministers, and adopt their own confessions and bylaws.

Associations

Although independent, congregational churches commonly form voluntary associations or fellowships for fellowship, missions and mutual advice. Such bodies are advisory only and cannot compel a member congregation. Historically the polity emerged from English Puritan Separatism in the late 16th and early 17th centuries and was codified in documents such as the Savoy Declaration of 1658.

Contrast with other polities

Congregational polity contrasts with episcopal polity, in which bishops govern, and with presbyterian polity, in which assemblies of elders exercise graded authority over local churches. Within congregational churches, authority in practice is exercised through church meetings, elected officers and pastoral leadership, the balance varying by tradition.
