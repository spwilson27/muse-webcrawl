Du Fu (Chinese: 杜甫; Wade–Giles: Tu Fu; 712–770) was a Chinese poet and politician of the Tang dynasty. Along with his elder contemporary Li Bai, he is frequently called the greatest of Chinese poets. His ambition to serve the state as a civil servant was frustrated, and the An Lushan Rebellion (755–763) shattered his career and turned his life into one of displacement and poverty.

Life and poetry

Du Fu repeatedly failed or was blocked in the civil-service examinations, briefly held a minor post at court, and was captured by rebels in Chang'an before escaping to join the exiled emperor Suzong. He later found patronage in Chengdu, Sichuan, where his thatched cottage of 760–765 — now a museum — produced some of his best-known poems. He spent his final years drifting down the Yangtze, dying in 770.

Style and reputation

About 1,500 of his poems survive. Du Fu mastered every form, especially the eight-line regulated verse (lüshi), and used it to record war, famine and corruption with moral seriousness, earning the title "poet-historian" and later canonisation as the "poet-sage". His dense, allusive realism — in poems such as "Spring View" ("The nation is broken, but mountains and rivers remain") — profoundly influenced later Chinese and Japanese poetry.
