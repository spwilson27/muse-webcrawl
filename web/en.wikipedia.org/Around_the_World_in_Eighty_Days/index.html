Around the World in Eighty Days

Around the World in Eighty Days (French: Le Tour du monde en quatre-vingts jours) is an adventure novel by the French writer Jules Verne, first published in French in 1872 and serialised in Le Temps in 1872–73. It tells of the English gentleman Phileas Fogg, who wagers £20,000 that he can circumnavigate the world in eighty days, and of his journeys with his valet, Jean Passepartout.

Plot

Fogg, a punctual and methodical member of the Reform Club in London, accepts the wager and departs the same evening with the newly hired Passepartout. Their route runs through the Suez Canal to Bombay, across India — where they rescue the young widow Aouda from a suttee ceremony — then to Hong Kong, Yokohama, across the United States by rail, and over the Atlantic to Liverpool. Throughout, they are pursued by Detective Fix, who wrongly believes Fogg to be a bank robber. Arriving in London apparently five minutes late, Fogg discovers he has gained a day by crossing the International Date Line eastward, and reaches the club in time to win the wager; he also marries Aouda.

Reception and adaptations

The novel was an immediate success — readers followed the serial and even wagered on Fogg's progress — and it remains Verne's best-known work, frequently adapted for stage, film and television, including the 1956 film that won the Academy Award for Best Picture. Real travellers, most famously Nellie Bly in 1889–90, subsequently raced around the world in imitation of Fogg's itinerary.
