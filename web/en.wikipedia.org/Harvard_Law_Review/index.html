The Harvard Law Review is a student-run law review published at Harvard Law School. Founded in 1887, it is one of the oldest and most prestigious law journals in the United States, and consistently among the most cited legal periodicals in the world. It is published eight times during the academic year by the Harvard Law Review Association, an organization independent of the law school.

History

The Review was founded with the support of Boston attorney Louis D. Brandeis, later a Justice of the U.S. Supreme Court, who helped raise funds and contributed its enduring model of student editorship. Early issues carried articles by Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., including material related to his work on the common law. The journal's first issue appeared on April 15, 1887.

Editorial process and membership

Membership is highly competitive. Harvard law students are selected through a writing competition held at the end of the first year, combined in part with first-year grades. Editors are known for the meticulous cite-checking process in which every footnote of accepted articles is verified. The Association also publishes the Harvard Law Review Forum online, featuring responses and shorter commentary.

Influence

The Review has published many foundational articles, including Warren and Brandeis's "The Right to Privacy" (1890) — although that piece appeared in the journal's early years — and works by legal realists, process theorists, and critical legal scholars. Its annual Supreme Court issue, published each November, reviews the preceding term of the U.S. Supreme Court and includes the widely cited statistics table of the Court's decisions. The Review is also associated with The Bluebook: A Uniform System of Citation, which it compiles jointly with the law reviews of Columbia, the University of Pennsylvania, and Yale.

Notable alumni

Former editors include U.S. presidents Barack Obama (the Review's first Black president, elected in 1990) and numerous Supreme Court justices, judges, and legal academics.
