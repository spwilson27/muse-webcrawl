Model minority

A model minority is a minority demographic group whose members are perceived as achieving a higher degree of socioeconomic success than the population average, thus serving as a reference group or "model" outgroup. This success is typically measured by educational attainment, representation in managerial and professional occupations, household income, and low involvement in crime.

Usage

In the United States, the term is most often applied to Asian Americans. The concept was popularized by sociologist William Petersen's 1966 New York Times Magazine article "Success Story, Japanese-American Style," which praised Japanese Americans for overcoming discrimination through hard work and family cohesion. Similar narratives have been applied, at various times, to Jewish Americans and other immigrant groups.

Criticism

Critics argue that the model minority concept is a harmful stereotype that:

  • Aggregates highly diverse populations, masking poverty and low attainment within specific ethnic groups
  • Creates pressure and mental-health burdens on individuals expected to conform to it
  • Is used politically to discount claims of systemic racism against other minority groups
  • Ignores the role of immigration policy, which has often selected for highly educated migrants

Scholars note that relative Asian American success coexists with discrimination, including the "bamboo ceiling" limiting advancement into executive leadership, and that the stereotype has been linked to the marginalization of Asian American needs in policy.
