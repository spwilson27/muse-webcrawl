Albrecht Dürer (21 May 1471 – 6 April 1528), sometimes spelled Albrecht Durer in English publications without diacritics, was a German painter, printmaker and theorist of the Northern Renaissance. Regarded as the greatest artist of the German Renaissance, he established his reputation across Europe in his twenties through his technically superb woodcuts, and his engravings and paintings transformed printmaking into an art form.

Life

Born in Nuremberg, the son of a Hungarian goldsmith, Dürer was apprenticed to the painter Michael Wolgemut, in whose workshop the Nuremberg Chronicle was illustrated. After journeyman travels along the Rhine he married Agnes Frey in 1494 and travelled to Italy, where Venetian art and classical theory profoundly influenced him. Two Italian journeys (1494–95 and 1505–07) and a trip to the Netherlands (1520–21), recorded in a celebrated diary, mark the stages of his career; Maximilian I and other princes were among his patrons.

Works

His woodcut series — the Apocalypse (1498), the Large Passion and the Life of the Virgin — and the three "master engravings" Knight, Death and the Devil (1513), Saint Jerome in His Study (1514) and Melencolia I (1514) remain canonical works of printmaking. His paintings include the Four Apostles, the Feast of the Rose Garlands and a series of penetrating self-portraits. His watercolours of landscapes and studies of animals and plants, such as the Young Hare (1502) and the Praying Hands, are among the most famous works on paper in Western art.

Theory and legacy

Dürer wrote treatises on geometry (Underweysung der Messung, 1525), fortification and human proportion, bringing Italian Renaissance theory north of the Alps. His "AD" monogram became one of art's first famous signatures — and one of the first subjects of a copyright dispute.
