Economy of the Soviet Union

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Magnitogorsk, built from 1929, symbolised the USSR's forced industrialisation.

The economy of the Soviet Union was based on state ownership of the means of production, central planning, and administrative allocation of resources. It was the world's first centrally planned economy at national scale. The state planning committee, Gosplan, set production targets in five-year plans, and nearly all land, industry and housing were publicly owned. By the 1970s the USSR had become the world's second-largest economy by most estimates, with particular strength in heavy industry, energy and armaments.

History

After the Russian Civil War, Lenin's New Economic Policy (1921) temporarily restored limited markets. From 1928, Stalin's First Five-Year Plan launched rapid industrialisation and the collectivisation of agriculture. The economy sustained the war effort after 1941 and post-war reconstruction, achieving early successes in steel, electricity and the space programme.

Stagnation and decline

From the 1970s, growth slowed markedly. Central planning struggled with innovation, quality, information problems and soft budget constraints: enterprises faced little pressure to economise, shortages of consumer goods were chronic, and an unofficial "second economy" grew. Mikhail Gorbachev's perestroika reforms after 1985 loosened controls without creating a functioning market. The economy collapsed amid the dissolution of the USSR in 1991.

Structure

Industry dominated output, while services and retail were underdeveloped by Western standards. Agriculture, collectivised into kolkhozes and sovkhozes, was a persistent weakness requiring grain imports from the 1960s onward, despite the USSR's vast farmland.
