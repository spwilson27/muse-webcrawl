Knapp Commission

The Knapp Commission, formally the Commission to Investigate Alleged Police Corruption, was a five-member panel formed in April 1970 by New York City Mayor John V. Lindsay to investigate corruption within the New York City Police Department (NYPD).

FormedApril 1970
ChairWhitman Knapp
SubjectCorruption in the NYPD
ReportDecember 1972

Background

The commission was created following revelations by patrolmen Frank Serpico and David Durk, who alleged widespread corruption in the department and were frustrated by the failure of city officials to act. Their accounts were published in the New York Times in 1970, prompting Lindsay to establish the panel.

Findings

The commission's final report, issued in December 1972, concluded that corruption in the NYPD was widespread, distinguishing between "grass-eaters," officers who passively accepted small payoffs, and "meat-eaters," who aggressively solicited large bribes. It recommended structural reforms, including greater accountability for commanders and changes to recruitment and internal investigation procedures.

Legacy

The Knapp Commission hearings were televised and became a landmark in American police reform. The story of Serpico's testimony was later dramatized in the 1973 film Serpico.
