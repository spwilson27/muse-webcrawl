Camera obscura

The camera obscura (Latin for "dark chamber") is the natural optical phenomenon that occurs when an image of a scene at the other side of a small aperture is projected through the screen as a reversed and inverted image on a surface opposite to the opening.

Principle

Light travels in straight lines; rays from a bright scene passing through a pinhole into a darkened space cross at the aperture and form an upside-down, left-right reversed image on the opposite surface. Adding a lens in place of the pinhole increases brightness and sharpness, and a mirror can redirect the image onto a horizontal viewing table.

History

The phenomenon was described in China by Mozi (5th century BC) and analysed by Aristotle and, in the 11th century, by Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen), whose Book of Optics gave the first clear account. Leonardo da Vinci described the device in his notebooks. From the 16th century, portable box and tent forms with lenses were used by artists — their role in Renaissance painting and in the work of Vermeer remains debated — and astronomers used them to observe sunspots and eclipses safely.

Legacy

The camera obscura is the direct ancestor of the photographic camera: once light-sensitive materials became available in the 1820s–30s, fixing the projected image produced photography. Room-sized examples survive as visitor attractions, including the Camera Obscura in Edinburgh (established 1853) and those at Dumfries, Bristol, and Santa Monica.
