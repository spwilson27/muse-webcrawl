Man-in-the-middle attack

In cryptography and computer security, a man-in-the-middle attack (MITM; also called an on-path attack) is an attack in which the attacker secretly relays, and possibly alters, the communication between two parties who believe they are communicating directly with each other. The attacker establishes independent connections with the victims and relays messages between them, making them believe they are talking to each other over a private connection when the entire conversation is in fact controlled by the attacker.

Examples

Common forms include ARP spoofing on a local network, rogue Wi-Fi access points ("evil twins"), DNS spoofing, and interception of unencrypted HTTP sessions. The classic example is active eavesdropping: Mallory intercepts Alice and Bob's public-key exchange, substituting her own keys, so that she can decrypt, read and re-encrypt every message. Compromised or fraudulently issued TLS certificates can enable man-in-the-middle interception of otherwise encrypted web traffic.

Defences

Defences rest on authentication of the communicating parties: public-key infrastructure and certificate validation, certificate pinning, mutual TLS, SSH host-key verification, and out-of-band verification of keys or fingerprints in secure messaging. Protocols that perform an authenticated key exchange prevent the attacker from establishing two independent trusted sessions.
