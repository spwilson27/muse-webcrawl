Matriarchy

Matriarchy
Definition: Social system in which women hold primary power
Related: Matrilineality, matrilocal residence, patriarchy

Matriarchy is a social system in which women hold the primary roles of political leadership, moral authority, social privilege and control of property. The concept is the counterpart of patriarchy and has been debated by anthropologists for over a century: while clearly matrilineal and matrilocal societies are well documented, whether any society has been a true matriarchy in the strong sense — a mirror image of patriarchy — remains contested.

History of the concept

The idea gained prominence in the nineteenth century through Johann Jakob Bachofen's Das Mutterrecht (1861), which proposed that early human societies were governed by women before a "patriarchal revolution", and Lewis Henry Morgan's work on the Iroquois. Friedrich Engels adopted the thesis in The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State (1884). Twentieth-century feminist writers, including Marija Gimbutas with her controversial vision of peaceful goddess-worshipping "Old Europe", revived interest in prehistorical matriarchy; most anthropologists today regard the universal-matriarchy thesis as unsupported.

Matrilineal societies

Several living societies organize descent, inheritance and residence through women without being straightforwardly ruled by them: the Mosuo (Na) of Yunnan and Sichuan, China, famous for matrilineal households and "walking marriages"; the Minangkabau of West Sumatra, the world's largest matrilineal society, where property passes through women; the Khasi of Meghalaya, India; and the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois), whose clan mothers could appoint and depose chiefs. In such systems men often still hold many formal political offices, leading scholars to prefer precise terms — matrilineal, matrilocal, matricentric — over "matriarchy".
