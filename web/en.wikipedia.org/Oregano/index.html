Oregano

Scientific nameOriganum vulgare L.
FamilyLamiaceae (mint family)
Native rangeMediterranean region and western Asia

Oregano (Origanum vulgare) is a species of flowering plant in the mint family, Lamiaceae. It is a perennial herb native to the Mediterranean region and much of western Asia, widely cultivated for its aromatic leaves, which are used fresh or dried as a culinary herb.

Description

Oregano grows 20–80 cm tall, with opposite leaves 1–4 cm long and upright stems that may be tinged purple. The small white or pink flowers are borne in upright spikes in summer. The flavour of the leaves is warm, aromatic, and slightly bitter, deriving mainly from the compounds carvacrol and thymol.

Culinary use

Oregano is a staple of Italian-American, Greek, Turkish, and Mexican cooking — though Mexican oregano is a different plant, Lippia graveolens. It is a key seasoning for pizza, tomato sauces, grilled meats, and salads, and the dried herb often has a stronger flavour than the fresh leaves.

History and other uses

The name comes from Greek origanon, "brightness of the mountain". Oregano was used in traditional medicine and its essential oil has been studied for antimicrobial properties. It became popular in the United States after the Second World War, when returning soldiers brought a taste for pizza home with them.
