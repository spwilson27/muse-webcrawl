Botany, also called plant science (or plant sciences), plant biology or phytology, is a branch of natural science and biology that involves the scientific study of plants. Traditionally it has included the study of fungi and algae as well. Botanists — plant scientists — study approximately 410,000 species of land plants, including some 391,000 species of vascular plants and about 20,000 bryophytes. Botany originated as herbalism, the identification and cultivation of plants for food, medicine, and other uses.

History

The Greek philosopher Theophrastus (c. 371–287 BC), a student of Aristotle, is called the "father of botany" for his works Historia Plantarum and On the Causes of Plants, which classified hundreds of plants. In the 16th and 17th centuries, herbals and botanical gardens (such as those at Padua and Leiden) advanced plant description, and in 1753 Carl Linnaeus published Species Plantarum, establishing the binomial nomenclature still used today. The 19th century added plant physiology and the cell theory of Schleiden, while Mendel's genetics — worked out on pea plants — and the synthesis of evolution transformed plant science in the 20th century, culminating in molecular genetics and genome sequencing (the first plant genome, Arabidopsis thaliana, was completed in 2000).

Scope and subdisciplines

Modern botany spans plant taxonomy and systematics, morphology and anatomy, physiology (including photosynthesis, which provides most of Earth's oxygen and underpins nearly all food chains), genetics and genomics, ecology and biogeography, pathology, and economic botany. Applied plant science supports agriculture, horticulture, forestry, and the search for new medicines; plants provide food, fibre, timber, fuels, and a large share of pharmaceuticals, including aspirin, morphine, and the cancer drug paclitaxel.

Importance

Because plants sustain nearly all other life — capturing energy, fixing carbon, and producing oxygen — plant science is central to addressing food security, climate change, and biodiversity loss. Herbaria, which preserve billions of specimens worldwide, remain essential reference collections for identifying plants and tracking environmental change.
