Ernst Walter Mayr (5 July 1904 – 3 February 2005) was a German-American evolutionary biologist, taxonomist, ornithologist, and historian and philosopher of science. He was one of the leading evolutionary biologists of the 20th century and a principal architect of the modern evolutionary synthesis that united Mendelian genetics with Darwinian natural selection.

Early career and fieldwork

Mayr earned his doctorate in ornithology at the University of Berlin at age 21. Between 1928 and 1930 he led expeditions to New Guinea and the Solomon Islands, collecting bird specimens — including for the Rothschild collection — and studying how species varied across islands. In 1931 he joined the American Museum of Natural History in New York.

Contributions to evolutionary theory

His book Systematics and the Origin of Species (1942) formulated the biological species concept — species as groups of actually or potentially interbreeding populations reproductively isolated from other groups — and championed allopatric speciation, in which new species arise in geographic isolation. He later developed the idea of peripatric speciation and the founder effect, and defended the distinction between proximate and ultimate causes in biology.

Later life

Mayr moved to Harvard in 1953, directed the Museum of Comparative Zoology, and continued publishing into his late nineties, including What Evolution Is (2001). He died in 2005 at the age of 100, having published more than 700 scientific articles and some 25 books.
