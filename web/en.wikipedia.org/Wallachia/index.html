Wallachia (Romanian: Țara Românească, literally "the Romanian Land") is a historical and geographical region of Romania situated north of the Lower Danube and south of the Southern Carpathians. It is traditionally divided into two sections, Muntenia (Greater Wallachia) in the east and Oltenia (Lesser Wallachia) in the west, separated by the Olt River. With Moldavia, Wallachia was one of the two Danubian Principalities, and their union in 1859 formed the nucleus of the modern Romanian state.

Origins

The principality emerged in the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries as Romanian voivodes south of the Carpathians consolidated their lands, breaking free of Hungarian suzerainty. Basarab I, regarded as the founder of Wallachia, defeated Charles I of Hungary at the Battle of Posada in 1330, securing the principality's independence. Its first capitals were Câmpulung and Curtea de Argeș; Bucharest, first mentioned in 1459, became the permanent capital in 1659.

Ottoman suzerainty

From the early fifteenth century Wallachia was drawn into the Ottoman sphere, and under Mircea the Elder it began paying tribute to the Sultan. It nevertheless retained internal autonomy, its own princes, army and laws, and was never incorporated as an Ottoman province. The principality was a frequent battleground of Ottoman–Habsburg and later Russo-Turkish wars; its princes alternately resisted and served the Porte. Vlad III, known as Vlad the Impaler, whose reigns in the fifteenth century combined fierce resistance to Ottoman expansion with notoriously cruel punishments, later became a source of inspiration for the fictional Count Dracula.

Phanariote period

After the Russo-Turkish war of 1711 and the execution of Constantin Brâncoveanu in 1714, the Porte appointed Greek Phanariote hospodars from Istanbul, a regime that lasted until 1821 and was marked by heavy taxation and administrative reforms inspired by Enlightenment models.

Nineteenth century and union

The Treaty of Adrianople (1829) ended the Ottoman trade monopoly, and under the Organic Regulations of 1831–32 Wallachia received its first quasi-constitutional framework. The 1848 Wallachian revolution proclaimed liberal demands before being suppressed by Ottoman and Russian intervention. In 1859 the assemblies of both principalities elected Alexandru Ioan Cuza as prince, creating the personal union that in 1862 became the United Principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia — Romania — with Bucharest as capital. In 1866 Carol I became prince, and Romania's independence was recognised by the Treaty of Berlin in 1878.

Geography

Wallachia spans roughly 77,000 km² between the Danube in the south, the Carpathian crest in the north, the Milcov River bordering Moldavia in the east and the Danube's Iron Gates in the west. The Wallachian Plain, part of the Lower Danubian Plain, is one of Romania's most fertile agricultural regions, long a major producer of grain and, in the twentieth century, oil around Ploiești.
