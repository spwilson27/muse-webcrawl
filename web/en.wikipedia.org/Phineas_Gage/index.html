Phineas Gage

Phineas P. Gage (1823–1860) was an American railroad construction foreman remembered for his improbable survival of an accident in which a large iron rod was driven completely through his head, destroying much of his brain's left frontal lobe. His case profoundly influenced 19th-century understanding of the brain and mind.

The accident

On September 13, 1848, near Cavendish, Vermont, Gage was preparing a powder charge when it exploded prematurely, driving his 3-foot-7-inch (1.1 m) tamping iron through his cheek, behind his eye, and out the top of his skull. Treated by Dr. John Martyn Harlow, Gage recovered physically to a remarkable degree.

Personality changes

Harlow reported that Gage's personality changed drastically: formerly capable and well-liked, he became fitful, irreverent, and unreliable — "no longer Gage," in his friends' phrase. Later scholarship, notably by Malcolm Macmillan, has argued the severity and permanence of these changes were exaggerated, and that Gage achieved substantial social recovery, working for years as a stagecoach driver in Chile.

Legacy

Gage's skull and tamping iron are preserved at the Warren Anatomical Museum at Harvard Medical School. The case remains a landmark in neurology and neuropsychology, cited in discussions of cerebral localization and the frontal lobes' role in personality and planning.
