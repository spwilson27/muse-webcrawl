Seaweed

Seaweed is a common name for thousands of species of macroscopic, multicellular marine algae, including red algae (Rhodophyta), brown algae (Phaeophyceae) and green algae (Chlorophyta). Seaweeds grow in oceans worldwide, typically attached to rock or other hard surfaces in coastal waters, and range in size from a few centimetres to giant kelps over 40 metres long that form underwater forests.

Kelp, a large brown seaweed

Biology and ecology

Seaweeds are not true plants in the strict taxonomic sense — the term spans several lineages — but like plants they photosynthesise. A typical seaweed has a holdfast anchoring it to the substrate, a stipe (stem-like part) and blades (leaf-like parts); many brown algae have gas-filled floats (pneumatocysts). Kelp forests and intertidal seaweed beds provide habitat and food for many marine animals and protect coasts.

Uses

Seaweeds have been eaten for millennia, especially in East Asia: nori (Porphyra/Pyropia) for sushi, kombu and wakame in Japanese cuisine, and in the British Isles laverbread from Wales and dulse and carrageen moss from Ireland. Seaweed farming (mariculture) is a major industry in China, Indonesia, Korea and Japan. Extracts — alginates, agar and carrageenan — are used as thickeners and gelling agents in food, cosmetics and biotechnology, and seaweed is also used as fertiliser, animal feed and a potential biofuel feedstock.

Invasive species and blooms

Some species become invasive outside their native range (e.g. Undaria pinnatifida, Sargassum muticum), while pelagic Sargassum blooms have in recent years washed ashore in huge quantities in the Caribbean and West Africa.
