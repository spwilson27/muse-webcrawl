Bubble chamber

A bubble chamber is a vessel filled with a superheated transparent liquid (most often liquid hydrogen) used to detect electrically charged particles moving through it. It was invented in 1952 by Donald A. Glaser, who received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1960 for the work.

Principle

The liquid is held just above its boiling point under pressure. Just before particles from an accelerator enter, the pressure is suddenly reduced, putting the liquid into a superheated state. A charged particle ionises atoms along its path, and the deposited energy triggers local boiling, forming a line of tiny bubbles that grows large enough to photograph within milliseconds. The chamber is then recompressed to clear the bubbles for the next cycle. Placed in a magnetic field, the curvature of the tracks reveals particle momentum and charge.

Discoveries

Bubble chambers dominated particle physics in the 1950s and 1960s. Luis Alvarez's group at Berkeley developed large liquid-hydrogen chambers and automated film scanning, discovering numerous resonance particles. The Gargamelle heavy-liquid chamber at CERN observed weak neutral currents in 1973, a landmark confirmation of electroweak theory, and the Big European Bubble Chamber recorded millions of interactions before its retirement in 1984.

Decline

Although unmatched in precision, bubble chambers could not be triggered selectively and produced data only as photographs requiring laborious analysis. From the 1970s they were displaced by electronic detectors such as multiwire proportional chambers and drift chambers, which feed data directly to computers.
