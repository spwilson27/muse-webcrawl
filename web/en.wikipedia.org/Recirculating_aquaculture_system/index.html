Recirculating aquaculture system

A recirculating aquaculture system (RAS) is a technology for farming fish and other aquatic organisms in which water is continuously treated and reused within the system, rather than being replaced by a flow of new water. Typically more than 90 percent of the water is recirculated, allowing production to be sited almost anywhere, with controlled temperature, water quality, and biosecurity, and with greatly reduced effluent discharge.

How it works

Water leaving the rearing tanks passes through a sequence of treatment steps: mechanical filtration (such as a rotating drum filter) removes solid waste; a biological filter of media colonised by nitrifying bacteria converts toxic ammonia excreted by the fish first to nitrite and then to comparatively harmless nitrate; carbon dioxide is stripped by degassing; the water is disinfected with ultraviolet light or ozone; oxygen is added; and pH and temperature are adjusted before the water returns to the tanks. A small flow of fresh water replaces losses and dilutes accumulated nitrate.

Advantages and challenges

RAS offers year-round production independent of climate, high stocking densities, reduced risk of disease and escape of farmed fish, and the possibility of locating farms near markets or on land unsuitable for other agriculture. Species raised commercially in RAS include Atlantic salmon (including large land-based "smolt" and full-cycle grow-out facilities), rainbow trout, barramundi, sturgeon, and shrimp. The technology's drawbacks are high capital and energy costs and the need for skilled operation: a failure of power, oxygenation, or biofiltration can kill the entire stock within hours.
