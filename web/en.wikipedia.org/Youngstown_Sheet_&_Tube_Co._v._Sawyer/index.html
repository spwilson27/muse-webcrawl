Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer, 343 U.S. 579 (1952), commonly called the Steel Seizure Case, is a landmark United States Supreme Court decision limiting the power of the President. The Court held that President Harry S. Truman lacked authority to seize privately owned steel mills in order to prevent a strike during the Korean War.

Background

In late 1951 a wage dispute between the major steel companies and the United Steelworkers threatened a nationwide strike just as steel was critical to the war effort in Korea. Truman declined to use the Taft–Hartley Act's injunction procedure and instead, on 8 April 1952, issued Executive Order 10340 directing Secretary of Commerce Charles Sawyer to take possession of the mills and keep them operating. The companies sued.

Decision

By 6–3, the Court struck down the seizure. Justice Hugo Black's majority opinion reasoned that the President's power must come from either an act of Congress or the Constitution itself, and neither authorised the seizure: Congress had considered and rejected seizure powers when enacting Taft–Hartley, and the Commander-in-Chief and executive-power clauses did not extend to lawmaking of this kind.

Jackson's concurrence

Justice Robert H. Jackson's concurring opinion, dividing presidential action into three categories — (1) action with congressional authorisation, where power is at its maximum; (2) action in the "zone of twilight" where Congress has been silent; and (3) action incompatible with Congress's expressed will, where power is at its "lowest ebb" — became the most influential framework in separation-of-powers law, routinely applied by later courts to questions of executive authority.

Aftermath

The mills were returned to their owners; the steel strike that followed lasted 53 days before a settlement on roughly the terms Truman had sought. The case remains a cornerstone precedent on the limits of inherent presidential power.
