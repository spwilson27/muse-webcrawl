Peptic ulcer disease
SpecialtyGastroenterology
DefinitionBreaks in the inner lining of the stomach (gastric ulcer) or first part of the small intestine (duodenal ulcer)
Main causesHelicobacter pylori infection; NSAID use
SymptomsBurning upper abdominal pain, bloating, belching, nausea; may be silent

Peptic ulcer disease is a break in the inner lining of the stomach (a gastric ulcer), the first part of the small intestine (a duodenal ulcer), or occasionally the lower oesophagus. The most common symptom is burning pain in the upper abdomen — classically waking the sufferer at night, eased by eating or antacids in duodenal ulcer and sometimes worsened by food in gastric ulcer — though many ulcers, especially in older people, cause no symptoms until they bleed or perforate.

Causes

The two dominant causes are infection with the bacterium Helicobacter pylori and use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs, including aspirin), which damage the mucosal barrier that normally protects the stomach from its own acid. Smoking increases risk and impairs healing. Contrary to older belief, stress and spicy food are not primary causes. The discovery of H. pylori by Barry Marshall and Robin Warren in 1982 transformed treatment — they received the Nobel Prize in 2005.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by upper endoscopy, with tests for H. pylori by biopsy, urea breath test or stool antigen. Treatment centres on acid suppression with proton pump inhibitors (such as omeprazole) and, when H. pylori is present, eradication therapy combining a PPI with two antibiotics (commonly clarithromycin and amoxicillin) for about two weeks. NSAIDs should be stopped where possible. Complications — bleeding, perforation or obstruction — may require endoscopic therapy or emergency surgery. Successful eradication has caused peptic ulcer incidence and ulcer surgery to fall dramatically since the 1980s.
