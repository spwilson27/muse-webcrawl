Greywacke
TypeSedimentary rock (sandstone)
CompositionQuartz, feldspar, rock fragments in clay-rich matrix

EnvironmentDeep-marine turbidites, submarine fans

Greywacke or graywacke is a variety of sandstone generally characterised by its hardness, dark colour, and poorly sorted angular grains of quartz, feldspar and small rock fragments (lithic fragments) set in a compact, clay-fine matrix. The term, from the German Grauwacke, was used historically by miners in the Harz Mountains of Germany.

Formation

Most greywackes are interpreted as marine deposits formed by turbidity currents — underwater avalanches that carry sediment rapidly down continental slopes into deep water, depositing graded beds in submarine fans. Their poor sorting and immature mineralogy indicate rapid erosion, short transport and quick burial, typically in tectonically active settings such as accretionary wedges and forearc basins along convergent plate margins.

Distribution

Greywackes are characteristic of Palaeozoic successions in Wales, Scotland, and the Harz, and form the basement rocks of much of New Zealand, where Mesozoic greywacke of the Torlesse composite terrane makes up the Southern Alps. They are commonly interbedded with mudstones and shales in thick "flysch" sequences.

Classification debate

Geologists have long debated the definition of greywacke; modern classifications based on matrix content and grain composition (such as Dott's scheme) often subsume it within lithic wackes, but the field term remains in wide use.
