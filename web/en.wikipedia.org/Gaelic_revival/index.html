The Gaelic revival (Irish: Athbheochan na Gaeilge) was the late-nineteenth-century national revival of interest in the Irish language and Irish Gaelic culture — including literature, sport, music, dance and dress — after a long period of decline under English rule. It formed part of a wider European pattern of romantic nationalism and is closely associated with the Irish literary revival and the movement toward Irish political independence.

Background

By the mid-19th century, Irish had receded to the poorer western districts, a decline accelerated by the Great Famine (1845–1852). Antiquarian scholars such as Eugene O'Curry and John O'Donovan preserved manuscripts, and the Society for the Preservation of the Irish Language (1876) and the Gaelic Union (1880) began organised language activism.

The movement

The revival proper is usually dated to the founding of the Gaelic League in 1893, following Douglas Hyde's address "The Necessity for De-Anglicising Ireland" (1892). The Gaelic Athletic Association (founded 1884) codified hurling and Gaelic football, while writers including W. B. Yeats, Lady Gregory and J. M. Synge created an English-language literature on Irish themes through the Irish Literary Theatre and the Abbey Theatre (1904). Modern Irish prose and poetry were reshaped by figures such as Pádraic Ó Conaire and the collectors of Blasket Island autobiographies.

Legacy

The revival supplied much of the cultural ideology of Irish nationalism and influenced the Easter Rising of 1916, whose leaders were steeped in League culture. After 1922 the Irish Free State made Irish an official language and compulsory in schools, though the revival's central goal — restoring Irish as the everyday vernacular — was not achieved, and the Gaeltacht regions continued to shrink.
