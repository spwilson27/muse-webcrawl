In thermodynamics, the chemical potential of a species is the energy that can be absorbed or released due to a change of the particle number of the given species, e.g. in a chemical reaction or phase transition. The chemical potential of a species in a mixture is defined as the rate of change of free energy of a thermodynamic system with respect to the change in the number of atoms or molecules of the species that are added to the system. It is thus the partial derivative of the free energy with respect to the amount of the species, all other species' concentrations in the mixture remaining constant.

μi = (∂G/∂ni)T,P,nj≠i

When both temperature and pressure are held constant, and the number of particles is expressed in moles, the chemical potential is the partial molar Gibbs free energy. At chemical equilibrium or in phase equilibrium, the total sum of the product of chemical potentials and stoichiometric coefficients is zero, as the free energy is at a minimum. In semiconductor physics, the chemical potential of a system of electrons at zero absolute temperature is known as the Fermi energy.

History

The concept was introduced by the American engineer, chemist and mathematical physicist Josiah Willard Gibbs in his foundational 1876 paper On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances. Gibbs called it simply the "potential" of a substance; the term "chemical potential" was later popularized by Wilder Dwight Bancroft and others.

Overview

Particles tend to move from higher chemical potential to lower chemical potential because this reduces the free energy. In this way, chemical potential is a generalization of "potentials" in physics such as gravitational potential: a ball rolls downhill because its gravitational potential decreases. Similarly, molecules diffuse from regions of higher concentration (higher chemical potential) to regions of lower concentration.

Thermodynamic equilibrium

At equilibrium, the chemical potential of each species is uniform throughout all phases of the system. For a substance that can exist in two phases (such as liquid water and ice), equilibrium requires μ(solid) = μ(liquid). In a chemical reaction Σ νi Ai = 0, equilibrium requires Σ νi μi = 0, which leads directly to the law of mass action and the equilibrium constant.

Chemical potential of solutions

For an ideal solution, the chemical potential of component i depends on its mole fraction xi:

μi = μi* + RT ln xi

where μi* is the chemical potential of the pure substance. For real solutions, activity replaces mole fraction. For dilute solutions this gives rise to colligative properties such as osmotic pressure and freezing-point depression.

Electrochemical potential

For charged species such as ions and electrons, the relevant quantity is the electrochemical potential, which adds the electrostatic energy ziFφ to the chemical potential. Gradients in electrochemical potential drive electric currents, ion transport across membranes, and the operation of batteries and nerve cells.
