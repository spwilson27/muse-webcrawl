Fatigue (material)

Fatigue fracture surface of a broken axle, showing characteristic progressive crack growth marks.

In materials science, fatigue is the weakening of a material caused by repeatedly applied loads. It is the progressive and localised structural damage that occurs when a material is subjected to cyclic loading, at stresses often well below the material's ultimate tensile strength. Fatigue is one of the most common causes of failure in metal components, responsible for the majority of in-service failures of machinery, vehicles, bridges, and aircraft structures.

Stages of fatigue

Fatigue damage develops in three stages. First, crack initiation: microscopic cracks form at stress concentrations such as surface scratches, inclusions, sharp corners, or persistent slip bands in the crystal structure. Second, crack propagation: the crack grows a small amount with each loading cycle, leaving striations and "beach marks" on the fracture surface. Third, final fracture: the remaining cross-section can no longer support the load and fails suddenly, often in a brittle manner.

S–N curves and fatigue life

Fatigue behaviour is commonly characterised by the S–N curve (Wöhler curve), which plots applied stress amplitude against the number of cycles to failure. Many ferrous alloys exhibit an endurance limit (fatigue limit): a stress below which failure does not occur even after very large numbers of cycles. Most non-ferrous alloys, such as aluminium, have no true endurance limit, and a fatigue strength at a specified number of cycles is quoted instead. The German engineer August Wöhler pioneered systematic fatigue testing in the nineteenth century while investigating railway axle failures.

Historical failures

Fatigue has been implicated in many famous disasters, including the Versailles rail accident of 1842 (a broken axle), the de Havilland Comet airliner crashes of 1954 (cracks from pressurisation cycles at square window corners), and the Eschede train disaster of 1998 (a fatigue crack in a steel tyre). These events drove the development of fracture mechanics, damage-tolerant design, and regular non-destructive inspection regimes.

Design against fatigue

Engineers mitigate fatigue by eliminating sharp stress concentrations, improving surface finish, applying compressive residual stresses by shot peening, selecting materials with suitable fatigue properties, and designing for inspectability. Miner's rule is a widely used, if approximate, method for estimating cumulative damage under variable-amplitude loading.
