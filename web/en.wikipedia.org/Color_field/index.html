Color field

Color field painting (often written Color Field) is a style of abstract painting that emerged in New York City during the 1940s and 1950s. It was inspired by European modernism and closely related to Abstract expressionism, and many of its early proponents were among the pioneers of that movement. Color field is characterized primarily by large fields of flat, solid color spread across or stained into the canvas, creating areas of unbroken surface and a flat picture plane.

Characteristics

The movement places less emphasis on gesture, brushstrokes and action in favour of an overall consistency of form and process. In color field painting, "color is freed from objective context and becomes the subject in itself." The resulting works often present color as a hovering, luminous presence rather than as description of objects.

History

The term was coined by the critic Clement Greenberg, who championed painters such as Mark Rothko, Barnett Newman and Clyfford Still. A second generation, including Helen Frankenthaler, Morris Louis and Kenneth Noland, developed the "soak-stain" technique, pouring thinned paint directly onto unprimed canvas so that pigment and support fused into a single surface.

Techniques

Frankenthaler's Mountains and Sea (1952) demonstrated the stain method that Louis and Noland adapted. Noland favored concentric targets and chevrons of unmodulated hue; Louis poured rivulets of color down the canvas in his Veil and Unfurled series. Later practitioners, such as Jules Olitski, experimented with spray guns to produce atmospheric fields of granulated color.

Legacy

Color field painting influenced minimalism and the Washington Color School, and remains a reference point for contemporary abstraction concerned with perception, scale and the materiality of pigment.
