Toluene

Toluene, also known as toluol, is a substituted aromatic hydrocarbon with the chemical formula C6H5CH3, often abbreviated PhCH3. It is a colourless, water-insoluble liquid with a sweet, pungent smell similar to paint thinners, consisting of a benzene ring bearing a single methyl group. Toluene occurs naturally in crude oil and in the tolu tree, from whose balsam it was first isolated in the 19th century.

Production

Toluene is produced industrially from petroleum by catalytic reforming of naphtha and as a by-product of ethylene steam cracking and coke manufacture; global production runs to tens of millions of tonnes per year. Much of it is converted by hydrodealkylation or disproportionation into benzene and xylenes, which are in greater demand; the rest is used as a solvent and gasoline component.

Uses

Toluene is a common solvent in paints, paint thinners, lacquers, adhesives, rubber, printing inks and nail-polish removers, able to dissolve many plastics and resins. Chemically it is a precursor to benzene derivatives including benzoic acid, benzaldehyde and the foam precursor toluene diisocyanate; nitration gives trinitrotoluene (TNT). It is also blended into motor fuel as an octane booster and is used as a cement for polystyrene and as a laboratory solvent and azeotrope former.

Health and safety

Toluene is flammable and moderately toxic: inhalation affects the central nervous system, causing dizziness, drowsiness and, at high or chronic exposures, neurological damage, which underlies its abuse as an inhalant ("glue sniffing"). Exposure limits in workplaces are commonly set around 20–50 ppm. In the body it is metabolised mainly to hippuric acid and excreted in urine. Unlike benzene, toluene is not classified as a carcinogen at ordinary exposures.
