Deep reactive-ion etching


Deep-etched silicon microstructures

Deep reactive-ion etching (DRIE) is a highly anisotropic etch process used to create deep, steep-sided holes and trenches in wafers and substrates, typically with aspect ratios of 20:1 or more. It is a key fabrication technique for microelectromechanical systems (MEMS).

The Bosch process

The most common implementation, the Bosch process, alternates rapidly between two plasma steps: an etching step using sulfur hexafluoride (SF₆) plasma, and a passivation step depositing a chemically inert fluorocarbon polymer (from C₄F₈) on the sidewalls. Each etch cycle removes a small amount of material from the trench bottom while the polymer protects the sidewalls, producing characteristic scalloped walls. The process was patented by Franz Lärmer and Andrea Schilp at Robert Bosch GmbH in the 1990s.

Applications and limitations

DRIE is used to fabricate accelerometers, gyroscopes, microfluidic devices, and through-silicon vias. Drawbacks include sidewall scalloping, microloading (etch-rate variation with feature density), and notching at insulating interfaces; an alternative cryogenic process achieves similar anisotropy by cooling the wafer so that a protective oxide layer forms on the sidewalls.
