Lactose tolerance

Global distribution of adult lactase persistence

Lactose tolerance, in adults usually called lactase persistence, is the continued activity of the enzyme lactase into adulthood, allowing the digestion of lactose, the sugar in milk. In most mammals and most human populations, lactase production declines after weaning, producing adult-type hypolactasia and, in many individuals, lactose intolerance. Lactase persistence is an exception that evolved independently in several human populations with histories of cattle, sheep or goat dairying.

Genetics

Lactase is encoded by the LCT gene on chromosome 2. Persistence is caused not by changes in LCT itself but by regulatory variants in an enhancer region within a neighbouring gene, MCM6. The best-known variant, −13910*T, predominates in Europe. Different mutations — including −14010*C, −13915*G and −13907*G — arose independently in East African and Middle Eastern pastoralist populations, a striking example of convergent evolution driven by gene–culture coevolution with dairying.

Distribution

Persistence reaches frequencies above 90 percent in parts of northern Europe (Scandinavia, Ireland, Britain) and is common among some African pastoralist groups such as the Tutsi, Fulani and Maasai, and in parts of Arabia. It is low in East Asia, most of sub-Saharan Africa's non-pastoralist populations, and Indigenous peoples of the Americas and Oceania. Ancient DNA studies show the European variant was rare among early Neolithic farmers and rose under strong positive selection within the last several thousand years.

Testing

Tolerance can be assessed by the lactose tolerance test (measuring blood glucose after a lactose load), the hydrogen breath test, or directly by genetic testing for persistence variants.
