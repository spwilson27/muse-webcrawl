Athletic shoe

An athletic shoe is a shoe designed for sporting activities, distinguished from dress and casual footwear by its flexible construction, cushioning, traction patterns and sport-specific support. Common names include trainers (British English), sneakers (American English — from the quiet rubber soles that allowed the wearer to "sneak"), runners, kicks and tennis shoes. Modern athletic shoes are highly engineered products, typically comprising a textile or synthetic upper, a foam midsole for shock absorption, and a rubber outsole, and are designed differently for running, court sports, football, basketball, cross-training and other activities.

History

The earliest ancestor of the athletic shoe was the rubber-soled canvas "plimsoll" developed in the 1830s–1870s in Britain as beach and exercise footwear. In the 1890s the U.S. Rubber Company marketed canvas shoes with rubber soles under the name Keds (from 1916), while the Converse All Star basketball shoe appeared in 1917 and was popularised by Chuck Taylor from the 1920s. Spiked competition shoes were developed by J.W. Foster and Sons (later Reebok) in England. In Germany, Adolf Dassler's company (from 1949, Adidas) and his brother Rudolf's Puma dominated post-war sport; in the United States, Blue Ribbon Sports (founded 1964, importing Onitsuka Tiger shoes) became Nike, whose waffle-soled running shoes and the Air Jordan line (from 1985) transformed the athletic shoe into a global fashion and collectibles market. The 1970s jogging boom, aerobics era, and hip-hop culture successively drove sneakers into everyday fashion, a trend now termed "athleisure".

Design

Running shoes emphasise cushioning and heel-to-toe drop for forward motion; court shoes provide lateral support and durable outsoles; cleated shoes (boots) for football and baseball use studs for grip on grass or dirt; basketball shoes historically used high tops for ankle support. Recent performance innovations include energy-returning foams (such as Nike ZoomX and Adidas Boost), carbon-fibre propulsion plates in racing shoes — which prompted World Athletics regulations on sole thickness and plate design after records fell in 2019–2020 — and computer-knitted one-piece uppers. Barefoot-style and minimalist shoes form a counter-trend. The global athletic footwear market is worth well over US$100 billion annually, led by Nike and Adidas, and a resale ("sneakerhead") culture has made limited-edition models valuable collectibles.
