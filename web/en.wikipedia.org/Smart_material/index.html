Smart material

Smart materials, also called intelligent or responsive materials, are materials that have one or more properties that can be significantly changed in a controlled fashion by external stimuli, such as stress, moisture, electric or magnetic fields, light, temperature, pH or chemical compounds. The response may be reversible, repeatable and proportional, distinguishing smart materials from passive materials whose properties are fixed. The term encompasses a wide family of materials used in sensors, actuators, adaptive structures and self-healing systems.

Types

Piezoelectric materials, such as quartz and lead zirconate titanate, generate an electric charge under mechanical stress and deform under an applied voltage, enabling both sensing and actuation. Shape-memory alloys (notably nitinol) and shape-memory polymers recover a programmed shape when heated. Electrochromic and photochromic materials change color or opacity with voltage or light, as in smart windows and self-darkening lenses. Magnetorheological and electrorheological fluids change viscosity dramatically in a magnetic or electric field, enabling controllable dampers and clutches. Other families include thermochromic materials, self-healing polymers, dielectric elastomers, and halochromic materials that respond to pH.

Applications

Smart materials are used in aerospace morphing structures, vibration and noise control, adaptive optics, biomedical devices (stents and orthodontic wires of nitinol, drug-delivery hydrogels), civil engineering (structural health monitoring and seismic damping), textiles that regulate temperature or change color, and consumer products such as impact-hardening sports gear made from shear-thickening materials. Combined with electronics, they form the basis of smart structures that sense and respond to their environment.

Challenges

Obstacles to wider use include cost, limited actuation force or speed for some classes, fatigue and durability concerns, and the need for power supplies and control systems. Research continues into materials that combine multiple responses — for example sensing, actuation and energy harvesting in a single system — moving toward truly autonomous adaptive materials.
