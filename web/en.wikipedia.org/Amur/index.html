The Amur River (Russian: Амур; Chinese: 黑龙江, Hēilóng Jiāng, "Black Dragon River"; Manchu: ᠰᠠᡥᠠᠯᡳᠶᠠᠨ ᡠᠯᠠ, Sahaliyan Ula, "Black River") is the world's tenth longest river, forming the border between the Russian Far East and northeastern China (Inner Manchuria). It flows 2,824 kilometres (1,755 mi) from the confluence of the Shilka and Argun rivers eastward to the Strait of Tartary opposite Sakhalin Island.

Geography

Major tributaries include the Zeya, Bureya, Sungari (Songhua), and Ussuri. The basin spans wetlands of global significance, including habitats of the Siberian tiger, Amur leopard, and great sturgeon such as the kaluga. Major cities on the river include Blagoveshchensk and Khabarovsk in Russia and Heihe in China; Khabarovsk sits near the confluence with the Ussuri.

History

The Amur basin was contested between Qing China and Imperial Russia in the 17th century, resulting in the Treaty of Nerchinsk (1689). Russia annexed the left bank under the Treaty of Aigun (1858) and the Convention of Peking (1860). The river remained a sensitive frontier through the Sino-Soviet split, with armed clashes in 1969 centred further east on the Ussuri at Zhenbao (Damansky) Island. Russia and China finally settled the boundary in agreements of 1991–2004.

Ecology and economy

The Amur is one of the largest free-flowing rivers remaining in the world, without main-stem dams, and supports fisheries of salmon and sturgeon. Flooding is frequent and severe in its low-lying middle reaches, notably in 2013. Bridges linking the Russian and Chinese banks, including the Tongjiang–Nizhneleninskoye railway bridge, have expanded cross-border trade in the 21st century.
