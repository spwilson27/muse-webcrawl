Alexander Sergeyevich Pushkin (1799–1837) was a Russian poet, playwright and novelist of the Romantic era, widely regarded as the greatest Russian poet and the founder of modern Russian literature. Writing with a clarity and economy that reshaped the Russian literary language, he influenced virtually every major Russian writer who followed.

Life

Born in Moscow to a noble family — his great-grandfather Abram Gannibal was an African page and general at the court of Peter the Great — Pushkin published his first poem at fifteen and graduated from the Imperial Lyceum at Tsarskoye Selo. His ode "Liberty" and other politically charged verses led to exile in southern Russia (1820–1824), followed by confinement at his mother's estate of Mikhailovskoye. There and during the fertile "Boldino autumn" of 1830 he produced much of his best work. Under the personal censorship of Nicholas I he remained under surveillance for the rest of his life. In 1837 he was fatally wounded in a duel with Georges d'Anthès, a French officer whose attentions to Pushkin's wife Natalia had provoked scandal, and died two days later, aged 37.

Works

His verse novel Eugene Onegin (serialised 1825–1832), written in a distinctive fourteen-line stanza, is a landmark of Russian literature. Other major works include the historical drama Boris Godunov, the narrative poem "The Bronze Horseman", the prose tales The Tales of Belkin and "The Queen of Spades", and the historical novel The Captain's Daughter (1836).
