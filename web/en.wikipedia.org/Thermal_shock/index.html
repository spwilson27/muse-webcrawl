Thermal shock

Thermal shock is a phenomenon characterized by a rapid change in temperature that results in a transient mechanical load on an object. The load is caused by the differential thermal expansion of different parts of the object, driven by the existence of a temperature gradient across it. This can lead to cracking or breaking of the object, if the load exceeds the strength of the material. Thermal shock is particularly a concern for brittle materials such as glass and ceramics.

Mechanism

When a material is rapidly heated or cooled, its surface changes temperature faster than its interior. The surface attempts to expand or contract, but is constrained by the bulk of the material, which is still at the original temperature. The resulting thermal stress is proportional to the temperature difference, the coefficient of thermal expansion, and the elastic modulus of the material, and inversely related to its thermal conductivity and fracture toughness. If the stress exceeds the material's strength, cracks form and may propagate catastrophically.

Thermal shock resistance

Materials differ greatly in their resistance to thermal shock. Borosilicate glass (such as Pyrex) has a low coefficient of thermal expansion and therefore withstands sudden temperature changes far better than ordinary soda-lime glass. Fused silica has an even lower expansion and excellent resistance. Certain ceramics, such as silicon carbide and zirconia, and glass-ceramics like those used in cooktops, are engineered for high thermal shock resistance. Ice cubes cracking when placed in a warm drink are a familiar household example of the phenomenon.

Examples and prevention

Thermal shock is a design concern in engine components, power-plant boilers, laboratory glassware, cookware, and electronic packages that undergo repeated heating and cooling cycles. Prevention strategies include choosing low-expansion materials, reducing temperature gradients by preheating or controlled cooling, annealing glass to relieve internal stresses, and tempering, which puts surfaces into compression so that thermally induced tensile stresses must overcome the residual compression before cracks can open.
