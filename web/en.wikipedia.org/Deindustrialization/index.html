Deindustrialization

Deindustrialization (American spelling; British: deindustrialisation) is a process of social and economic change caused by the removal or reduction of industrial capacity in a country or region, especially of heavy industry or manufacturing industry. It is usually marked by a sustained decline in manufacturing's share of employment and output, and is the inverse of industrialization. Since the 1970s and 1980s the phenomenon has been particularly associated with the "Rust Belt" of the United States, northern England, and other regions of the developed world.

Causes

Explanations for deindustrialization include rising labour productivity in manufacturing, which allows output to grow even as factory jobs decline; the shift of consumer spending toward services as incomes rise; globalization and trade liberalization, which relocated production to lower-wage countries; automation and technological change; and policy choices concerning exchange rates, trade, and support for industry. Economists debate how much of the employment decline in countries such as the United States is attributable to trade — particularly China's accession to the World Trade Organization in 2001 — as opposed to productivity growth and domestic factors.

Consequences

The decline of manufacturing has had profound consequences for affected communities: job losses concentrated among workers without university degrees, wage stagnation, population decline, deteriorating public finances and services, and associated social problems including family breakdown and opioid addiction. Politically, deindustrialized regions have been fertile ground for populist movements and opposition to trade liberalization. At the same time, some accounts emphasize positive aspects: the growth of service-sector employment, falling prices for manufactured goods, and environmental improvements in former industrial areas.

Responses

Policy responses have included retraining programmes, regional development aid, protectionist measures, and — especially since the late 2010s — industrial policies intended to reshore strategic production such as semiconductors and batteries, motivated in part by supply-chain security concerns.
