Fondue Bourguignonne

Fondue bourguignonne: diners cook cubes of beef in hot oil at the table.

Fondue bourguignonne is a dish of Swiss and French origin in which diners cook cubes of raw meat — traditionally beef — in a communal pot of hot oil placed in the center of the table. Each diner spears a piece of meat on a long fondue fork, immerses it in the oil until cooked to taste, and eats it with one of a variety of accompanying sauces. The name bourguignonne refers to Burgundy, the French region famed for its beef cattle and wine, though the dish in its modern form was popularized in Switzerland and spread internationally in the mid-20th century.

Preparation and serving

The oil — typically a neutral vegetable oil with a high smoke point, sometimes clarified butter or a mixture — is heated in a heavy pot, often of cast iron or stainless steel, over a table burner to around 180–190 °C (350–375 °F). Bite-sized cubes of tender beef cuts such as fillet (tenderloin) or sirloin are provided raw on platters. Diners cook each cube for under a minute for rare meat or somewhat longer for well done.

The cooked meat is traditionally accompanied by an assortment of sauces, which may include béarnaise, garlic mayonnaise, cocktail sauce, curry sauce, mustard-based sauces, and chutneys, together with side dishes such as pickles, cornichons, salads, and bread or potatoes.

Variations

In a related variation sometimes called fondue bressane, cubes of poultry are coated in egg and breadcrumbs before frying. Other meats, seafood, and vegetables can also be cooked in the same manner. A broth-based variant, in which meat and vegetables are cooked in seasoned stock rather than oil, is closer to the Chinese hot pot and to the French fondue vigneronne, where wine-based broth is used.

Relation to other fondues

Fondue bourguignonne is one of several dishes sharing the fondue name, the best known being Swiss cheese fondue, in which bread is dipped into melted cheese, and chocolate fondue, in which fruit or cake is dipped into melted chocolate. The communal, cook-at-the-table format of fondue bourguignonne made it a fashionable dinner-party dish in Europe and North America during the 1950s through 1970s.

Safety

Because the dish involves a pot of very hot oil at the table, care is required to keep the pot stable, to keep water and wet ingredients out of the oil to prevent splattering, and to fully separate raw-meat utensils from cooked food to avoid cross-contamination.
