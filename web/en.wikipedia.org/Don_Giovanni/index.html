Don Giovanni (Italian pronunciation: [ˌdɔn dʒoˈvanni]; K. 527) is an opera in two acts with music by Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart to an Italian libretto by Lorenzo Da Ponte. Its full title is Il dissoluto punito, ossia il Don Giovanni ("The Rake Punished, or Don Giovanni"). Based on the legends of the libertine Don Juan, it premiered at the Estates Theatre in Prague on 29 October 1787 and is widely regarded as among the greatest operas ever written.

Synopsis

The dissolute young nobleman Don Giovanni seduces and discards women across Spain, catalogued by his servant Leporello in the famous "Catalogue Aria" — 1,003 conquests in Spain alone. After killing the Commendatore, father of Donna Anna, whom he attempted to seduce, Giovanni continues his escapades: he courts the peasant girl Zerlina on her wedding day, mocks Donna Elvira, a woman he abandoned, and escapes every trap set by his outraged victims.

In a cemetery, Giovanni jokingly invites the statue of the Commendatore to dinner. The statue accepts. At the banquet the Stone Guest arrives and demands that Giovanni repent; Giovanni refuses, and amid flames and a chorus of demons he is dragged down to Hell. The survivors conclude with a moral: "This is the end of the evildoer: the death of a sinner always reflects his life."

Music and reception

Mozart classified the work as an opera buffa, but its mixture of comedy, melodrama, and the supernatural led him to call it a dramma giocoso. The opera is famous for its overture, the Champagne Aria, the duet "Là ci darem la mano", Donna Anna's "Or sai chi l'onore", and the terrifying final scene with its trombones — instruments then associated with the church and the underworld.

Philosophers and writers including Søren Kierkegaard, who devoted a celebrated essay in Either/Or to the opera, have treated Don Giovanni as the embodiment of pure sensuous immediacy. The opera holds a central place in the repertoire; a performance typically lasts about three hours.
