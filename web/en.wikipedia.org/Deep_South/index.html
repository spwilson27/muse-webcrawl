Deep South

The Deep South is a cultural and geographic subregion of the Southern United States. It is generally taken to comprise the states most dependent on plantation agriculture and slavery before the American Civil War: typically South Carolina, Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi and Louisiana, sometimes extending to parts of Florida, Texas and Tennessee.

History

The region's economy was built on cotton, made profitable by the cotton gin and by the labour of enslaved African Americans, who formed majorities in many counties. The Deep South states led secession in 1860–61, forming the core of the Confederate States of America. After defeat and Reconstruction, sharecropping, Jim Crow segregation laws and disenfranchisement preserved a racial caste system until the civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s, whose landmark events — the Montgomery bus boycott, the Birmingham campaign and the Selma marches — took place in Alabama.

Culture

The Deep South is associated with distinctive speech patterns, Southern cuisine, blues and gospel music, and strong church attendance, particularly in the Baptist and Methodist traditions. Politically the region formed the Democratic "Solid South" for a century after Reconstruction before realigning toward the Republican Party from the late twentieth century.
