Non-zero-sum game

In game theory, a non-zero-sum game is a situation in which the players' gains and losses do not sum to zero, so that cooperation can make all players better off, or competition can make all worse off. Most real-world interactions—trade, negotiation, and coordination problems—are non-zero-sum.

Examples

The prisoner's dilemma is the best-known non-zero-sum game: mutual cooperation beats mutual defection for both players, yet individual incentives push toward defection. Coordination games, bargaining, and markets are also non-zero-sum; international trade is commonly cited as a positive-sum activity in which both parties gain. Conversely, arms races and price wars can be negative-sum.

Significance

Recognizing non-zero-sum structure reframes strategy: rather than only asking how to beat an opponent, players can seek strategies, norms, and institutions—repeated interaction, reputation, enforceable contracts—that sustain cooperative outcomes, a theme developed in Robert Axelrod's work on the evolution of cooperation.
