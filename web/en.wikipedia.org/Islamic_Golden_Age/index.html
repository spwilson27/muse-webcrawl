Islamic Golden Age


Medieval Islamic scholars advanced astronomy, mathematics and medicine
Islamic Golden Age
Period8th century – 13th/14th century CE
CentresBaghdad, Cairo, Córdoba, Samarkand, Isfahan
Key fieldsMathematics, astronomy, medicine, optics, chemistry, philosophy

The Islamic Golden Age was a period of cultural, economic and scientific flourishing in the Muslim world, traditionally dated from the 8th century to the 13th or 14th century. Beginning under the Abbasid Caliphate, it saw the translation of Greek, Persian and Indian learning into Arabic and major original contributions across the sciences and humanities.

Foundations

Baghdad's House of Wisdom (Bayt al-Hikma), patronized by caliphs such as Harun al-Rashid and al-Ma'mun, coordinated the translation movement. Cheap paper, adopted from Chinese technology, spread literacy and scholarship. The empire's extent linked trade and ideas from Iberia to Central Asia, with Arabic as a lingua franca of learning.

Science and mathematics

Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi systematized algebra (from his book Al-Kitab al-Mukhtasar fi Hisab al-Jabr) and popularized Hindu–Arabic numerals; the word "algorithm" derives from his name. Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen) founded modern optics through experimental method. Astronomers such as al-Battani refined planetary tables, and Nasir al-Din al-Tusi developed mathematical models later echoed in Copernican astronomy. In medicine, al-Razi distinguished smallpox from measles, and Ibn Sina's Canon of Medicine remained a standard text in Europe for centuries.

Philosophy and culture

Philosophers including al-Kindi, al-Farabi, Ibn Sina (Avicenna) and Ibn Rushd (Averroes) engaged deeply with Aristotle and Plato, transmitting and commenting on Greek philosophy in ways that shaped medieval European scholasticism. Poetry, calligraphy, architecture and historiography — notably the work of Ibn Khaldun in the later period — also flourished.

Decline

Historians debate the period's end: candidates include the Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258, the Reconquista in Iberia, economic shifts, and changing patterns of patronage and religious thought. Scholarship continued in centres such as Timurid Samarkand and later in the Ottoman, Safavid and Mughal empires.
