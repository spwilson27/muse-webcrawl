Diffraction-limited system

In optics, a diffraction-limited system is one in which all optical aberrations have been reduced below the level set by the fundamental limit of diffraction. The angular resolution of such a system is approximately θ ≈ 1.22λ/D, where λ is the wavelength of light and D is the diameter of the aperture — the criterion identified by Lord Rayleigh.

The Airy pattern

Even a perfect lens focuses a point source not to a point but to a small pattern, the Airy disk, surrounded by faint rings, formed by diffraction at the aperture. Two point sources are conventionally regarded as just resolvable when the center of one Airy disk falls on the first minimum of the other (the Rayleigh criterion). The quality of a nearly diffraction-limited system is often quantified by the Strehl ratio.

Applications

Space telescopes such as the Hubble Space Telescope operate near the diffraction limit, free of atmospheric turbulence. Ground-based telescopes reach it only with adaptive optics. In microscopy, the diffraction limit (about 200 nm for visible light) motivated super-resolution techniques, honored by the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, that circumvent it.
