Imperial and US customary measurement systems

The imperial and United States customary measurement systems are two closely related systems of weights and measures derived from the English units used in the British Isles before 1824. The imperial system was defined by the British Weights and Measures Act of 1824; US customary units developed from the earlier English units in use in the United States and were never formally reformed in the same way.

Units

Both systems use the inch, foot, yard, and mile for length; the acre for area; and the pound and ounce for mass. They differ principally in volume: the imperial gallon is about 4.546 litres, while the US gallon is about 3.785 litres, with correspondingly different pints, quarts, and fluid ounces. The imperial system also uses the stone (14 pounds) in Britain; US customary units use the short ton of 2,000 pounds versus the imperial long ton of 2,240 pounds.

Current use

The United States remains the major user of customary units in everyday commerce, though science and medicine use the metric system. The United Kingdom officially metricated most sectors from 1965 onward but retains imperial units for road distances (miles), draught beer (pints), and personal measurements. Since the Mendenhall Order of 1893 and the international yard and pound agreement of 1959, both systems' units are defined in terms of metric standards: the yard is exactly 0.9144 metres and the pound exactly 0.45359237 kilograms.
