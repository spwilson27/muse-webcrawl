The Farm Security Administration (FSA) was a New Deal agency created in 1937 to combat rural poverty during the Great Depression in the United States. It succeeded the Resettlement Administration (1935–1937). The FSA is famous for its small but highly influential photography program (1935–1944) that portrayed the challenges of rural poverty and helped build public support for New Deal programs.

Programs

The FSA made loans to tenant farmers and sharecroppers to buy land and equipment, resettled destitute families, built camps for migrant farm workers in California and elsewhere, and provided medical care and debt adjustment for farm families. Under Rexford Tugwell the predecessor Resettlement Administration had also built greenbelt towns and planned communities. Conservative opposition in Congress curtailed the agency's ambitions, and in 1946 its functions passed to the Farmers Home Administration.

Photography program

The Historical Section, headed by Roy Stryker, employed photographers including Dorothea Lange, Walker Evans, Arthur Rothstein, Gordon Parks, Russell Lee, Jack Delano and Marion Post Wolcott. Their images — such as Lange's Migrant Mother (1936, taken under the RA) — became iconic documents of the Depression. The file of some 175,000 negatives is now held by the Library of Congress.

Criticism

The agency was criticised by conservatives as socialist and by some for favouring comparatively few families; its camps and resettlement projects were also controversial. Nevertheless its photographic archive remains one of the most important visual records of American life in the 1930s.
