Lepton

In particle physics, a lepton is an elementary particle of half-integer spin (spin 1/2) that does not undergo strong interactions. Two main classes of leptons exist: charged leptons (also known as electron-like leptons) and neutral leptons, better known as neutrinos.

Generations

There are six types (flavours) of leptons, forming three generations. The first generation comprises the electron and electron neutrino; the second, the muon and muon neutrino; and the third, the tau and tau neutrino. Each charged lepton has a corresponding antiparticle. The electron is stable, while the muon and tau decay quickly into lighter particles.

Properties

Charged leptons have electric charge −1 e and masses ranging from 0.511 MeV/c² for the electron to about 1,777 MeV/c² for the tau. Neutrinos have tiny but nonzero masses and interact only via the weak interaction and gravity. Lepton number — with separate electron, muon, and tau flavours — is approximately conserved in particle reactions, though neutrino oscillation shows that individual flavour numbers are not exactly conserved.

History

The electron was identified by J. J. Thomson in 1897. The muon was discovered in cosmic rays in 1936, prompting I. I. Rabi's famous remark "Who ordered that?" The tau was found in the 1970s at SLAC, and the neutrinos were detected between 1956 (electron antineutrino) and 2000 (tau neutrino, by the DONUT experiment).
