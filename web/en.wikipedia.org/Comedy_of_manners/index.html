Comedy of manners

The comedy of manners is a genre of witty, satirical drama that depicts the social conventions, affectations, and amorous intrigues of a sophisticated, usually upper-class society. It relies on polished dialogue, repartee, and stock characters — the fop, the coquette, the scheming valet, and the worldly rake — rather than on broad physical humour.

Origins

The form has roots in Greek New Comedy and the plays of Menander, and in the Roman comedies of Plautus and Terence. It flourished above all in Restoration England after the theatres reopened in 1660, in works such as William Wycherley's The Country Wife, George Etherege's The Man of Mode, and William Congreve's The Way of the World (1700), often regarded as the genre's masterpiece.

Later development

Molière brought a related satire of manners to seventeenth-century France in plays like The Misanthrope and Tartuffe. In the eighteenth century Richard Brinsley Sheridan revived the English form with The Rivals and The School for Scandal. Oscar Wilde perfected it in the Victorian era with The Importance of Being Earnest (1895), and the tradition continued in the twentieth century in the drawing-room comedies of Noël Coward.
