Maple leaf

An autumn maple leaf

The maple leaf is the characteristic leaf of the maple tree (genus Acer) and is best known as the national symbol of Canada. Maple leaves are typically palmately lobed, with three to nine pointed lobes radiating from a single point, although some species have pinnately compound or unlobed leaves.

Botany

Maples are mostly deciduous trees and shrubs of the family Sapindaceae, with about 130 species distributed mainly across the temperate Northern Hemisphere. The leaves are opposite, and in most species palmately veined. In autumn, many species develop brilliant red, orange, and yellow foliage as chlorophyll breaks down and anthocyanin pigments accumulate — the spectacle that makes maples prized ornamental trees and the source of the sugar maple's sap used for maple syrup.

Symbol of Canada

The maple leaf was used as an emblem by French Canadians as early as the 18th century and was adopted by the Société Saint-Jean-Baptiste in 1834. It appeared on the arms of Ontario and Quebec in 1868 and on Canadian military insignia in both World Wars. In 1965, after the Great Canadian Flag Debate, the stylized eleven-point red maple leaf became the centrepiece of the new national flag of Canada, designed by George Stanley and inspired by the flag of the Royal Military College of Canada. The maple leaf also appears on the Canadian one-cent coin (minted until 2012) and is the namesake of the Toronto Maple Leafs hockey team and Air Canada's branding.

Other uses

Maple leaves appear in the arms and logos of many municipalities and organizations, on the gold bullion "Maple Leaf" coins of the Royal Canadian Mint, and in heraldry well beyond Canada. In Japan, the maple (momiji) is celebrated in the autumn tradition of leaf-viewing (momijigari), and the leaf is a common motif in art and confectionery.
