Deconstruction

Deconstruction is a method of critical analysis developed by the French philosopher Jacques Derrida in the 1960s, beginning with works such as Of Grammatology and Writing and Difference (both 1967). It examines the relationship between text and meaning, showing how texts undermine their own stated or intended meanings through internal tensions, binary oppositions, and the play of differences among signs.

Core ideas

Derrida argued that Western thought is organised by hierarchical binary oppositions — speech/writing, presence/absence, nature/culture, reason/madness — in which the first term is privileged. Deconstructive reading shows that the privileged term depends on the subordinate one and that the opposition collapses under scrutiny. His concept of différance (with an "a") combines the senses of differing and deferring: meaning arises from differences between signs and is never fully present, but endlessly deferred along a chain of signifiers. Derrida also criticised "logocentrism," the assumption that meaning is anchored by a stable centre or presence outside language.

Method and influence

Deconstruction is not simply destruction or demolition; Derrida borrowed and adapted the term partly from Heidegger's Destruktion. A deconstructive reading typically proceeds in two moves: it first reconstructs a text's dominant interpretation, then locates the points — contradictions, margins, footnotes, metaphors — where the text deconstructs itself. The approach profoundly influenced literary criticism (notably the Yale School of Paul de Man and J. Hillis Miller), legal studies, architecture, and theology. It became a defining strategy of postmodern thought.

Criticism

Critics, including philosophers such as John Searle and Jürgen Habermas, have accused deconstruction of obscurity, relativism, and evasiveness about truth; the public dispute between Derrida and Searle became a famous episode in late-20th-century philosophy. Defenders reply that deconstruction is a rigorous, close form of reading that exposes unexamined metaphysical assumptions rather than denying meaning altogether.
