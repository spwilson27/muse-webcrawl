Structuralism

Structuralism is an intellectual movement and method of analysis which holds that phenomena of human life — language, myth, kinship, literature, even cuisine and fashion — are intelligible only through their interrelations, that is, through the underlying structures that organise them. Individual elements have meaning not in themselves but by virtue of their differences from, and oppositions to, other elements in a system.

Origins in linguistics

Structuralism grew out of the linguistics of Ferdinand de Saussure, whose posthumous Course in General Linguistics (1916) distinguished between langue (the system of a language) and parole (individual speech), and argued that the linguistic sign is arbitrary, its value determined by relations within the system. The Prague and Copenhagen schools extended these ideas in phonology and grammar, and Roman Jakobson carried structural linguistics across the Atlantic.

Expansion to the human sciences

In the 1950s and 1960s, especially in France, structural methods were applied across the humanities. Claude Lévi-Strauss analysed myth and kinship as systems of relations governed by unconscious rules, most famously in The Elementary Structures of Kinship (1949) and Mythologiques (1964–71). Roland Barthes applied structural analysis to literature and popular culture (Mythologies, 1957); Jacques Lacan re-read psychoanalysis through Saussurean linguistics; Louis Althusser proposed a structural interpretation of Marxism; and the early Michel Foucault analysed the epistemic structures underlying the human sciences.

Post-structuralism

From the late 1960s, many former structuralists turned against the movement's ambitions, arguing that structures are not stable, closed, or fully knowable. Jacques Derrida's 1966 critique of Lévi-Strauss and his subsequent development of deconstruction helped inaugurate post-structuralism, which retained structuralism's focus on language and systems while denying that any structure has a fixed centre or final meaning.
