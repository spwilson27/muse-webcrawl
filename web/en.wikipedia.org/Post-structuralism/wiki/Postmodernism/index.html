Postmodernism

Postmodernism is a broad movement that developed in the mid- to late 20th century across philosophy, art, architecture, and literature, marked by scepticism toward grand narratives and ideologies, a self-conscious use of irony and pastiche, and a questioning of the stability of meaning, authorship, and truth. It emerged partly as a reaction against the perceived certainties and universal claims of modernism.

Philosophy

In philosophy, postmodernism is associated with thinkers such as Jean-François Lyotard, whose The Postmodern Condition (1979) famously defined the postmodern as "incredulity toward metanarratives" — the sweeping stories (progress, emancipation, absolute knowledge) by which modernity legitimised itself. Jacques Derrida's deconstruction and Michel Foucault's analyses of power and discourse were also central influences, as was Jean Baudrillard's account of simulation and "hyperreality," in which images and signs cease to refer to an underlying reality.

Art and architecture

In architecture, postmodernism rejected the austere functionalism of the International Style, reviving ornament, historical quotation, colour, and wit. Landmarks include Michael Graves's Portland Building (1982) and Philip Johnson's AT&T Building in New York (1984) with its Chippendale-style broken pediment. In art, postmodern practices embraced appropriation, mixing of "high" and "low" culture, and challenges to originality, in the work of artists such as Andy Warhol, Cindy Sherman, and Jeff Koons.

Literature

Postmodern literature is characterised by metafiction, unreliable narration, intertextuality, temporal distortion, and play with genre. Representative authors include Thomas Pynchon, Jorge Luis Borges, Italo Calvino, Umberto Eco, Salman Rushdie, and Don DeLillo. Such works often foreground their own constructedness and invite the reader to participate in making meaning.

Criticism

Postmodernism has been criticised from many directions: by Jürgen Habermas, who saw modernity as an unfinished project worth defending; by Noam Chomsky and others who charged its prose with obscurantism; and during the 1996 "Sokal affair," in which a deliberately nonsensical article was accepted by a cultural-studies journal. Defenders argue that postmodern thought provides essential tools for questioning power, representation, and received authority.
