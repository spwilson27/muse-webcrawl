Jacques Derrida (born Jackie Élie Derrida; 15 July 1930 – 9 October 2004) was a French philosopher. He is best known for developing deconstruction, a method of textual analysis that became one of the most influential — and contested — intellectual movements of the late 20th century, with a major impact on philosophy, literary theory, law, and the humanities generally.

Life

Derrida was born in El Biar, a suburb of Algiers, into a Sephardic Jewish family. His schooling was disrupted by the Vichy regime's anti-Jewish laws, under which he was expelled from his lycée in 1942. In 1949 he moved to Paris and, after repeated attempts, was admitted to the École Normale Supérieure, where he studied under and alongside figures such as Louis Althusser and Michel Foucault. He taught at the Sorbonne and the École Normale, later holding posts at the École des Hautes Études en Sciences Sociales and regular visiting professorships in the United States, notably at Yale and the University of California, Irvine. He died of pancreatic cancer in Paris in 2004.

Work

Derrida's breakthrough came in 1967 with the near-simultaneous publication of three books: Of Grammatology, Writing and Difference, and Speech and Phenomena. In them he critiqued structuralism and phenomenology from within, introducing concepts such as différance, the "trace," and "logocentrism." His 1966 lecture at Johns Hopkins University, "Structure, Sign, and Play in the Discourse of the Human Sciences," is often taken as a founding moment of post-structuralism. Later work ranged over ethics, politics, law, and religion, including Specters of Marx (1993) and writings on hospitality, forgiveness, and the death penalty.

Reception

Derrida was a polarising figure. Analytic philosophers criticised his style and arguments; the award of an honorary doctorate by Cambridge University in 1992 was publicly opposed by a group of philosophers, though it proceeded after a vote of the Regent House. Admirers regard him as a meticulous reader of the philosophical tradition whose work exposed deep assumptions about language, presence, and identity.
