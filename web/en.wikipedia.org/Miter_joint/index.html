Miter joint

A miter joint (often spelled mitre joint in British English) is a joint made by cutting two pieces of material, usually wood, at an angle — most often 45° each to form a 90° corner — and joining them together. Miter joints are widely used in picture frames, window and door casing, skirting boards, and decorative mouldings, because they present a clean corner with no visible end grain.

Characteristics

The joint's chief advantage is appearance: the grain of each piece wraps continuously around the corner. Its weakness is mechanical: the glued surfaces are largely end grain, which absorbs glue and produces a weak bond, so miter joints are commonly reinforced with splines, keys, biscuits, dowels, or brads. Because the cut angle must be shared exactly between the two pieces, miters are sensitive to errors; a compound miter is needed when pieces meet at angles in two planes, as in crown moulding.

Making the joint

Miters may be cut with a hand saw guided by a miter box, with a powered miter saw, or on a table saw with a miter gauge. For non-90° corners the required saw setting is half the included corner angle.
