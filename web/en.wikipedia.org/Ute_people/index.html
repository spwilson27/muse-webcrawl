The Ute are an Indigenous people of the Great Basin and western Colorado. They call themselves Núuchi-u ("the people"). Speakers of a Uto-Aztecan language, they historically ranged across what is now Utah, Colorado, and parts of New Mexico and Wyoming, living as skilled hunter-gatherers who became some of the earliest Native American horsemen after acquiring Spanish horses.

History

Early Ute bands were organized around family hunting territories. After contact with the Spanish in the 1600s, horses transformed Ute society, enabling long-range bison hunting on the plains. Conflicts with Spanish colonists, then with American settlers after the 1849 gold rushes, escalated into the Black Hawk War (1865–1872) in Utah. Treaties of 1868 and later cessions confined the Utes to reservations.

Modern tribes

Today three federally recognized tribes descend from the Utes: the Ute Indian Tribe of the Uintah and Ouray Reservation in Utah; the Ute Mountain Ute Tribe of Towaoc, Colorado; and the Southern Ute Indian Tribe of Ignacio, Colorado. Collectively they number around 10,000 enrolled members.

Culture

Traditional Ute culture includes the Bear Dance, an annual spring ceremony of renewal that remains central to community life, along with beadwork, basketry, and a rich oral tradition.
