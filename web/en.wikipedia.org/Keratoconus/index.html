Keratoconus

Keratoconus (from Greek kerato- "cornea" and Latin conus "cone") is a disorder of the eye that results in progressive thinning and bulging of the cornea into a cone-like shape. As the cornea changes shape, it distorts vision, causing blur, ghosting, double vision, glare, and sensitivity to light. It usually affects both eyes, though often asymmetrically.

Causes and prevalence

Keratoconus typically begins in adolescence or early adulthood and affects roughly 1 in 2,000 people, though modern imaging suggests it may be more common. Its causes are incompletely understood; genetic predisposition, chronic eye rubbing, atopic disease, and connective tissue disorders are all associated with it. Oxidative damage and weakening of the corneal collagen structure appear central to the disease.

Diagnosis and symptoms

Diagnosis is made by corneal topography or tomography, which maps the curvature of the cornea. Early signs include frequent changes in glasses prescription, irregular astigmatism that cannot be fully corrected with spectacles, and characteristic findings on slit-lamp examination such as Fleischer rings and Vogt's striae.

Treatment

Mild cases can be managed with glasses or soft contact lenses; more advanced cases often require rigid gas-permeable or scleral lenses. Corneal cross-linking, approved in the United States in 2016, can halt progression by stiffening the cornea with riboflavin and ultraviolet light. Severe cases may require corneal transplantation (keratoplasty), which has a high success rate in this condition.
