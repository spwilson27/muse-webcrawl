Flag of convenience

Flag of convenience (FOC) is a business practice whereby a merchant ship is registered in a country other than that of its owners, and flies that state's civil ensign. The term comes from the maritime practice of flying the flag of the registry country. Ship owners adopt flags of convenience to obtain advantages such as lower registration fees, low or no taxes, and the ability to employ cheaper labour; the registries themselves — called open registries — earn revenue from the tonnage they register.

Open registries

The largest open registries are those of Panama, Liberia and the Marshall Islands, which together account for a very large share of world shipping tonnage; other significant registries include the Bahamas, Malta, Cyprus and several small island states. The modern system grew after the Second World War, when American owners — notably of oil tankers — transferred vessels to the Panamanian and Liberian registers to reduce costs. Liberia's registry, established in 1948 with American support, is administered from the United States. Some open registries are run by private companies on behalf of the flag state.

Legal framework

Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, every ship must be registered in a state whose flag it flies, and that flag state is responsible for exercising jurisdiction over the vessel's safety, crewing and environmental compliance. The Convention calls for a "genuine link" between ship and flag state, but there is no effective mechanism to enforce the requirement. Port state control regimes — regional agreements under which inspectors examine foreign ships in port — partially compensate for weak flag-state enforcement.

Criticism

The International Transport Workers' Federation has campaigned against flags of convenience since 1948, arguing that the system enables substandard wages and conditions, obscures beneficial ownership, and can facilitate illegal fishing, sanctions evasion and poor safety records. Defenders point out that several open registries now perform well in port state control statistics, and that the practice is a legitimate form of regulatory competition in a global industry.
