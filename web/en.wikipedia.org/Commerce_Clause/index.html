Commerce Clause

The Commerce Clause is a provision of the United States Constitution (Article I, Section 8, Clause 3) that gives Congress the power to regulate commerce:

"[The Congress shall have Power] To regulate Commerce with foreign Nations, and among the several States, and with the Indian Tribes."

The clause is one of the principal sources of federal regulatory authority, and its interpretation—together with the related Necessary and Proper Clause—has been central to controversies over the balance of power between the federal government and the states.

Interpretation

In Gibbons v. Ogden (1824), Chief Justice John Marshall gave "commerce" a broad reading, encompassing navigation and commercial intercourse, and held that the power reaches activities occurring within a state when they affect other states. During much of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the Supreme Court read the clause more narrowly, striking down federal regulation of manufacturing, mining, and agriculture as purely local activities.

During the New Deal era, the Court's doctrine expanded dramatically: NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp. (1937) upheld federal labour regulation; Wickard v. Filburn (1942) held that even home-grown wheat for personal consumption could be regulated because, in aggregate, such activity substantially affects interstate commerce. This expansive reading underpinned landmark civil rights legislation such as the Civil Rights Act of 1964.

Modern limits

In United States v. Lopez (1995) and United States v. Morrison (2000), the Court for the first time in decades struck down statutes as exceeding the commerce power. In National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012), a majority held that the clause does not permit Congress to compel individuals to engage in commerce.

Dormant Commerce Clause

Courts have also inferred a "dormant" or negative aspect of the clause: state laws that discriminate against or unduly burden interstate commerce may be invalid even where Congress has not legislated.
