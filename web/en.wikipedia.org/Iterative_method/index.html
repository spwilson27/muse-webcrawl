Iterative method

In computational mathematics, an iterative method is a mathematical procedure that uses an initial value to generate a sequence of improving approximate solutions for a class of problems, in which the n-th approximation is derived from the previous ones.

Description

Iterative methods contrast with direct methods, which attempt to solve a problem in a finite sequence of operations. Iteration is essential when direct methods are too expensive or impossible, as with large sparse systems of linear equations, nonlinear equations, and optimisation problems. Examples include Newton's method for finding roots, fixed-point iteration, the Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel methods for linear systems, and gradient descent.

Convergence

An iterative method is said to be convergent if the sequence it generates approaches the exact solution as the number of iterations increases. The speed of convergence is often expressed as the rate at which the error decreases per iteration; methods may converge linearly, superlinearly, or quadratically. In practice, iterations are stopped when the residual or the change between successive approximations falls below a chosen tolerance.
