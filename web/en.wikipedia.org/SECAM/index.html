SECAM

Distribution of the SECAM, PAL, and NTSC colour systems.

SECAM, also written SÉCAM (French: Séquentiel de couleur à mémoire, "sequential colour with memory"), is an analogue colour television system first used in France beginning in 1967. It was one of the three major colour formats for analogue television, the others being PAL and NTSC.

Technical overview

SECAM transmits colour information sequentially: the two colour-difference signals are sent on alternate lines, and an ultrasonic analogue delay line in the receiver stores one line so that both signals are available for each line. Unlike NTSC and PAL, SECAM uses frequency modulation for the colour signal, making it robust against phase errors but impossible to mix in the analogue domain in certain ways. SECAM broadcasts used 625 lines and 25 frames per second, as did PAL.

History

Work on SECAM began in 1956 by a team led by Henri de France at Compagnie Française de Télévision. France adopted it for its second channel in 1967. It was subsequently adopted by many countries, including the Soviet Union and much of Eastern Europe, parts of Africa and the Middle East, and Greece for a time. Its adoption was often influenced by political as well as technical considerations during the Cold War.

Variants

Several variants existed, differing mainly in the sound carrier and channel spacing: SECAM B/G in much of the Middle East and Greece, SECAM D/K in Eastern Europe and the former USSR, SECAM L in France, and SECAM K in French overseas territories. Brazil used the related hybrid system PAL-M, while a SECAM-like system was never used in the Americas.

Decline

With the transition to digital broadcasting (DVB and its successors) in the 2000s and 2010s, analogue transmissions including SECAM were progressively switched off; France ended analogue broadcasts in 2011.
