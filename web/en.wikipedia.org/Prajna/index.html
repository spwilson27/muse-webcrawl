Prajna

Prajñā (Sanskrit: प्रज्ञा) or paññā (Pāli) is a Buddhist term often translated as "wisdom", "insight", or "discernment". It signifies the understanding of the true nature of phenomena—particularly impermanence, unsatisfactoriness, and non-self—and is considered the decisive factor in attaining enlightenment, dispelling the delusion that is the root of suffering. Prajna is one of the threefold trainings (śīla, samādhi, prajñā) and one of the six (or ten) perfections (pāramitās) cultivated by a bodhisattva.

In early Buddhism

In the Pali Canon, paññā is developed through study, reflection, and meditative cultivation (bhāvanā), and it functions together with concentration (samādhi) in the path to liberation. Analytical insight into the Four Noble Truths and dependent origination is a standard characterization. In the Abhidharma traditions, prajna is analysed as a mental factor that discriminates the characteristics of phenomena.

In Mahayana Buddhism

In Mahayana Buddhism, prajna is especially associated with insight into emptiness (śūnyatā), the absence of intrinsic existence in all phenomena. The Prajnaparamita ("Perfection of Wisdom") sutras, among the earliest Mahayana scriptures, elaborate this wisdom at great length; the concise Heart Sutra and Diamond Sutra are its best-known expressions. Prajnaparamita is also personified as a goddess, "the mother of all Buddhas". In the Madhyamaka philosophy of Nagarjuna, prajna is union of insight into emptiness with compassion (karuṇā), the two wings of the bodhisattva path. In Zen, sudden insight into one's true nature (kenshō) reflects the same emphasis on direct wisdom beyond conceptual thought.
