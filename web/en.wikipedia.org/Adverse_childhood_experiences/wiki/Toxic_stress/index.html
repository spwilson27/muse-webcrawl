Toxic stress

Diagram of positive, tolerable and toxic stress responses in child development.

Toxic stress is a term used by child development researchers and paediatricians to describe the strong, frequent, or prolonged activation of the body's stress response systems in the absence of the buffering protection of a supportive adult relationship. The concept was developed by the National Scientific Council on the Developing Child at Harvard University and popularized by paediatrician Jack Shonkoff and the American Academy of Pediatrics.

Categories of stress

The framework distinguishes three categories of stress response in children. A positive stress response is a normal and essential part of healthy development, such as the brief increase in heart rate and stress hormones when a child receives an immunization. A tolerable stress response activates the body's alert systems to a greater degree — for example after the loss of a loved one — but is buffered by supportive relationships that help the child adapt. A toxic stress response occurs when adversity is strong, frequent or prolonged, as with chronic neglect, abuse, or extreme poverty, without adequate adult support.

Effects

Extended activation of stress systems can disrupt developing brain architecture and other maturing organs, increasing the risk of stress-related disease and cognitive impairment well into adult life. Toxic stress is closely linked with the literature on adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), which links early adversity to later chronic disease, mental illness and health-risk behaviours. Proposed mechanisms include altered hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis function, chronic inflammation and epigenetic changes.

Prevention

Because the defining feature of toxic stress is the absence of buffering relationships, prevention focuses on strengthening caregiver capacity, treating parental mental illness and substance misuse, reducing family economic hardship, and ensuring that young children exposed to adversity have stable, responsive adults in their lives. Paediatric screening for social determinants of health and early intervention programmes are common policy responses.
