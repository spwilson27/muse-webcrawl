Adverse childhood experiences


The ACE pyramid, illustrating the mechanism by which adverse childhood experiences influence health and well-being throughout the lifespan.

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are potentially traumatic events that occur in childhood (0–17 years), including experiencing or witnessing violence, abuse or neglect; growing up in a household with substance use problems, mental health problems or instability due to parental separation or the incarceration of a household member. Research beginning with the landmark CDC–Kaiser Permanente Adverse Childhood Experiences Study in the 1990s has demonstrated a strong, graded relationship between the number of ACEs a person experiences and negative health and well-being outcomes across the lifespan, including heart disease, cancer, depression and early death.

The ACE Study

The original study grew out of an obesity clinic run by Vincent Felitti at Kaiser Permanente in San Diego in the 1980s. Felitti noticed that many patients who dropped out of a weight-loss programme had histories of childhood sexual abuse. Together with Robert Anda of the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), he designed a large survey of more than 17,000 adults, asking about ten categories of childhood adversity: emotional, physical and sexual abuse; emotional and physical neglect; and five forms of household dysfunction — parental separation or divorce, witnessing domestic violence, household substance abuse, household mental illness or suicide, and incarceration of a household member.

The first results, published in 1998 in the American Journal of Preventive Medicine, showed that ACEs were both common — about two-thirds of respondents reported at least one — and strongly associated in a dose–response fashion with the leading causes of death in adults. A person with four or more ACEs, compared with someone with none, had a dramatically higher risk of alcoholism, drug use, depression, suicide attempts, ischemic heart disease, chronic lung disease and liver disease.

Mechanisms

Researchers explain the findings through the concept of toxic stress: prolonged activation of the stress-response system in childhood, in the absence of protective adult relationships, can disrupt brain development and alter the functioning of the immune, hormonal and cardiovascular systems. The "ACE pyramid" model proposes that childhood adversity leads to disrupted neurodevelopment, then to social, emotional and cognitive impairment, the adoption of health-risk behaviours such as smoking and overeating, and ultimately to disease, disability and early mortality. Epigenetic changes have also been proposed as a pathway by which early adversity becomes biologically embedded.

Prevalence and expanded measures

Subsequent surveys, such as the CDC's Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) ACE module used in many U.S. states, have broadly replicated the prevalence findings. Scholars have proposed expanded lists of adversities, sometimes called expanded ACEs, including community-level stressors such as bullying, discrimination, community violence, neighbourhood poverty and foster care. The World Health Organization developed the ACE International Questionnaire (ACE-IQ) for use in international surveys.

Prevention and response

Public health agencies frame ACEs as preventable: strategies include strengthening economic supports for families, promoting social norms that protect against violence, teaching parenting and social-emotional skills, and ensuring access to quality childcare and healthcare. In clinical and educational settings, "trauma-informed care" approaches seek to recognise the prevalence of trauma, avoid re-traumatisation, and build resilience. Protective factors — safe, stable, nurturing relationships and environments — can buffer children against the effects of adversity, and researchers emphasise that ACE scores are population-level risk indicators rather than deterministic predictions for any individual.

Criticism

Critics have noted that the original sample was predominantly white, middle-class and insured, limiting generalisability; that retrospective self-report is subject to recall bias; and that summing very different experiences into a single score can obscure which specific adversities drive which outcomes. Some commentators also caution against routine screening without adequate services in place to respond to disclosures.
