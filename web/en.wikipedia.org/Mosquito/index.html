Mosquito

Mosquitoes, the Culicidae, are a family of small flies consisting of 3,600 species. The word "mosquito" (formed by mosca and diminutive -ito) is Spanish and Portuguese for "little fly". Mosquitoes have a slender segmented body, one pair of wings, three pairs of long hair-like legs, and specialized, highly elongated mouthparts. The mosquito life cycle consists of four stages: egg, larva, pupa, and adult. Almost all species feed on nectar and plant juices; in many species, the mouthparts of females are adapted for piercing the skin of animal hosts and sucking their blood as ectoparasites.


A female Anopheles mosquito taking a blood meal.

In female mosquitoes, the blood meal provides proteins needed for egg production. In this feeding behaviour, mosquitoes can act as vectors for disease-causing microorganisms that they transmit from host to host, including viruses (such as dengue and Zika), bacteria, protozoans (notably the malaria parasites Plasmodium), and parasitic worms responsible for lymphatic filariasis. Mosquitoes are estimated to transmit disease to more than 700 million people a year in Africa, South America, Central America, Mexico, Russia, and much of Asia, with millions of resulting deaths; they are often described as among the deadliest animals to humans in history.

Taxonomy and distribution

Mosquitoes belong to the order Diptera (true flies). The family Culicidae is divided into subfamilies including Anophelinae and Culicinae. Important genera include Anopheles (the sole vectors of human malaria), Aedes (vectors of dengue, yellow fever, chikungunya, and Zika), Culex (vectors of West Nile virus and Japanese encephalitis), and Mansonia. Mosquitoes occur on every continent except Antarctica and on many isolated islands. They have existed since at least the Jurassic period; the oldest known fossil mosquito with modern anatomy is preserved in Burmese amber around 99 million years old.

Life cycle

Female mosquitoes lay eggs on or near water. Depending on the species, eggs are laid singly (as in Anopheles) or in floating rafts (Culex); Aedes eggs are laid on damp surfaces and can survive desiccation for months, hatching when flooded. Larvae — known as "wrigglers" — live in water, feeding on microorganisms and organic matter, and come to the surface to breathe through a siphon or, in Anopheles, lying parallel to the surface. The aquatic pupal stage ("tumblers") does not feed but remains mobile. Adults emerge after a few days; males form mating swarms, and females of most species seek a blood meal before laying eggs. The whole cycle can take as little as a week in warm conditions.

Feeding behaviour

Female mosquitoes locate hosts by detecting carbon dioxide, body odours (including lactic acid and octenol), heat, and visual cues. The fascicle — a bundle of six needle-like stylets — pierces the skin and probes for a blood vessel, while saliva containing anticoagulants and anaesthetic compounds is injected; the itchy welt following a bite is an immune reaction to this saliva. Males, and females of some species, feed only on nectar and other plant sugars and are pollinators of some plants, including certain Arctic orchids.

Disease transmission

When a mosquito feeds on an infected host, pathogens taken up with the blood may replicate or develop within the mosquito and migrate to the salivary glands, to be injected into the next host. Malaria — caused by Plasmodium parasites and transmitted by Anopheles — caused an estimated 249 million cases and about 608,000 deaths in 2022 according to the World Health Organization, mostly among children in sub-Saharan Africa. Dengue has expanded dramatically with the spread of Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus into temperate regions.

Control

Control methods include source reduction (eliminating standing water), larvicides and adulticides, biological control with fish or Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis, personal protection (repellents such as DEET, bed nets, window screens), and insecticide-treated nets, which have driven large reductions in malaria. Newer approaches include the sterile insect technique, release of males carrying the bacterium Wolbachia (which reduces viral transmission), and gene-drive research aimed at suppressing or modifying wild populations — methods that remain under scientific and ethical evaluation.
