Commons

The commons is the cultural and natural resources accessible to all members of a society, including natural materials such as air, water, and habitable land, and shared creations such as knowledge and public spaces. In its classic legal sense it referred to common land over which villagers held traditional rights of grazing, fuel gathering, and foraging. The concept has expanded to cover shared resources of every kind, from fisheries and forests to the internet and the "knowledge commons".

History

In England, common land was a central institution of the medieval open-field system. From the 16th to the 19th centuries, parliamentary enclosure acts privatised millions of acres, extinguishing common rights — a process historians link both to agricultural improvement and to rural dispossession. Surviving commons are now protected, notably under the Commons Act 2006.

Theory

Garrett Hardin's "tragedy of the commons" (1968) argued shared resources are doomed to overuse; Elinor Ostrom's research showed instead that communities often govern commons sustainably through their own rules and monitoring. This work founded modern commons scholarship, now applied to digital goods, open-source software, and global resources like the climate system.

Modern commons

Creative Commons licences, Wikipedia itself, and open data are contemporary examples of deliberately created knowledge commons governed by community norms.
