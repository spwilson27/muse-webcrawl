Hawala

Hawala (Arabic: حِوالة, "transfer") is an informal method of transferring money without any physical movement of cash, based on a network of money brokers (hawaladars) and trust. It originated in South Asia and the Middle East many centuries ago and remains widely used today, especially for remittances from migrant workers.

How it works

A customer gives money to a hawaladar in one city, who contacts a counterpart in the recipient's city and instructs them to pay out the equivalent amount, minus a small commission. No money crosses borders at the time of the transaction; brokers settle their balances later, often through trade, reverse transfers or bank movements. The system relies on honour, kinship and reputation within the broker network, and transactions may be confirmed with nothing more than a code word or a torn banknote matched by its serial number.

Uses and regulation

Hawala provides fast, cheap transfers to places with weak banking infrastructure and is a lifeline in countries such as Somalia. Because it can operate outside formal financial oversight, regulators worry it may be abused for money laundering or terrorist finance; many jurisdictions now require hawaladars to register and keep records, while in others the practice is prohibited.
