English verbs

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Verbs constitute one of the main parts of speech in English, and carry the clause's expression of tense, aspect, mood and voice. Compared with most European languages, English verb inflection is minimal: a regular verb has at most five forms — the base (walk), the third-person singular present (walks), the past tense (walked), the past participle (walked) and the present participle or gerund (walking) — and many of these coincide. The complexity of the English verb system lies instead in its auxiliaries.

Forms and inflection

Verbs divide into regular ("weak") verbs, which form their past tense and participle with the suffix -ed, and irregular ("strong" and other) verbs, some 200–300 in common use, with vowel changes or other patterns inherited from Old English (sing–sang–sung, go–went–gone). The verb be is uniquely irregular, with eight forms. Modal verbs are defective and do not inflect at all.

Auxiliaries and constructions

Tense, aspect and mood are built chiefly by combining auxiliaries: have plus the past participle for the perfect ("has eaten"), be plus the -ing form for the progressive ("is eating"), be plus the past participle for the passive ("was eaten"), and the modal verbs for possibility, necessity and futurity. Do-support supplies question and negative forms for lexical verbs ("Did you go?", "I do not know"). Combinations of these produce the full traditional paradigm of "tenses" — past perfect progressive passive and the like — though linguists generally analyse English as having only two morphological tenses, present and past, with the future expressed modally.

Valency and complementation

Verbs differ in the complements they license: intransitive (sleep), transitive (eat lunch), ditransitive (give her the book), and copular verbs linking subject to a predicative complement (seem, become, be). Phrasal verbs — verb plus particle combinations such as give up or look into — are a conspicuous and productive feature of English.
