Commodity fetishism

Commodity fetishism is a concept introduced by Karl Marx in the first chapter of Capital: Critique of Political Economy (1867). It describes how, in a capitalist market economy, the social relations between producers — whose labour creates value — come to appear instead as economic relations between objects, the commodities exchanged. Value seems to be a natural property of things rather than a product of human labour.

Marx's argument

Marx compared the phenomenon to religious fetishism: just as people worship objects they themselves have made, market participants treat prices and values as if they belonged to commodities by nature. Because producers meet only through exchange, the labour embodied in products is visible only in the form of exchange-value, disguising the social character of production.

Related concepts

The idea underpins later Marxist theories of reification (developed by Georg Lukács) and ideology, and has influenced cultural criticism of advertising and consumer society, where brand identities attach social meanings to goods.

Criticism

Critics dispute both the labour theory of value on which the concept rests and the claim that market prices systematically obscure social relations; defenders argue the notion remains a powerful account of how economic categories are naturalised.
