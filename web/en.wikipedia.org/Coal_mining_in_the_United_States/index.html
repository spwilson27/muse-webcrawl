Coal mining in the United States

Coal mining in the United States is an industry extending from the colonial era to the present. The United States holds the world's largest recoverable coal reserves, and coal long served as the backbone of American industrialisation and electricity generation. Production peaked at about 1.17 billion short tons in 2008 and has since fallen by more than half as natural gas and renewable energy displaced coal in power generation.

History

Commercial mining began in the 1740s near Richmond, Virginia, and expanded rapidly with the railroads and the anthracite fields of north-eastern Pennsylvania. The industry employed over 860,000 miners in 1923 and was marked by violent labour conflict, including the Ludlow Massacre of 1914 and the Battle of Blair Mountain in 1921, the largest labour uprising in American history. The United Mine Workers under John L. Lewis became a central force in the CIO era. Disasters such as Monongah (1907, 362 dead) and Farmington (1968) drove successive waves of safety legislation, culminating in the Federal Coal Mine Health and Safety Act of 1969 and the creation of the Mine Safety and Health Administration (MSHA) in 1977.

Regions and methods

The three great producing regions are Appalachia (West Virginia, Kentucky, Pennsylvania), the Illinois Basin, and the Powder River Basin of Wyoming and Montana, where thick, low-sulfur seams are extracted in vast open-pit surface mines and which supplies the largest tonnage of any single region. Underground mining — room-and-pillar and highly productive longwall methods — predominates in the East. Mountaintop removal mining in Appalachia, in which summits are blasted away to expose seams, has removed hundreds of peaks and buried headwater streams, provoking sustained environmental controversy.

Decline

From its 2008 peak, United States coal production and consumption declined steeply as shale gas, wind, and solar undercut coal on cost and as environmental regulation tightened. Coal's share of electricity generation fell from about half in the mid-2000s to under one-fifth by the early 2020s, and major producers including Peabody and Arch passed through bankruptcy. Employment fell below 45,000 miners, with acute economic effects on coalfield communities. Black lung disease (coal workers' pneumoconiosis) has resurged among central Appalachian miners exposed to silica-bearing rock dust, prompting new regulatory attention in the 2020s.
