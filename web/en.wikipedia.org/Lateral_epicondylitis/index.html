Lateral epicondylitis, commonly known as tennis elbow, is a painful condition of the elbow caused by overuse of the forearm extensor muscles, resulting in pain and tenderness on the outside (lateral side) of the elbow at the origin of the common extensor tendon on the lateral epicondyle of the humerus. Despite the name, most people with the condition do not play tennis; it is common among manual workers, cooks, painters and others whose activities involve repetitive gripping or wrist extension.

Pathophysiology

Although the suffix "-itis" implies inflammation, histological studies show that lateral epicondylitis is chiefly a degenerative tendinopathy (tendinosis) rather than a true inflammatory condition. Repetitive eccentric stress at the origin of the extensor carpi radialis brevis tendon produces microtears, disorganised collagen and angiofibroblastic change, without significant inflammatory cell infiltration.

Signs and symptoms

Pain is felt over the lateral epicondyle and may radiate down the forearm. It is provoked by resisted extension of the wrist or middle finger, by gripping, lifting, or twisting actions such as turning a door handle or shaking hands. Grip strength is often reduced. Symptoms usually develop gradually and, in most people, resolve within 6–24 months even without specific treatment.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical, based on the history and localised tenderness about 1 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle, together with provocative tests such as Cozen's test (pain on resisted wrist extension with the elbow extended) and Mill's test. Imaging is not routinely required but ultrasound or MRI may be used in persistent cases to confirm tendon degeneration and exclude other causes such as radial tunnel syndrome, osteoarthritis or referred cervical pain.

Treatment

Management is primarily conservative: activity modification, ice, and analgesics or NSAIDs for short-term relief; physiotherapy with progressive loading and eccentric strengthening exercises; and counterforce braces or wrist splints. Corticosteroid injections give good short-term relief but are associated with worse long-term outcomes and recurrence. Platelet-rich plasma and other injection therapies have mixed evidence. Surgery — open or arthroscopic release and debridement of the degenerated tendon — is reserved for the small minority with disabling symptoms persisting beyond 6–12 months.
