Streptococcus pneumoniae

Streptococcus pneumoniae, commonly known as the pneumococcus, is a Gram-positive, spherical bacterium, alpha-haemolytic (under aerobic conditions) or anaerobic, and a member of the genus Streptococcus. It is usually found in pairs (diplococci) and does not form spores; it is non-motile. A significant human pathogenic bacterium, S. pneumoniae was recognised as a major cause of pneumonia in the late 19th century, and is the subject of many pneumococcal immunity studies.

The pneumococcus is a common inhabitant of the human respiratory tract, colonising the nasopharynx of many healthy people, especially children. It is the leading bacterial cause of community-acquired pneumonia, and also causes meningitis, bacteraemia, otitis media, sinusitis and other infections.

History

The organism was first isolated in 1881 by Louis Pasteur in France and, independently, by George Miller Sternberg in the United States. In the 1940s, experiments on pneumococcal transformation by Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod and Maclyn McCarty using strains of this bacterium provided the first strong evidence that DNA carries genetic information. The pneumococcus was earlier known as Diplococcus pneumoniae because of its characteristic paired appearance in sputum.

Virulence and disease

The polysaccharide capsule is the principal virulence factor, protecting the bacterium from phagocytosis; more than 100 capsular serotypes are distinguished. Other virulence factors include pneumolysin, autolysin, and surface proteins that mediate adherence to respiratory epithelium. Pneumococcal disease ranges from mucosal infections (otitis media, sinusitis, pneumonia) to invasive disease (bacteraemia and meningitis), which carries high mortality, particularly in infants, the elderly and immunocompromised people.

Prevention and treatment

Pneumococcal vaccines — the polysaccharide vaccine PPSV23 and conjugate vaccines (PCV13, PCV15, PCV20 and successors) — protect against the serotypes most often causing invasive disease, and their introduction has greatly reduced childhood pneumococcal disease and, through herd immunity, disease in adults. Treatment relies on antibiotics such as beta-lactams and macrolides, though penicillin-non-susceptible strains are a growing concern.
