Title insurance

Title insurance is a form of indemnity insurance predominant in the United States that insures against financial loss arising from defects in the title to real property. Unlike most insurance, which protects against future events, title insurance protects against claims for past occurrences that affect ownership.

How it works

Before a policy is issued, the insurer (or a title company acting for it) conducts a title search of public records to identify liens, encumbrances, easements, unpaid taxes and other defects, many of which are resolved before closing. Two basic policy types exist: a lender's policy, which typically is required by mortgage lenders and protects only the lender's interest, and an owner's policy, which protects the buyer. The premium is paid once, at closing, and coverage lasts as long as the insured holds an interest in the property.

History

Title insurance arose in the 19th century after weaknesses in conveyancing practice, illustrated by the Pennsylvania case Watson v. Muirhead (1868), led to the founding of the first title insurance company in Philadelphia in 1876. In most other countries, state-run land registration systems such as Torrens title reduce the need for private title insurance.
