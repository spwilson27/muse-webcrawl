Hung parliament

Hung parliament
DefinitionNo party holds an overall majority
UK examplesFeb 1974, 2010, 2017
OutcomesMinority government, coalition, fresh election

A hung parliament is a legislature in a parliamentary system in which no single political party wins an outright majority of seats, so no party can govern alone without support from others. The term is most associated with Westminster-style systems such as the United Kingdom, Canada and Australia.

Consequences

After a hung parliament, the incumbent prime minister usually has the first opportunity to try to form a government. Options include a formal coalition with a shared programme (as with the UK Conservative–Liberal Democrat coalition of 2010), a minority government sustained by confidence-and-supply agreements (as in the UK in 2017, when the Conservatives relied on the Democratic Unionist Party), or, failing agreement, a new election.

Frequency

Hung parliaments are rare in Britain under first-past-the-post — occurring in February 1974, 2010 and 2017 — but are routine in countries using proportional representation, where coalition negotiation is a normal part of government formation.
