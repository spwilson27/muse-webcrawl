Day of the Dead

The Day of the Dead (Spanish: Día de los Muertos or Día de Muertos) is a Mexican holiday, observed mainly on 1 and 2 November, in which families welcome back the souls of deceased relatives for a brief reunion that includes food, drink, and celebration. The multi-day observance blends pre-Hispanic Aztec and Nahua rituals honoring the dead with the Catholic feasts of All Saints' Day and All Souls' Day.

Observances

Families build altars called ofrendas decorated with photographs of the dead, candles, incense, and bright orange Mexican marigolds (cempasúchil), whose scent is said to guide spirits home. Offerings include the favorite foods and drinks of the departed, sugar skulls, and pan de muerto, a sweet bread. Many families spend the night in cemeteries beside the graves of their loved ones, sharing meals and music.

History and symbolism

The festival descends from rituals dedicated by the Aztecs to the goddess Mictecacihuatl, "Lady of the Dead," adapted after Spanish colonization to the Catholic calendar. November 1 honors deceased children (angelitos), while November 2 is dedicated to adults. The skeletal figure La Calavera Catrina, created by printmaker José Guadalupe Posada, has become the holiday's most recognizable icon.

Recognition

In 2008 UNESCO inscribed the "Indigenous Festivity dedicated to the Dead" on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The holiday is celebrated throughout Mexico and by Mexican communities abroad, and has gained wider international attention through events such as Mexico City's large Day of the Dead parade and the 2017 Pixar film Coco.
