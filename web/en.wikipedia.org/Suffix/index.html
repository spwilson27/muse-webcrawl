Suffix

In linguistics, a suffix is an affix which is placed after the stem of a word. Common examples are case endings, which indicate the grammatical case of nouns or adjectives, and verb endings, which form the conjugation of verbs. Suffixes can carry grammatical information (inflectional suffixes) or lexical information (derivational suffixes).

Inflectional suffixes

Inflectional suffixes do not change the part of speech or core meaning of a word; they mark categories such as number, tense, or case. English examples include the plural -s in "dogs", the past tense -ed in "walked", the progressive -ing, and the comparative -er. English has relatively few inflectional suffixes; languages such as Latin, Russian, Finnish, and Turkish have far richer suffixal inflection.

Derivational suffixes

Derivational suffixes create new words, often changing the part of speech: -ness turns adjectives into nouns ("happy" → "happiness"), -ify forms verbs ("simple" → "simplify"), and -able forms adjectives ("read" → "readable"). Derivational suffixes may also change meaning without changing category, as in diminutives.

Other languages

Agglutinative languages such as Turkish, Finnish, and Japanese build long words from chains of suffixes, each with a distinct grammatical function. In Semitic languages, by contrast, much morphology is non-concatenative, altering the interior of the root rather than adding suffixes.
