Priest hole

A priest hole is a hiding place for a priest built into many principal Catholic houses in England during the period when Catholics were persecuted by law in the reigns of Queen Elizabeth I and her successors. When pursuivants ("priest hunters") arrived to search for Catholic priests—whose presence in England was treason under the Jesuits, etc. Act 1584—a priest could conceal himself in a priest hole, sometimes for days, while the house was searched, measured, and even partly dismantled.

Construction

Priest holes were built into walls, floors, staircases, chimney breasts, and panelling, often in clusters so that a priest could move between them. The most famous builder was the Jesuit lay brother Nicholas Owen, a master carpenter who constructed hides at houses including Harvington Hall, Baddesley Clinton, and Hindlip Hall, and who was canonized after dying under torture in the Tower of London in 1606. The hides were so well concealed, with double walls and offset dimensions, that some were rediscovered only centuries later.
