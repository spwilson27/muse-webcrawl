The Second Bank of the United States was the second federally authorized Hamiltonian national bank in the United States. Located in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, the bank was chartered from 1816 to 1836, like its predecessor the First Bank of the United States (1791–1811). It was essentially a private corporation with public duties, holding Treasury deposits, transferring government funds, and issuing bank notes that served as a national currency. With $35 million in capital and 25 branches, it was by far the largest corporation in America, and its power to restrain state bank lending made it a proto-central bank.

Origins

The War of 1812 exposed the chaos of a currency of hundreds of state-chartered bank notes, and in 1816 Congress chartered the Second Bank with the support of President James Madison, who set aside his earlier constitutional objections. The bank's constitutional status was upheld by the Supreme Court in McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), which also struck down Maryland's tax on the bank's notes. Mismanagement and fraud in its early years contributed to the Panic of 1819, but under Langdon Cheves and then Nicholas Biddle (from 1823) it became a stabilising force, regulating state banks by regularly redeeming their notes.

The Bank War

President Andrew Jackson came to office convinced the bank was an unconstitutional monopoly serving eastern elites. When Biddle and his allies in Congress, led by Henry Clay and Daniel Webster, pushed through an early recharter bill in 1832, Jackson vetoed it — a veto message that became a landmark statement of Jacksonian democracy. Jackson then removed federal deposits to selected state banks ("pet banks"), and Biddle retaliated by contracting credit, deliberately deepening the ensuing recession in a bid to force recharter. The bank's federal charter expired in 1836; it continued briefly under a Pennsylvania charter before failing in 1841. The "Bank War" reshaped American politics and left the United States without a central bank until the Federal Reserve in 1913.

Building

The bank's headquarters, designed by William Strickland in the Greek Revival style and completed in 1824, is a landmark of early American architecture modelled on the Parthenon; it now forms part of Independence National Historical Park and houses a portrait gallery.
