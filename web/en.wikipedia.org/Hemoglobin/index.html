Hemoglobin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hemoglobin (also spelled haemoglobin, abbreviated Hb or Hgb) is the iron-containing oxygen-transport metalloprotein in the red blood cells of almost all vertebrates and the tissues of some invertebrates. Hemoglobin carries oxygen from the lungs or gills to the rest of the body, where it releases the oxygen to permit aerobic respiration, and carries carbon dioxide back toward the lungs, partly in the form of carbaminohemoglobin and partly by buffering blood pH.

Structure

In adult humans, the most common form, hemoglobin A, is a tetramer of two alpha and two beta globin chains, each folded around a heme group. Each heme contains a single iron atom in the ferrous (Fe2+) state that binds one oxygen molecule, so one hemoglobin molecule can carry four O2 molecules. Fetal hemoglobin (HbF) substitutes gamma chains for beta chains and binds oxygen more avidly, drawing it across the placenta. The three-dimensional structure was solved by Max Perutz in 1959–1960 using X-ray crystallography, work that contributed to his Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1962.

Cooperative binding

Hemoglobin binds oxygen cooperatively: binding of the first O2 shifts the molecule from the tense (T) state to the relaxed (R) state and increases the affinity of the remaining sites, producing the characteristic sigmoid oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation curve. Affinity is lowered by acidity and carbon dioxide (the Bohr effect) and by 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate, all of which promote oxygen release in metabolically active tissues.

Disorders

Mutations in globin genes cause the hemoglobinopathies. In sickle cell disease, a single point mutation in the beta chain produces hemoglobin S, which polymerises when deoxygenated and deforms red cells into sickles. The thalassemias result from reduced production of alpha or beta chains. If the iron is oxidised to the ferric (Fe3+) state, the resulting methemoglobin cannot bind oxygen. Hemoglobin concentration is measured in every complete blood count; abnormally low values define anaemia. Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) reflects average blood glucose over preceding months and is a standard test for diabetes.

History

Hemoglobin was described by Friedrich Ludwig Hünefeld in 1840, crystallised and named in the following decades, and its oxygen-binding function recognised by Felix Hoppe-Seyler in the 1860s. It became a model system for the study of allosteric proteins.
