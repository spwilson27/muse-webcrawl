Frequency analysis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In cryptanalysis, frequency analysis is the study of the frequency of letters or groups of letters in a ciphertext. The method is used as an aid to breaking classical substitution ciphers, exploiting the fact that in any written language certain letters and combinations of letters occur with characteristic, predictable frequencies.

History

The technique was first described by the 9th-century Arab philosopher and cryptologist Al-Kindi in his Manuscript on Deciphering Cryptographic Messages. Frequency analysis became the principal tool of European codebreakers during the Renaissance and was applied famously against the Babington plot cipher of Mary, Queen of Scots, in 1586.

Method

In English text, the letter E is by far the most common (about 12.7% of letters), followed by T, A, O, I and N. Common pairs (digraphs) include TH, HE, IN and ER, and the most common trigraph is THE. An analyst counts the symbols in a substitution ciphertext and matches the most frequent ciphertext symbol to E, the next to T, and so on, refining the mapping with digraphs and probable words until readable plaintext emerges.

Limitations and countermeasures

Frequency analysis works only when the cipher preserves letter frequencies. Homophonic substitution, polyalphabetic ciphers such as the Vigenère cipher, and later rotor machines were all developed largely to defeat it, by flattening or shifting letter distributions. Reliable results also require a sufficiently long ciphertext: short messages can deviate strongly from average letter statistics.

In other fields

The term is also used for the statistical study of distributions more generally — for example in linguistics, seismology, and the analysis of repeated events — and in cryptanalysis of modern systems for analysing the frequency of patterns such as block repetitions in ECB-mode encryption.
