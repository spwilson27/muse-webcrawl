Electrical conductor

In physics and electrical engineering, a conductor is an object or type of material that allows the flow of electric charge in one or more directions. Materials made of metal are common electrical conductors, because their valence electrons are loosely bound and free to move.

Copper is the standard conductor in most electrical wiring owing to its high conductivity and moderate cost.

Conductivity

The electrical conductivity of a material is measured in siemens per metre. Silver is the best metallic conductor at room temperature, followed by copper and gold. Aluminium, though less conductive by volume, is lighter and cheaper and is widely used for overhead power lines. Conductivity generally decreases as temperature rises in metals.

Resistance and heating

A conductor's resistance depends on its material, length, and cross-sectional area. Current flowing through a resistance dissipates energy as heat (Joule heating), an effect exploited in heating elements and fuses but which causes losses in power transmission.

Other conductors

Conductors need not be solid metals: electrolytes conduct through ion motion, plasmas conduct as ionized gases, and graphite conducts along its planes. Superconductors carry current with zero resistance below a critical temperature. Materials with intermediate behaviour are semiconductors, the basis of modern electronics.
