Volatility (finance)

In finance, volatility (symbol σ) is the degree of variation of a trading price series over time, usually measured by the standard deviation of logarithmic returns. High volatility means the price can change dramatically over a short period in either direction; low volatility means it changes slowly and steadily.

Historical and implied volatility

Historical volatility is computed from past prices over a chosen window. Implied volatility is derived from the market price of an option using a pricing model such as Black–Scholes: it represents the market's expectation of future volatility over the option's life. Implied volatility typically rises in bear markets — the so-called "fear gauge" effect — and falls in calm bull markets.

Volatility indices and trading

The best-known measure is the VIX, calculated by the Chicago Board Options Exchange from S&P 500 index options. Volatility is itself tradable through variance swaps, volatility futures and exchange-traded products. Volatility tends to cluster: large price changes are followed by large changes, a stylised fact captured by ARCH and GARCH models.

Terminology

Volatility is annualised by multiplying the daily standard deviation by the square root of the number of trading days (usually 252). Related concepts include beta, which measures an asset's volatility relative to the market as a whole, and the volatility smile, the pattern of implied volatility across strike prices.
