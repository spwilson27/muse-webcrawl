Thrust-to-weight ratio

The thrust-to-weight ratio (T/W) is a dimensionless ratio of thrust to weight of a rocket, jet engine, propeller engine, or a vehicle propelled by such an engine. It is an indicator of the performance of the engine or vehicle: the instantaneous thrust-to-weight ratio of a vehicle varies continuously during operation because of progressive consumption of fuel or propellant and, in some cases, a gravity gradient. A thrust-to-weight ratio greater than 1 indicates the vehicle can accelerate vertically without relying on aerodynamic lift.

Calculation

The ratio is computed by dividing the thrust (in newtons or pounds-force) by the weight of the engine or vehicle. For aircraft, the quoted figure is usually the maximum static thrust at sea level divided by the maximum take-off weight, though values at combat weight are often more meaningful for fighters.

Examples

Rocket vehicles typically lift off with a thrust-to-weight ratio between about 1.2 and 1.5; the Saturn V left the pad at roughly 1.2. Modern fighter aircraft such as the F-15 and F-22 exceed 1.0 at combat weight, permitting sustained vertical climb and high acceleration. Most airliners have ratios around 0.3, adequate for horizontal flight with lift from the wings. Jet engines themselves have thrust-to-weight ratios ranging from about 2 for early engines to more than 9 for modern fighter engines such as the F119.

Performance implications

For aircraft, thrust-to-weight ratio strongly affects climb rate, acceleration, maximum speed and manoeuvrability. For rockets, the initial ratio determines launch acceleration and gravity losses: a ratio only slightly above 1 wastes propellant fighting gravity, while a very high ratio increases structural loads and aerodynamic drag losses.
