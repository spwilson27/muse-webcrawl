Mechanical ventilation
[Image: A modern intensive-care ventilator]
SpecialtyIntensive care medicine, pulmonology, anaesthesiology
UsesRespiratory failure, anaesthesia, acute respiratory distress syndrome

Mechanical ventilation, or assisted ventilation, is the medical term for using a machine called a ventilator to fully or partially provide artificial ventilation. Mechanical ventilation helps move air into and out of the lungs, with the main goal of helping the delivery of oxygen and removal of carbon dioxide. It is used when a person is unable to breathe adequately on their own, for example in respiratory failure, during general anaesthesia, or in severe brain injury.

Ventilation may be invasive, delivered through an endotracheal tube or tracheostomy, or non-invasive, delivered through a face or nasal mask (such as CPAP or BiPAP). Modern ventilators offer many modes — volume control, pressure control, pressure support, and synchronised intermittent mandatory ventilation among them — tailored to the patient's condition.

Indications and risks

Common indications include acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), pneumonia, sepsis, exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and protection of the airway in coma. Risks include ventilator-associated pneumonia, barotrauma and ventilator-induced lung injury; lung-protective strategies using low tidal volumes reduce mortality in ARDS. Patients are weaned from the ventilator as their underlying condition improves.

History

Negative-pressure ventilators such as the iron lung were widely used during the polio epidemics of the mid-20th century. The 1952 Copenhagen polio epidemic spurred the development of positive-pressure ventilation and the modern intensive care unit.
