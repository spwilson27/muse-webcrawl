Camino de Santiago

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Camino de Santiago (Latin: Peregrinatio Compostellana; Galician: Camiño de Santiago), known in English as the Way of St James, is a network of pilgrims' ways leading to the shrine of the apostle James in the cathedral of Santiago de Compostela in Galicia, northwestern Spain, where tradition holds that the remains of the saint are buried.

History

The pilgrimage began in the 9th century after the reported discovery of the tomb of St James the Great. By the 12th century the Camino was one of the three great pilgrimages of Christendom, alongside Rome and Jerusalem, guided by the Codex Calixtinus, a manuscript that included descriptions of the route, advice to travellers, and accounts of miracles. Pilgrims sought indulgences, healing, or fulfilment of vows, and a dense infrastructure of monasteries, hospitals, and bridges grew up along the ways.

Routes

The most popular route is the Camino Francés (French Way), some 780 kilometres from Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port in France across the Pyrenees through Pamplona, Logroño, Burgos, and León to Santiago. Other routes include the Camino Portugués from Lisbon or Porto, the Camino del Norte along the Cantabrian coast, the Vía de la Plata from Seville, and the Camino Primitivo from Oviedo. Pilgrims traditionally carry a credencial (pilgrim's passport) stamped along the way; those walking at least the final 100 kilometres (or cycling 200) receive the Compostela certificate on arrival.

Modern revival

Numbers dwindled after the Reformation but revived strongly in the late 20th century. The routes were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site (the Routes of Santiago de Compostela: Camino Francés and Routes of Northern Spain) and designated the Council of Europe's first European Cultural Route in 1987. In recent years nearly half a million pilgrims annually have received the Compostela, drawn by religious devotion, culture, walking, and personal reflection.
