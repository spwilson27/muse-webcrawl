Refractive index

In optics, the refractive index (or index of refraction) of a material is a dimensionless number that describes how fast light travels through the material relative to vacuum. It is defined as:

n = c / v

where c is the speed of light in vacuum and v is the phase velocity of light in the medium. Typical values at visible wavelengths are about 1.0003 for air, 1.333 for water, 1.5 for crown glass, and 2.42 for diamond.

Refraction at an interface: the ray bends toward the normal entering the denser medium.

Refraction and Snell's law

The refractive index determines the bending of light at an interface through Snell's law, n₁ sinθ₁ = n₂ sinθ₂, and the critical angle for total internal reflection. Dispersion — the variation of n with wavelength — is responsible for rainbows, the splitting of light by prisms, and chromatic aberration in lenses; it is often summarised by a material's Abbe number.

Microscopic explanation

At the microscopic level, photons are absorbed and re-emitted by atoms, delaying their passage through the medium; the collective effect appears as a reduced phase velocity. The index is related to the material's relative permittivity and permeability (n = √(εrμr) for non-absorbing media). For absorbing materials the refractive index is complex, with an imaginary part describing attenuation.

Applications

Refractive indices are measured with refractometers and used to identify substances and measure concentrations (for example, sugar content in Brix measurements). The concept underlies lenses and optical fibres, immersion microscopy, gemmology, and gradient-index optics. Exotic materials can have indices below 1 (for X-rays), near zero, or — in engineered metamaterials — negative.
