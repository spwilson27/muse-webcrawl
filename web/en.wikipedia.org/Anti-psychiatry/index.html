Anti-psychiatry

Anti-psychiatry, sometimes spelled antipsychiatry, is a broad movement based on the view that psychiatric treatment can be more damaging than helpful to patients. The movement emerged in the 1960s and challenged the fundamental practices and concepts of psychiatry, including the validity of psychiatric diagnoses, the efficacy and safety of psychiatric medications, and the use of involuntary commitment.

History

The term was coined by psychiatrist David Cooper in 1967. Influential figures included R. D. Laing, whose book The Divided Self (1960) reframed schizophrenia as a meaningful response to impossible family situations; Thomas Szasz, whose The Myth of Mental Illness (1961) argued that mental illness is a metaphor rather than a medical condition; Michel Foucault, who traced the historical confinement of the mad; and Erving Goffman, who analyzed asylums as total institutions.

Criticisms of psychiatry

Anti-psychiatry critics questioned the reliability of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), citing David Rosenhan's 1973 experiment "On Being Sane in Insane Places". They also opposed electroconvulsive therapy, psychosurgery, and the long-term effects of antipsychotic drugs, and campaigned against forced treatment.

Contemporary developments

The movement contributed to deinstitutionalization and to reforms in mental health law. Modern successors include the psychiatric survivor movement and critical psychiatry, while mainstream psychiatry maintains that its treatments are evidence-based and that the movement's claims are overstated.
