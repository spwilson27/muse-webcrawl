Henry Ireton (3 November 1611 – 26 November 1651) was an English general in the Parliamentary army during the English Civil War and the son-in-law of Oliver Cromwell. A leading political thinker of the New Model Army, he played a central part in the Putney Debates and in the events leading to the trial and execution of Charles I.

Early life and Civil War

Ireton was educated at Trinity College, Oxford, and studied law at the Middle Temple. On the outbreak of war in 1642 he joined the Parliamentarian forces, fighting at Edgehill and later in the campaigns of the Eastern Association. Serving closely with Cromwell, he was wounded and briefly captured before the Battle of Naseby in 1645, but escaped to take part in the battle itself. He was instrumental in the storming of Bristol later that year.

Political role

In 1646 Ireton married Bridget Cromwell, Oliver Cromwell's daughter, cementing the alliance between the two men. As the army's Commissary-General he drafted many of its political statements, including the Heads of the Proposals of 1647, which offered a constitutional settlement with the king. At the Putney Debates he argued against the Levellers' more radical demands for near-universal male suffrage, insisting that voting rights be tied to a property qualification. When negotiations with Charles broke down, Ireton became a leading advocate of bringing the king to trial; he was a signatory of Charles's death warrant in January 1649.

Ireland and death

Ireton accompanied Cromwell to Ireland in 1649 and remained as Lord Deputy when Cromwell returned to England. He conducted the sieges of Waterford and Limerick with characteristic severity and method. He died of plague (or fever) shortly after the fall of Limerick in November 1651 and was buried in Westminster Abbey with great ceremony. After the Restoration, his body was exhumed and posthumously hanged at Tyburn in January 1661 as a regicide.
