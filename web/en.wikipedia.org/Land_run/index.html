Land run

A land run or land rush was an event in which previously restricted land was opened to homestead settlement in the United States. Land was distributed on a first-come, first-served basis, with participants racing — on foot, horseback, by wagon or train — to stake claims at a designated signal.

The most famous land runs took place in Oklahoma Territory between 1889 and 1895, opening lands that had been assigned to Native American nations under treaties and later unassigned or purchased by the federal government.

The 1889 run

The first and most iconic run, on 22 April 1889, opened the "Unassigned Lands" in central Oklahoma. At noon, an estimated 50,000 people rushed in to claim some 2 million acres; tent cities such as Oklahoma City and Guthrie sprang up in a single day. Those who sneaked in early to claim prime lots were dubbed "Sooners", a name that became Oklahoma's nickname.

Later runs

Subsequent Oklahoma runs included the Sac and Fox and other openings in 1891, the Cheyenne–Arapaho lands in 1892, and the Cherokee Outlet in 1893 — the largest, drawing perhaps 100,000 participants for about 6.5 million acres. The final run opened the Kickapoo lands in 1895. After that, most remaining land was distributed by lottery or sealed bidding, which administrators judged fairer.

Legacy

The land runs are commemorated in Oklahoma by monuments, re-enactments and the Land Run Monument in Oklahoma City. They remain controversial for the Native American dispossession on which the openings were premised.
