The Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paradox (EPR paradox) is a thought experiment proposed by physicists Albert Einstein, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen in 1935 which argues that the description of physical reality provided by quantum mechanics is incomplete. In their paper "Can Quantum-Mechanical Description of Physical Reality Be Considered Complete?", they considered a pair of particles prepared in an entangled state: measuring one particle appears to determine the state of the other instantaneously, however far apart they are.

Einstein called this "spooky action at a distance" (spukhafte Fernwirkung). EPR argued that either quantum mechanics is incomplete — there must be additional "hidden variables" determining the outcomes — or physical reality is not local. Niels Bohr replied that the argument rested on an unjustified assumption about reality independent of measurement.

Bell's theorem and experiment

In 1964 John Stewart Bell proved that no local hidden-variable theory can reproduce all the predictions of quantum mechanics: correlations between distant measurements must violate certain inequalities (Bell inequalities) if local realism holds. Experiments beginning with Alain Aspect's in the early 1980s, and "loophole-free" tests in 2015, consistently violated the inequalities, confirming quantum mechanics and ruling out local hidden variables — although non-local hidden-variable theories such as Bohmian mechanics remain logically possible.

Significance

The EPR paradox turned a philosophical dispute into an experimental question and made entanglement, once considered a defect of the theory, into a resource now exploited in quantum cryptography, teleportation and computing.
