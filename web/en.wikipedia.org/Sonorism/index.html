Sonorism


Krzysztof Penderecki, the best-known exponent of sonorism

Sonorism (Polish: sonoryzm) is an approach to musical composition associated with a number of Polish composers in the 1960s, notably Krzysztof Penderecki, Henryk Górecki, and Wojciech Kilar. The term was introduced by the musicologist Józef Michał Chomiński in 1956, derived from his concept of sonoristics, the study of sound structures. In sonorist music, sound itself — timbre, texture, dynamics, and mass — becomes the primary structural material, subordinating traditional melody, harmony, and rhythm.

Characteristics

Sonorist works make extensive use of tone clusters, glissandi, quarter tones, and unconventional instrumental techniques such as bowing behind the bridge, striking the body of the instrument, or playing on the tailpiece. Notation often becomes graphic, specifying sound masses and durations rather than exact pitches. Instruments are frequently treated collectively in blocks, with large orchestral forces divided into many independent parts.

Key works

Penderecki's Threnody to the Victims of Hiroshima (1960), written for 52 string instruments, and his Polymorphia (1961) are emblematic sonorist compositions. Other important works include Górecki's Scontri and Kazimierz Serocki's compositions, as well as pieces by Grażyna Bacewicz from her later period. The movement was showcased at the Warsaw Autumn festival and represented a distinctly Polish contribution to the post-war avant-garde.

Legacy

Although many sonorist composers, including Penderecki and Górecki, moved toward more traditional idioms in the 1970s, sonorism's emphasis on timbre and texture influenced spectralism and later sound-mass composition.
