Critical theory

Critical theory originated with the Frankfurt School in the 1930s.

Critical theory is a social philosophy pertaining to the reflective assessment, critique, and transformation of society and culture. Rooted in the ideas of Karl Marx and Sigmund Freud, critical theory argues that social problems stem more from social structures and cultural assumptions than from individuals alone, and that ideology is a principal obstacle to human emancipation. In its narrower sense, the term designates the work of the Frankfurt School — the Institute for Social Research founded in Frankfurt in 1923.

Frankfurt School

Max Horkheimer's 1937 essay "Traditional and Critical Theory" set the programme: theory should be interdisciplinary, historically situated and oriented toward emancipation rather than mere description. With Theodor W. Adorno he wrote Dialectic of Enlightenment (1944), arguing that Enlightenment rationality had mutated into domination and introducing the concept of the "culture industry" — mass-produced culture that pacifies consumers. Herbert Marcuse's One-Dimensional Man (1964) extended this critique to advanced industrial society and influenced the New Left.

Later developments

Jürgen Habermas, the leading second-generation figure, recast critical theory around communication, arguing in The Theory of Communicative Action (1981) that undistorted communication offers a normative basis for critique, and defending the unfinished project of modernity against postmodern critics. Axel Honneth subsequently developed a theory of recognition as the school's third generation.

Broad usage and criticism

In the humanities and social sciences, "critical theory" has broadened to encompass approaches — including post-structuralism, feminist theory, critical race theory and postcolonial theory — that interrogate power, language and identity. Critics charge the tradition with obscurantism, political pessimism, and a tendency to totalise critique while offering few institutional proposals; defenders reply that exposing domination is itself a contribution to human freedom.
