Conformational isomerism

In chemistry, conformational isomerism is a form of stereoisomerism in which the isomers can be interconverted just by rotations about formally single bonds (refer to figure on single bond rotation). While any two arrangements of atoms in a molecule that differ by rotation about single bonds can be referred to as different conformations, conformations that correspond to local minima on the potential energy surface are specifically called conformational isomers or conformers. Conformations which correspond to local maxima on the energy surface are the transition states between conformers.

Types

Rotations about single bonds yield conformers commonly described with Newman projections. In ethane, the staggered conformation is about 12.5 kJ/mol lower in energy than the eclipsed conformation because of torsional strain. In butane, anti and gauche conformers differ by steric interactions between methyl groups. In cyclohexane, ring flipping interconverts axial and equatorial positions; the chair conformation is normally most stable.

Energy differences

Energy differences between conformers are usually small, so conformers interconvert rapidly at room temperature and are generally not separable. If rotation is restricted — for example in sterically hindered biaryls — the conformers may be stable and separable, a case called atropisomerism.

Populations

The relative populations of conformers follow the Boltzmann distribution according to their free energies. Spectroscopic methods such as NMR spectroscopy can determine conformer populations and exchange rates.
