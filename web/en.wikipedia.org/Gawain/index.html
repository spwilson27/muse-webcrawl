Sir Gawain is a character in Arthurian legend, in which he is a knight of King Arthur's Round Table. He is one of the most prominent and oldest figures of the cycle, derived from the Welsh hero Gwalchmei, and appears in Geoffrey of Monmouth, Chrétien de Troyes, and many later romances. In most versions he is Arthur's nephew, the son of King Lot and Arthur's sister Morgause (or Anna).

Character

Gawain is renowned for his courtesy, compassion, and loyalty, and in early tradition his strength waxes until noon and wanes thereafter. In French prose romances his character is gradually darkened, contrasting him unfavourably with Lancelot and the spiritually pure Galahad, while English tradition — notably in Sir Gawain and the Green Knight — preserves him as a paragon of chivalry tested by temptation and the exchange-of-winnings bargain at Hautdesert.

In literature

Major works centred on Gawain include Sir Gawain and the Green Knight (late 14th century), Chrétien's Perceval and Yvain, Wolfram von Eschenbach's Parzival, and Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, in which his feud with Lancelot after the deaths of his brothers helps bring about the fall of the Round Table.
