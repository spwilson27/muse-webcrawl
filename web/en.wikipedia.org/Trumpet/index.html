Trumpet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The trumpet is a brass instrument commonly used in classical and jazz ensembles. The trumpet group ranges from the piccolo trumpet, with the highest register, to the bass trumpet, and the standard instrument is the B♭ trumpet. A trumpet is played by blowing air through nearly closed lips into a cup-shaped mouthpiece, producing a vibration that resonates in the instrument's cylindrical tubing; pitch is changed with three piston (or rotary) valves and by altering lip tension.

History

Trumpet-like instruments date to ancient Egypt — bronze and silver trumpets were buried with Tutankhamun around 1323 BC — and were used for signalling in warfare and ceremony across many cultures. The natural trumpet, lacking valves, flourished in Baroque music in the hands of players skilled in the high clarino register. Valves invented in the early 19th century made the trumpet fully chromatic, and it became a staple of orchestras, military bands, big bands, and jazz, where soloists such as Louis Armstrong and Miles Davis defined its modern voice.

Construction and range

A typical B♭ trumpet has about 1.48 metres of tubing coiled into a compact form, ending in a bell of roughly 12 cm diameter. Its written range extends from F♯ below middle C upward for about two and a half octaves, though advanced players go much higher. Variants include trumpets in C, D, and E♭, the flugelhorn with its darker tone, and the cornet, its mellower cousin.
