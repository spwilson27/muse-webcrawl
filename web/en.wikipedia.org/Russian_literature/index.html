Russian literature

Russian literature refers to the literature of Russia and its émigrés, and to Russian-language literature generally. Its roots lie in the written culture of Kievan Rus', but its most celebrated achievement is the 19th-century "Golden Age", when a sequence of poets and novelists — Pushkin, Gogol, Turgenev, Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, and Chekhov — created works that profoundly influenced the world novel, drama, and short story.

Alexander Pushkin, Leo Tolstoy and Fyodor Dostoevsky, central figures of the Golden Age.

Early literature

Medieval East Slavic writing was dominated by religious texts, chronicles, and epics such as The Tale of Igor's Campaign (12th century). Secular literature developed in the 18th century with the classicism of Lomonosov and Derzhavin and the sentimentalism of Karamzin, who modernised Russian prose style.

The Golden Age

Alexander Pushkin (1799–1837) is considered the founder of modern Russian literature, forging a literary language in works such as the verse novel Eugene Onegin. His contemporaries included Lermontov and Gogol, whose Dead Souls and short stories combined satire with the fantastic. The great realist novels followed: Turgenev's Fathers and Sons, Goncharov's Oblomov, Dostoevsky's Crime and Punishment and The Brothers Karamazov, and Tolstoy's War and Peace and Anna Karenina. Anton Chekhov transformed both the short story and modern drama.

Silver Age and Soviet era

The early 20th century "Silver Age" brought Symbolists such as Blok and Bely, Acmeists such as Akhmatova and Mandelstam, and Futurists such as Mayakovsky. After the Revolution, literature split between an official Soviet culture — eventually codified as socialist realism, with figures like Gorky and Sholokhov — and suppressed or émigré writing by Bulgakov, Nabokov, Pasternak, and Solzhenitsyn, whose works exposed the Gulag. Five Russian-language writers have won the Nobel Prize in Literature: Bunin, Pasternak, Sholokhov, Solzhenitsyn, and Brodsky.
