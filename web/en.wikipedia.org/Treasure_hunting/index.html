Treasure hunting

Treasure hunting is the physical search for treasure — hidden valuables such as gold, silver, jewels, or artifacts — typically motivated by profit or adventure. It ranges from hobbyist metal detecting and magnet fishing to professional marine salvage of shipwrecks and the search for legendary caches such as the lost treasure of the Flor de la Mar or Captain Kidd's gold. Modern treasure hunting frequently collides with archaeology, heritage law, and property rights.

History

Pirates' buried treasure is largely a literary invention popularized by works such as Robert Louis Stevenson's Treasure Island, though genuine hoards — from Viking silver to coin deposits buried in wartime — are regularly found. In England and Wales, the Treasure Act 1996 and the Portable Antiquities Scheme govern finds; notable discoveries include the Staffordshire Hoard of Anglo-Saxon gold found by a metal detectorist in 2009. Marine salvage of Spanish galleons, such as Mel Fisher's recovery of the Nuestra Señora de Atocha in 1985, has produced long legal battles over ownership.

Legal and ethical issues

Unauthorized excavation can destroy archaeological context, and many countries claim state ownership of antiquities. The UNESCO Convention of 1970 and the 2001 Convention on the Protection of the Underwater Cultural Heritage frame the international debate between salvors and archaeologists.
