Tanya

The Tanya (Hebrew: תניא), also known by its formal title Likutei Amarim ("Collected Sayings"), is the foundational work of Chabad Hasidism, a movement within Hasidic Judaism. Written by Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi, the founder of Chabad, it was first published in 1797 in Slavita. The Tanya combines elements of Jewish mysticism, Kabbalah, and practical ethical guidance, and it serves as the central text studied daily by members of the Chabad-Lubavitch movement.

Structure

The Tanya is divided into five parts. The first and longest, Sefer shel Beinonim ("The Book of the Intermediates"), presents Shneur Zalman's system of Jewish psychology and ethics, centred on the figure of the beinoni, the "intermediate" person who struggles with inner conflict but never sins in deed, speech, or thought. Subsequent sections include Sha'ar HaYichud VehaEmunah ("The Gate of Unity and Faith"), on the unity of God; Iggeret HaTeshuvah ("Epistle on Repentance"); Iggeret HaKodesh ("Holy Epistle"), a collection of letters; and Kuntres Acharon ("Later Notes").

Teachings

Central to the Tanya is the doctrine of two souls: the divine soul, a literal "portion of God," and the animal soul, the seat of natural inclinations and desires. The struggle between them defines human spiritual life. Unlike earlier Hasidic works that focused on the tzaddik (righteous person), the Tanya holds up the beinoni as an attainable ideal for ordinary people, teaching that constant mastery of one's actions — even without mastery of one's feelings — constitutes true service of God.

Influence

The Tanya is one of the most widely printed works of Jewish thought; a bibliographic project has documented thousands of editions. Chabad emissaries famously print copies of the Tanya in cities around the world, following a directive of the sixth Lubavitcher Rebbe, Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn, to "conquer" places spiritually by printing the text there. A daily study cycle, Chitas, includes a portion of Tanya each day.
