Dejima (Japanese: 出島, "exit island") was a fan-shaped artificial island built in the bay of Nagasaki in 1634–1636. For over two centuries it was Japan's only point of direct contact and trade with the outside world during the Edo period's policy of national seclusion (sakoku), first housing Portuguese traders and then, from 1641 to 1859, the Dutch East India Company factory.

History

Dejima was originally built to segregate Portuguese Catholic traders from Japanese society and prevent the spread of Christianity. After the expulsion of the Portuguese in 1639, the Dutch — who had traded from Hirado — were ordered to move their factory to Dejima in 1641. The Dutch, willing to forgo missionary activity, thus became Japan's sole European trading partner.

Life and trade on the island

The island measured roughly 120 by 75 metres and was connected to the city by a single guarded stone bridge. Dutch staff (usually under twenty men, headed by the opperhoofd) were not permitted to leave freely, and Japanese access was restricted to officials, interpreters, merchants, and courtesans. Trade centred on Chinese and Dutch imports — raw silk, sugar, books, scientific instruments — in exchange for Japanese silver, copper, camphor, and lacquerware. The chief factor made an annual court journey (hofreis) to Edo to pay respects to the shōgun.

Rangaku

Through Dejima flowed Western learning, known in Japan as rangaku ("Dutch learning"). Interpreters and scholars studied Dutch books on medicine, astronomy, geography, and military science. Figures such as Philipp Franz von Siebold, resident physician in the 1820s, used Dejima as a base for research on Japan while teaching Western medicine to Japanese students.

End and restoration

After Japan's opening in the 1850s and the 1858 treaties, Dejima lost its function and was absorbed into Nagasaki as reclaimed land surrounded the island. In the modern era the city of Nagasaki has restored many of the island's buildings and its fan shape is again recognizable; it is a designated National Historic Site.
