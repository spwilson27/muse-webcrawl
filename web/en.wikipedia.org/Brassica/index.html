Brassica is a genus of plants in the cabbage and mustard family (Brassicaceae). The members of the genus are informally known as cruciferous vegetables, cabbages, or mustard plants. Crops from this genus are sometimes called cole crops — derived from the Latin caulis, meaning stem or cabbage. The genus contains many of the world's most important vegetable and oilseed crops.

Domestication and the Triangle of U

The species Brassica oleracea alone has been bred into an extraordinary range of vegetables: cabbage, kale, collard greens, broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, kohlrabi, and many more, selected for leaves, stems, buds, or flowers. Other major species include B. rapa (turnip, bok choy, and oilseed rape), B. napus (rapeseed/canola and swede), and B. juncea and B. nigra (mustards). The "Triangle of U" model, proposed by the Korean-Japanese botanist Woo Jang-choon in 1935, describes how three ancestral diploid species (B. rapa, B. nigra, B. oleracea) hybridized to produce three allotetraploid species (B. napus, B. juncea, B. carinata).

Economics and nutrition

Brassica crops are grown worldwide; rapeseed (canola) is one of the world's principal vegetable oil sources, and mustard seeds are a major spice. The vegetables are rich in vitamins C and K, folate, and fibre, and contain glucosinolates — sulphur compounds responsible for their pungent flavour, some of which (such as sulforaphane) are studied for possible health effects. Almost all parts of many species are edible, though the roots of some are too fibrous or the seeds dangerously toxic in quantity.
