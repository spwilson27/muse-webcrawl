Utopia is a work of fiction and political philosophy by Sir Thomas More, published in Latin in 1516. Its full title translates as "A Truly Golden Little Book, No Less Beneficial Than Entertaining, of the Best State of a Commonwealth and the New Island Utopia". The book coined the word utopia, from Greek ou-topos, "no place".

Structure and content

Book I is a dialogue criticising the corruption and injustice of contemporary Europe, including enclosure and harsh punishments for theft. Book II describes the island of Utopia through the traveller Raphael Hythloday: property is held in common, citizens work six hours a day, goods are distributed from communal storehouses, there is no money, and religious toleration prevails — though slavery and patriarchal households also feature.

Interpretation

Scholars debate whether More intended Utopia as a blueprint, a satire, or an ironic mirror for European society. Ambiguity is built into the names: Hythloday roughly means "speaker of nonsense", and the island's river is called Anydrus, "no water".

Legacy

The book founded the utopian genre, influencing later works from Campanella's City of the Sun to modern utopian and dystopian fiction, and remains a central text in Renaissance humanism and political theory.
