Augustine of Hippo

Augustine of Hippo (13 November 354 – 28 August 430), also known as Saint Augustine, was a theologian and philosopher of Berber origin and the bishop of Hippo Regius in Roman North Africa. His writings influenced the development of Western philosophy and Western Christianity, and he is viewed as one of the most important Church Fathers of the Latin Church. His many important works include The City of God, On Christian Doctrine, and the Confessions.

Augustine, bishop of Hippo and Doctor of the Church.

Life

Born in Thagaste (in modern Algeria) to a Christian mother, Monica, Augustine was educated in rhetoric at Carthage and pursued a life of pleasure before a long intellectual journey through Manichaeism and Neoplatonism. In Milan in 386, under the influence of Bishop Ambrose and readings of Plato and Paul, he converted to Christianity; his conversion scene in a garden, prompted by a child's chant of "take up and read", is recounted in the Confessions. He was ordained priest at Hippo in 391 and became its bishop in 395, serving until his death during the Vandal siege of the city in 430.

Thought

Augustine developed doctrines of original sin, divine grace and predestination that shaped Western theology, and he contested the Manichaeans, Donatists and Pelagians. The City of God, written after the sack of Rome in 410, contrasted the earthly city with the City of God and offered a philosophy of history. His thinking influenced medieval scholasticism, the Reformers, and modern philosophy; his reflections on time, memory and the self anticipate later phenomenology.
