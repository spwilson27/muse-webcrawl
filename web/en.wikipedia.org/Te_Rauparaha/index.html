Te Rauparaha

Te Rauparaha (c. 1768 – 27 November 1849) was a Māori rangatira (chief) of the Ngāti Toa iwi (tribe) and one of the most formidable military leaders of the Musket Wars era in New Zealand. From his base on Kāpiti Island he built a sphere of influence across the Cook Strait region, conquered and redistributed large territories in the lower North Island and northern South Island, and engaged — sometimes cooperatively, sometimes in conflict — with European traders, whalers, missionaries, and the New Zealand Company settlers. He is also credited with composing the haka "Ka Mate".

The great heke

Pressed by musket-armed Waikato and Maniapoto rivals in the 1820s, Te Rauparaha led Ngāti Toa on the great migration (heke) south from Kawhia to the Cook Strait. From fortified Kāpiti Island he dominated trade in flax and provisions with Sydney merchants and whalers — exchanging goods for muskets — and launched campaigns into the South Island, including the 1830 Elizabeth incident, in which a brig's captain helped him capture the Ngāi Tahu chief Tamaiharanui. The Wairau Affray of 1843 — the first armed clash between Māori and British settlers over land — arose when New Zealand Company settlers attempted to arrest him over the disputed Wairau purchase; he was exonerated by Governor FitzRoy's inquiry.

Arrest and death

In 1846 Governor George Grey, moving to assert control, arrested Te Rauparaha without charge at Taupo pā near Porirua and held him for ten months aboard HMS Calliope and in Auckland, an act many regard as an abuse of power. Released in 1848, he returned to Ōtaki, where he died the following year. The Anglican church Rangiātea in Ōtaki, whose construction he had supported, stands as part of his legacy.

"Ka Mate"

The haka "Ka Mate" ("'Tis death!") is attributed to Te Rauparaha, composed around 1820 as he hid from pursuers in a food-storage pit and emerged to find a friendly chief rather than his enemy. It was popularised worldwide by the All Blacks rugby team, and legislation in 2014 formally recognised Ngāti Toa's association with the haka.
