Henry David Thoreau (July 12, 1817 – May 6, 1862) was an American naturalist, essayist, poet, and philosopher. A leading transcendentalist, he is best known for his book Walden (1854), a reflection upon simple living in natural surroundings, and his essay "Resistance to Civil Government" (1849, later titled "Civil Disobedience"), an argument in favour of disobedience to an unjust state.

Life

Thoreau was born in Concord, Massachusetts, and graduated from Harvard College in 1837. He worked in his family's pencil factory and as a schoolteacher and surveyor. Through his friendship with Ralph Waldo Emerson, he became part of the transcendentalist circle that included Margaret Fuller and Bronson Alcott. In 1845 he built a small cabin on land owned by Emerson at Walden Pond and lived there for two years, two months, and two days, an experiment in deliberate living that produced Walden.

In July 1846 Thoreau spent a night in jail for refusing to pay the poll tax, in protest against slavery and the Mexican–American War. The experience informed his essay on civil disobedience, which later influenced leaders such as Mahatma Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr.

Writing and natural history

Thoreau's books, articles, essays, journals, and poetry amount to more than 20 volumes. His first book, A Week on the Concord and Merrimack Rivers (1849), was a commercial failure. In his later years he devoted himself increasingly to natural history: his detailed phenological records of flowering times and seed dispersal in Concord are now used by scientists studying climate change. He was an early advocate for the preservation of wilderness and supported the abolition of slavery; his 1854 speech "Slavery in Massachusetts" and his defence of John Brown in "A Plea for Captain John Brown" (1859) were widely noted.

Death and legacy

Thoreau died of tuberculosis on May 6, 1862, aged 44. Largely overlooked in his lifetime, he is now regarded as one of the most important American writers. Walden is a classic of American literature, and "Civil Disobedience" remains a foundational text on nonviolent resistance.
