Ethnocentrism

Ethnocentric depictions contrast one's own group favourably with outsiders

Ethnocentrism in social science and anthropology is the tendency to view one's own culture, ethnic group, or nation as central and superior, and to judge other cultures by its standards. The term was coined by the American sociologist William Graham Sumner in his book Folkways (1906), where he defined it as the view "in which one's own group is the center of everything, and all others are scaled and rated with reference to it."

Concepts

Sumner linked ethnocentrism with in-group loyalty and out-group hostility, connecting it to patriotism and chauvinism. In anthropology, ethnocentrism is contrasted with cultural relativism — the methodological principle, associated with Franz Boas and his students, that beliefs and practices should be understood within their own cultural context. Consumer ethnocentrism, adapted from Sumner's concept in marketing research, describes consumers' beliefs about the appropriateness of buying foreign-made products.

Effects and study

Ethnocentrism is associated with prejudice, stereotyping, and intergroup conflict, and social identity theory holds that even arbitrary group distinctions can trigger in-group favouritism. Researchers distinguish benign attachment to one's own group from hostile ethnocentrism directed at out-groups, the latter correlating with authoritarianism and support for aggressive foreign policies in measures such as generalised ethnocentrism scales. In history and historiography, Eurocentrism — judging the world from a European standpoint — is a prominent special case, criticised for distorting accounts of non-European societies.
