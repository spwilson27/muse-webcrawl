Angelus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Angelus (Latin: Angelus Domini nuntiavit Mariae, "The angel of the Lord declared unto Mary") is a Catholic devotion commemorating the Incarnation, traditionally recited three times a day — at dawn, noon and dusk — at the ringing of a church bell known as the Angelus bell. The devotion consists of three antiphons, each followed by a Hail Mary, versicles and a concluding prayer.

The prayer

The text alternates versicle and response:

  • The Angel of the Lord declared unto Mary. — And she conceived of the Holy Spirit. (Hail Mary)
  • Behold the handmaid of the Lord. — Be it done unto me according to thy word. (Hail Mary)
  • And the Word was made flesh. — And dwelt among us. (Hail Mary)

It concludes with the collect: "Pour forth, we beseech thee, O Lord, thy grace into our hearts…"

History

The devotion developed during the later Middle Ages out of the monastic custom of reciting three Hail Marys at the evening bell, and was extended to morning and midday over the following centuries; popes including John XXII granted indulgences for its recitation. During the fifty days of Eastertide the Angelus is replaced by the Regina caeli. The Pope traditionally leads the Angelus from the window of the Apostolic Palace in St Peter's Square each Sunday at noon.

In art and culture

The evening recitation, halting work in the fields, was immortalised in Jean-François Millet's painting The Angelus (1857–59), showing two peasants bowing over a basket of potatoes as the bell sounds. In Ireland, the Angelus bell is broadcast daily on radio and television at 6 p.m., a longstanding feature of national life that has periodically prompted debate about religion in public media.
