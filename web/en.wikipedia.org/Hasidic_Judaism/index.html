Hasidic Judaism

Hasidic Judaism (Hebrew: חסידות, Hasidut, "piety"; Ashkenazi pronunciation: Chasidus) is a Jewish religious movement that arose as a spiritual revival movement in Eastern Europe in the 18th century and spread rapidly across the region. Founded by Rabbi Israel ben Eliezer, known as the Baal Shem Tov (c. 1700–1760), in the area of present-day Ukraine, Hasidism emphasized the presence of God in everyday life, joyful worship, prayer, and the spiritual leadership of the rebbe (or tzadik).

Today, Hasidic Jews form a significant part of Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) Judaism, with large communities in Israel, the United States (especially Brooklyn, New York), Canada, Belgium, and the United Kingdom. Major Hasidic dynasties include Chabad-Lubavitch, Satmar, Ger, Belz, Bobov, Vizhnitz, and Breslov.

History

Hasidism emerged in Podolia and Volhynia in the aftermath of the Khmelnytsky uprisings and a period of spiritual despondency among East European Jewry. The Baal Shem Tov taught that sincere prayer and devotion (devekut, cleaving to God) were accessible to every Jew, not only scholars. His teachings were systematized by his disciple, the Maggid of Mezeritch, and spread by figures such as Jacob Joseph of Polonnoye and Elimelech of Lizhensk.

The movement met fierce opposition from the Mitnagdim ("opponents"), led by the Vilna Gaon, who issued bans against the Hasidim in the late 18th century. Over time the conflict subsided as both camps united against the Jewish Enlightenment (Haskalah) and assimilation. The Holocaust devastated Hasidic communities in Europe, but survivors rebuilt dynastic courts in Israel and America, where the movement has grown rapidly due to high birth rates.

Beliefs and practices

Central Hasidic concepts include devekut (communion with God), simcha (joy in worship), prayer with fervor and melody (niggunim), storytelling, and the doctrine of the tzadik — the righteous leader of a community who serves as an intermediary between his followers and God. Hasidim typically dress distinctively, often in black coats, fur hats (shtreimels) on Shabbat and holidays, and speak Yiddish in daily life. Men and women marry young, and communal life revolves around the rebbe's court, synagogue (shtiebel), and ritual meals (tish).
