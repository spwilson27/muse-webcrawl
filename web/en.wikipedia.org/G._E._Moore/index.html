George Edward Moore (1873–1958)

George Edward Moore (4 November 1873 – 24 October 1958), usually cited as G. E. Moore, was an English philosopher who was one of the founders of analytic philosophy. Along with Bertrand Russell, Ludwig Wittgenstein, and (in an earlier generation) Gottlob Frege, Moore helped to inspire philosophy's "rebellion" against idealism and its turn toward conceptual analysis and common sense.

Life

Moore was born in Upper Norwood, London, and educated at Dulwich College and Trinity College, Cambridge, where he was elected to the Cambridge Apostles society. He spent most of his academic career at Cambridge, where he was a lecturer and later professor of philosophy, and he edited the journal Mind from 1921 to 1947. He was a central figure in the Bloomsbury Group's social world, and his personal manner — earnest, precise, and famously innocent — made a deep impression on contemporaries such as John Maynard Keynes.

Philosophy

Moore's most influential work is Principia Ethica (1903), in which he argued that the concept of "good" is simple, indefinable, and non-natural. Attempts to define good in terms of any natural property — pleasure, evolution, desire — commit what he called the naturalistic fallacy. He supported this with the "open-question argument": for any proposed definition of good, it remains an open question whether things with that property really are good.

In epistemology, Moore defended common-sense realism against scepticism and idealism. His 1925 essay "A Defence of Common Sense" listed truisms he claimed to know with certainty — that he had a body, that the earth existed long before his birth, and so on — and his 1939 lecture "Proof of an External World" famously held up his two hands as proof that at least two material objects exist ("here is one hand, and here is another").

Moore's method of philosophical analysis, his insistence on clarity, and the "Moore's paradox" problem (the absurdity of believing "p, and I do not believe that p") all remain live topics in philosophy.
