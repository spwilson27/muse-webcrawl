Haitian Vodou

Haitian Vodou (French: Vaudou haïtien) is an African diasporic religion that developed in Haiti between the 16th and 19th centuries. It arose through a process of syncretism between several traditional religions of West and Central Africa and the Roman Catholic form of Christianity. There is no central authority in control of the religion, and much diversity exists among practitioners.

Beliefs and practice

Practitioners, called Vodouizan, believe in a supreme creator god, Bondye, who is remote from human affairs. Devotion focuses on the lwa (also spelled loa), spirits subordinate to Bondye who govern domains of life and embody ancestral and natural forces; well-known lwa include Papa Legba, guardian of the crossroads; Ezili Dantor; Ogou, spirit of iron and war; and Baron Samedi, head of the Gede family of death spirits. Worship takes place in temples (ounfò) led by priests (oungan) or priestesses (manbo) and involves prayer, song, drumming, dance, offerings, and spirit possession, through which the lwa is believed to ride and counsel the possessed devotee.

History

Vodou coalesced among enslaved Africans brought to the French colony of Saint-Domingue, drawing on Fon, Yoruba, and Kongo traditions. According to tradition, the 1791 ceremony at Bois Caïman helped launch the Haitian Revolution, the only successful slave revolt to found a state. After Haitian independence in 1804 the religion survived periods of official hostility, anti-superstition campaigns, and persecution, including under the Duvalier regime's manipulation of its imagery, even as it remained central to Haitian identity and culture.

Terminology

The word derives from the Fon vodún, "spirit" or "deity". The spelling "Vodou" is preferred by practitioners and scholars to distinguish the Haitian religion from the distorted portrayals associated with the word "Voodoo" in popular culture.
