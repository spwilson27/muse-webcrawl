Southern Alps

The Southern Alps (Māori: Kā Tiritiri o te Moana) are a mountain range extending along much of the length of New Zealand's South Island, reaching their greatest elevations near the island's western side. The range includes the South Island's Main Divide, which separates the water catchments of the more heavily populated eastern side of the island from those on the west coast.

Geography

The Southern Alps run approximately 500 kilometres from north-east to south-west. The tallest peak is Aoraki / Mount Cook, at 3,724 metres (12,218 ft); other prominent peaks include Mount Tasman and Mount Dampier. The range contains numerous glaciers, including the Tasman, Fox, and Franz Josef glaciers.

Geology

The range was formed by uplift along the Alpine Fault, where the Pacific Plate and the Indo-Australian Plate collide. Ongoing uplift, of the order of several millimetres per year, is offset by intense erosion from the heavy rainfall on the western side.

Climate

The Alps create a strong rain-shadow effect: the West Coast is among the wettest regions in New Zealand, while the Canterbury Plains to the east are comparatively dry. Prevailing westerly winds also produce the warm foehn wind known locally as the Nor'wester.
