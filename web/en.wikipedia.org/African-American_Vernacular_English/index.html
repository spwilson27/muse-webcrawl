African-American Vernacular English


AAVE is spoken in urban and rural communities across the US

African-American Vernacular English (AAVE) is the variety of English natively spoken, particularly in urban communities, by most working- and middle-class African Americans and some Black Canadians. It has its own pronunciation, vocabulary and grammatical structures, and shares many features with Southern American English. AAVE gained widespread public attention during the 1996 "Ebonics" controversy in Oakland, California.

Features

Distinctive grammatical features include the habitual "be" (marking habitual action, e.g. "He be working"), copula deletion ("He happy"), negative concord, and "stressed BIN" marking a remote past. Phonological features include reduction of final consonant clusters, metathesis in words such as "ask", and th-stopping. Linguists emphasise that AAVE is a systematic, rule-governed variety, not a deviant form of standard English.

Origins

Scholarly debate concerns whether AAVE descends mainly from a creole formed among enslaved Africans (the creole hypothesis) or from the dialects of British and Irish settlers with whom enslaved people interacted (the dialectologist/Anglicist hypothesis), with most researchers recognising influences from both.
