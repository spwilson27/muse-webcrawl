The Year of Africa is a name given to 1960, the year in which seventeen African nations gained independence from European colonial rule. The events of the year marked a decisive acceleration of decolonisation across the continent and transformed the politics of the United Nations, where the new states soon formed a powerful voting bloc.

Background

After the Second World War, independence movements strengthened across Africa, and Ghana's independence under Kwame Nkrumah in 1957 demonstrated that colonial rule could end rapidly. The 1958 All-African Peoples' Conference in Accra called for the liberation of the whole continent. Guinea's abrupt break with France in 1958 further weakened the colonial powers' position.

The seventeen states

In the course of 1960, Cameroon, Togo, Mali, Senegal, Madagascar, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Somalia, Dahomey (Benin), Niger, Upper Volta (Burkina Faso), Ivory Coast, Chad, the Central African Republic, Congo-Brazzaville, Gabon, Nigeria and Mauritania all became independent. Nigeria, the continent's most populous country, achieved independence on 1 October 1960.

International response

In December 1960 the UN General Assembly adopted Resolution 1514, the Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples, declaring that all peoples have the right to self-determination and that colonialism should be brought to a speedy end. Sharpeville in South Africa, where police killed 69 anti-pass-law protesters in March 1960, also focused world attention on apartheid.

Legacy

The Year of Africa is commemorated as the symbolic hinge of African decolonisation. The Organisation of African Unity was founded three years later, in 1963, in part to complete the liberation of the continent and coordinate the new states' foreign policies.
