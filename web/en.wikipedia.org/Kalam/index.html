Kalām (Arabic: علم الكلام, ʿilm al-kalām, literally "science of discourse") is the discipline of Islamic speculative theology that seeks to give rational expression and defence to the doctrines of the Islamic faith. Scholars of kalam, known as mutakallimūn (singular mutakallim), employed dialectical reasoning and philosophical argumentation to establish and defend articles of belief, in particular the oneness of God (tawhid), divine attributes, free will, and the nature of revelation.

Origins and development

Kalām emerged in the eighth century CE, in part through debates with Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians, and sceptics in the cosmopolitan cities of Iraq, and in part through internal Muslim disputes about the status of grave sinners, political legitimacy, and the createdness of the Qur'an. The Mu'tazila, associated with figures such as Wasil ibn Ata, emphasised divine justice and human free will, holding that God's unity precluded eternal attributes distinct from His essence. The traditionalist backlash against Mu'tazilite rationalism, and the attempt by Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. 935) to steer a middle course, produced the Ash'ari school, which became dominant in Sunni Islam alongside the Maturidi school of Transoxiana, named after Abu Mansur al-Maturidi (d. 944).

Method and topics

The mutakallimūn developed sophisticated arguments for the existence and unity of God, including the kalām cosmological argument, which reasons from the temporal origination of the world (and of accidents inhering in substances) to an originating cause. Atomism — the doctrine that bodies are composed of indivisible atoms and accidents continually recreated by God — underpinned much classical kalam physics and its occasionalist account of causation, most famously elaborated by al-Ghazali in The Incoherence of the Philosophers.

Later history

From the twelfth century, kalam increasingly absorbed the logic and metaphysics of Avicenna and the falsafa tradition it had once opposed, producing the "later" (muta'akhkhirūn) kalam of scholars such as Fakhr al-Din al-Razi and Adud al-Din al-Iji. Kalam remained a central discipline of the madrasa curriculum for centuries and experienced revivals in the modern period, when Muslim thinkers sought to answer the challenges of science and Western philosophy.
