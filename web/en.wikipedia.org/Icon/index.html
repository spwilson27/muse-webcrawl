Icon

An icon (from Ancient Greek eikṓn, "image, resemblance") is a religious work of art, most commonly a painting, in the cultures of the Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, and Catholic churches. Icons are not simply artworks; they are sacred objects used as an aid to devotion. They typically depict holy figures or objects such as Jesus Christ, the Virgin Mary, saints, angels, or crosses, and are usually painted in egg tempera on wood panels, though they may also be made of metal, stone, mosaic, embroidery, or other media.

The Theotokos of Vladimir (c. 1130), one of the most venerated Orthodox icons.

Theology and veneration

Orthodox theology holds that icons are "windows to heaven": the honour shown to the image passes to its prototype, as St. Basil the Great taught and the Second Council of Nicaea (787) affirmed. Believers venerate icons by kissing, bowing, burning incense and candles before them, but insist this veneration (proskynesis) is distinct from the worship (latreia) due to God alone. Iconography follows strict canonical conventions — inverse perspective, gold backgrounds signifying divine light, and standardised gestures and inscriptions (such as IC XC for Christ and MP ΘY for the Mother of God) — so that icons "write" doctrine in images, parallel to scripture.

History

The earliest surviving icons, encaustic panels from Saint Catherine's Monastery in Sinai, date to the 6th–7th centuries. The Byzantine Iconoclasm controversy (726–843) saw icons destroyed under emperors Leo III and Constantine V, defended by John of Damascus and Theodore the Studite, and finally restored under Empress Theodora — an event commemorated as the Triumph of Orthodoxy. Icon painting flourished in Byzantium, Rus' (with masters such as Theophanes the Greek and Andrei Rublev, whose Trinity is the most famous Russian icon), the Balkans, and Ethiopia. In the West, medieval and Renaissance religious painting developed along different lines, though Catholic churches also preserve revered icons such as Our Lady of Perpetual Help.

Other uses of the term

By extension, "icon" denotes any image or person regarded as emblematic — a cultural icon — and in computing, a small pictorial symbol representing a program, file or function on a graphical user interface, a usage popularised from the 1970s Xerox PARC work onward and central to systems from the Macintosh to iTunes-era software design. In semiotics, following Charles Sanders Peirce, an icon is a sign that signifies by resemblance, contrasted with index and symbol.
