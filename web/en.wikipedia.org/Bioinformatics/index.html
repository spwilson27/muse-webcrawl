Bioinformatics

FieldInterdisciplinary: biology, computer science, statistics, mathematics
Key toolsBLAST, sequence alignment, genome assembly, phylogenetics
Major databasesGenBank, UniProt, Protein Data Bank
Term coined1970, by Paulien Hogeweg and Ben Hesper

Bioinformatics is an interdisciplinary field of science that develops methods and software tools for understanding biological data, especially when the data sets are large and complex. Bioinformatics uses biology, chemistry, physics, computer science, data science, computer programming, information engineering, mathematics and statistics to analyse and interpret biological data.


Sequence alignment and genome browsing are core bioinformatics workflows.

The field emerged as the volume of DNA and protein sequence data began to outstrip manual analysis. The term was coined by Paulien Hogeweg and Ben Hesper in 1970 to refer to the study of information processes in biotic systems. Modern bioinformatics underpins genomics, drug discovery, evolutionary biology, and personalised medicine.

History

Early milestones include Margaret Dayhoff's Atlas of Protein Sequence and Structure (1965), which compiled protein sequences for computational comparison, and the Needleman–Wunsch algorithm (1970) for global sequence alignment. The founding of GenBank in 1982 and the launch of BLAST in 1990 made sequence searching routine. The Human Genome Project (1990–2003) drove the development of high-throughput sequencing analysis, genome assembly, and large public databases.

Sequence analysis

Sequence alignment compares DNA, RNA, or protein sequences to identify regions of similarity that may indicate functional, structural, or evolutionary relationships. Pairwise methods such as Smith–Waterman and BLAST search databases for homologues, while multiple sequence alignment underlies phylogenetic inference and motif discovery. Hidden Markov models power gene finding and profile searches such as those in Pfam and HMMER.

Genome annotation

After a genome is assembled, annotation identifies genes, regulatory elements, repeats, and other features. Structural annotation predicts the locations of coding sequences; functional annotation assigns putative functions by similarity to characterised genes. Pipelines combine ab initio prediction with transcript and protein evidence.

Structural bioinformatics

Structural bioinformatics predicts and analyses the three-dimensional structures of macromolecules. The Protein Data Bank archives experimentally determined structures, while prediction methods — culminating in deep-learning systems such as AlphaFold, which in 2020–2021 produced accurate models for most known proteins — have transformed the field.

Other applications

  • Gene expression analysis — RNA-Seq and microarrays quantify transcript abundance.
  • Phylogenetics — reconstructs evolutionary trees from molecular data.
  • Systems biology — models interaction networks and metabolic pathways.
  • Drug discovery — virtual screening and molecular docking identify candidate compounds.
  • Metagenomics — analyses DNA sampled directly from environments.

Databases and organisations

Key resources include GenBank and the European Nucleotide Archive for sequences, UniProt for proteins, and Ensembl for genome annotation. The International Society for Computational Biology (ISCB) is the field's main professional society, and journals such as Bioinformatics and BMC Bioinformatics publish its research.
