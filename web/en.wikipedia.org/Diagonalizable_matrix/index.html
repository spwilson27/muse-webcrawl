Diagonalizable matrix

Diagonalization expresses a matrix as PDP−1.

In linear algebra, a square matrix A is called diagonalizable if it is similar to a diagonal matrix: that is, there exists an invertible matrix P and a diagonal matrix D such that A = PDP−1. Equivalently, the linear map represented by A has a basis consisting entirely of eigenvectors, the columns of P, with the corresponding eigenvalues on the diagonal of D.

Characterization

An n × n matrix over a field is diagonalizable if and only if the sum of the dimensions of its eigenspaces equals n — that is, for every eigenvalue, its geometric multiplicity equals its algebraic multiplicity. A sufficient (but not necessary) condition is that the matrix have n distinct eigenvalues. A matrix with a repeated eigenvalue may still be diagonalizable (the identity matrix is already diagonal), or may be defective, like a non-trivial Jordan block, which has too few eigenvectors.

Simultaneous diagonalization

A family of diagonalizable matrices can be simultaneously diagonalized by a single change of basis if and only if the matrices pairwise commute.

Applications

Diagonalization greatly simplifies computing powers of a matrix, since Ak = PDkP−1 and powers of a diagonal matrix are trivial. This is used in solving systems of linear differential equations and recurrence relations, computing matrix exponentials, and analysing Markov chains. By the spectral theorem, every real symmetric matrix (and every complex normal matrix) is diagonalizable by an orthogonal (or unitary) transformation.

Generalizations

Matrices that are not diagonalizable can still be put in Jordan normal form over an algebraically closed field. Over the reals, the Schur decomposition into triangular form serves a similar purpose.
