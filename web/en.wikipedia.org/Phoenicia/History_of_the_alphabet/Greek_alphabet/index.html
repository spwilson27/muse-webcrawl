Greek alphabet

The Greek alphabet has been used to write the Greek language since the late 9th or early 8th century BC. It is derived from the earlier Phoenician alphabet, and was the earliest known alphabetic script to have distinct letters for vowels as well as consonants. In Archaic and early Classical times, the Greek alphabet existed in many local variants, but by the end of the 4th century BC, the Euclidean alphabet, with 24 letters ordered from alpha to omega, had become standard, and it is this version that is still used for Greek writing today.

The uppercase and lowercase forms of the 24 letters are: Α α, Β β, Γ γ, Δ δ, Ε ε, Ζ ζ, Η η, Θ θ, Ι ι, Κ κ, Λ λ, Μ μ, Ν ν, Ξ ξ, Ο ο, Π π, Ρ ρ, Σ σ/ς, Τ τ, Υ υ, Φ φ, Χ χ, Ψ ψ, Ω ω.

History

The alphabet was adapted from the Phoenician consonantal script, probably through contact with Phoenician traders. The Greeks reassigned letters for Semitic consonants they did not use to represent vowels: for example, Phoenician ʾālep became alpha (a), hē became epsilon (e), and ʿayin became omicron (o). Additional letters such as phi, chi, and psi were created for Greek aspirated consonants. Early Greek was written right-to-left or in boustrophedon style before settling on left-to-right.

Uses

Greek letters are widely used as symbols in mathematics, science, and engineering — for example π for the circle constant, Δ for change, Σ for summation, and Ω for the ohm. The letters also name fraternities and sororities, stars in astronomy (Bayer designations), and variants in virology and other fields.

Descendants

The Greek alphabet gave rise, directly or indirectly, to the Latin alphabet (via the Euboean variant used in Italy), the Cyrillic script, the Coptic script, and the Gothic alphabet.
