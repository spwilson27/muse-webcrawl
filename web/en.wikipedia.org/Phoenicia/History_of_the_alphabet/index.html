History of the Alphabet

Nearly all the world's alphabets descend from a single ancestor refined in Phoenicia.

The history of the alphabet traces the development of letter writing from its roots in Egyptian hieroglyphs to the scripts used around the world today. The pivotal step was taken by Semitic-speaking peoples of the Levant, and its most influential form was the Phoenician alphabet, standardized in the cities of Phoenicia — Tyre, Sidon, and Byblos — by about the eleventh century BC.

Proto-Sinaitic origins

Semitic workers in Egyptian mines in Sinai, around 1800 BC, adapted hieroglyphic signs by the acrophonic principle: a picture stood for the first consonant of the Semitic word for the thing depicted — an ox head (alp) for /ʔ/, a house (bet) for /b/. This Proto-Sinaitic script, and its descendant Proto-Canaanite, contained only consonant signs.

The Phoenician standardization

The Phoenicians reduced the script to 22 letters, all consonants, written right to left. Compact, easy to learn, and suited to ink on papyrus, it was ideal for a trading people and spread with Phoenician commerce across the Mediterranean. From it descend, directly or indirectly, the Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic, Greek, Latin, and Cyrillic traditions — the Aramaic branch also gave rise to the Indic scripts, making Phoenicia's 22 signs ancestors of most writing on Earth.

Greek adaptation

The Greeks borrowed the script and, by reassigning unneeded consonant signs to vowels, created the first true alphabet (see Greek alphabet). Latin, through Etruscan intermediaries, inherited this system, and the modern English letter shapes still preserve the Phoenician sequence A, B, C, D.
