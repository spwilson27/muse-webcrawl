The Phoenician alphabet is an abjad — a writing system using only consonantal letters — developed by the Phoenicians of the ancient Levant by around 1050 BC. It is one of the most widely used writing systems in history and the ancestor, directly or indirectly, of nearly all modern alphabets, including Greek, Latin, Hebrew, and Arabic scripts.

Development

The alphabet derived from Proto-Sinaitic (Proto-Canaanite) signs, themselves adapted from Egyptian hieroglyphic models by the acrophonic principle — a sign stood for the first consonant of the word it depicted. The standardized 22-letter Phoenician script spread with Phoenician trade throughout the Mediterranean.

Descendants

The Greeks adopted the script around the 8th century BC, repurposing letters for sounds Greek lacked as vowels — creating the first true alphabet — and passing it on to the Etruscans and Romans. In the east, the Aramaic script that developed from Phoenician gave rise to Hebrew, Syriac, Arabic, and (through Brahmi, in one widely held view) the scripts of India and Southeast Asia.

Letters

Letter names such as ʾālep ("ox"), bēt ("house"), gīml ("camel/throwstick"), and dālet ("door") survive recognizably in Greek alpha, beta, gamma, and delta. The earliest long Phoenician inscription is the sarcophagus of King Ahiram of Byblos (c. 1000 BC).
