Indigo Dye

Indigo produces the deep blue paired, in Phoenician workshops, with Tyrian purple.

Indigo dye is an organic compound with a distinctive blue color, historically obtained from the leaves of plants of the genus Indigofera and from woad (Isatis tinctoria). Along the Phoenician coast, dyers combined plant-derived blues with the famous shellfish purple to produce the full range of prestige textiles for which Phoenicia was renowned; cloth remains from the Levant show indigo among the oldest dyestuffs in use, and blue-dyed textiles were traded alongside purple garments throughout the Mediterranean.

Chemistry and dyeing

Indigo is a vat dye: insoluble in water, it must be reduced in an alkaline fermentation vat to a soluble, yellowish leuco-form. Cloth immersed in the vat emerges yellow-green and turns blue as the dye oxidizes in air. The resulting color is notably lightfast, which made it prized for work clothes in later centuries and for denim in the modern era.

Trade and production

In the ancient world indigo came chiefly from India — the Greek word indikon means "the Indian (dye)" — and traveled west along the same trade routes Phoenician merchants plied for spices and aromatics. Phoenician dyers at Tyre and Sidon worked imported indigo with local mordants and with murex purple (see Natural dye), sometimes over-dyeing one with the other to achieve deep blue-violet shades.

Modern synthesis

Adolf von Baeyer determined indigo's structure in the nineteenth century, and BASF brought synthetic indigo to market in 1897, collapsing the plantation trade. Today nearly all indigo is synthetic, used overwhelmingly for blue jeans.
