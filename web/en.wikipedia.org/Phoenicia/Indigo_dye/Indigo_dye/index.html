Indigo dye

Indigo dye is an organic compound with a distinctive blue colour. It is a well-known dye historically extracted from the leaves of plants of the genus Indigofera, in particular Indigofera tinctoria, as well as from woad (Isatis tinctoria). Indigo is a vat dye: it is insoluble in water and must be reduced to a soluble leuco form before it can penetrate fibres, re-oxidising to blue in air.

History

Indigo has been used for millennia; dyed fabrics survive from ancient Egypt and Peru. It became a valuable trade commodity in the ancient and medieval worlds and later a major plantation crop in the Americas and India, implicated in exploitative colonial labour systems that provoked resistance such as the Bengal indigo revolt of 1859.

Modern use

Adolf von Baeyer determined indigo's chemical structure in the 19th century, and BASF brought a synthetic production process to market in 1897, collapsing the natural indigo trade. Today synthetic indigo is produced in large quantities, most famously to dye the blue cotton yarn of denim jeans.
