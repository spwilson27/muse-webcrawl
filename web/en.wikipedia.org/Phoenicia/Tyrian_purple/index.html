Tyrian purple (Ancient Greek: πορφύρα, porphýra; Latin: purpura), also known as royal or imperial purple, is a reddish-purple natural dye extracted from the mucus glands of predatory sea snails of the family Muricidae, principally the spiny dye-murex Bolinus brandaris. It is named for Tyre, the Phoenician city most famous for its production.

Production

The dye was extraordinarily laborious to make. Tens of thousands of snails were needed to dye a single garment: ancient sources and modern reconstructions indicate roughly 10,000–12,000 snails yield only about a gram of pure dye. The snails' glands were extracted, salted, and heated in vats; the initially pale secretion turns purple on exposure to air and sunlight. Pliny the Elder described the process in detail, including the notorious stench of the dye works.

History and status

Purple-dyed cloth was produced on the Levantine coast from at least the 16th–15th centuries BC and became the pre-eminent luxury textile dye of the ancient world, worth more than its weight in gold. Because of its cost, the colour became indelibly associated with royalty and high office — hence the expression "born in the purple" for Byzantine imperial children. In Rome, sumptuary laws restricted its use; by late antiquity a state monopoly controlled production, and the industry effectively ended with the fall of Constantinople in 1453.

Chemistry

The principal dye component, 6,6′-dibromoindigo, was identified by Paul Friedländer in 1909 after extracting it from some 12,000 snails. Unlike indigo, the dibromo compound gives a rich purple hue and is exceptionally resistant to fading.
