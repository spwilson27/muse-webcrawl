Aramaic alphabet

The Aramaic alphabet is the consonantal writing system (abjad) used for the Aramaic language from about the ninth century BC. It was adapted directly from the alphabet of Phoenicia, whose twenty-two-letter signary the Aramaeans of Syria took over along with the maritime traders' network that spread it — retaining the Phoenician letter names and order while gradually developing distinctive, more cursive letter forms.

Descent from the Phoenician script

The Phoenician alphabet was itself the ancestor of nearly all later alphabetic scripts, and the Aramaic branch became its most consequential offshoot in Asia. Early Aramaic inscriptions, such as the royal stelae from Sam'al and the bilingual Tell Fekheriye statue, still closely resemble Phoenician lettering; by the Persian period, a standardised "Imperial Aramaic" chancellery script was written from Egypt to the Indus.

Descendants

From Aramaic descend the Hebrew square script (adopted for Jewish texts after the Babylonian exile), the Syriac and Palmyrene scripts, Nabataean — the direct ancestor of the Arabic alphabet — and, through debated lines of transmission, possibly the Brahmi script of India. The alphabet's Phoenician origin thus underlies writing systems used today by hundreds of millions of people. Compare the Greek branch of the same Phoenician inheritance, which produced the Latin alphabet through Etruscan.
