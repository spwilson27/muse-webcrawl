Hannibal

Hannibal Barca
Born: 247 BC, Carthage
Died: c. 183–181 BC, Libyssa, Bithynia
Allegiance: Carthage
Known for: Command in the Second Punic War

Hannibal Barca (Punic: Ḥannībaʿl; 247 – between 183 and 181 BC) was a Carthaginian general and statesman who commanded the forces of Carthage in their battle against the Roman Republic during the Second Punic War. He is widely considered one of the greatest military commanders in history. His father, Hamilcar Barca, was a leading Carthaginian commander during the First Punic War.

Early life

Hannibal was the son of Hamilcar Barca and accompanied his father to Hispania as a boy. According to tradition, Hamilcar made the young Hannibal swear an oath of enmity toward Rome. After Hamilcar's death, Hannibal served under his brother-in-law Hasdrubal the Fair, whom he succeeded in command in 221 BC.

Second Punic War

In 218 BC Hannibal attacked Saguntum, an ally of Rome, triggering the Second Punic War. He then led his army — including dozens of war elephants — overland from Spain, across the Pyrenees and the Alps, into Italy, an extraordinary feat of logistics and endurance. In Italy he won a series of spectacular victories: at the Trebia (218 BC), Lake Trasimene (217 BC), and above all Cannae (216 BC), where his double-envelopment destroyed a much larger Roman army and remains a classic studied in military academies.

Despite these victories, Hannibal could not force Rome to terms: the Romans avoided pitched battle, his supply lines were strained, and Rome's Italian allies mostly stayed loyal. After fifteen years in Italy he was recalled to defend Carthage against Scipio Africanus, who defeated him at the Battle of Zama in 202 BC, ending the war.

Later life and death

After the war Hannibal led reforms of Carthage's government and finances, but Roman pressure forced him into exile. He served the Seleucid king Antiochus III and later Prusias of Bithynia. Rather than be handed over to Rome, he took poison and died at Libyssa, traditionally in 183 BC.

Legacy

Hannibal's tactics, especially at Cannae, are still studied. His crossing of the Alps is one of the most celebrated feats of ancient warfare. Latin literature's phrase "Hannibal ad portas" ("Hannibal is at the gates") preserved his memory as Rome's most dangerous enemy.
