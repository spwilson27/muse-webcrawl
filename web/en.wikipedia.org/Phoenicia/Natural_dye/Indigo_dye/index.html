Indigo dye

Indigo dye is an organic compound with a distinctive blue color. It is a vat dye, meaning it must be chemically reduced to a soluble, nearly colorless form (leuco-indigo) to impregnate fibers, after which exposure to air oxidizes it back to the insoluble blue pigment trapped in the cloth. Historically, indigo was a natural dye extracted from the leaves of plants of the genus Indigofera and, in Europe, from woad (Isatis tinctoria). Today nearly all indigo is produced synthetically, and its best-known use is dyeing the cotton yarn used for denim and blue jeans.

History

Indigo use dates back millennia: indigo-dyed fabric fragments have been found in Peru dating to about 6,000 years ago, and indigo was used in ancient Egypt, India, Mesopotamia, Greece, and Rome. The name derives from the Greek indikon, "Indian (dye)", reflecting the Indian origin of much of the trade. In the colonial era, indigo became a major plantation crop in the Americas and the Caribbean, produced largely by enslaved labor; in British India, coercive indigo cultivation provoked the Indigo Rebellion of 1859–60 and was later a focus of Gandhi's Champaran movement of 1917.

Production

Natural indigo is obtained by fermenting plant leaves, precipitating the dye, and pressing it into cakes. Adolf von Baeyer determined indigo's chemical structure in 1883, and a commercially viable synthesis was developed by BASF, which brought synthetic indigo to market in 1897, collapsing the natural indigo trade within two decades. Industrial synthesis today typically proceeds from aniline.

Uses

Indigo is used overwhelmingly for denim: the warp yarn is dyed while the weft is left white, producing the characteristic look of blue jeans. In an average pair of jeans a few grams of indigo are used. Indigo derivatives include Tyrian purple (6,6′-dibromoindigo) and the food colorant indigo carmine.

Chemistry

Indigo's chemical formula is C16H10N2O2. Its deep color and insolubility arise from a compact, hydrogen-bonded molecular structure. Because it binds only superficially to cotton fibers, indigo-dyed denim fades attractively with wear.
