Natural Dye

Ancient dyestuffs: madder, weld, indigo, and the Phoenician murex.

Natural dyes are colorants derived from plants, invertebrates, or minerals without synthetic chemistry. The most celebrated ancient natural dye was produced in Phoenicia: Tyrian purple, extracted from the hypobranchial glands of murex sea snails (Bolinus brandaris and Hexaplex trunculus). Thousands of snails were needed for a single gram of dye, and the shell middens of Tyre and Sidon still mark the workshops. Purple cloth became a byword for royalty — the very name "Phoenicia" may derive from the Greek phoinix, purple-red.

Sources

Plants supplied most historical dyes: madder root for red, weld for yellow, woad and indigo for blue, and tannin-rich barks for browns and blacks. Insects gave crimson from kermes and, after contact with the Americas, cochineal. Mineral pigments such as ochre colored the earliest textiles.

Mordants and technique

Most natural dyes require a mordant — a metal salt such as alum or iron — to bind color to fiber. Phoenician purple was exceptional: as a vat dye closely related to indigo, it fixed directly to wool and grew more intense with exposure to sunlight, a quality that made it nearly permanent and fabulously expensive.

Decline and revival

The synthesis of mauveine in 1856 and alizarin in 1869 launched the synthetic dye industry and swept natural dyes from commerce within decades. Tyrian purple itself was synthesized in the twentieth century. Interest in natural dyeing has revived among textile artists for its subtle, harmonious color palette.
