Phoenicia (from Ancient Greek Phoiníkē) was an ancient Semitic-speaking civilisation that originated in the Levant region of the eastern Mediterranean, primarily located in modern Lebanon, with additional territory in coastal Syria and northern Israel. The Phoenicians were organised not as a unified state but as a network of independent maritime city-states, the most important of which were Byblos, Sidon, and Tyre. Between roughly 1500 BCE and 300 BCE they dominated trade throughout the Mediterranean, founding colonies as far west as Iberia and North Africa — most famously Carthage — and are credited with inventing and disseminating the Phoenician alphabet, the ancestor of nearly all modern alphabetic scripts.

Origins and society

The Phoenicians were a Canaanite people, culturally continuous with the Bronze Age inhabitants of the Levantine coast. Their self-designation appears to have been Kinaʿani ("Canaanites") or by their city of origin; the name "Phoenician" derives from the Greek word for purple (phoinix), a reference to the prized Tyrian purple dye extracted from murex sea snails that was among their most famous exports. Phoenician society was organised around merchant oligarchies and kings whose power was checked by councils of elders and, in some cities, elected magistrates called suffetes. Their religion was polytheistic, centred on deities such as Baal, Astarte, Melqart (the patron god of Tyre), and Eshmun.

Trade and colonisation

Phoenician ships — among the most advanced of the ancient world, including the two-tiered bireme and large merchantmen — carried cedar timber, purple-dyed cloth, glassware, wine, oil, and metalwork across the Mediterranean. In exchange they sought raw materials, especially metals: silver from Iberia, tin from as far as Britain, and gold from Africa. Herodotus even reports that Phoenician sailors, commissioned by Pharaoh Necho II around 600 BCE, circumnavigated Africa. From the 9th century BCE the Phoenicians founded colonies and trading posts including Gadir (Cádiz), Utica, and Carthage (traditionally founded 814 BCE), which grew into an empire of its own that survived the decline of the mother cities by centuries.

Alphabet and culture

The Phoenician alphabet, developed by about the 11th century BCE, was a consonantal script (abjad) of 22 signs written right to left. Its simplicity, compared with cuneiform and hieroglyphic systems, made literacy far more accessible. Adapted by the Greeks — who added vowels — it became the basis of the Greek, Latin, Cyrillic, and (through Aramaic) Hebrew, Arabic, and other scripts. Phoenician craftsmen were also renowned for ivory carving, metalwork, glass production, and shipbuilding; King Hiram of Tyre supplied cedar and craftsmen for Solomon's Temple in Jerusalem, according to the Hebrew Bible.

Decline

From the 9th century BCE the Phoenician cities fell successively under Assyrian, Babylonian, and Persian domination, though they retained considerable autonomy and commercial importance; Phoenician fleets formed the backbone of the Persian navy. Alexander the Great conquered the region in 332 BCE after the famous siege of Tyre, and Hellenisation followed. The last Phoenician territory was absorbed when Rome annexed Syria in 64 BCE, but Phoenician language and identity persisted for centuries, and the Punic descendants of Phoenician colonists in Carthage remained a Mediterranean power until Rome destroyed the city in 146 BCE.
