Temple of Eshmun

The sanctuary of Eshmun, healing god of Sidon, amid the orchards of Bostan el-Sheikh.

The Temple of Eshmun is an ancient place of worship dedicated to Eshmun, the Phoenician god of healing, located about two kilometres northeast of Sidon in southwestern Lebanon, at Bostan el-Sheikh on the Awali River. Built and enlarged from the seventh century BC onward, it is one of the best-preserved Phoenician religious sites in the homeland of Phoenicia, and remained in use into Roman times.

History

The sanctuary was founded under the Sidonian king Eshmunazar II in the sixth century BC, during the Persian period; a famous inscription on his sarcophagus records that he and his mother built temples for the gods of Sidon, including Eshmun at the "Ydll spring" — the sacred water source integral to the healing cult. Later rulers added an monumental podium, colonnades, and mosaic-floored courts in Hellenistic and Roman styles.

The healing cult

Eshmun was identified by the Greeks with Asclepius, god of medicine. Pilgrims sought cures through ritual bathing in the sanctuary's elaborate system of channels, basins, and hydrotherapy installations fed by the sacred spring, and through incubation — sleeping in the temple to receive a healing dream. Votive offerings, including statues of healed children, were dedicated in thanks.

Excavation

Excavations led by Maurice Dunand from 1963 to 1975 uncovered the great podium, marble basins, and inscriptions in Phoenician and Greek. The site suffered during Lebanon's civil war, when some mosaics were damaged or stolen, but the core complex remains visitable and is on UNESCO's tentative World Heritage list.
