History of the alphabet

The history of the alphabet goes back to the consonantal writing system used to write Semitic languages in the Levant in the 2nd millennium BCE. The first fully phonemic script, the Proto-Canaanite script (also called the Proto-Sinaitic script), is thought to have been developed by Semitic-speaking workers in Egypt around the 19th–15th centuries BCE, adapting Egyptian hieroglyphs by the acrophonic principle: a sign was used for the first sound of the Semitic word for the object depicted.

Origins

Almost all of the world's alphabets are descended ultimately from the Phoenician alphabet, a consonantal script (abjad) of 22 letters standardized by the Phoenicians around the 11th century BCE. The earliest known alphabetic inscriptions include the Proto-Sinaitic inscriptions from Serabit el-Khadim in the Sinai Peninsula and Wadi el-Hol in Egypt.

Phoenician and its descendants

Phoenician merchants spread their script around the Mediterranean. Its descendants include the Aramaic alphabet — ancestor of Hebrew, Arabic, and through Syriac and Mongolian, many scripts of Asia — and the Greek alphabet, adapted around the 8th century BCE. The Greeks innovated by reassigning some consonant signs to represent vowels, producing the first "true alphabet" with separate letters for consonants and vowels alike.

Greek and Latin traditions

From Greek, through the Euboean alphabet used in Italy, came the Old Italic scripts and the Latin alphabet, now the most widely used in the world. Greek also gave rise to the Cyrillic and Coptic alphabets. Other descendants of the Aramaic branch include the Brahmic scripts of South and Southeast Asia, such as Devanagari, from which most Indic scripts derive.

Later developments

Over time, alphabets have been adapted to new languages by adding letters, diacritics, and digraphs. Runes developed in northern Europe from Old Italic models. The Armenian and Georgian alphabets were created in late antiquity. In the modern era, missionaries and linguists have designed alphabetic orthographies for previously unwritten languages.
