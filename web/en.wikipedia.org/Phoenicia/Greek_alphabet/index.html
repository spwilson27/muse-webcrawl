Greek Alphabet

The Greek alphabet, adapted from the Phoenician consonantal script.

The Greek alphabet has been used to write the Greek language since the late ninth or early eighth century BC. It was derived from the earlier Phoenician alphabet, and was the earliest known alphabetic script to have distinct letters for vowels as well as consonants. In Archaic and early Classical times, the Greek alphabet existed in many local variants, but by the end of the fourth century BC, the Euclidean alphabet, with 24 letters, had become standard, and it is this version that is still used to write Greek today.

Debt to Phoenicia

The Greeks themselves credited the Phoenicians with bringing writing to Greece; Herodotus called the letters Phoinikeia grammata, "Phoenician letters." Greek letter names — alpha, beta, gamma, delta — are meaningless in Greek but correspond to Phoenician words: aleph (ox), beth (house), gimel (camel or throwing stick), and daleth (door). The shapes and order of the letters likewise follow the Phoenician prototype closely.

The vowel innovation

The Phoenician script was an abjad: it wrote consonants only. Greek, whose meaning often hinges on vowels, repurposed several Phoenician consonant signs for vowels: aleph became alpha (a), he became epsilon (e), yodh became iota (i), ayin became omicron (o), and waw split into digamma (consonantal) and upsilon (u). This created the first true alphabet, in which vowels and consonants enjoy equal status.

Legacy

From Greek the alphabet spread to the Etruscans and Romans, giving rise to the Latin alphabet used for English and most European languages, and via a different branch to the Cyrillic scripts. See also History of the alphabet.
