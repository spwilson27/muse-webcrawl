Hannibal

Hannibal Barca, the Carthaginian general descended from Phoenician settlers.

Hannibal Barca (247 – c. 183–181 BC) was a Carthaginian general and statesman who commanded the forces of Carthage in its battle against the Roman Republic during the Second Punic War. Carthage had been founded by Phoenician colonists from Tyre in the ninth century BC, and Hannibal's career is the most famous chapter in the long Phoenician diaspora: a Semitic, Punic-speaking city in North Africa challenging Rome for mastery of the western Mediterranean.

Background

Hannibal was the son of Hamilcar Barca, Carthage's leading general in the First Punic War. Raised in the Barcid stronghold in Iberia, he allegedly swore an oath as a boy never to be a friend of Rome. In 218 BC he marched an army including war elephants from Spain across the Pyrenees and the Alps into Italy — one of the most celebrated feats of military logistics in antiquity.

Italian campaign

Hannibal won a series of devastating victories at the Trebia (218 BC), Lake Trasimene (217 BC), and Cannae (216 BC), the last often studied as a classic double envelopment. Yet he lacked the siege equipment and reinforcements to take Rome itself, and Roman refusal to negotiate after Cannae gradually turned the war. Recalled to Africa, he was defeated by Scipio Africanus at Zama in 202 BC.

Later life and legacy

After the war Hannibal reformed Carthage's finances as suffete, then fled into exile, serving the Seleucid king Antiochus III and later Prusias of Bithynia. Facing extradition to Rome, he took his own life, traditionally by poison kept in a ring. Military historians rank him among the greatest commanders of the ancient world.
