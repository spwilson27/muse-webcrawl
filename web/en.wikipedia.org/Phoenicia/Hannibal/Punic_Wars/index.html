Punic Wars

The Punic Wars were a series of wars between 264 and 146 BC fought between the Roman Republic and Ancient Carthage. The three Punic Wars involved some of the largest wars of the ancient world and were pivotal in establishing Rome as the dominant power of the western Mediterranean. The Romans called the Carthaginians Poeni (from their Phoenician ancestry), hence "Punic".

First Punic War (264–241 BC)

The First Punic War began over control of Sicily. Rome, previously a land power, built a navy and developed the corvus boarding device. After victories at Mylae and Cape Ecnomus and a failed invasion of Africa under Regulus, Rome won the decisive naval battle of the Aegates Islands in 241 BC. Carthage ceded Sicily and paid a large indemnity.

Second Punic War (218–201 BC)

The Second Punic War, also called the Hannibalic War, began when the Carthaginian general Hannibal attacked Saguntum in Spain. Hannibal famously crossed the Alps with war elephants and inflicted crushing defeats on Rome at Trebia, Lake Trasimene, and Cannae (216 BC), one of the most famous battles in history. Rome refused to surrender, avoided pitched battle, and eventually carried the war to Africa, where Scipio Africanus defeated Hannibal at Zama in 202 BC. Carthage lost Spain and its fleet and became a Roman client in all but name.

Third Punic War (149–146 BC)

The Third Punic War was essentially a siege of Carthage itself. Despite Carthage's compliance with Roman demands, Rome declared war and, after a three-year siege, Scipio Aemilianus captured and systematically destroyed the city in 146 BC. Its territory became the Roman province of Africa.

Consequences

The wars ended Carthaginian power, established Roman supremacy in the western Mediterranean, and set Rome on the path to empire. The same year Carthage fell, Rome also destroyed Corinth, symbolizing its dominance of the Mediterranean world.
