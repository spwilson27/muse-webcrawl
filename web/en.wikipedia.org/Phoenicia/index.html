Phoenicia

A Phoenician ship, from a sarcophagus relief.

Phoenicia (from Ancient Greek: Φοινίκη, Phoiníkē) was an ancient Semitic thalassocracy (seaborne civilization) in the Levant, composed of independent city-states along the coast of the eastern Mediterranean, in modern Lebanon and parts of coastal Syria and northern Israel. The Phoenicians were a Canaanite people whose civilization flourished from about 1500 BC to 300 BC.

The major Phoenician cities were Tyre, Sidon, Byblos, Arwad and Beirut. The Phoenicians never formed a single unified state; each city was ruled by its own king, though Tyre was pre-eminent from the 10th century BC under kings such as Hiram I.

Trade and colonies

Famed as merchants and sailors — the Greek historian Herodotus credited them with circumnavigating Africa around 600 BC — the Phoenicians traded timber (the cedars of Lebanon), glass, metalwork, ivory and above all the purple dye Tyrian purple, extracted from murex sea snails, from which their Greek name (phoinix, "purple-red") likely derives. From the 9th century BC they founded colonies across the Mediterranean, including Carthage in North Africa (traditionally 814 BC), as well as settlements on Cyprus, Sicily, Sardinia, and in Iberia (Gadir, modern Cádiz; Malaca, modern Málaga).

The alphabet

Phoenicia's most enduring legacy is the Phoenician alphabet, a 22-letter consonantal script (abjad) developed by about 1050 BC. Adapted from earlier Proto-Canaanite writing, it was spread by Phoenician trade, adapted by the Greeks (who added vowels) and by the Aramaeans; through these it is the ancestor of nearly all modern alphabets, including Latin, Greek, Cyrillic, Hebrew, Arabic and the Brahmic scripts.

Decline

The Phoenician cities successively fell under Assyrian, Babylonian and Persian domination, though they retained considerable autonomy as naval and trading partners. Alexander the Great conquered the region after the famous siege of Tyre (332 BC), and Phoenicia was absorbed into the Hellenistic world, its language and identity gradually fading during Roman times — while Punic-speaking Carthage survived as a great power until Rome destroyed it in 146 BC.
