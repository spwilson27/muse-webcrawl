Born in the purple

"Born in the purple" is an expression for a child born to a reigning sovereign — and, by extension, to any great privilege. Its roots lie in Phoenicia, whose city of Tyre produced Tyrian purple, the fabulously expensive dye made from the secretions of murex sea snails, which coloured the garments of royalty across the ancient world.

Tyrian purple

The Phoenicians — whose very name in Greek, Phoinikes, may derive from phoinix, "purple-red" — harvested murex shells in industrial quantities; shell middens still mark the dye works at Tyre and Sidon. Tens of thousands of snails were needed for a single garment, and the colour-fast, light-resistant dye became the defining status symbol of kings and emperors. Roman law progressively reserved purple silk for the imperial family.

Porphyrogennetos

In the Byzantine Empire the phrase took literal form: children of the reigning emperor were born in the Porphyra, a chamber in the Great Palace of Constantinople lined with purple porphyry stone, and bore the title Porphyrogennetos, "born in the purple". The title outranked even children of the same emperor born before his accession. The association between Phoenician dye, imperial purple and inherited supreme status survived into modern idiom — much as the Phoenician letters survived, transformed, in the Aramaic alphabet and its descendants.
