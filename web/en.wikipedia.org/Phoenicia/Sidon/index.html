Sidon (Arabic: صيدا, Ṣaydā; Phoenician: 𐤑𐤃𐤍, Ṣīdūn) is the third-largest city in Lebanon, on the Mediterranean coast about 40 km south of Beirut. One of the oldest and most important Phoenician cities, it was a great commercial centre famed for glassmaking and the production of Tyrian purple dye.

Ancient history

Sidon was inhabited from prehistory and rose to prominence in the Bronze Age. In the Persian period (from 539 BC) Sidonian kings ruled as vassals and supplied fleets to the Achaemenid Empire; the sarcophagi of kings such as Eshmunazar II, with their Egyptian-influenced anthropoid coffins, are now in the Louvre. Sidon surrendered to Alexander the Great in 333 BC and subsequently passed among Hellenistic, Roman, and Byzantine rulers.

Medieval and modern

Crusaders fortified the city, building the Sea Castle on an island offshore in the 13th century. Sidon later became a trading port of the Ottoman Empire, notably under Emir Fakhr al-Din II in the 17th century. The modern city is a commercial and fishing centre; landmarks include the Sea Castle, the Temple of Eshmun (just northeast of the city), the Soap Museum, and the old souks.

Economy

Historically based on trade, fishing, and crafts, Sidon's economy today rests on commerce, services, and agriculture in the surrounding citrus-growing plain.
