Lepidodendron

Lepidodendron is an extinct genus of primitive lycopod plants that grew as tall trees during the Carboniferous period, roughly 359 to 299 million years ago. It was one of the dominant plants of the coal forests that covered much of the tropical world.

Description

Lepidodendron trees could reach heights of 40 to 50 metres, with trunks over a metre in diameter. Their bark was covered in diamond-shaped leaf scars arranged in regular rows, giving the trunk a distinctive scaly pattern — the source of the genus name, from Greek words meaning "scale tree". The crown consisted of a repeatedly forking branch system bearing long, narrow leaves and cones.

Life cycle

Unlike modern trees, Lepidodendron grew rapidly to full height with little branching, then produced a crown of branches and cones in a final reproductive phase before dying. It reproduced by spores borne in large cones, and its rooting system, known as Stigmaria, spread horizontally through swampy soils.

Fossil record and coal

Fossils of Lepidodendron are common in Carboniferous rocks worldwide. Together with its relatives such as Sigillaria, it formed a major component of the plant matter that was buried and transformed into the coal seams mined today.
