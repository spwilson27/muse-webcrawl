Pennsylvanian (geology)

The Pennsylvanian is a subperiod of the Carboniferous Period in the geologic time scale, spanning from about 323.2 to 298.9 million years ago. It is the later of the two subperiods into which North American geologists divide the Carboniferous, the earlier being the Mississippian; together they correspond roughly to the upper part of the European usage.

Name and strata

The name derives from the U.S. state of Pennsylvania, where coal-bearing strata of this age are extensively exposed and were intensively studied. In the international chronostratigraphic scale the Pennsylvanian is divided into the Bashkirian, Moscovian, Kasimovian, and Gzhelian stages.

Life and environment

Pennsylvanian rocks are famous for their cyclothems — rhythmic alternations of marine and non-marine strata, including the great coal seams laid down in tropical swamp forests of giant lycophytes (Lepidodendron), horsetails (Calamites), and seed ferns as Gondwanan ice sheets waxed and waned. Oxygen levels well above today's supported giant arthropods. Amphibians were diverse, and the earliest true reptiles (amniotes), such as Hylonomus, appeared — a key step enabling vertebrate life to spread into dry interiors.
