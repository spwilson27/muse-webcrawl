Tribalism

Tribalism is the state of being organized by, or advocating for, tribes or tribal lifestyles. Human evolution has primarily occurred in small groups, as opposed to mass societies, and humans naturally maintain a social network. In popular culture, tribalism may also refer to a way of thinking or behaving in which people are loyal to their social group above all else, or, derogatorily, a type of discrimination or animosity based upon group differences.

Definition

The term "tribalism" has many overlaps. According to historian Pauline M. H. Mazumdar, the term originally implied the structure of kinship-based societies, but in political discourse it came to denote factional loyalty and intergroup conflict, particularly in postcolonial states. Generally, tribalism implies the possession of a strong cultural or ethnic identity that separates one member of a group from another member of another group.

Group dynamics

Anthropologists such as Elman Service distinguish between tribal societies, organized around kinship and informal leadership, and more stratified chiefdoms and states. In contemporary usage, political commentators apply "tribalism" to partisan politics, describing voters who support their party regardless of policy content — a form of in-group loyalty and out-group hostility studied by social psychologists under concepts such as social identity theory.

Criticism of the term

Some scholars criticize the term as Eurocentric and imprecise: Africanist scholars in particular argue that "tribalism" was used by colonial administrations to essentialize fluid social identities and justify indirect rule, and that it obscures the effects of colonial borders and state policies.
