Value pluralism

In ethics, value pluralism (also known as ethical pluralism or moral pluralism) is the idea that there are several values which may be equally correct and fundamental, and yet in conflict with each other. In addition, value pluralism postulates that in many cases, such incompatible values may be incommensurable, in the sense that there is no objective ordering of them in terms of importance.

Isaiah Berlin

The concept is closely associated with the philosopher Isaiah Berlin, who argued in essays such as "Two Concepts of Liberty" (1958) that human values are plural and sometimes irreconcilable: liberty can conflict with equality, justice with mercy, and the pursuit of one good may entail the sacrifice of another. For Berlin, this plurality is a permanent feature of the human condition, and attempts to impose a single harmonising system of values lead to coercion and tyranny.

Pluralism versus monism and relativism

Value pluralism differs from monism, the view that all values can be ordered under one supreme value, and from relativism, the view that values are merely matters of subjective preference or cultural convention. Pluralists hold that values are objective and knowable, but that no single value or consistent set of values can capture all of them: hard choices between genuine goods are unavoidable, and not always resolvable without loss.

Incommensurability

A central claim of many pluralists is that competing values are often incommensurable — there is no common unit by which, say, freedom and equality can both be measured. This does not make choice irrational: reasons still apply, but they may not determine a uniquely correct answer. Critics respond that incommensurability threatens rational decision-making, while defenders argue it better reflects lived moral experience.
