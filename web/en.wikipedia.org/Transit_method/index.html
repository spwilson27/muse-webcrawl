Transit method

A transit light curve: the star dims slightly and periodically as the planet crosses its disc.

The transit method, or transit photometry, is a method of detecting exoplanets by observing periodic dips in the brightness of a star caused by a planet passing (transiting) in front of it as seen from Earth. It is one of the two most productive exoplanet detection techniques, and the method used by NASA's Kepler and TESS space telescopes, which together have discovered the majority of known exoplanets.

Principle

When a planet transits its host star, it blocks a small fraction of the star's light. The fractional dip in brightness is approximately the ratio of the areas of the planet and star:

ΔF/F ≈ (Rp/R*)2

An Earth-size planet crossing a Sun-like star dims it by only about 0.008% (84 parts per million), while a Jupiter-size planet causes a dip of about 1%. Repeated dips at a constant interval reveal the planet's orbital period, and the shape and duration of the transit yield the planet's radius, orbital inclination, and impact parameter. Combined with radial-velocity measurements of the planet's mass, the radius gives its density and bulk composition.

Advantages and limitations

The method's main limitation is geometric: a transit is visible only if the orbital plane is nearly edge-on to our line of sight, with a probability of roughly R*/a (about 0.5% for an Earth analogue). It is therefore strongly biased toward close-in planets. It also requires observing at least two or three transits to establish periodicity, favouring short-period orbits. On the other hand, transits enable atmospheric characterisation through transmission spectroscopy, and phase curves and secondary eclipses can reveal temperatures and albedo.

History

The first transiting exoplanet observed was HD 209458 b in 1999, whose transits confirmed the nature of a planet previously found by radial velocity. Dedicated surveys followed: the ground-based SuperWASP and HATNet projects, then the space missions CoRoT (2006), Kepler (2009–2018), and TESS (2018– ), which surveys nearly the whole sky. The European PLATO mission is planned to extend the search toward longer-period, potentially habitable planets.
