In mathematics, subharmonic and superharmonic functions are important classes of functions in potential theory and complex analysis. A real-valued function u defined on a domain in Euclidean space (or a Riemann surface) is subharmonic if it is upper semicontinuous and, for every closed ball contained in the domain, its value at the center does not exceed the average of its values on the boundary sphere — the function lies below the harmonic function with the same boundary values.

Definition and properties

Formally, u is subharmonic if for every point x and every sufficiently small radius r, u(x) ≤ the mean of u over the sphere of radius r about x. If u is twice continuously differentiable, this condition is equivalent to the Laplacian condition Δu ≥ 0 throughout the domain; harmonic functions satisfy equality, and superharmonic functions reverse the inequality.

Key properties include the maximum principle: a subharmonic function that attains a maximum inside its domain must be constant. The pointwise maximum of two subharmonic functions is subharmonic, and uniform limits (and suitable decreasing limits) of subharmonic functions remain subharmonic.

Examples and applications

The absolute value of a holomorphic function, and more generally log|f| for holomorphic f, is subharmonic — a fact underlying many growth estimates in complex analysis. Convex functions of one variable are subharmonic, and subharmonic functions on the whole plane with suitable growth control are constrained by Liouville-type theorems. In higher dimensions, plurisubharmonic functions — subharmonic on every complex line — play the analogous central role in several complex variables.
