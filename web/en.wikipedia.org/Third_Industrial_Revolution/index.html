Third Industrial Revolution

The Third Industrial Revolution, also called the Digital Revolution, is the shift from mechanical and analogue electronic technology to digital electronics which began in the latter half of the 20th century, with the adoption and proliferation of digital computers and digital record-keeping, that continues to the present day. Central to this revolution is the mass production and widespread use of digital logic circuits, and its derived technologies, including the computer, mobile phone, and the Internet.

Origins

Key enabling inventions included the transistor (1947) at Bell Labs, the integrated circuit (1958–59), and the microprocessor (1971), which made computing cheap and small. Programmable logic controllers and industrial robots, such as the Unimate installed by General Motors in 1961, brought automation into factories. The development of packet-switched networks and ARPANET in the 1970s, followed by the World Wide Web (proposed by Tim Berners-Lee in 1989) and its popularisation in the 1990s, connected the world's computers and transformed commerce, media and social life.

Impact

The Digital Revolution converted analogue processes — print publishing, film photography, recorded music, telephony, retail — into digital forms, dramatically lowering the cost of storing, copying and transmitting information. It automated many clerical and manufacturing tasks, created new industries in software and online services, and globalised supply chains. The term is used alongside later framings: Jeremy Rifkin's book The Third Industrial Revolution (2011) used the phrase for a convergence of internet communication with renewable energy and electric transport, while the World Economic Forum popularised the idea of a succeeding "Fourth Industrial Revolution" centred on cyber-physical systems.
