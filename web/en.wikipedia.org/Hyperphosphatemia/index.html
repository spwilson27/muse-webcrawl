Hyperphosphatemia

Hyperphosphatemia is an electrolyte disorder in which there is an elevated level of phosphate in the blood. In adults, a serum phosphate concentration above about 4.5 mg/dL (1.46 mmol/L) is considered elevated. Phosphate balance is regulated by parathyroid hormone, vitamin D, and FGF23, and phosphate is excreted mainly by the kidneys.

Causes

The most common cause is reduced renal excretion in chronic kidney disease and acute kidney injury. Other causes include hypoparathyroidism, excessive phosphate intake or administration (including phosphate-containing laxatives and enemas), massive cell breakdown releasing intracellular phosphate (tumor lysis syndrome, rhabdomyolysis, hemolysis), and transcellular shifts in lactic acidosis or diabetic ketoacidosis. Spurious elevation can occur from hemolysis of the blood sample.

Signs and complications

Hyperphosphatemia itself often causes no direct symptoms; symptoms usually come from the accompanying hypocalcemia (as phosphate binds calcium), including muscle cramps, tetany, itching and bone pain. Chronically, a high calcium–phosphate product promotes vascular and soft-tissue calcification and is a driver of secondary hyperparathyroidism and renal osteodystrophy in kidney failure.

Treatment

Management includes dietary phosphate restriction, phosphate binders taken with meals (such as calcium acetate or sevelamer), and dialysis in kidney failure.
