Concretion

A concretion is a hard, compact mass formed by the precipitation of mineral cement within the spaces between particles in sedimentary rock or soil. Concretions form around a nucleus — often a fossil, shell fragment, leaf, or other organic material — and are commonly roughly spherical or disc-shaped, though many other forms occur. They are frequently harder and more resistant to erosion than the rock surrounding them, so they weather out and accumulate on the surface.

Formation

Concretions develop after sediment is deposited but typically before deep burial and lithification, as mineral-laden groundwater precipitates cement — commonly calcite, silica, or iron oxides such as hematite — in a growing shell around the nucleus. Because they grow early, they can preserve delicate fossils in three dimensions that are flattened or lost in the surrounding rock; the Mazon Creek fossil beds of Illinois are famous for their siderite concretions containing exceptionally preserved plants and animals.

Notable examples

Famous concretions include the large spherical Moeraki Boulders on the coast of New Zealand, the septarian nodules of the Jurassic Coast in England, the cannonball concretions of North Dakota, and the ironstone concretions ("Kansas Pop Rocks") of the Smoky Hill Chalk. Concretions are sometimes mistaken for fossils, dinosaur eggs, or artefacts; the small hematite spherules found by the Mars rover Opportunity in 2004 — nicknamed "blueberries" — are terrestrial-style concretions whose formation apparently involved liquid water on early Mars.
