Mole (sauce)

Mole (Spanish pronunciation: [ˈmole], from Nahuatl mōlli, "sauce") is a traditional sauce and marinade originally used in Mexican cuisine. In contemporary Mexico the term is used for a number of sauces, some quite dissimilar, including mole negro, mole rojo, mole coloradito, mole almendrado, mole michoacano, mole prieto, mole ranchero, mole tamaulipeco, mole xiqueño, pipián (made with pumpkin seeds), mole rosa, mole blanco, mole estofado, chimole, guacamole (which actually derives from ahuacamolli, meaning avocado sauce), huaxmole, mole de cacahuete, mole de fiesta, mole de guajolote, mole de olla, mole de xoconostle, mole verde, mole de chicatana, mole de chichilo, and mole de tortilla quemada.

Generally, a mole sauce contains a combination of a fruit, a chili pepper, a nut or seed such as a sesame seed, and spices including black pepper, cinnamon, or cumin. It is a staple in some households of Mexico and can be prepared in many ways.

History

Two states in Mexico claim to be the origin of mole: Puebla and Oaxaca. The best-known moles are native to these two states, but other regions in Mexico also make various types of mole sauces. Moles come from prehispanic times, when the Aztecs served some types of moles in feasts to honor their gods. After the Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire, the moles incorporated ingredients brought by the Spanish, such as cinnamon and almonds.

The most popular legends about the origin of mole date to the colonial period. In one, nuns at the Convent of Santa Rosa in Puebla, upon learning that the archbishop was coming for a visit, created a sauce from what few ingredients they had on hand, including old bread, chili peppers, spices, and a little chocolate, and served it over turkey. In another, Fray Pascual supposedly created the sauce by accident when he spilled a tray of spices into pots of turkey being prepared for a visiting viceroy.

Types

Mole poblano

Mole poblano is perhaps the best-known of all mole varieties. An ancient dish of pre-Hispanic origin made new, it contains about 20 ingredients, including dried chili peppers such as mulato, ancho, and pasilla, as well as chocolate, which works to counteract the heat of the peppers but whose flavor does not dominate. It is typically served over turkey or chicken.

Mole negro

Oaxaca is known as "the land of the seven moles." Its large size, mountainous terrain, variety of indigenous peoples, and many microclimates make for numerous regional variations. From these, several moles are derived, including negro (black), rojo (red), coloradito (flushed or little red), amarillo (yellow), verde (green), chichilo, and manchamantel (tablecloth stainer).

Mole negro is the most complex of the seven. It contains chocolatl, chilhuacle negro chilis, chilhuacle rojo chilis, mulato chilis, blackened tortillas, hoja santa, and many other ingredients.

Preparation

All mole preparations begin with one or more types of chili pepper. The other ingredients are then roasted and ground into a powder or paste. This process is painstaking and traditionally involved grinding by hand on a metate. The ground ingredients form a powder that is mixed with water or broth and then simmered until it is thick.

Moles are often prepared in large quantities for celebrations and festivals. The traditional time to eat mole is on special occasions, such as weddings, baptisms, and Day of the Dead celebrations.
