Homicide

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Homicide is an act in which an individual human kills another human. A homicide requires only a volitional act, or an omission, that causes the death of another; it does not necessarily imply criminal liability. Homicides can be divided into many overlapping legal categories, such as murder, manslaughter, justifiable homicide, and excusable homicide, and can also be analysed by criminologists in terms of circumstance and victim–offender relationship. The state of mind of the killer — intent, recklessness, or negligence — is usually central to how a homicide is classified and punished.

Legal categories

Murder is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought — intent to kill, intent to inflict grievous bodily harm, depraved-heart recklessness, or (in many U.S. jurisdictions) intent to commit a felony under the felony murder rule. Manslaughter covers unlawful killings without malice: voluntary manslaughter, where a killing follows adequate provocation in the heat of passion, and involuntary manslaughter, where death results from criminal negligence or a misdemeanour. Justifiable homicide includes killings in lawful self-defence, by police in the line of duty, and executions carried out under legal sentence, while excusable homicide includes certain killings by accident or misadventure without fault.

Rates and patterns

The United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime estimated about 458,000 homicides globally in 2021, a rate of roughly 5.8 per 100,000 people. Rates vary enormously by region: the Americas and parts of Africa record rates many times higher than most of Europe and East Asia, where rates around or below 1 per 100,000 are common. Most homicide victims worldwide are men, and most known offenders are men as well; a large share of female victims are killed by intimate partners or family members. Firearms account for roughly half of homicides globally and a large majority in the United States.

History

Historical criminology based on coroners' rolls and court records suggests interpersonal homicide rates in medieval Europe were ten to fifty times higher than today, declining over centuries in what some scholars link to state consolidation, courts displacing private vengeance, and changing norms of self-control — a trend associated with Norbert Elias's "civilising process." Blood feud, duelling, and honour killings, once institutionalised in many societies, have been progressively criminalised, though honour-related killings persist in some regions.
