Sahyadri

Sahyadri is another name for the Western Ghats, the mountain range that runs parallel to the western coast of the Indian peninsula. The name, of Sanskrit origin, is commonly used for the northern portion of the range, particularly within the state of Maharashtra, where the Sahyadris form a defining feature of the landscape, climate, history, and culture.

Geography

The Sahyadri range begins near the Tapti River in Gujarat and extends southward through Maharashtra, Goa, and Karnataka. In Maharashtra the range runs roughly north–south, with an escarpment facing west toward the Konkan coastal plain and a gentler slope eastward into the Deccan Plateau. Its crest intercepts the moisture-laden southwest monsoon, producing some of the heaviest rainfall in India on the windward side, while the leeward districts lie in a marked rain shadow.

Rivers and ecology

Many of peninsular India's great rivers rise in the Sahyadris: the Godavari, Krishna, and Bhima flow eastward across the Deccan, while short, swift rivers such as the Vashishti and Ulhas drain westward to the Arabian Sea. The range is one of the world's recognised biodiversity hotspots, harbouring moist evergreen forests, shola–grassland mosaics at higher elevations, and many endemic species of amphibians, reptiles, and plants. Several national parks and wildlife sanctuaries, including those of the Sahyadri Tiger Reserve and Radhanagari, protect its forests.

History

The Sahyadris are dotted with hill forts — such as Raigad, Rajgad, Pratapgad, Sinhagad, and Torna — many of them associated with the Maratha ruler Shivaji Maharaj in the seventeenth century, who used the rugged terrain to build and defend his kingdom. Ancient trade routes crossed the range through passes such as the Bhor Ghat and Thal Ghat, which today carry major road and rail links between Mumbai and the Deccan interior. Rock-cut cave complexes, including Karla, Bhaja, and Bedse, attest to the range's importance for Buddhism and trade from the second century BCE onward.
