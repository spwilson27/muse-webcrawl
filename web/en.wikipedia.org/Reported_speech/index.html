Reported speech

Reported speech
FieldGrammar, linguistics
Also calledIndirect speech
ContrastDirect speech (quotation)
Key featureBackshift of tense; changes to pronouns and time expressions

Reported speech, also called indirect speech, is a way of relating what someone said without quoting their exact words. Instead of direct speech — She said, "I am tired" — reported speech integrates the content into the reporter's own sentence: She said that she was tired. It is fundamental to narration, journalism, testimony and everyday conversation in English and many other languages.

Grammatical changes in English

When the reporting verb is in the past tense (said, told), English typically applies a backshift to the verbs in the reported clause: present simple becomes past simple ("I know" → he said he knew), present perfect and past simple become past perfect, will becomes would, and can becomes could. Pronouns and possessives change to match the reporter's perspective (I → he/she, my → his/her), and expressions of time and place shift too: now → then, today → that day, tomorrow → the next day, here → there. Backshift may be omitted when the reported content is still true at the time of reporting.

Reported questions and commands

Reported questions use statement word order and a reporting structure such as asked if/whether for yes–no questions or the original question word for wh-questions: "Where do you live?" → She asked where I lived. Commands and requests are usually reported with an infinitive: "Sit down" → He told us to sit down.

In other languages

Languages handle reported speech differently. Some, like French and German, mark it morphologically — German uses the subjunctive (Konjunktiv I) in formal reporting. Others use evidential markers or logophoric pronouns to distinguish the original speaker. Free indirect speech is a literary technique that blends narrator and character voices, keeping the character's expressions and questions but the narrator's tense and pronouns, as in much of Jane Austen and Virginia Woolf.
