Inflation (cosmology)

In physical cosmology, cosmic inflation, or simply inflation, is a theory of exponential expansion of space in the early universe. The inflationary epoch is believed to have lasted from about 10−36 seconds to around 10−33–10−32 seconds after the Big Bang singularity, during which the universe expanded by a factor of at least 1026.

Inflation proposes a burst of exponential expansion in the universe's first fraction of a second.

Motivation

Inflation was proposed by Alan Guth in 1980 to solve several problems in the standard Big Bang model. The horizon problem: distant regions of the cosmic microwave background have nearly identical temperatures despite apparently never having been in causal contact. The flatness problem: the density of the universe is remarkably close to the critical density. The magnetic monopole problem: grand unified theories predict abundant relic monopoles that are not observed. A period of exponential expansion driven by a scalar field — the inflaton — makes a small, causally connected, smooth region grow to encompass the observable universe, dilutes unwanted relics, and flattens spatial curvature.

Predictions and evidence

Inflation predicts a nearly scale-invariant spectrum of primordial density fluctuations with small non-Gaussianity, arising from quantum fluctuations stretched to cosmic scales. These seeds later grew into galaxies and large-scale structure. Measurements of the cosmic microwave background by COBE, WMAP and Planck strongly support these predictions. Inflation also predicts a background of primordial gravitational waves, which would imprint a distinctive B-mode polarization pattern on the microwave background; experiments such as BICEP continue to search for it.

Models and open questions

Many inflationary models exist, involving different inflaton potentials and mechanisms such as slow-roll inflation, chaotic inflation (developed by Andrei Linde), and eternal inflation, in which inflation continues forever in some regions, producing a multiverse. Critics note the theory's flexibility and the difficulty of testing it directly; the identity of the inflaton remains unknown.
