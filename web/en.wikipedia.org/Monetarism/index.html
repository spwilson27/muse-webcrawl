Monetarism

Monetarism
School: Chicago school of economics
Key figure: Milton Friedman
Core claim: changes in the money supply drive nominal income and, in the short run, output; in the long run, inflation
Policy rule: steady money growth (k-percent rule)

Monetarism is a school of thought in monetary economics holding that the money supply is the chief determinant of the performance of an economy, and that variation in the money supply has major influences on national output in the short run and on the price level over longer periods. Monetarists, led by Milton Friedman, contend that the objectives of monetary policy are best met by targeting the growth rate of the money supply rather than by discretionary central-bank intervention.


Milton Friedman (1912–2006)

Theory

Monetarism restates the quantity theory of money, MV = PY, arguing that velocity (V) is stable or predictable, so changes in money (M) drive nominal output and, once expectations adjust, prices. In A Monetary History of the United States (1963), Friedman and Anna Schwartz argued the Federal Reserve's contraction of the money supply turned the 1929 downturn into the Great Depression. Friedman's natural rate hypothesis held that unemployment cannot be held below its "natural rate" by inflation except temporarily, because expectations adapt—an account of the stagflation of the 1970s that damaged the simple Keynesian Phillips curve.

Policy

Friedman proposed replacing discretionary policy with a rule: expand the money supply at a constant rate (the "k-percent rule") matching long-run output growth, avoiding destabilizing "long and variable lags." In October 1979, Federal Reserve chairman Paul Volcker adopted a monetarist-style operating procedure targeting bank reserves; Margaret Thatcher's government in Britain also pursued monetary targets. By the mid-1980s most central banks had abandoned strict money-growth targeting because financial innovation made velocity unstable, but monetarism's legacy endured in central-bank independence, inflation targeting, skepticism of fine-tuning, and the expectation-focused conduct of monetary policy. After 2008, quantitative easing revived debates over monetarist predictions of inflation.
