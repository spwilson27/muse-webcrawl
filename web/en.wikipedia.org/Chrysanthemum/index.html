Chrysanthemum

Chrysanthemums, sometimes called mums or chrysanths, are flowering plants of the genus Chrysanthemum in the aster family, Asteraceae. They are native to East Asia and north-eastern Europe, and most species originate in East Asia, with a centre of diversity in China. Countless horticultural varieties and cultivars exist, with flower heads ranging from small pompons to large incurved blooms in white, yellow, pink, red, purple and bronze.

History and culture

Chrysanthemums were first cultivated in China as a flowering herb as early as the 15th century BC. The flower was introduced to Japan by the 8th century AD, where it became the Imperial Seal of Japan and a symbol of the Chrysanthemum Throne; the Supreme Order of the Chrysanthemum remains Japan's highest order. In China the chrysanthemum is one of the "Four Gentlemen" of painting and symbolises longevity and autumn. The flower reached Europe in the 17th century, and Linnaeus named it from the Greek chrysos (gold) and anthemon (flower).

Cultivation and uses

Garden mums are popular autumn-flowering perennials, and florist's chrysanthemums are among the most important cut flowers commercially. Chrysanthemum tea is drunk in parts of East Asia, and the natural insecticide pyrethrum is extracted from the seeds of related species (now classified as Tanacetum cinerariifolium). In several European countries, by contrast, chrysanthemums are associated with funerals and mourning.
