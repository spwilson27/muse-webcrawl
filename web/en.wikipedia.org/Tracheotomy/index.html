A tracheotomy (or tracheostomy) is a surgical procedure that creates an opening through the neck into the trachea (windpipe). A tube is usually placed through the opening to provide an airway and to remove secretions from the lungs. The resulting stoma can be temporary or permanent.

Indications

Tracheotomy is performed to bypass an obstructed upper airway, to support prolonged mechanical ventilation, to assist with the removal of respiratory secretions, and in some head and neck surgeries. In emergencies, a related but distinct procedure, cricothyrotomy, opens the cricothyroid membrane.

Technique

In a surgical tracheotomy, an incision is made between the second and fourth tracheal rings and a flanged tube is inserted. In percutaneous dilatational tracheotomy, commonly used in intensive care, the opening is created with dilators over a guidewire inserted through a needle, often with bronchoscopic or ultrasound guidance.

History

Airway incisions were described in ancient Egyptian and Indian texts, and the procedure was refined in the 19th century during diphtheria epidemics. Chevalier Jackson standardized the modern operation and its instruments in the early 20th century.

Complications

Possible complications include bleeding, infection, tube displacement, tracheal stenosis, and, rarely, a tracheo-oesophageal fistula. Speech and swallowing are affected while the tube is in place, though speaking valves and downsizing can help.
