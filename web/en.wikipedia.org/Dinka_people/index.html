Dinka people

The Dinka people (Dinka: Jiɛ̈ɛ̈ŋ) are a Nilotic ethnic group native to South Sudan, living along the Nile Basin, especially in the Bahr el Ghazal region, the Upper Nile, and the Abyei area. They are the largest ethnic group in South Sudan and speak the Dinka language, a group of closely related Western Nilotic languages.

Cattle are central to Dinka culture, wealth, and identity.

Culture and society

Dinka society is organized around clans and cattle camps. Cattle are central to social life, serving as wealth, bridewealth, and a focus of songs and personal names. The Dinka are noted for their stature, among the tallest peoples in Africa. Traditional religion centers on a high god, Nhialic, and clan divinities, with ritual specialists known as "masters of the fishing spear". Since the nineteenth century, many Dinka have adopted Christianity.

History

The Dinka migrated into their present territories over several centuries, coming into conflict and cooperation with neighboring peoples such as the Nuer and Shilluk. They suffered heavily during the slave raids of the nineteenth century and during the Second Sudanese Civil War (1983–2005), which led to the displacement of thousands, including the "Lost Boys of Sudan". South Sudan became independent in 2011, and Dinka figures have held prominent political roles in the new state.
