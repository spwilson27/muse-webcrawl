Papal infallibility

Papal infallibility Defined: 18 July 1870
Council: First Vatican Council
Document: Pastor aeternus
Proclaiming pope: Pius IX
Scope: Ex cathedra definitions on faith and morals

Papal infallibility is a dogma of the Catholic Church which states that, in virtue of the promise of Jesus to Peter, the pope when he speaks ex cathedra is preserved from the possibility of error on doctrine "initially given to the apostolic Church and handed down in Scripture and tradition". The doctrine was defined dogmatically at the First Vatican Council of 1869–1870 in the document Pastor aeternus.

Definition

According to the teaching of the First Vatican Council and Catholic tradition, the conditions required for an ex cathedra statement are: (1) the pope must speak as pastor and teacher of all Christians, not merely as a private person or theologian; (2) he must define a doctrine concerning faith or morals; and (3) he must intend the definition to bind the whole Church. Teaching satisfying these conditions is held to be infallible and irreformable of itself (ex sese), and not by the consent of the Church.

History

The idea of papal infallibility developed over centuries, with medieval theologians and canonists debating the limits of papal authority, and gained strength in the Counter-Reformation. The doctrine was nonetheless controversial within the Church: at the First Vatican Council a minority of bishops, including many from Germany, Austria-Hungary, and the United States, considered the definition inopportune, and some — most famously the historian Ignaz von Döllinger — refused to accept it, contributing to the Old Catholic schism.

Ex cathedra statements

Instances universally recognised as ex cathedra definitions are rare. The two clearest examples are Pope Pius IX's definition of the Immaculate Conception of Mary in Ineffabilis Deus (1854), and Pope Pius XII's definition of the Assumption of Mary in Munificentissimus Deus (1950). Catholic theologians debate whether certain other solemn pronouncements, such as the canonisation of saints or earlier definitions, qualify. Papal infallibility does not extend to everything a pope says, to his personal opinions, political judgements, or scientific views, and it is distinct from the broader indefectibility of the Church.
