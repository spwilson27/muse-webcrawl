Collapsar

A collapsar is a collapsed star. The term is most commonly used for the model proposed by Stan Woosley in 1993 for the central engine of long gamma-ray bursts: a rapidly rotating massive star whose core collapses to form a black hole, surrounded by a dense accretion disk whose energy powers relativistic jets.

The collapsar model

In the collapsar scenario, a massive star (at least several tens of solar masses) with low metallicity retains enough angular momentum that, when its iron core collapses, the infalling material does not fall directly into the newborn black hole but forms a hot, dense disk. Accretion onto the black hole, together with processes such as neutrino annihilation and magnetohydrodynamic effects, launches narrow jets along the rotation axis. If the jets break out of the star, they produce a long-duration gamma-ray burst and a bright, broad-lined Type Ic supernova (sometimes called a hypernova).

Evidence and variants

The association of long gamma-ray bursts with star-forming regions and with energetic supernovae supports the collapsar picture, distinguishing them from short bursts produced by neutron-star mergers. Variants include collapsars that produce magnetars instead of black holes, and "failed supernovae", in which the jet does not escape and much of the star falls into the hole with little explosion. Collapsar disks are also candidate sites of rapid neutron-capture (r-process) nucleosynthesis, contributing heavy elements such as gold to the universe.
