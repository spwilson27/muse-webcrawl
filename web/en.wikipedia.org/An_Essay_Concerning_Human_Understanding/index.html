An Essay Concerning Human Understanding

An Essay Concerning Human Understanding is a work by the English philosopher John Locke, first published in December 1689 (dated 1690), concerning the foundation of human knowledge and understanding. It is considered one of the principal sources of modern empiricism and a landmark of Enlightenment thought. Locke contrasts his account with the rationalism of the continental tradition and argues against the doctrine of innate ideas.

Contents

The Essay is divided into four books. Book I argues that there are no innate principles or ideas in the mind. Book II holds that all ideas derive from experience, either from sensation (of external objects) or from reflection (on the mind's own operations); the mind at birth is a blank slate, a tabula rasa. Book II also introduces the distinction between primary qualities (such as solidity, extension, and figure, which resemble qualities in objects themselves) and secondary qualities (such as colors, sounds, and tastes, which exist as powers in objects to produce ideas in us). Book III treats language and the imperfections and abuses of words, and Book IV defines knowledge, distinguishes its degrees, and examines probability, faith, and enthusiasm.

Influence

The Essay shaped the work of later empiricists such as George Berkeley and David Hume, influenced the philosophy of the Enlightenment, and helped found British empiricism. Its chapter "Of Identity and Diversity," added in the second edition, is the classic starting point for modern debate about personal identity. Leibniz wrote his New Essays on Human Understanding as a point-by-point reply.
