Cinder cone

A cinder cone (also called a scoria cone) is a steep conical hill of loose pyroclastic fragments, such as volcanic clinkers and scoria, that have been built around a volcanic vent. Cinder cones are among the smallest and most common volcanic landforms.

Formation

Cinder cones form from Strombolian eruptions, in which gas-charged lava is thrown into the air and falls back around the vent, piling up at the angle of repose—typically about 33 degrees. The fragments weld lightly together and often show a bowl-shaped crater at the summit.

Parícutin

The most famous modern example is Parícutin in Michoacán, Mexico, which grew from a cornfield beginning in 1943, rising hundreds of metres within a year and continuing to erupt until 1952. Its birth was observed and documented from the first day, making it a landmark case study in volcanology. See also the related topics of Parícutin's setting in Michoacán and its classification as a monogenetic volcanic field.

Occurrence

Cinder cones commonly occur on the flanks of shield volcanoes and in volcanic fields such as the San Francisco volcanic field in Arizona.
