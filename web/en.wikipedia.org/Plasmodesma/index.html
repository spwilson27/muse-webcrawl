Plasmodesma

Plasmodesmata (singular: plasmodesma) are microscopic channels which traverse the cell walls of plant cells and some algal cells, enabling transport and communication between them. Unlike the gap junctions of animal cells, plant cells are encased in rigid cell walls, and plasmodesmata provide the principal route for direct cell-to-cell exchange of molecules. Species with plasmodesmata include members of the Charophyceae, Charales, Coleochaetales, Phaeophyceae, and land plants.

Structure

A typical plasmodesma is a roughly cylindrical, membrane-lined pore about 20–40 nm in diameter. Running through its centre is the desmotubule, a strand of appressed endoplasmic reticulum continuous with the ER of the connected cells. Between the desmotubule and the plasma membrane lining the pore is the cytoplasmic sleeve, through which most transport occurs. Depositions of callose around the pore neck can constrict the channel and regulate its permeability.

Function

Plasmodesmata allow small molecules such as sugars, amino acids, ions, and signalling molecules to pass between cells; under some conditions they dilate to permit the passage of proteins, RNA, and even viral genomes, a property exploited by plant viruses to spread through a host. Networks of cells connected by plasmodesmata form a symplastic domain, distinct from the apoplast outside the cells. Transport through plasmodesmata is essential to development, defence signalling, and the loading of sugars into the phloem.

Formation

Primary plasmodesmata are formed during cell division, when strands of the endoplasmic reticulum are trapped in the developing cell plate. Secondary plasmodesmata can be inserted into existing walls between mature cells, including across graft unions.
