The Viterbi algorithm is a dynamic programming algorithm for obtaining the maximum a posteriori probability estimate of the most likely sequence of hidden states — called the Viterbi path — that results in a sequence of observed events. It is especially used for Markov information sources and hidden Markov models (HMM).

The algorithm was proposed by Andrew Viterbi in 1967 as a decoding method for convolutional codes over noisy digital communication links. It has since found wide application in decoding convolutional codes used in cellular telephony, satellite and deep-space communications, and in speech recognition, computational linguistics and bioinformatics.

Principle

The algorithm works on a trellis of states over time. For each state at each time step it keeps the probability of the most likely path reaching that state, computed recursively from the previous step: the probability of the best path to state j at time t is the maximum, over predecessor states i, of the best-path probability to i, multiplied by the transition probability from i to j and the emission probability of the observed symbol from j. Alongside each probability, a back-pointer records the winning predecessor; at the end, the path is recovered by backtracking from the most likely final state.

Applications

Beyond channel decoding — where Viterbi decoders were central to early CDMA cell phones and to missions such as Voyager — the algorithm is the standard inference step for part-of-speech tagging in natural language processing, gene finding and protein sequence analysis with profile HMMs, and handwriting and speech recognition.
