Thoroughbred

Thoroughbred
Country of origin: England
Height: typically 15.2–17.0 hands (157–173 cm)
Use: Horse racing, show jumping, dressage, eventing
Traits: Hot-blooded, athletic, spirited

The Thoroughbred is a horse breed developed for horse racing in 17th- and 18th-century England, where native mares were crossbred with imported Oriental stallions of Arabian, Barb, and Turkoman breeding. Although the word "thoroughbred" is sometimes used generically for any purebred horse, it technically refers only to this breed, whose pedigree is recorded in the General Stud Book. Thoroughbreds are considered "hot-blooded" horses, known for their agility, speed, and spirit, and they form the foundation of modern horse racing worldwide.

History

All modern Thoroughbreds trace their pedigrees to three foundation sires imported into England around the turn of the 18th century: the Byerley Turk, the Darley Arabian, and the Godolphin Arabian. Remarkably, genetic studies show that some 95% of modern Thoroughbred male lines descend from a single stallion, the Darley Arabian, through his great-great-grandson Eclipse (1764), an undefeated racehorse. James Weatherby published the first volume of the General Stud Book in 1791, and the breed's pedigree has been closed to outside blood since.

Characteristics

Thoroughbreds typically stand 15.2 to 17.0 hands high and weigh around 450–550 kg. They have a refined head, long neck, deep chest, short back, and long, lean legs built for speed. Coat colours include bay, chestnut, brown, black, and grey; white markings are common. Bred for racing distances of roughly 5 furlongs to over 2 miles, the breed excels at the gallop, and top racehorses can exceed 65 km/h.

Uses and influence

Besides flat and jump racing, Thoroughbreds compete in eventing, show jumping, dressage, polo, and fox hunting. The breed has been used to improve many other breeds and to found new ones, including the Standardbred, the American Quarter Horse, and numerous warmblood breeds. Retired racehorses ("off-the-track Thoroughbreds") are commonly retrained for equestrian sport. The breed's emphasis on speed has raised welfare concerns, including a relatively high incidence of musculoskeletal injury and exercise-induced pulmonary haemorrhage.
