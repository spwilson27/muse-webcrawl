Liberalism is a political and moral philosophy based on the rights of the individual, liberty, consent of the governed, political equality, the right to private property and equality before the law. Liberals espouse various and often conflicting views depending on their understanding of these principles, but generally support private property, market economies, individual rights (including civil rights and human rights), liberal democracy, secularism, rule of law, economic and political freedom, freedom of speech, freedom of the press, freedom of assembly and freedom of religion.

History

Liberalism grew out of the Age of Enlightenment, challenging hereditary privilege, state religion and absolute monarchy. John Locke's Two Treatises of Government (1689) supplied its foundational argument for natural rights and government by consent. The philosophy powered the American and French Revolutions and spread through the 19th century with campaigns for free trade, abolition of slavery, widening suffrage and constitutional government, drawing on the economics of Adam Smith and the utilitarianism of Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, whose On Liberty (1859) remains a canonical statement of the harm principle.

Variants

Classical liberalism stresses limited government, free markets and individual liberty. In the late 19th and 20th centuries, social liberalism — associated with T. H. Green, L. T. Hobhouse and later John Rawls — argued that poverty and ill-health constrain real freedom, justifying welfare provision and some economic regulation; it underpinned the New Deal in the United States and the post-war welfare state in Britain. Neoliberalism, prominent from the 1970s, revived market-oriented policies of deregulation, privatisation and fiscal restraint. In American usage, "liberalism" generally denotes the social-liberal tradition, while in Europe it often retains its classical economic sense.

Criticism

Liberalism has been criticised by conservatives for undermining tradition and social cohesion, by socialists for entrenching private property and inequality, by feminists and postcolonial thinkers for historically excluding women and colonised peoples from its universal promises, and by communitarians for an excessively individualistic picture of the self.
