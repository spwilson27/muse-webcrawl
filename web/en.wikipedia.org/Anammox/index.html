Anammox

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anammox, an abbreviation for "anaerobic ammonium oxidation", is a globally important microbial process of the nitrogen cycle that takes place in many natural environments. The bacteria that perform this process were identified in the Netherlands in the 1990s, having first been observed in a wastewater treatment plant. In the anammox reaction, nitrite and ammonium ions are converted directly into dinitrogen gas and water:

NH4+ + NO2− → N2 + 2 H2O

Biology

Anammox bacteria belong to the phylum Planctomycetota (planctomycetes) and possess a unique membrane-bound organelle, the anammoxosome, in which the reaction occurs. The pathway proceeds through the highly toxic intermediates nitric oxide and hydrazine — anammox bacteria are the only known organisms that produce hydrazine as a normal metabolic intermediate. Their membranes contain unique ladderane lipids, among the densest known biological membranes, which help contain these reactive intermediates. Known genera include Candidatus Brocadia, Kuenenia, and Scalindua, the latter dominating in marine environments.

Significance

In the oceans, anammox is estimated to account for a major share — in some regions around half — of the production of dinitrogen gas, and thus of the conversion of fixed nitrogen back to inert N2, revising earlier models that attributed marine nitrogen loss almost entirely to denitrification.

Applications

Anammox-based processes (such as partial nitritation–anammox systems marketed under names like ANITA Mox and DEMON) are increasingly used in wastewater treatment to remove nitrogen with much lower oxygen demand, no external carbon source, and reduced sludge production compared with conventional nitrification–denitrification.
