Juche

Juche (Korean: 주체, "subject" or "self-reliance") is the official state ideology of North Korea (the Democratic People's Republic of Korea), described by the regime as the application of Marxism–Leninism to Korean conditions and as the guiding idea of the Korean revolution. Developed under the country's founder, Kim Il Sung, Juche emphasises political independence (jaju), economic self-sustenance (jarip), and military self-defence (jawi), together with the centrality of the leader and the masses led by the party.

Origins

Kim Il Sung is officially said to have first articulated the Juche idea in 1930, but it entered public discourse prominently in a speech of 28 December 1955, "On Eliminating Dogmatism and Formalism and Establishing Juche in Ideological Work", in which he criticised the mechanical copying of Soviet models. During the 1960s, as the Sino-Soviet split deepened, Juche was elaborated as North Korea's path of independence from both Moscow and Beijing. Kim Jong Il systematised the doctrine in works such as On the Juche Idea (1982), which presented it as a complete worldview centred on the proposition that "man is the master of everything and decides everything."

Principles

Official formulations stress that the popular masses are the subject of history, but that they can act as such only when united behind the leader (suryŏng) and the Workers' Party of Korea. The ideology underpins the personality cults of the Kim family and, later, the military-first (songun) policy emphasised under Kim Jong Il. North Korea adopted a Juche calendar counting years from Kim Il Sung's birth in 1912, and the Tower of the Juche Idea, completed in Pyongyang in 1982, symbolises the doctrine.

Assessment

Outside analysts variously describe Juche as a nationalist ideology of self-reliance, a legitimating doctrine for hereditary leadership, or a quasi-religious system; in practice North Korea has remained dependent on trade, aid, and energy imports despite the rhetoric of autarky. References to Juche replaced explicit mentions of Marxism–Leninism and later of communism in successive revisions of the North Korean constitution.
