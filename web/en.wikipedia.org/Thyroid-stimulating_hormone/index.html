The HPT axis regulating thyroid hormone levels

Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), also known as thyrotropin, is a pituitary hormone that stimulates the thyroid gland to produce thyroxine (T4) and a smaller amount of triiodothyronine (T3). It is secreted by thyrotroph cells of the anterior pituitary in response to thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) from the hypothalamus.

Physiology

TSH is a glycoprotein composed of alpha and beta subunits; the alpha subunit is shared with other pituitary glycoprotein hormones (LH, FSH and hCG), while the beta subunit is specific. Binding to the TSH receptor on thyroid follicular cells increases iodine uptake and the synthesis and release of thyroid hormones. Rising T3 and T4 levels feed back to suppress TRH and TSH secretion, forming the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis.

Clinical significance

Serum TSH measurement is the standard first-line test of thyroid function. In primary hypothyroidism, TSH is elevated because the failing thyroid no longer suppresses it; in primary hyperthyroidism, TSH is suppressed. A high TSH with normal free T4 defines subclinical hypothyroidism. Rarely, abnormal TSH results from pituitary disease (secondary hypothyroidism, with low or inappropriately normal TSH). Recombinant human TSH (thyrotropin alfa) is used in the follow-up of differentiated thyroid cancer.
