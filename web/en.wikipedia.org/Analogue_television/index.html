Analogue television

Analogue television (or analog television) is the original television technology that uses analogue signals to transmit video and audio. It encodes picture and sound information as continuous variations in a broadcast radio signal, in contrast to digital television, which transmits discrete binary data. Analogue broadcasting dominated from the mid-20th century until the worldwide digital switchover of the 2000s and 2010s.

How it works

An analogue television picture is built by scanning the image into horizontal lines, transmitted sequentially, with synchronisation pulses marking the end of each line and field. Colour systems — NTSC in the Americas, PAL in much of Europe, Africa and Asia, and SECAM in France, Russia and elsewhere — encoded colour information in a manner compatible with earlier black-and-white broadcasts. Audio was typically carried on a separate FM or AM subcarrier within the channel.

Broadcast standards

Standards differed in line count and frame rate: 525-line NTSC at roughly 30 frames per second, and 625-line PAL/SECAM at 25 frames per second, usually interlaced. Reception quality degraded gracefully with distance, producing noise ("snow") and ghosting rather than the abrupt failure characteristic of digital signals.

Switchover

Beginning in the 2000s, countries switched off analogue terrestrial transmitters to free spectrum and improve efficiency. The United States ended full-power analogue broadcasts in 2009 and the United Kingdom completed switchover in 2012; by the 2020s analogue television remained in regular terrestrial use in only a small number of countries.
