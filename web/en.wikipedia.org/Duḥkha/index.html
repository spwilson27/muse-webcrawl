Duḥkha (Sanskrit: दुःख; Pali: dukkha) is an important concept in Buddhism, Hinduism, and Jainism, commonly translated as "suffering", "pain", "unsatisfactoriness", or "stress". In Buddhism, duḥkha is one of the three marks of existence (along with anattā, "non-self", and anicca, "impermanence"), and understanding it is central to the Four Noble Truths taught by the Buddha.

The Four Noble Truths

The Buddha's first sermon set out the Four Noble Truths: (1) there is duḥkha; (2) duḥkha arises from craving (taṇhā); (3) duḥkha can cease; and (4) the path leading to its cessation is the Noble Eightfold Path. The first truth is often rendered "life is suffering", but scholars note that duḥkha is broader, covering everything from acute pain to the subtle unsatisfactoriness of pleasant experiences because they are impermanent.

Three types

Buddhist texts classify duḥkha into three categories: the suffering of suffering (duḥkha-duḥkha), such as illness and old age; the suffering of change (vipariṇāma-duḥkha), the anxiety and loss attached to impermanent pleasures; and all-pervasive conditioned suffering (saṅkhāra-duḥkha), the unsatisfactoriness inherent in conditioned existence itself.

Etymology

One traditional analysis derives duḥkha from dus- ("bad") and kha ("axle-hole"), evoking a wheel whose axle does not fit properly and so runs roughly, in contrast to sukha ("good axle-hole"), ease or happiness.
