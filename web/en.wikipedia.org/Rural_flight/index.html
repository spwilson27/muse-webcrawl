Rural flight

Rural flight (also rural-to-urban migration or rural exodus) is the migratory pattern of people moving from rural areas to urban areas. It is driven by the search for employment, education, services and amenities, and by the declining labour needs of mechanised agriculture. The counterpart processes include urbanisation generally and, in some regions, counter-urbanisation. Rural flight is observed historically on every continent and remains pronounced in many developing economies.

Causes

Mechanisation and consolidation of farms have steadily reduced agricultural employment since the Industrial Revolution. Young people leave for higher education and often do not return, producing ageing rural populations and a "brain drain" of the most educated. Closure of schools, hospitals, shops and transport services follows population decline, encouraging further departures in a self-reinforcing cycle. In Germany, the term Landflucht described the heavy east-to-west rural migration of the late nineteenth century; in East Germany, Republikflucht was the state's term for emigration to the West.

Effects

Depopulating regions face labour shortages, falling land values, difficulties sustaining public services and political marginalisation. In the United States, many Great Plains counties have fewer people than a century ago, with some land reverting toward the "Buffalo Commons" proposed by geographers. Elsewhere, abandoned villages are common in interior Spain (the España vaciada) and parts of Japan, where akiya — empty houses — number in the millions.

Responses

Policies to counter rural flight include rural development subsidies, broadband and infrastructure investment, decentralisation of public employment, remote-work promotion and incentives for return migration. Amenity migration to scenic rural areas and tourism have revived some communities, though often unevenly.
