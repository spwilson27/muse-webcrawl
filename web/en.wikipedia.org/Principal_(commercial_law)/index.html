Principal (commercial law)

A principal authorises an agent to act on their behalf in commerce.

In commercial law, a principal is a party who authorises another party, the agent, to act on their behalf in dealings with third parties, typically to create legal relations such as contracts. The relationship is the foundation of the law of agency: acts performed by the agent within the scope of their authority are, in general, treated in law as acts of the principal.

Authority

An agent's authority may be actual — express, where the principal states it, or implied, where it is incidental to the agent's position — or apparent (ostensible), where the principal's conduct leads a third party reasonably to believe the agent is authorised. Under the doctrine summarised by the maxim qui facit per alium facit per se ("one who acts through another acts oneself"), a disclosed principal is normally bound by contracts the agent makes within authority, and in some circumstances third parties may also hold an undisclosed principal liable once discovered.

Duties and liability

The principal owes the agent duties including payment of agreed commission and indemnification for expenses properly incurred. The principal may be liable in vicarious liability for the agent's torts committed in the course of the agency, and remains liable on contracts even where the agent exceeds instructions, if the agent acted within apparent authority. Conversely, an agent who acts without authority may be liable to the third party for breach of warranty of authority.

Related uses

In commercial practice the term "principal" also denotes a party trading on its own account — for example, a dealer buying and selling securities as principal rather than as broker — and, in finance, the original sum of a loan, as distinct from interest. In company law, undisclosed principals and nominee arrangements raise distinct questions of disclosure and attribution.
