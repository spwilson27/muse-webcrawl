Equivalence (translation)

Equivalence in translation studies

Key theorists
Eugene Nida, Werner Koller, Roman Jakobson
Types
Formal vs. dynamic; denotative, connotative, pragmatic…

In translation studies, equivalence is the relationship between a source text and its translation, describing the extent to which the target text corresponds to or achieves the same effect as the original. The concept was central to mid-20th-century translation theory, though it has since been criticised as ill-defined.

Approaches

Eugene Nida distinguished formal equivalence, which seeks close adherence to the form and content of the source, from dynamic equivalence, which aims to reproduce on target readers the same effect the original had on its readers — an approach influential in Bible translation. Roman Jakobson famously noted "equivalence in difference": there is ordinarily no full equivalence between code-units of different languages. Werner Koller systematised several types: denotative, connotative, text-normative, pragmatic, and formal-aesthetic equivalence.

Criticism

Later scholars, particularly within descriptive translation studies and postcolonial approaches, questioned whether equivalence is achievable or even a coherent theoretical primitive, treating translation instead as rewriting governed by target-culture norms. The term nonetheless remains a working concept in translator training and quality assessment.
