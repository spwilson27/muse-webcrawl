Presumption of innocence

FieldCriminal law; human rights law
Related conceptsBurden of proof; proof beyond a reasonable doubt; right to a fair trial
Key instrumentsUniversal Declaration of Human Rights, Art. 11; ICCPR, Art. 14(2); ECHR, Art. 6(2)

The presumption of innocence is a legal principle according to which every person accused of a crime is considered innocent until proven guilty. It places the burden of proof on the prosecution, which must establish guilt — in many systems to the standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt — while the accused is not required to prove innocence.

History

Antecedents appear in Roman law, in the maxim attributed to the jurist Paulus that proof lies on the accuser rather than the accused, and in medieval canon law. The principle was forcefully articulated during the Enlightenment, notably by Cesare Beccaria in On Crimes and Punishments (1764), and was enshrined in Article 9 of the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789).

International law

Article 11 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) provides that everyone charged with a penal offence has the right to be presumed innocent until proved guilty according to law in a public trial. The same guarantee appears in the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, the European Convention on Human Rights, the American Convention on Human Rights, and the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union.

Consequences

The presumption underlies allied rights such as the right to silence, the privilege against self-incrimination, and restrictions on pre-trial detention and on public statements by officials declaring an accused person guilty before conviction. In some jurisdictions the principle has been qualified for certain offences by reverse burdens of proof, which remain a subject of legal controversy.
