Andromeda–Milky Way collision

The Andromeda–Milky Way collision is a predicted galactic collision between the Andromeda Galaxy (M31) and the Milky Way, expected to occur in roughly 4.5 to 5 billion years. The two galaxies are approaching each other at about 110 km/s, as shown by the blueshift of Andromeda's spectral lines.

Course of the merger

Measurements from the Hubble Space Telescope (2012) of Andromeda's proper motion confirmed that the galaxies are on a nearly head-on course. As they pass through each other repeatedly, tidal forces will distort both spiral galaxies; individual stars almost never collide because of the vast distances between them, but gas clouds will collide and trigger bursts of star formation. The remnants will eventually settle into a single giant elliptical or lenticular galaxy, sometimes nicknamed "Milkomeda" or "Milkdromeda."

Fate of the Solar System

Simulations suggest the Solar System will likely be flung farther from the galactic centre but has a small chance of being ejected or drawn toward the core. By the time of the merger, the Sun's increasing luminosity will already have made Earth's surface uninhabitable.

Uncertainty

The Triangulum Galaxy (M33) and the Large Magellanic Cloud may also participate; recent Gaia-based studies have suggested the merger's timing and even likelihood could shift, with some analyses finding a significant chance the two galaxies pass without merging in the next 10 billion years.
