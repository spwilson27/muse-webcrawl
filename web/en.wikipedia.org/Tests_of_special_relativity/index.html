Tests of special relativity

The Michelson–Morley experiment, a foundational test

Tests of special relativity constitute a series of experiments examining the predictions of Albert Einstein's 1905 theory—principally the constancy of the speed of light, time dilation, length contraction, and the equivalence of mass and energy. Special relativity is among the most thoroughly verified theories in physics.

Classic experiments

The Michelson–Morley experiment (1887) found no evidence of the luminiferous aether wind expected by classical physics, providing the null result that special relativity naturally explained. The Kennedy–Thorndike experiment (1932) tested with unequal interferometer arms, and the Ives–Stilwell experiment (1938) directly measured the transverse Doppler effect, confirming relativistic time dilation. The Fizeau experiment and stellar aberration further probed the speed of light in moving media.

Modern tests

Time dilation is verified routinely: unstable particles such as muons in cosmic rays and particle accelerators survive far longer than their rest lifetimes would allow, and atomic clocks flown around the world (the Hafele–Keating experiment) matched relativistic predictions. GPS satellites must correct for both special and general relativistic effects to function. Modern Michelson–Morley-type experiments with optical resonators constrain any anisotropy in the speed of light to parts in 10^17, while test theories such as the Robertson–Mansouri–Sexl framework and the Standard-Model Extension organize the search for possible violations.
