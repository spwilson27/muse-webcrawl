G-force

The g-force or gravitational force equivalent is a measurement of the type of force per unit mass — typically acceleration — that causes a perception of weight, with a g-force of 1 g (not gram) equal to the conventional value of gravitational acceleration on Earth, g, of about 9.8 m/s². Since g-forces indirectly produce weight, any g-force can be described as a "weight per unit mass".

When the g-force is produced by the surface of one object being pushed by the surface of another object, the reaction force to this push produces an equal and opposite weight for every unit of each object's mass. The types of forces involved are transmitted through objects by interior mechanical stresses. Gravitational acceleration (except certain electromagnetic force influences) is the cause of an object's acceleration in relation to free fall.

Definition

The g-force acting on an object is the vector sum of all non-gravitational forces acting on the object, divided by the magnitude of standard gravity. An object at rest on Earth's surface experiences 1 g upward from the ground's normal force, which counteracts gravity; an object in free fall experiences 0 g, which is why astronauts in orbit feel weightless even though gravity there is nearly as strong as on the surface.

Human tolerance

Human tolerance of g-forces depends on magnitude, duration, and direction. Sustained positive g-forces (blood driven toward the feet) cause visual symptoms, "blackout", and g-LOC (g-induced loss of consciousness) at around 4–6 g for untrained people; fighter pilots with anti-g suits and training can withstand about 9 g. Negative g (blood driven toward the head) is tolerated far worse. John Stapp famously survived a peak of 46.2 g in rocket-sled experiments in 1954, and Formula One drivers have survived brief crash peaks far higher still.

Measurement and applications

Accelerometers measure proper acceleration and hence g-force. G-forces matter in aerospace medicine, roller-coaster design, automotive safety, and packaging of shock-sensitive goods; typical values range from about 0.2 g in a braking car to 5 g on a launched roller coaster and 8–12 g in a hard aircraft manoeuvre.
