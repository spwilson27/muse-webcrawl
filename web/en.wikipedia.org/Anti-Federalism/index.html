Anti-Federalism

Anti-Federalism was a late-18th-century political movement in the United States that opposed the creation of a stronger federal government and the ratification of the 1787 Constitution, preferring instead the decentralized system of the Articles of Confederation. Anti-Federalists feared that a powerful central government would threaten individual liberties and state sovereignty.

History

After the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia proposed a new Constitution in 1787, debate over ratification divided Americans into Federalists, who supported the document, and Anti-Federalists, who opposed it. Anti-Federalists published essays under pseudonyms such as "Brutus", "Cato", and "Centinel", arguing against the proposed presidency, the federal judiciary, standing armies, and the absence of a bill of rights.

Major arguments

Anti-Federalists contended that a republic could only function in a small territory, drawing on Montesquieu, and warned that the necessary and proper clause and the supremacy clause would allow Congress to swallow the states. They demanded explicit protections for freedom of speech, religion, and the press, trial by jury, and limits on direct taxation.

Legacy

Although the Constitution was ratified in 1788, Anti-Federalist pressure led directly to the adoption of the Bill of Rights in 1791. Many Anti-Federalists later joined Thomas Jefferson's Democratic-Republican Party. Their writings remain an important source for interpreting the Constitution and American federalism.
