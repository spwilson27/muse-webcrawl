Helen Adams Keller (27 June 1880 – 1 June 1968) was an American author, disability rights advocate, political activist and lecturer. Born in Tuscumbia, Alabama, she lost both sight and hearing after an illness at the age of nineteen months and became the first deafblind person in the United States to earn a Bachelor of Arts degree.

Early life and education

In 1887 the Keller family hired Anne Sullivan, a young teacher from the Perkins School for the Blind, to instruct Helen. The breakthrough came at the water pump at the family home, when Keller suddenly grasped that the signs Sullivan had been spelling into her hand named the water flowing over her other hand — the insight that "everything had a name". Keller went on to learn to read Braille, to speak, and to read lips by touch. She attended Radcliffe College and graduated cum laude in 1904.

Writings and activism

Her autobiography The Story of My Life (1903) became a classic and was later dramatised as the play and film The Miracle Worker. Keller wrote a dozen books, travelled and lectured in many countries, and worked for the American Foundation for the Blind for over forty years. She was also a committed political activist: a member of the Socialist Party, she campaigned for women's suffrage, labour rights and birth control, supported causes including the NAACP, and co-founded the American Civil Liberties Union in 1920.

Legacy

Keller received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1964. Her life remains a symbol of the educational potential of deafblind people, and 27 June is commemorated as Helen Keller Day in Pennsylvania.
