Arts and Crafts movement

The Arts and Crafts movement was an international trend in the decorative and fine arts that developed earliest and most fully in the British Isles and subsequently spread across the British Empire and to the rest of Europe and America. Initiated in reaction against the perceived impoverishment of the decorative arts and the conditions in which they were produced, the movement flourished in Europe and North America between about 1880 and 1920.

Origins

The movement took its name from the Arts and Crafts Exhibition Society, founded in London in 1887. Its intellectual roots lay in the writings of John Ruskin, whose critique of industrial production in The Stones of Venice (1853) championed Gothic craftsmanship, and in the work of William Morris, who put theory into practice by founding the decorative-arts firm Morris & Co. in 1861.

Principles

The movement stood for traditional craftsmanship using simple forms, truth to materials, and often medieval, romantic, or folk styles of decoration. It advocated a return to a pre-industrial mode of production and a revaluation of the dignity of hand labour, reacting against both factory production and the ornate excess of Victorian design.

Key figures

  • William Morris — textile and wallpaper designer, founder of Morris & Co. and the Kelmscott Press
  • John Ruskin — critic and theorist of the Gothic
  • Charles Robert Ashbee — designer and founder of the Guild of Handicraft
  • Charles Rennie Mackintosh — Scottish architect and designer associated with the Glasgow School
  • Gustav Stickley — leading figure of the American Craftsman style

Influence

Arts and Crafts ideas influenced Art Nouveau, the Bauhaus, and modern design more broadly. In architecture it promoted the English domestic revival — red-brick, tile-hung houses with generous gardens — and in America the bungalow and the Craftsman style. Its ideals of honest materials and integrated design remain influential.
