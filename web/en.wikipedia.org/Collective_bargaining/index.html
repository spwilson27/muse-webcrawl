Collective bargaining

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Collective bargaining is a process of negotiation between employers and a group of employees aimed at agreements to regulate working salaries, working conditions, benefits, and other aspects of workers' compensation and rights. Employees are typically represented by a trade union. The agreements reached are called collective bargaining agreements (CBAs) or collective labour agreements.

History

The term was coined in 1891 by British labour historian Beatrice Webb, though negotiated agreements between employers and organised workers emerged with the growth of trade unionism in the nineteenth century. In the United States, the National Labor Relations Act of 1935 (the Wagner Act) established legal protections for collective bargaining in the private sector, while in much of Europe sector-wide bargaining between unions and employer associations became the norm after the Second World War.

Process

Bargaining typically proceeds through proposals and counter-proposals on mandatory subjects such as wages, hours, and terms of employment. Parties generally have a legal duty to bargain in good faith. If negotiations deadlock, workers may resort to industrial action such as strikes, while employers may lock workers out; mediation, fact-finding, and arbitration are commonly used to resolve impasses.

International recognition

The right to collective bargaining is recognised as a fundamental right by the International Labour Organization, notably in Conventions No. 87 (freedom of association) and No. 98 (the right to organise and bargain collectively, 1949). It is also affirmed in Article 23 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and in regional instruments such as the European Social Charter.

Coverage and debate

Bargaining coverage varies widely: it is extensive in much of continental Europe, where agreements are often extended across whole industries, and narrower in the United States and United Kingdom, where bargaining is usually at enterprise level and union density has declined. Economists debate the effects of collective bargaining on wages, employment, and firm flexibility; studies generally find that it raises members' wages and compresses wage inequality, with contested macroeconomic effects.
