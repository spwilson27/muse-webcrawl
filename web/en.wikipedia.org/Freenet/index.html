Freenet is a peer-to-peer platform for censorship-resistant, anonymous communication. It uses a decentralized distributed data store to keep and deliver information, and has a suite of free software for publishing and communicating on the Web without fear of censorship. Both Freenet and some of its associated tools were originally designed by Ian Clarke, who defined Freenet's goal as providing freedom of speech, with strong anonymity protection. In 2023 the project was renamed Hyphanet, following a dispute over the Freenet name with a similarly named organisation.

History and design

Freenet originated as Clarke's student project at the University of Edinburgh, and the first version was released in March 2000. The network stores encrypted blocks of data distributed across participating computers; each node contributes bandwidth and a portion of its hard drive (the "data store") to cache content, and requests are routed through several nodes so that neither the requester nor the publisher of a piece of content is readily identifiable. Content addressed by cryptographic keys (Content Hash Keys and Signed Subspace Keys) provides tamper-evident publishing: "freesites" (websites hosted inside the network), forums, and file sharing are built on top of the store.

Opennet and darknet modes

Freenet supports two modes of operation. In opennet mode, the node automatically discovers and connects to strangers' nodes, which is convenient but more vulnerable to harvesting and blocking. In darknet (friend-to-friend) mode, the user connects only to trusted friends, making the network's very existence much harder to detect and enumerate. This hybrid design was intended to let users trade off ease of use against resistance to hostile monitoring.

Reception

Freenet has been praised by privacy advocates and used by journalists, dissidents, and whistleblowers in countries with heavy internet censorship, and it has received awards for its contributions to free expression. Like other anonymity networks, it has also been criticised because the same anonymity can shelter illicit material, a concern its developers answer by arguing that freedom of communication is a prerequisite for democracy and cannot be safely limited to approved speech.
