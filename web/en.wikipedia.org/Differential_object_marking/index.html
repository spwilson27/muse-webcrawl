Differential object marking

Objects higher in animacy and definiteness are more likely to be marked.

Differential object marking (DOM) is a linguistic phenomenon in which the direct object of a verb receives overt case marking (or adpositional marking) only under certain conditions, while other direct objects are unmarked. It is found in hundreds of the world's languages — the term was coined in the typological literature building on work by Georg Bossong in the 1980s — and is the object-side counterpart of differential subject marking.

Examples

In Spanish, human and specific direct objects are preceded by the "personal a": Veo a María ("I see María") but Veo la casa ("I see the house"). In Persian, definite objects take the marker -rā. Hebrew marks definite objects with et, Turkish marks specific objects with the accusative case, and Hindi-Urdu marks animate and definite objects with -ko. Romanian uses pe for human objects, often together with clitic doubling.

Conditioning factors

Which objects get marked follows cross-linguistic prominence scales: first and second person pronouns outrank third person pronouns, which outrank proper names, humans, animates, and finally inanimates; similarly definites outrank specific indefinites, which outrank non-specifics. Languages draw the line at different points on these scales, and the same scales govern related phenomena such as split ergativity and differential agreement.

Explanations

Two functional explanations dominate. On the distinguishing account, marking flags objects that resemble typical subjects (animate, definite, topical), reducing ambiguity about who did what to whom. On the indexing/prominence account, marking simply signals that the object is highly prominent. Formal analyses in generative grammar relate DOM to structural position, case licensing, and object movement out of the verb phrase.
