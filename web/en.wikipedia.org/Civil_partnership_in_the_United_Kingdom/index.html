Civil partnership in the United Kingdom

Civil partnerships in the United Kingdom, granted under the Civil Partnership Act 2004, give couples legal recognition of their relationship with rights and responsibilities similar to those of marriage. The scheme was introduced for same-sex couples because marriage was not then open to them; following a Supreme Court ruling and further legislation, civil partnerships were extended to opposite-sex couples in England and Wales in 2019, and subsequently elsewhere in the UK.

History

The Civil Partnership Act 2004 received royal assent in November 2004 and came into force on 5 December 2005, with the first registrations taking place later that month. When same-sex marriage was legalised in England, Wales and Scotland (2013–14), civil partnerships remained available, and existing partners could convert their partnership into a marriage.

Legal effects

Civil partners have the same property rights as married spouses, similar social security and pension benefits, parental responsibility for a partner's children, and next-of-kin rights. A partnership is ended by dissolution, which follows a process similar to divorce.

Extension to opposite-sex couples

In 2018 the Supreme Court held that restricting civil partnerships to same-sex couples was incompatible with the European Convention on Human Rights. The government responded by extending eligibility to opposite-sex couples, with the first such registrations in England and Wales on 31 December 2019.
