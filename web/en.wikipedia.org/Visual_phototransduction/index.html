Visual phototransduction

The phototransduction cascade in a rod photoreceptor

Location: Retina (rods and cones)

Key molecules: Rhodopsin, transducin, phosphodiesterase, cyclic GMP

Visual phototransduction is the process by which light is converted into electrical signals in the rod cells, cone cells, and photosensitive ganglion cells of the retina of the eye. It is the first step of vision: photons absorbed by visual pigments trigger a biochemical cascade that changes the photoreceptor's membrane potential.

The cascade

In rods, the pigment rhodopsin consists of the protein opsin bound to 11-cis retinal. Absorption of a photon isomerizes the retinal to all-trans, converting rhodopsin to its active form, metarhodopsin II. This activates the G protein transducin, which in turn activates a phosphodiesterase (PDE6) that hydrolyses cyclic GMP. Falling cGMP levels close cyclic nucleotide-gated channels in the cell membrane, stopping the inward "dark current" of sodium and calcium ions and hyperpolarizing the cell. The photoreceptor then releases less of the neurotransmitter glutamate onto bipolar cells, signalling that light has been detected.

Termination and adaptation

The response is shut off by phosphorylation of rhodopsin by rhodopsin kinase and binding of arrestin, by GTP hydrolysis in transducin, and by calcium feedback that restores cGMP levels — mechanisms that also underlie light adaptation, allowing vision over an enormous range of intensities.
