Ba'ath Party


Flag and emblem of the Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party.

The Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party (Arabic: حزب البعث العربي الاشتراكي, Ḥizb al-Baʿth al-ʿArabī al-Ishtirākī; "Ba'ath" means "resurrection" or "renaissance") is a political party founded in Syria in 1947 that advocated pan-Arab nationalism, Arab socialism and secularism. Its stated aim was the unification of the Arab world into a single state. Branches of the party took power in Iraq (1968–2003) and in Syria (1963–2024), but the two regimes became bitter rivals after the party split in 1966, and each claimed to be the party's legitimate leadership.

Founding and ideology

The party was founded in Damascus on 7 April 1947 by Michel Aflaq, a Greek Orthodox Christian intellectual, Salah al-Din al-Bitar, a Sunni Muslim, and Zaki al-Arsuzi, whose Arab Ba'ath movement merged with Aflaq and Bitar's organisation. Its slogan was "Unity, Freedom, Socialism" (Waḥda, Ḥurriya, Ishtirākiyya) — the unity of the Arab nation, freedom from colonial domination, and a specifically Arab form of socialism distinct from Marxist internationalism. Aflaq, the party's chief ideologue, envisioned the Ba'ath as a vanguard movement that would revive the Arab nation in the way the original Islamic mission of the Prophet Muhammad had, in his secular reading, created an Arab nation in the seventh century.

In 1953 the party merged with Akram al-Hawrani's Arab Socialist Party to form the Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party. It participated in the union with Egypt as the United Arab Republic (1958–1961), but the subordination of Syrian parties under Gamal Abdel Nasser's rule strained the organisation, and it was Syria's secession from the union that pushed younger Ba'athist officers toward seizing power themselves.

In power

Ba'athist military committees seized power in Iraq (briefly in 1963, and durably from 1968) and in Syria in the coup of 8 March 1963. In 1966 a radical faction overthrew the party's old guard in Damascus; Aflaq and Bitar were expelled, and the party split into rival Syrian- and Iraqi-dominated movements, each with its own "National Command". The Syrian branch, after Hafez al-Assad's "Corrective Movement" of 1970, became the vehicle of the Assad family's rule; the Iraqi branch became the instrument of Saddam Hussein, who took the presidency in 1979.

Both Ba'athist states were one-party regimes that used extensive security services to suppress opposition, while pursuing state-led development, land reform and mass literacy and education programmes. Iraq's Ba'ath government was overthrown by the United States-led invasion of 2003, after which the party was banned and its members purged under de-Ba'athification policy. In Syria, the party remained the ruling organisation — constitutionally the "leading party in society and state" until 2012 — throughout Bashar al-Assad's presidency until the fall of the Assad regime in December 2024, when the party suspended its activities.

Organisation

The party was organised hierarchically, with cells, sections, branches and a Regional Command governing each country ("region", in Ba'athist terminology, since the party regarded Arab states as regions of one nation), topped in theory by a National Command for the whole Arab world. Its membership numbered in the millions in Syria and Iraq, where joining was often a prerequisite for public employment and promotion, although genuine ideological commitment varied widely.
