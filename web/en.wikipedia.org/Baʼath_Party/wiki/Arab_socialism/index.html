Arab socialism

Arab socialism (Arabic: الاشتراكية العربية, romanized: al-ishtirākiyya al-ʿarabiyya) is a political ideology based on a synthesis of pan-Arabism and socialism. It differs from Marxism in that it accepts private property within limits, rejects class struggle as divisive of the Arab nation, and grounds its appeal in Arab heritage and, for many adherents, in Islam's egalitarian traditions.

Ideology

The concept was developed largely by Michel Aflaq, co-founder of the Ba'ath Party, who saw socialism as the necessary economic expression of Arab unity: a single Arab nation could not be free while exploited by feudalism, colonial monopolies, and foreign capital. Arab socialism advocated land reform, nationalization of natural resources, large industries, banking, and foreign trade, while permitting small-scale private ownership. Unlike Marxist-Leninist doctrine, it treated religion as a living cultural force of the Arab nation rather than an obstacle.

In practice

Arab socialism was the official ideology of Ba'athist Syria and Iraq, and of Nasser's Egypt, where it was codified in the 1962 National Charter. Governments pursued agrarian reform, public-sector industrialization, free education and healthcare, and extensive state planning. Critics argued that in practice the system degenerated into bureaucratic state capitalism dominated by military and party elites. After Nasser's death, Egypt under Anwar Sadat reversed many measures with the infitah (opening) policy, while Syria and Iraq gradually liberalized parts of their economies as well.
