Ba'athism

Ba'athism (Arabic: البعثية, romanized: al-baʿthiyya, from بعث baʿth, meaning "renaissance" or "resurrection") is the ideology of the Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party. It combines Arab nationalism, pan-Arabism, Arab socialism, and anti-imperialism, summarized in the party slogan "Unity, Freedom, Socialism" (Arabic: وحدة، حرية، اشتراكية).

Foundations

Ba'athism was formulated in the 1940s by Michel Aflaq, Salah al-Din al-Bitar, and Zaki al-Arsuzi in Syria. Aflaq, the movement's principal theorist, held that the Arab nation was eternal and that its "resurrection" required a single party that would lead a comprehensive revolution in politics, economics, and culture. The Ba'ath Party was formally founded in Damascus in 1947 and merged with Akram al-Hawrani's Arab Socialist Party in 1952.

In power

Ba'athists seized power in Iraq in 1963 (briefly) and again in 1968, and in Syria in 1963. A bitter schism in 1966 split the movement into rival Syrian- and Iraqi-dominated factions, each claiming to be the legitimate party. In Iraq, Ba'athism became associated with Saddam Hussein, who ruled from 1979 until the 2003 invasion. In Syria, Hafez al-Assad's 1970 "Corrective Movement" produced a more pragmatic, state-centered variant, and the party remained the ruling force under Bashar al-Assad until the fall of the regime in December 2024.

Criticism

Critics contend that in practice Ba'athist governments became authoritarian one-party states in which the ideology served chiefly to legitimize military rule, personality cults, and security apparatuses, and that pan-Arab unity was never achieved.
