The Law of the Twelve Tables (Latin: Leges Duodecim Tabularum) was the legislation that stood at the foundation of Roman law. Formulated in 451–450 BC, the Tables formed the centerpiece of the constitution of the early Roman Republic and were the first written laws accessible to all Roman citizens, produced as a result of the Conflict of the Orders between plebeians and patricians, who had previously monopolized legal interpretation.

Creation

According to tradition (principally Livy), the plebeians demanded that the laws be written down and published. A board of ten men, the decemviri legibus scribundis, was appointed in 451 BC and produced ten tables; a second board added two more in 450 BC. The laws were inscribed on bronze (or, in some accounts, ivory or wooden) tablets and displayed in the Forum.

Content

The Tables covered legal procedure, property and debt, family law (including the extensive patria potestas of the father), inheritance, land boundaries, torts, and public and sacred law. Provisions included strict rules on debt (a defaulting debtor could, after due process, be sold into slavery abroad), the prohibition of intermarriage between patricians and plebeians (soon repealed by the Lex Canuleia), and fixed penalties for theft and injury. The originals were probably destroyed when the Gauls sacked Rome in 390 BC; their contents survive in quotations by later authors such as Cicero, who called them the "fountainhead of all public and private law" and recalled schoolboys memorizing them.

Legacy

The Twelve Tables established the principle that law should be published and apply to citizens equally, and they remained a revered foundation of Roman jurisprudence, influencing later codifications and, through them, modern civil law systems.
