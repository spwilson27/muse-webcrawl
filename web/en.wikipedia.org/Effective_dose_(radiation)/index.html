Effective dose (radiation)

Effective dose is a dose quantity used in radiation protection to express the overall health risk of exposure to ionizing radiation, taking into account both the type of radiation and the differing sensitivities of the organs and tissues irradiated. It is defined by the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) and measured in sieverts (Sv), usually expressed in millisieverts (mSv).

Calculation

The effective dose E is calculated as the sum over all organs and tissues of the product of the equivalent dose in that tissue and a tissue weighting factor, wT, which represents the relative contribution of that tissue to the total health detriment (primarily cancer risk) from a uniform whole-body exposure. The equivalent dose itself is the absorbed dose multiplied by a radiation weighting factor, wR, that accounts for the biological effectiveness of the radiation type: 1 for photons and electrons, 2 for protons, and 20 for alpha particles.

Tissue weighting factors in the ICRP's 2007 recommendations (Publication 103) include 0.12 each for red bone marrow, colon, lung, stomach and breast; 0.08 for the gonads; and smaller values for less sensitive tissues such as the skin and bone surface (0.01). The factors sum to 1.

Uses

Effective dose is the basis of regulatory dose limits for occupational and public exposure; the ICRP recommends an occupational limit of 20 mSv per year averaged over five years, and a public limit of 1 mSv per year from artificial sources. It is also used to compare the doses from different diagnostic procedures: a chest X-ray delivers roughly 0.02–0.1 mSv, while a CT scan of the abdomen may deliver around 8–10 mSv. Average natural background radiation is about 2.4 mSv per year worldwide.

Limitations

Effective dose is a protection quantity intended for prospective assessment and optimisation for a reference person; it is not measurable directly and is not intended for detailed retrospective assessment of individual risk, particularly at high doses where tissue reactions dominate.
