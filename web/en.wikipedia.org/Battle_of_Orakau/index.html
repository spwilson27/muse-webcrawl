Battle of Ōrākau

Date31 March – 2 April 1864
LocationŌrākau, near modern Kihikihi, Waikato, New Zealand
ResultBritish victory; Māori breakout
BelligerentsBritish Empire and colonial forces vs. Kīngitanga Māori
CommandersBrig. Gen. George Carey vs. Rewi Maniapoto and others

The Battle of Ōrākau (31 March – 2 April 1864) was a famous engagement of the New Zealand Wars, fought during the Invasion of the Waikato. Some 300 Māori defenders of the Kīngitanga (Māori King Movement), including women and children, held a hastily constructed pā at Ōrākau against roughly 1,400–1,800 British and colonial troops for three days. The battle is remembered in New Zealand folklore for the defenders' refusal to surrender — summed up in the celebrated reply attributed to them, "Ka whawhai tonu mātou, ake! ake! ake!" ("We will fight on forever, forever, forever!").

Background

Governor George Grey and Lieutenant-General Duncan Cameron had invaded the Waikato in July 1863 to suppress the Kīngitanga and open Māori land to settlement. After the battles of Rangiriri and the fall of Rangiaowhia in February 1864, a party of Kīngitanga warriors, largely Ngāti Maniapoto, Ngāti Raukawa, Tuhoe and others under chiefs including Rewi Maniapoto, began building a pā at Ōrākau. The position was incomplete — lacking a proper water supply and rifle pits still unfinished — when Cameron's flying column under Brigadier-General George Carey surrounded it on 31 March.

Battle

The defenders repulsed repeated assaults and endured bombardment by Armstrong guns while British sappers dug toward the pā. Without water and short of ammunition, the garrison was reduced to firing peach stones and pieces of wood. On 2 April, asked to surrender or send out the women and children, the defenders refused — tradition holds that Rewi or the chief Hitiri Te Paerata delivered the immortal reply, and the women declared they too would fight. That afternoon the garrison made a sudden breakout through the British lines to the south-east, surprising a weakly held sector. Many were killed in the pursuit across the swamps; estimates suggest about 160 Māori died in total, while British losses were about 17 killed and 50 wounded.

Legacy

Ōrākau effectively ended the Waikato war, though the Kīngitanga retreated into what became the King Country. The stand at Ōrākau became a cornerstone of New Zealand national memory, commemorated by a monument at the site and by generations of school histories; modern scholarship has examined both the accuracy of the famous quotation and the battle's place in the history of land confiscation (raupatu) that followed, under which over a million acres of Waikato land were seized.
