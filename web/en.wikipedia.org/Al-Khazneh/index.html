Al-Khazneh

Al-Khazneh (Arabic: الخزنة, "The Treasury") is one of the most elaborate temples in the ancient Arab Nabataean city of Petra in Jordan. Carved directly out of a sandstone rock face, it is believed to have been built as a mausoleum and crypt in the early 1st century AD, probably for the Nabataean king Aretas IV. It is Petra's most famous monument and a UNESCO World Heritage symbol of Jordan.

Description

The facade is about 39 metres high and 25 metres wide, cut into the rose-red cliff at the end of the narrow gorge known as the Siq. Its two storeys display Hellenistic architecture: the lower level has six Corinthian columns framing a doorway flanked by figures of Castor and Pollux, while the upper level centres on a tholos (circular structure) bearing a figure often identified as the goddess Isis or the Nabataean goddess al-Uzza, flanked by Victories and Amazons.

Name and legend

The Arabic name "the Treasury" comes from a Bedouin legend that an Egyptian pharaoh hid his treasure in the stone urn at the top of the facade; bullet marks on the urn are attributed to attempts to break it open. In fact the urn is solid sandstone.

Interior and archaeology

Behind the facade lies a large square burial chamber with smaller side chambers. Excavations in front of the monument in 2003 uncovered a lower level containing royal tombs.
