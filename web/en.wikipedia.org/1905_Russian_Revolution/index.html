1905 Russian Revolution

Date22 January 1905 – 16 June 1907
LocationRussian Empire
Caused byAutocracy, Russo-Japanese War defeats, Bloody Sunday, economic hardship
ResultOctober Manifesto; creation of the State Duma; 1906 Constitution

The Russian Revolution of 1905, also known as the First Russian Revolution, was a wave of mass political and social unrest that spread through vast areas of the Russian Empire. The unrest included worker strikes, peasant revolts, and military mutinies, directed against the autocracy of Tsar Nicholas II.

Bloody Sunday

The revolution was ignited on 22 January 1905 (9 January Old Style), when troops of the Imperial Guard fired on a peaceful procession of workers led by the priest Georgy Gapon, who were marching to the Winter Palace in St. Petersburg to present a petition to the Tsar. Hundreds were killed or wounded in the event known as Bloody Sunday, which destroyed the traditional image of the Tsar as a benevolent father of his people.

Escalation

News of the massacre sparked strikes and uprisings across the empire. Sailors mutinied aboard the battleship Potemkin in June. In October, a general strike paralyzed the country, and the St. Petersburg Soviet of Workers' Deputies — in which Leon Trotsky played a leading role — emerged as an alternative center of power.

October Manifesto and aftermath

Forced to concede, Nicholas issued the October Manifesto on 30 October 1905, promising civil liberties and an elected legislative body, the State Duma. The Fundamental Laws of 1906 formalized the new order, though the Tsar retained extensive powers. Under Prime Minister Pyotr Stolypin, the government combined repression with agrarian reform. The revolution is often regarded as a "dress rehearsal" for the revolutions of 1917.
