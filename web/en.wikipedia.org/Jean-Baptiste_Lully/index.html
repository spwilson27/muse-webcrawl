Jean-Baptiste Lully

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jean-Baptiste Lully (born Giovanni Battista Lulli; 28 November 1632 – 22 March 1687) was an Italian naturalised French composer, guitarist, violinist, and dancer who spent most of his life working in the court of Louis XIV of France. He is considered a master of the French Baroque style of music. Lully disavowed any Italian influence in French music of the period. He became a French subject in 1661.

Life and career

Lully was born in Florence, Grand Duchy of Tuscany, the son of a miller. Brought to France as a boy in the household of the Chevalier de Guise, he entered the service of the Grande Mademoiselle, Anne Marie Louise d'Orléans, as a kitchen scullion and studied music, becoming a gifted violinist and dancer. In 1653 he danced with the young Louis XIV in the Ballet de la nuit and won the king's favour, entering royal service in the prestigious string orchestra Les Vingt-quatre Violons du Roi.

Lully composed court ballets, incidental music, and, in collaboration with Molière, a series of comédie-ballets including Le Bourgeois gentilhomme (1670). In 1672 he acquired the royal privilege for opera in France and directed the Académie Royale de Musique, gaining near-total control over French operatic production.

Tragédies en musique

With librettist Philippe Quinault, Lully created the tragédie en musique (later called tragédie lyrique), a genre of French opera combining a serious five-act drama based on classical mythology, a prologue glorifying the king, French-style recitative modelled on theatrical declamation, sung choruses and dances, and spectacular stage effects. His operas include Cadmus et Hermione (1673), Atys (1676), Persée (1682), Phaëton (1683), Armide (1686), and Acis et Galatée (1686). The French overture and the chaconne/passacaille forms were codified in his works, which dominated French opera for a century.

Death

Lully died from gangrene in Paris after hitting his foot with his long conducting staff while conducting a Te Deum in January 1687 to celebrate the king's recovery from surgery; the wound became infected and he refused amputation of his toe, dying on 22 March 1687.
