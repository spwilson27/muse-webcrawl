Cancer screening

Cancer screening aims to identify cancer before symptoms appear, or to detect precancerous lesions whose treatment can prevent cancer from developing. Screening may involve blood tests, urine tests, DNA tests, or medical imaging such as mammography. Because early-stage cancers are often more treatable, screening can reduce mortality for some cancers, but it also carries risks including false positives, overdiagnosis, and complications from follow-up procedures.

Principles

Effective screening requires that the disease be an important health problem, that it have a detectable preclinical phase, and that earlier treatment improve outcomes compared with treatment after symptoms arise. Classic criteria were set out by Wilson and Jungner for the World Health Organization in 1968. Screening programmes must also weigh benefits against harms at the population level.

Common screening tests

Well-established programmes include the Pap test and HPV testing for cervical cancer, mammography for breast cancer, colonoscopy and faecal occult blood testing for colorectal cancer, and low-dose CT scanning for lung cancer in heavy smokers. Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing for prostate cancer remains controversial because of overdiagnosis.

Risks and limitations

False-positive results can cause anxiety and unnecessary invasive follow-up; false negatives can falsely reassure. Lead-time bias and length bias can make survival statistics appear better than they are, and overdiagnosis detects cancers that would never have caused harm. Guidelines from bodies such as the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force therefore grade recommendations by the strength of evidence for net benefit.

Whole-body and multi-cancer screening

Newer approaches include multi-cancer early detection blood tests that look for circulating tumour DNA methylation patterns, and whole-body MRI. These remain under evaluation in large trials.
