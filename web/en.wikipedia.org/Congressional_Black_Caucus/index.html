Congressional Black Caucus

The Congressional Black Caucus (CBC) is a caucus within the United States Congress composed of African American members. Founded in March 1971 by thirteen members of the House of Representatives, it describes itself as "the conscience of the Congress" and advocates for legislation addressing civil rights, voting rights, economic opportunity and social welfare. Its membership has grown to roughly sixty members in recent Congresses, including senators and non-voting delegates.

History

The caucus grew out of the informal "Democratic Select Committee" formed in 1969. Its founding members included Shirley Chisholm, the first Black woman elected to Congress, Charles Rangel, John Conyers and future mayor of Washington, D.C., Walter Fauntroy. In 1971 the caucus famously boycotted President Richard Nixon's State of the Union address after he refused a meeting; Nixon subsequently met with them.

Activities

The CBC presents an alternative federal budget in most years, holds an annual legislative conference in Washington, and works through its affiliated Congressional Black Caucus Foundation on research and leadership development.
