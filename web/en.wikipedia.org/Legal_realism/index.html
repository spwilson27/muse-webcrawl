Legal realism

Legal realism is a naturalistic approach to law that holds that law should be understood through the empirical methods of the social sciences, and that judicial decisions are shaped not only by formal legal rules but also by social, economic, and psychological factors. It was the dominant school of legal thought in the United States during the first half of the 20th century, associated with scholars at Yale and Columbia law schools, and it remains influential in the study of how courts actually behave.

Origins

The movement drew on the pragmatism of Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., who wrote in The Path of the Law (1897) that "the life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience" and defined law as a prediction of what courts will do in fact. Roscoe Pound's sociological jurisprudence, which urged attention to law in action rather than law in books, also paved the way.

Core ideas

American legal realists such as Karl Llewellyn, Jerome Frank, and Felix Cohen argued that general rules underdetermine outcomes in individual cases, that judges respond to the facts and equities of disputes as much as to doctrine, and that legal concepts should be studied in terms of their real-world effects. Frank emphasised the uncertainty introduced by fact-finding, while Llewellyn, the movement's chief theorist and principal draftsman of the Uniform Commercial Code, stressed craft norms and the "situation sense" of appellate judges. In Scandinavia, a parallel realist tradition—associated with Axel Hägerström, Alf Ross, and Karl Olivecrona—treated legal rights as metaphysical fictions and analysed law as a set of psychological and social facts.

Legacy

Legal realism shaped the New Deal's regulatory state, modern legal education, and successor movements including law and economics, critical legal studies, and empirical legal research. Critics, notably H. L. A. Hart, charged that realism exaggerated judicial discretion and neglected the internal, rule-guided aspect of legal reasoning, but its insistence that law be studied as it operates in society is now mainstream.
