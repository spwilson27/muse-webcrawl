Anglo-Russian Convention

The Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907, signed on 31 August 1907 in St. Petersburg, settled the outstanding colonial disputes between the British Empire and the Russian Empire in Central Asia — particularly over Persia, Afghanistan, and Tibet — and brought to an end the long rivalry known as the Great Game. Together with the Entente Cordiale (1904) and the Franco-Russian Alliance, it completed the formation of the Triple Entente opposing the Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy.

Terms

Persia was divided into a Russian sphere of influence in the north, a British sphere in the southeast, and a neutral buffer zone between them, though both powers formally recognized Persian independence and integrity. Russia recognized Afghanistan as lying within the British sphere and agreed to conduct relations with Kabul through Britain. Both powers recognized Chinese suzerainty over Tibet and pledged not to interfere in its internal administration.

Negotiations and motives

The convention was negotiated by British foreign secretary Sir Edward Grey and Russian foreign minister Alexander Izvolsky. Britain sought to secure the approaches to India; Russia, weakened by defeat in the Russo-Japanese War and the Revolution of 1905, sought to stabilize its southern flank and improve relations as both governments grew concerned about Germany's rising power and its Baghdad Railway project.

Reception and consequences

The agreement was unpopular in Persia, where it was seen as a partition imposed without Persian consent, fuelling resentment during the Persian Constitutional Revolution. In Britain, some critics attacked the accord with autocratic Russia. Nevertheless it resolved a century of Anglo-Russian friction in Asia and aligned the two empires on the same side in the First World War; the convention lapsed after the Russian Revolution of 1917.
