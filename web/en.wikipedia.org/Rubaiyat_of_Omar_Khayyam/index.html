Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam

An illuminated edition of the Rubaiyat.

The Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam is the title that Edward FitzGerald gave to his 1859 translation from Persian to English of a selection of quatrains (rubāʿiyāt) attributed to Omar Khayyam (1048–1131), a Persian astronomer, mathematician, and poet. A rubāʿī is a four-line poem with a characteristic rhyme scheme, and collections of them circulated under Khayyam's name for centuries after his death.

FitzGerald's translation

FitzGerald's first edition appeared anonymously in 1859 and initially attracted little notice, but it was taken up by the Pre-Raphaelite circle, including Dante Gabriel Rossetti and Algernon Charles Swinburne, and became one of the most popular works of Victorian poetry. FitzGerald published five editions, substantially revising the text; the first edition contains 75 quatrains, the fourth 101.

Themes

The quatrains meditate on the brevity of life, the certainty of death, the pleasures of wine and love, and scepticism toward religious dogma and the promise of an afterlife. Famous lines include:

A Book of Verses underneath the Bough,
A Jug of Wine, a Loaf of Bread — and Thou
Beside me singing in the Wilderness —
Oh, Wilderness were Paradise enow!

Authorship question

Scholars have long debated which quatrains, if any, were actually composed by Khayyam. Manuscripts attribute a growing number to him over the centuries, and modern scholarship generally treats the corpus as a tradition that gathered verses around his name rather than a single authored work.

Influence

The Rubaiyat influenced English literature, music, and popular culture, inspiring parodies, illustrated editions by artists such as Edmund Dulac, and the naming of places and products. A copy of a lavish edition went down with the Titanic in 1912. The work also shaped the founding of the Omar Khayyam Club in London in 1892.
