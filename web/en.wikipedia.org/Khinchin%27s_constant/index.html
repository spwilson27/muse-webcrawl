Khinchin's constant describes the typical geometric mean of continued fraction coefficients.

In number theory, Khinchin's constant is a constant found for almost all real numbers x when one considers the coefficients ai of their continued fraction expansion. Aleksandr Khinchin proved in 1934 that the geometric mean of the first n coefficients of the continued fraction of almost every real number converges, as n tends to infinity, to a constant independent of the choice of x. This limiting value is Khinchin's constant, denoted K0 or K.

Numerically, Khinchin's constant is approximately

K0 = 2.6854520010653064453097148354817956938203822939944629530511523455…

The constant can be expressed as an infinite product over all prime numbers p:

K0 = ∏p (1 + 1/(p(p − 1)))log2 p

Gauss–Kuzmin distribution

The result follows from the Gauss–Kuzmin distribution, which states that the probability that a partial quotient of a randomly chosen real number equals k approaches −log2(1 − 1/(k+1)2) as the number of terms grows. From this distribution one computes the expected value of the logarithm of a partial quotient, which yields the logarithm of Khinchin's constant.

Known exceptional sets

Rational numbers and quadratic irrationals do not obey the theorem, since their continued fractions are finite or periodic. Numbers constructed from bounded coefficient sequences, such as e (whose continued fraction follows a regular pattern), are also exceptions. It is widely conjectured, though not proven, that numbers such as π obey the Khinchin property; numerical experiments agree closely with the theoretical value.

Computation

David H. Bailey and others have computed Khinchin's constant to several thousand decimal digits using the product formula combined with high-precision arithmetic. Despite this, it is not known whether K0 is rational, algebraically irrational, or transcendental.
