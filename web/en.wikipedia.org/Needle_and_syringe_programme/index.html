Needle and syringe programme

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A needle and syringe programme (NSP), also known as a syringe-exchange programme (SEP) or needle exchange programme (NEP), is a social service that allows injecting drug users to obtain clean and unused hypodermic needles and associated paraphernalia at little or no cost, usually exchanging them for used equipment. It is based on the philosophy of harm reduction, which prioritises health outcomes over abstinence.

History and evidence

The first programmes began in Amsterdam in the mid-1980s in response to a hepatitis B outbreak, and expanded rapidly during the HIV epidemic. Programs in cities such as Amsterdam, Sydney, and New York were credited with sharp reductions in new HIV infections among people who inject drugs. A substantial body of research finds that NSPs reduce transmission of HIV and hepatitis C without increasing drug use, crime, or discarded syringes, and they provide referrals into treatment, vaccination, and overdose prevention with naloxone.

Legal status

Provision varies widely: national programmes operate across much of Europe, Australia, and Canada, while in the United States legality historically varied by state and a federal funding ban applied for long periods. WHO, UNAIDS, and the UN Office on Drugs and Crime endorse NSPs as part of a comprehensive package for HIV prevention. See also Harm reduction.
