Windrush generation
OriginCaribbean (and other Commonwealth) migration to Britain, 1948–1971
Named forHMT Empire Windrush

The Windrush generation refers to the people who arrived in the United Kingdom from Caribbean countries between 1948 and 1973, many at the invitation of the British government to help rebuild post-war Britain. The generation is named after the ship HMT Empire Windrush, which docked at Tilbury, Essex, on 22 June 1948 carrying 492 passengers from Jamaica — a date commemorated annually as Windrush Day.

Migration and settlement

Under the British Nationality Act 1948, citizens of British colonies held Citizenship of the United Kingdom and Colonies, with the right to settle in Britain. Caribbean migrants, including many ex-servicemen, filled labour shortages in the new National Health Service, public transport, and industry. Migration continued until the Commonwealth Immigrants Acts of 1962 and 1968 and the Immigration Act 1971 progressively closed the door. The arrivals and their descendants profoundly shaped British culture, from music and carnival (the Notting Hill Carnival was founded in 1964) to sport and politics.

Later injustice

From the 2010s, many members of the generation — despite having lived in Britain lawfully for decades — were caught by "hostile environment" immigration policies, losing jobs, homes, and healthcare, and in some cases being detained or deported, because landing cards proving their arrival had been destroyed by the Home Office and they often lacked paperwork. This became the Windrush scandal of 2018, prompting an official apology, a compensation scheme, and a review by Wendy Williams.
