Prussian Crusade

The Teutonic Order conquered Prussia over five decades of campaigning.

The Prussian Crusade was a series of campaigns in the thirteenth century by German and Polish crusaders, principally the Teutonic Order, to conquer and Christianise the pagan Old Prussians of the Baltic coast. Beginning in earnest in 1230 and effectively complete by 1283, the crusade established the monastic state of the Teutonic Order in Prussia and opened the region to German-speaking settlement.

Background

The Prussians, a Baltic people organised in tribal territories without a unified state, had long raided the neighbouring Polish duchy of Masovia. After failed Polish and Cistercian missions and counter-raids, Duke Konrad of Masovia invited the Teutonic Knights to assist him, granting them the Chełmno Land by the Treaty of Kruszwica (1230). Imperial and papal bulls — the Golden Bull of Rimini (1226) and subsequent privileges — authorised the Order to hold conquered Prussian lands.

Campaigns

The Knights advanced along the Vistula and the Baltic coast, founding strongholds such as Thorn (1231), Elbing (1237) and Königsberg (1255), named for King Ottokar II of Bohemia, who campaigned with the crusade. Conquest proceeded tribe by tribe against fierce resistance, including the Great Prussian Uprising of 1260–1274, triggered by the Order's defeat at Durbe. Seasonal reinforcements of visiting crusaders from across Europe (the Preussenreise tradition) supplemented the Order's brethren. By 1283 the last Prussian tribes had submitted, and the Order turned its attention to Lithuania.

Consequences

Prussian culture and language were gradually displaced: the Old Prussian language survived into the early eighteenth century but died out as German and Polish settlement, colonisation under Kulm law and assimilation proceeded. The crusade remains controversial, remembered in Polish and Lithuanian historiography as German expansionism under a religious banner, and its state — the Teutonic Order's Prussia — shaped the region's politics for centuries.
