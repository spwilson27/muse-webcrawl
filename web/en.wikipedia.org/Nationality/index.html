Nationality

Nationality is a legal identification of a person in international law, establishing the person as a subject, a national, of a sovereign state. It affords the state jurisdiction over the person and the person protection from the state under international law. Article 15 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights states that "Everyone has the right to a nationality" and "No one shall be arbitrarily deprived of his nationality nor denied the right to change his nationality".

Nationality and citizenship

Nationality differs conceptually from citizenship. Citizenship is the domestic legal relationship between a person and a state, carrying specific legal rights and duties within that state — such as voting, working and residing — while nationality is the international legal bond recognised by other states. In many states the terms coincide; in others they do not. For example, British nationality law recognises several classes of national, not all of whom have the right of abode in the United Kingdom, and under United States law, persons born in American Samoa are U.S. nationals but not U.S. citizens at birth.

Acquisition of nationality

Nationality is commonly acquired by:

  • Jus soli ("right of the soil") — birth within the territory of the state, prevalent in the Americas.
  • Jus sanguinis ("right of blood") — descent from a national parent, prevalent in Europe and Asia.
  • Naturalisation — a grant by the state after a period of lawful residence, usually subject to conditions such as language knowledge, good character and an oath of allegiance.
  • Jus matrimonii — acquisition through marriage, now usually not automatic.

Multiple nationality and statelessness

Dual or multiple nationality arises when a person is a national of two or more states, for example by birth in a jus soli country to parents from a jus sanguinis country. States vary in whether they permit, tolerate or restrict plural nationality. At the opposite extreme, a stateless person is recognised as a national by no state; statelessness can result from conflicts of law, state succession, discrimination or deprivation of nationality, and is addressed by the 1954 and 1961 statelessness conventions.

Loss of nationality

Nationality may be lost by voluntary renunciation, by voluntary acquisition of another nationality (in states that prohibit dual nationality), by prolonged residence abroad in some legal systems, or by deprivation — for fraud in naturalisation or, controversially, for conduct such as serving a foreign government or terrorism. Modern international law restricts deprivations that would render a person stateless.

Nationality in international law

Nationality determines which state may exercise diplomatic protection over a person abroad. In the Nottebohm case (1955), the International Court of Justice held that a person's nationality must reflect a "genuine link" with the state to be opposable to third states in that context. Within the European Union, citizenship of the Union derives from member-state nationality and confers free-movement rights across the bloc.
