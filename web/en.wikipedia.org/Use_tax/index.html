Use tax

Use tax commonly arises on goods bought out of state or online for use at home

A use tax is a tax imposed by U.S. state (and some local) governments on the use, storage or consumption of tangible personal property within the jurisdiction when a sales tax was not collected at the time of purchase — most commonly for goods bought in another state or from a remote seller such as an online retailer. It is designed as the complement of the sales tax so that out-of-state purchases do not enjoy a tax advantage over local ones.

How it works

The use tax rate is generally the same as the local sales tax rate, and credit is usually given for sales tax lawfully paid to another state. Whereas vendors collect sales tax from buyers — after the Supreme Court's 2018 decision in South Dakota v. Wayfair, states may require remote sellers with sufficient "economic nexus" to collect it — use tax is in principle self-assessed by the purchaser, frequently through a line on the state income tax return. Voluntary compliance by individual consumers has historically been low.

Purpose and issues

Use taxes date to the 1930s, when states adopted them to protect local merchants and sales tax revenue from mail-order competition; the U.S. Supreme Court upheld their constitutionality in Henneford v. Silas Mason Co. (1937). The rise of e-commerce revived debates over collection burdens and fairness, culminating in Wayfair, which overturned the physical-presence rule of Quill Corp. v. North Dakota and shifted much of the practical burden from buyers' use tax to sellers' sales tax collection.
