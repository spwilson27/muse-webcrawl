Nekton

Nekton (from Greek νηκτόν, "swimming") refers to the aggregate of actively swimming aquatic organisms in a body of water. The term was coined by the German biologist Ernst Haeckel to distinguish actively swimming organisms from plankton, the passively drifting organisms that are carried along by water currents. As a guideline, nektonic organisms have a high Reynolds number and are generally large relative to plankton, though there is no sharp boundary between the two groups.

Nekton includes a wide variety of animals: fish, squid, marine mammals such as whales and dolphins, sea turtles, penguins and other swimming birds, and large crustaceans. Unlike plankton, nekton are capable of sustained, directed swimming against currents, allowing them to migrate, hunt, and seek favourable conditions over large distances.

Definition and characteristics

The distinction between nekton and plankton is functional rather than taxonomic. Many species are nektonic only during part of their life cycle: the larvae of many fish and invertebrates drift as plankton before growing large enough to swim actively. Organisms that live on or near the bottom but can swim in the water column are sometimes classified as nektobenthos.

Because nekton can move independently of currents, their distribution is governed more by temperature, food availability, and reproductive requirements than by hydrodynamics alone. Many nektonic species undertake long-distance migrations between feeding and breeding grounds.

Examples

Most commercially important marine species are nekton, including tunas, salmon, sharks, and many squid. The largest animal that has ever lived, the blue whale, is a member of the nekton, as are fast swimmers such as sailfish, which can exceed 100 km/h in short bursts.
