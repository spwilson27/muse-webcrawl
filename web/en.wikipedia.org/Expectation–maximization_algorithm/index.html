Expectation–maximization algorithm

TypeIterative optimisation / statistical estimation
IntroducedDempster, Laird & Rubin (1977)
StepsExpectation (E) step, Maximization (M) step
UseMaximum likelihood with latent variables

In statistics, an expectation–maximization (EM) algorithm is an iterative method to find (local) maximum likelihood or maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimates of parameters in statistical models, where the model depends on unobserved latent variables. The EM iteration alternates between performing an expectation (E) step, which creates a function for the expectation of the log-likelihood evaluated using the current estimate for the parameters, and a maximization (M) step, which computes parameters maximizing that expected log-likelihood. These parameter estimates are then used to determine the distribution of the latent variables in the next E step.

History

The algorithm was named and explained in a celebrated 1977 paper by Arthur Dempster, Nan Laird, and Donald Rubin, who showed that each iteration is guaranteed not to decrease the likelihood. Special cases and components of the method had appeared earlier, including in work on gene counting and in the Baum–Welch algorithm for hidden Markov models.

Applications

EM is widely used for fitting Gaussian mixture models used in clustering, for training hidden Markov models in speech recognition and bioinformatics, for imputing missing data, and in medical imaging reconstruction. Because EM converges only to a local optimum, results depend on initialisation, and techniques such as multiple restarts or k-means seeding are commonly used.

Properties and variants

Convergence can be slow near the optimum, motivating accelerations and variants such as generalized EM (which merely increases rather than maximizes the expected log-likelihood), and Monte Carlo EM for intractable E steps.
