The Declaration of Geneva is a statement of the ethical principles governing the medical profession, adopted by the second General Assembly of the World Medical Association in Geneva, Switzerland, in September 1948. Sometimes called the "Physician's Pledge," it is a contemporary revision of the ancient Hippocratic Oath, drafted in the aftermath of the Second World War and the revelations of Nazi medical crimes.

Background

The World Medical Association was founded in 1947, and one of its first acts was to formulate a modern oath for physicians entering practice. The Declaration was intended to restate the core duties of medicine — service to humanity, respect for human life, and confidentiality — in language suited to post-war conditions.

Content

In the pledge, the physician solemnly promises to consecrate their life to the service of humanity; to make the health and well-being of the patient their first consideration; to respect the autonomy, dignity, and secrets of patients; to maintain the honour and noble traditions of the profession; not to permit considerations of age, disease, creed, ethnic origin, gender, nationality, political affiliation, race, sexual orientation, or social standing to intervene between duty and patient; and to practise with conscience and dignity, in accordance with good medical practice. The 2017 revision added, among other changes, an explicit commitment to attend to the physician's own health and well-being, and expanded the respect clause to include patient autonomy.

Use

The Declaration, or texts based on it, is administered at graduation ceremonies in many medical schools around the world and is referenced in codes of medical ethics in numerous countries. It has been amended several times, most recently in Chicago in 2017.
