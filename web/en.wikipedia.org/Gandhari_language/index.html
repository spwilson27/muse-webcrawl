Gandhari language

Gāndhārī is a Prakrit language of the ancient region of Gandhara, in what is now northwestern Pakistan and eastern Afghanistan. It was written in the Kharosthi script and was used by the administration of the Kushan Empire and along the Silk Road. Gāndhārī is notable for preserving some of the earliest surviving Buddhist manuscripts, dating to around the 1st century CE.

History and texts

The language appears in Achaemenid-era Aramaic-influenced chancelleries, in edicts of Ashoka inscribed in Kharosthi in the northwest, and later in coins, inscriptions, and documents from Central Asian sites such as Niya and Khotan. In the 1990s, the British Library acquired a collection of Gāndhārī birch-bark scrolls — the "Dead Sea Scrolls of Buddhism" — including early versions of texts such as the Rhinoceros Sutra and portions of the Dharmapada. The Gāndhārī Dharmapada was the first such text known to modern scholarship, discovered in the 19th century.

Features

Like other Prakrits, Gāndhārī derives from Old Indo-Aryan but shows distinctive features, including retention of all three sibilants of Sanskrit and certain consonant clusters, influenced by both its northwestern location and contact with Iranian languages. In China, early Buddhist translations were often made from Gāndhārī originals rather than Sanskrit.
