Supercomputer

A supercomputer is a type of computer with a high level of performance compared to a general-purpose computer. The performance of a supercomputer is commonly measured in floating-point operations per second (FLOPS) instead of million instructions per second (MIPS). Since 2017, supercomputers have performed at over 1017 FLOPS (a hundred petaFLOPS), and the first exascale systems exceeding 1018 FLOPS entered service in the 2020s.

History

The term emerged in the 1960s with machines such as the CDC 6600, designed by Seymour Cray, who later founded Cray Research. Supercomputing leadership has passed among the United States, Japan, and China. Notable systems include the Cray-1, IBM's Blue Gene series, Japan's K computer and Fugaku, China's Sunway TaihuLight, and the US exascale machines Frontier, Aurora, and El Capitan.

Architecture

Modern supercomputers are massively parallel clusters of thousands of nodes, each combining multicore CPUs with GPU accelerators, joined by high-speed interconnects such as InfiniBand or proprietary networks. They consume megawatts of power and require industrial-scale cooling.

Applications

Supercomputers are used for weather forecasting and climate modelling, nuclear weapons simulation, molecular dynamics and drug discovery, astrophysics, cryptanalysis, and training large artificial intelligence models. The twice-yearly TOP500 list ranks the world's fastest systems using the LINPACK benchmark.
