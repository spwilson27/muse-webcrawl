Tiber Island

Tiber Island
Italian: Isola Tiberina
Location: Tiber River, Rome, Italy
Length: about 270 m; width: about 67 m
Bridges: Ponte Fabricio, Ponte Cestio

Tiber Island (Italian: Isola Tiberina, Latin: Insula Tiberina) is the only river island in the part of the Tiber that runs through Rome. Boat-shaped and roughly 270 metres long and 67 metres wide, it has been connected to the rest of the city by two bridges since antiquity — the Ponte Fabricio (62 BC, the oldest bridge in Rome still in its original state) to the east bank and the Ponte Cestio to the west. The island has been associated with healing and medicine for more than two millennia and still hosts a hospital.

History

According to legend, the island was formed from sheaves of wheat cut from the Campus Martius and thrown into the Tiber after the expulsion of the last king of Rome, Tarquin the Proud, in 510 BC, which silted up into an island — though geologically it is founded on volcanic rock. In 293 BC, during a plague, a Roman embassy sent to the temple of Asclepius at Epidaurus returned with a sacred snake, which leapt from the ship and swam to the island; a temple to Aesculapius (the Latin Asclepius) was built there in 291 BC, and the island became a sanctuary for the sick. In the late Republic the island was faced with travertine in the shape of a ship, complete with an obelisk representing the mast, portions of which survive at its southern end.

The island today

On the island stands the Fatebenefratelli Hospital, founded in 1585 by the Brothers Hospitallers of Saint John of God and still operating, near the site of the ancient temple. The church of San Bartolomeo all'Isola, built around the year 1000 by Emperor Otto III over the temple ruins, houses relics of St Bartholomew. The eastern bridge leads toward the Theatre of Marcellus and the Jewish Ghetto; each summer the island hosts the Isola del Cinema film festival. During the Second World War, the hospital's staff famously protected Roman Jews by inventing a fictitious contagious disease, "Syndrome K", to keep Nazi searchers away from the wards.
