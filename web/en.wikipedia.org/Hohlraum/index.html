Hohlraum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A gold hohlraum (right) containing a fuel pellet, shown next to a pencil tip for scale.

In radiation thermodynamics, a hohlraum (German for "hollow space" or "cavity") is a cavity whose walls are in radiative equilibrium with the radiant energy within the cavity. This idealized cavity can be approximated in practice by making a small perforation in the wall of a hollow container of any opaque material. The radiation escaping through such a perforation will be a good approximation of black-body radiation at the temperature of the container.

Inertial confinement fusion

The term is most commonly associated with inertial confinement fusion (ICF). In the indirect-drive approach, a fuel capsule — a tiny sphere of deuterium–tritium ice or gas at the centre of a cylindrical gold (or other high-Z) hohlraum, typically about a centimetre long — is irradiated not directly, but by the intense X-rays produced when laser beams or ion beams heat the inside of the hohlraum. The X-rays smoothly and symmetrically ablate the capsule's surface, imploding the fuel to the densities and temperatures of thermonuclear ignition.

The largest such experiments are carried out at the National Ignition Facility (NIF) at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, where 192 laser beams enter a hohlraum through holes at its ends. In December 2022 the NIF announced the first laboratory achievement of fusion ignition — a target gain greater than one — using hohlraum-driven implosions, a result repeated in subsequent shots.

Hohlraums are also relevant to nuclear weapons design (as radiation cases in thermonuclear secondaries), to laboratory astrophysics experiments that reproduce stellar interiors, and to high-energy-density physics generally.
