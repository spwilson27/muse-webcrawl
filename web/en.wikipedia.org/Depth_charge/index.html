Depth charge

A depth charge is an anti-submarine warfare (ASW) weapon designed to destroy submarines by causing nearby underwater explosions that damage the submarine's pressure hull. Most depth charges use high explosives with a fuze set to detonate at a specific depth, usually determined by hydrostatic pressure.

Depth charges were developed by the Royal Navy during the First World War and were the standard anti-submarine weapon of both world wars. They were typically cylindrical canisters, rolled off stern racks or projected from launchers ("throwers") aboard escort vessels, sinking at a controlled rate until reaching the preset depth.

History

The first effective depth charge, the Type D, entered Royal Navy service in early 1916. By 1917, depth-charge attacks were credited with a growing number of U-boat sinkings. In the Second World War, pattern dropping — laying a spread of charges set to different depths — greatly improved lethality. Later war developments included ahead-thrown weapons such as Hedgehog and Squid, which kept sonar contact during the attack.

Effectiveness and decline

An explosion within roughly 6 metres (20 ft) of a submarine's hull could rupture it, while near misses could shake machinery loose and demoralise the crew. Nevertheless, early-war kill rates per attack were low, on the order of a few percent. Depth charges were largely superseded after the Second World War by homing torpedoes and rocket-delivered weapons, though some navies retain them in updated forms.
