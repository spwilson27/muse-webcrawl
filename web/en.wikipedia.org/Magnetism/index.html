Magnetism

Magnetism is a class of physical phenomena mediated by magnetic fields, arising from the motion of electric charges and the intrinsic magnetic moments of elementary particles. Magnetic forces are among the fundamental interactions of nature, unified with electricity in the theory of electromagnetism, described by Maxwell's equations.

Sources of magnetism

Electric currents produce magnetic fields (Ampère's law), and changing magnetic fields induce electric fields (Faraday's law). At the atomic level, the magnetic moment of an atom comes mainly from electron spin and orbital motion. How these moments align determines a material's behaviour: in ferromagnetism (iron, cobalt, nickel) neighbouring moments align in domains, producing permanent magnets; paramagnetic materials are weakly attracted to fields; diamagnetic materials are weakly repelled. Other orderings include antiferromagnetism and ferrimagnetism.

History

Magnetic lodestones were known in antiquity, and compasses were used for navigation in China by the Song dynasty and in Europe by the 12th century. William Gilbert's De Magnete (1600) founded the experimental science of magnetism. In 1820 Hans Christian Ørsted discovered that electric currents deflect compass needles, and within decades Faraday and Maxwell unified electricity and magnetism. The quantum-mechanical exchange interaction, explained in the 1920s, underlies ferromagnetism.

Applications

Magnetism is essential to electric motors, generators, transformers, magnetic storage media, MRI scanners, maglev trains, and particle accelerators. The Earth itself acts as a giant magnet, its field — generated by the dynamo action of its liquid outer core — shielding the surface from solar wind and enabling compass navigation.
