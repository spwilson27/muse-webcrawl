Feedback

Feedback occurs when outputs of a system are routed back as inputs as part of a chain of cause and effect that forms a circuit or loop. In negative feedback, the loop counteracts changes and tends to stabilise the system — as in a thermostat or the regulation of blood glucose. In positive feedback, the loop amplifies changes, driving the system away from its initial state, as in microphone squeal, nuclear chain reactions or the contractions of childbirth. Feedback is a central concept in control theory, electronics, biology, climate science and economics.

History

Self-regulating mechanisms are ancient — float regulators in Hellenistic water clocks, and Cornelis Drebbel's 17th-century thermostat — but the governor of the steam engine, refined by James Watt in 1788, made feedback a practical cornerstone of industrial technology. Harold Black's negative feedback amplifier, conceived in 1927, transformed electronics by trading gain for stability and fidelity. During and after the Second World War, Norbert Wiener's cybernetics generalised feedback into a theory of control and communication in animals and machines, and physiologist Walter Cannon had earlier described the same stabilising logic in biology as homeostasis.

Applications

In electronics, feedback determines the gain, bandwidth and linearity of amplifiers and the behaviour of oscillators. In biology, endocrine and neural loops regulate temperature, blood pressure and population dynamics, while predator–prey cycles produce oscillations. In climate science, feedbacks such as the ice–albedo and water vapour effects amplify or damp initial warming. Feedback is also used metaphorically in management and education for information returned about performance.

Stability

Loops with delay or excessive gain can oscillate or become unstable, a problem analysed with tools such as the Nyquist stability criterion and Bode plots. The balance between responsiveness and stability is a recurring design problem in control engineering.
