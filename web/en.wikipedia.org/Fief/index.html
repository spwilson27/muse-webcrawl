Fief

A fief (also called a feudum or fee) was the central element of feudalism in medieval Europe. It consisted of heritable property or rights — most typically land — granted by an overlord to a vassal who held it (in fealty) in return for a form of feudal allegiance and service, usually military service. The grant was made in a ceremony of commendation, in which the vassal performed homage and swore an oath of fealty to his lord.

Characteristics

Originally the fief was a benefice held only for the lifetime of the vassal, but from the ninth and tenth centuries onwards fiefs became hereditary, passing to the vassal's heir, who customarily paid a "relief" to the lord on succession. The vassal in turn could grant portions of his fief to sub-vassals, a process known as subinfeudation, creating chains of tenure reaching from great magnates down to single knights. Along with land, fiefs could include rights such as mills, markets, tolls, fishing, and even offices or churches.

Obligations

In return for the fief, the vassal owed his lord feudal aids and incidents: military service for a set number of days per year, attendance at the lord's court, hospitality, ransom contributions, and payments on occasions such as the knighting of the lord's eldest son or the marriage of his eldest daughter. Failure to perform service could result in forfeiture of the fief.

Decline

From the later Middle Ages, the spread of money payments (scutage) in place of personal military service, the growth of royal administration and standing armies, and the commutation of services into rents gradually hollowed out the classical fief, though feudal tenures survived in law in some countries into the modern era — Scotland formally abolished its feudal system only in 2004.
