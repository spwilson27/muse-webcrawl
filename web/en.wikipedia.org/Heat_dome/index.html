Heat dome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A heat dome is a weather phenomenon consisting of extreme heat that is caused by a ridge of high pressure trapping warm air beneath it, acting like a lid or cap. Heat domes occur when strong high-pressure atmospheric conditions remain stationary for an extended period, preventing convection and keeping hot air masses trapped close to the surface.

Formation

Heat domes typically form when a difference in ocean temperatures, combined with the behaviour of the jet stream, creates a pattern of warm air that becomes trapped by a high-pressure system. As air within the dome sinks, it is compressed and warmed further. Clear skies under the ridge allow intense solar heating of the ground, and the stagnant air mass can persist for days or even weeks until the upper-atmosphere pattern shifts.

Effects

Temperatures under a heat dome can far exceed seasonal norms, stressing agriculture, energy grids and human health. A notable example was the 2021 Western North America heat wave, when a powerful heat dome settled over the Pacific Northwest and British Columbia, producing Canada's all-time record temperature of 49.6 °C at Lytton, British Columbia, and contributing to hundreds of deaths and a destructive wildfire that destroyed most of the village. Similar events have affected Europe, the Middle East and South Asia.
