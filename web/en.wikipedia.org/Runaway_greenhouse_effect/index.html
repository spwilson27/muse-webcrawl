Runaway greenhouse effect

Image: Venus, whose atmosphere is thought to result from a runaway greenhouse

A runaway greenhouse effect will occur when a planet's atmosphere contains greenhouse gas in an amount sufficient to block thermal radiation from leaving the planet, preventing the planet from cooling and having liquid water on its surface. In such an event, the surface temperature rises until the oceans evaporate entirely.

Mechanism

As temperature increases, more water evaporates into the atmosphere; water vapour is itself a greenhouse gas, so warming feeds on itself in a positive feedback loop. Eventually the atmosphere becomes optically thick at thermal wavelengths, outgoing radiation is capped (the Simpson–Nakajima limit), and the oceans boil away. Water vapour reaching the stratosphere is photodissociated and the hydrogen escapes to space, permanently desiccating the planet.

Venus and Earth

The runaway greenhouse is widely held to explain Venus, which likely lost early oceans and now has a surface temperature of about 460 °C beneath a dense CO₂ atmosphere. On Earth, models indicate the natural brightening of the Sun could trigger a "moist greenhouse" and eventually a runaway in roughly one to two billion years; the scientific consensus is that human-caused warming, while dangerous for other reasons, cannot by itself trigger a full Venus-style runaway on Earth.
