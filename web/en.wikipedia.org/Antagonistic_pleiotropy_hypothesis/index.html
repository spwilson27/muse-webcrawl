Antagonistic pleiotropy hypothesis

The antagonistic pleiotropy hypothesis is an evolutionary explanation for ageing (senescence) proposed by the American evolutionary biologist George C. Williams in 1957. It holds that genes which produce beneficial effects early in life — increasing survival or reproduction — can be favoured by natural selection even if the same genes cause harmful effects later in life, because the force of selection declines with age.

Background

Pleiotropy is the phenomenon in which a single gene influences multiple phenotypic traits. Williams argued that such genes can have antagonistic effects across the lifespan: a trait that helps a young organism reproduce will spread through a population even if it contributes to deterioration in old age, since most reproduction happens before those late costs are paid. The hypothesis complements Peter Medawar's earlier mutation accumulation theory and is contrasted with the disposable soma theory of Thomas Kirkwood.

Examples

Proposed examples include high testosterone, which promotes reproductive success in young males but raises prostate-cancer risk in older men; cellular senescence, which suppresses cancer early in life but contributes to tissue ageing later; and the Huntington's disease allele, which some studies have associated with increased early-life fertility. Laboratory selection experiments, notably Michael Rose's work breeding long-lived fruit flies, produced evidence consistent with trade-offs between early reproduction and longevity.

Significance

Antagonistic pleiotropy is one of the leading evolutionary theories of ageing and frames ageing not as a directly selected adaptation but as a by-product of selection for early-life fitness. Identifying antagonistically pleiotropic genes remains an active research goal in biogerontology.
