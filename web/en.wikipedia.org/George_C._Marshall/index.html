George Catlett Marshall Jr. (1880–1959) was an American soldier and statesman, widely regarded as the "organizer of victory" in the Second World War. As Chief of Staff of the United States Army he built and directed the largest military establishment in American history, then as Secretary of State he authored the European recovery programme that bears his name, the Marshall Plan.

Military career

A graduate of the Virginia Military Institute, Marshall served in the Philippines and France in the First World War, where he won recognition as a staff planner under John J. Pershing. Between the wars he reshaped the Army's schools at Fort Benning. Appointed Chief of Staff on September 1, 1939 — the day Germany invaded Poland — he oversaw the expansion of the Army from some 200,000 men to more than eight million, and sat at the centre of Allied grand strategy alongside Franklin D. Roosevelt and Winston Churchill.

Secretary of State

Under President Harry S. Truman, Marshall became Secretary of State in 1947. In a commencement address at Harvard University that June he proposed American assistance for European economic recovery; the resulting Marshall Plan channelled some $13 billion to Western Europe and is credited with reviving its economies and anchoring the Western alliance. Marshall received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1953, the only professional soldier to do so while best known for statecraft.

Later service and legacy

Marshall briefly headed the American Red Cross, then returned as Secretary of Defense during the Korean War (1950–51). He died in 1959 and was buried at Arlington National Cemetery. Historians consistently rank him among the greatest American soldier-statesmen; Truman called him "the greatest living American."
