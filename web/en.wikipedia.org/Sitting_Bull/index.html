Sitting Bull (Lakota: Tȟatȟáŋka Íyotake; c. 1831 – 15 December 1890) was a Hunkpapa Lakota leader who led his people during years of resistance against United States government policies. He was killed by Indian agency police on the Standing Rock Indian Reservation during an attempt to arrest him in connection with the Ghost Dance movement.

Early life

Sitting Bull was raised in the northern Great Plains, earning a reputation as a brave warrior and wise leader. He first gained notice in battle against rival Crow warriors and later became a holy man (wičháša wakȟáŋ) and chief of the Hunkpapa.

Great Sioux War

After gold was discovered in the Black Hills — land guaranteed to the Lakota by the 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie — the United States moved against the non-treaty bands. Sitting Bull's camp became a gathering point for Lakota, Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho. Before the campaign of 1876 he performed a sun dance during which he reported a vision of soldiers falling upside down into camp. On 25 June 1876, Lakota and Cheyenne forces destroyed Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer's command at the Battle of the Little Bighorn.

Exile and surrender

In 1877 Sitting Bull led his followers into exile in Canada (the "Grandmother's Country"), but game scarcity forced his return; he surrendered at Fort Buford in 1881. He later toured with Buffalo Bill's Wild West show in 1885, during which he met Annie Oakley, whom he nicknamed "Little Sure Shot".

Death and legacy

Amid fears that he would join the Ghost Dance movement, agency police attempted to arrest Sitting Bull on 15 December 1890; in the ensuing struggle he was shot and killed. He was buried at Fort Yates, and his remains were later moved near Mobridge, South Dakota. Sitting Bull remains an iconic figure of Native American resistance.
