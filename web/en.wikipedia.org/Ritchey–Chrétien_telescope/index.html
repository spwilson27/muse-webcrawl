Ritchey–Chrétien telescope

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Ritchey–Chrétien telescope is a specialised variant of the Cassegrain reflector that uses two hyperbolic mirrors to eliminate optical coma and provide a wider field of view free of major aberration. It was invented in the early 1910s by American astronomer George Willis Ritchey and French astronomer Henri Chrétien.

Design

In a classic Cassegrain, the primary mirror is parabolic and the secondary hyperbolic; the combination is free of spherical aberration on axis but suffers from coma away from the axis. By making both mirrors hyperbolic and sharing the correction between them, the Ritchey–Chrétien design cancels both spherical aberration and coma, leaving only relatively mild astigmatism and field curvature, which corrector lenses can address in wide-field applications.

Use

The design is used by most modern professional research telescopes because of its excellent imaging over wide fields. Famous examples include the Hubble Space Telescope, the 10-metre Keck telescopes, and the telescopes of the Very Large Telescope. Ritchey's own 24-inch and 40-inch reflectors were among the first of the type. Smaller Ritchey–Chrétien instruments are also popular with advanced amateur astrophotographers, though the hyperbolic surfaces are difficult and costly to figure and test.
