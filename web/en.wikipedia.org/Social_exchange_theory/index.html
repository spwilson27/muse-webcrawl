Social exchange theory

Social exchange theory
FieldSociology; social psychology
Key figuresGeorge Homans; Peter Blau; John Thibaut & Harold Kelley
Core ideaRelationships as exchanges of costs and rewards

Social exchange theory is a sociological and social-psychological perspective that explains social change and stability as a process of negotiated exchanges between parties. Social behaviour is understood as the result of actors weighing the costs and rewards of relationships and actions, much as in economic exchange, though the "goods" involved include approval, status, information, and love as well as material resources.

Foundations

George C. Homans laid out the theory in "Social Behavior as Exchange" (1958) and Social Behavior: Its Elementary Forms (1961), grounding it in behavioural psychology. Peter Blau extended the framework to larger social structures and power in Exchange and Power in Social Life (1964). John Thibaut and Harold Kelley contributed the ideas of comparison level and comparison level for alternatives, by which people judge relationships against past experience and available options.

Key concepts

Central concepts include reciprocity (the expectation that benefits will be returned), distributive justice or equity, dependence and power (which arise when one party controls resources the other needs, as developed by Richard Emerson), and trust, which grows from repeated successful exchanges.

Applications and criticism

The theory is widely applied to close relationships, organisations, and networks. Critics argue it overstates rational calculation, understates altruism and cultural norms, and fits market-like interactions better than communal ones.
