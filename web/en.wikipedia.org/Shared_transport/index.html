Shared transport

A bike-share docking station

Shared transport or shared mobility is a transport system in which travellers share a vehicle either simultaneously as a group (such as ridesharing, carpooling, or a shared taxi) or over time as a personal rental (such as car-sharing or bicycle-sharing), so that the vehicle is used more intensively than a privately owned one. The concept spans traditional forms — public transport, taxis, and jitneys — as well as newer, typically app-based services coordinated through digital platforms, sometimes grouped under the heading Mobility as a Service (MaaS).

Forms of shared transport

Simultaneous sharing

  • Ridesharing / carpooling: private drivers share empty seats with passengers travelling the same way, from informal arrangements to platform-matched services.
  • Ride-hailing with pooling: commercial services (Uber, Lyft, DiDi and others) offer pooled rides matching strangers with similar routes.
  • Shared taxis and minibuses: dolmuş, matatu, marshrutka, jeepneys and similar paratransit modes pick up and drop off passengers along semi-fixed routes.
  • Demand-responsive transport: publicly supported minibuses booked by phone or app, often serving rural areas or people with reduced mobility.

Sequential sharing

  • Car-sharing: members rent cars by the minute or hour from fleets parked on-street (free-floating) or at stations (round-trip), e.g. Zipcar.
  • Bicycle-sharing: docked or dockless bikes rented for short trips, pioneered at scale by systems such as Paris's Vélib' and China's dockless operators.
  • Scooter-sharing: shared e-scooters and mopeds in many cities since the late 2010s.
  • Peer-to-peer rentals: platforms letting individuals rent out their own cars or bikes.

Effects

Studies of car-sharing generally find that each shared car replaces several privately owned cars and that members drive less, because the marginal cost of each trip is salient. Bike-share and pooled ride services can extend the reach of public transport by solving first/last-mile problems. Conversely, unpooled ride-hailing has been shown in several cities to add vehicle-kilometres through deadheading (empty driving) and to draw some trips away from public transport, walking, and cycling. Effects depend heavily on pooling rates, local regulation, and integration with mass transit.

Regulation and criticism

Shared transport services have prompted regulatory disputes over driver employment status, licensing, insurance, congestion, data sharing, accessibility, and effects on taxi industries. Dockless schemes have faced complaints about pavement clutter and vandalism, leading cities to impose permit systems and fleet caps.
