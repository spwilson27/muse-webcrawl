Tar (string instrument)

The tar (Persian: تار, literally "string") is a long-necked, waisted lute central to the classical music of Iran, Azerbaijan, and neighbouring regions of the Caucasus and Central Asia. It is characterised by its double-bowl body, a membrane soundboard traditionally of lambskin, and movable frets that allow the microtonal intervals of the Persian and Azerbaijani modal systems.

Description

The tar's body is carved from mulberry wood in a figure-eight or double-chamber shape. The face is covered with a thin membrane of skin. A long fretted neck carries gut or nylon frets tied so they can be adjusted for different modes (dastgah in Persia, mugham in Azerbaijan). The instrument is played with a small brass or wax plectrum held between thumb and forefinger, producing a bright, ringing tone with rapid tremolo.

Persian tar

The modern Persian tar has six strings in three double courses, and is a principal instrument of the Persian classical tradition, used both as a solo instrument and in ensembles. Its repertoire is organised in the radif, the canonical collection of melodies.

Azerbaijani tar

In Azerbaijan the tar was redesigned in the 1870s by Sadigjan, who flattened the body, increased the number of strings to eleven, and adapted it to be held against the chest. This version became the lead instrument of mugham trios alongside the kamancha and daf, and its craftsmanship and performance were inscribed by UNESCO on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2012.
