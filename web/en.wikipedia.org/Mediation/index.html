Mediation

Mediation is a structured, interactive process where an impartial third party, the mediator, assists disputing parties in resolving conflict through the use of specialized communication and negotiation techniques. All participants in mediation are encouraged to actively participate in the process. Mediation is a "party-centered" process in that it is focused primarily upon the needs, rights, and interests of the parties. The mediator uses a wide variety of techniques to guide the process in a constructive direction and to help the parties find their optimal solution.

A mediator facilitates negotiation but does not impose a decision.

Features

A mediator is neutral and does not "decide" the dispute, unlike an arbitrator or judge; instead, the mediator helps the parties reach their own agreement. Mediation is typically voluntary, confidential, and informal. It is used in a wide range of contexts: family and divorce disputes, workplace conflict, commercial and contract disputes, community disagreements, and even international diplomacy and peace processes.

Process

Mediation generally proceeds through stages: the mediator's opening statement, the parties' opening statements, joint discussion, private caucuses between the mediator and each party, negotiation, and closure — either with a written settlement agreement or an acknowledgment that no agreement was reached. Agreements reached in mediation may be enforceable as contracts, and courts increasingly require parties to attempt mediation before litigation under court-connected mediation programmes.

Styles

  • Facilitative mediation — the mediator focuses on process and communication, asking questions to help the parties find their own solution.
  • Evaluative mediation — the mediator gives an assessment of the strengths and weaknesses of each side's case.
  • Transformative mediation — aims at empowering the parties and improving their mutual recognition, beyond the immediate dispute.

Benefits

Compared with litigation, mediation is usually faster, cheaper, and less adversarial; it preserves relationships, gives the parties control over the outcome, and produces higher rates of compliance with agreements. Its confidentiality allows candid discussion that would not be possible in open court.
