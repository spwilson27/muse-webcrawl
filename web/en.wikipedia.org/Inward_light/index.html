Inward light

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Inward Light (also called the Inner Light, or the Light of Christ) is a central doctrine of the Religious Society of Friends (Quakers). It holds that within every person there is a direct, inward experience of God — "that of God in everyone" — which enlightens conscience, reveals truth, and guides moral and spiritual life without necessary mediation by clergy, ritual or even scripture. Early Friends taught that this Light, identified with Christ, was the primary source of religious authority, while the Bible was a secondary, though valuable, record of it.

Origins

The doctrine was articulated by George Fox, the founder of Quakerism, from the 1640s. Fox taught that "Christ has come to teach his people himself", and Friends' silent worship — waiting in stillness for the Light to speak — grew directly from the belief. Robert Barclay's Apology for the True Christian Divinity (1678) gave the doctrine its classic systematic statement, arguing that the Light is universal (given to all people, including non-Christians) but can be resisted, and that it never contradicts scripture rightly understood.

Belief and practice

For Friends, heeding the Inner Light produces the Quaker testimonies — simplicity, peace, integrity, community, equality and stewardship — and corporate discernment: business meetings seek the "sense of the meeting" through collective listening to the Light. The doctrine underpins historic Quaker positions such as refusal of oaths and military service, opposition to slavery, and prison reform, since the Light in every person confers equal worth on all.

Interpretations

Interpretations vary among modern Friends: orthodox and evangelical Friends emphasise the Light as specifically Christ; liberal Friends may understand it more broadly as the divine spark or human capacity for goodness and truth. The phrase "Inner Light" became common in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, though "Inward Light" is the older form.
