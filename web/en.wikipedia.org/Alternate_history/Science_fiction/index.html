Alternate history in science fiction

Alternate history (also called alternative history, uchronia, or by the German term Nebenwelt) is one of the most durable subgenres of science fiction, in which a story is set in a world where history has diverged from the historical record as we know it. The divergence, conventionally called a point of divergence (POD) or, in fan terminology borrowed from the genre's own lore, a "Jonbar hinge," is a single event whose outcome differs from real history — a battle won by the other side, an assassination that succeeds or fails, a technology invented earlier or never at all. From that hinge the writer extrapolates a coherent alternative world, and the tension between the familiar and the altered gives the subgenre much of its appeal. Within science fiction, alternate history occupies a special place: it applies the genre's characteristic method — rigorous extrapolation from a single changed premise — to history itself rather than to physics or biology.

Definitions and terminology

The term uchronia was coined by the French philosopher Charles Renouvier in his 1876 work Uchronie, formed by analogy with utopia: where a utopia is "no place," a uchronia is "no time" — a history that never happened. Modern critics usually distinguish alternate history proper, in which the divergence results from plausible historical contingency, from secret history, in which hidden events explain the world we already know, and from parallel-world stories, in which characters travel between timelines rather than inhabiting a single divergent one. The British tradition long preferred the term "what-if" story, and historians have practised the same exercise in nonfictional form as counterfactual history, though academics remain divided over its scholarly value. Within science fiction fandom, the Sidewise Award, founded in 1995, takes its name from Murray Leinster's 1934 story "Sidewise in Time" and honours the year's best work in the form.

History of the subgenre

Antecedents of the alternate-history story reach back to antiquity — the Roman historian Livy briefly speculated on whether Rome could have defeated Alexander the Great had Alexander turned west — and to early modern works such as Louis-Napoléon Geoffroy's 1836 Napoléon et la conquête du monde, which imagined Napoleon's complete triumph. The form flourished in the pulp science fiction magazines of the twentieth century. Leinster's "Sidewise in Time" (1934) introduced shifting timelines; L. Sprague de Camp's Lest Darkness Fall (1939) sent a modern man back to sixth-century Rome to stave off the Dark Ages, establishing the template for the "competent time traveller" story. Ward Moore's Bring the Jubilee (1953) portrayed an impoverished, backward United States in a world where the Confederacy won the Civil War, closing with a time-travel twist that restores — or creates — our own history.

The subgenre's canonical novel is Philip K. Dick's The Man in the High Castle (1962), which won the Hugo Award and depicts a United States partitioned between Nazi Germany and Imperial Japan after an Axis victory in the Second World War; its novel-within-the-novel, set in yet another timeline, made the book a meditation on the nature of historical reality itself. In the 1990s and 2000s, Harry Turtledove — often called the "master of alternate history" — popularized the large-scale military alternate history in series such as the Southern Victory sequence, which runs from a Confederate triumph in 1862 through alternate First and Second World Wars. The subgenre expanded into the mainstream with works such as Robert Harris's thriller Fatherland (1992), Stephen Fry's Making History (1996), and Michael Chabon's The Yiddish Policemen's Union (2007), a detective novel set in a Jewish settlement in Alaska. Television adaptations of The Man in the High Castle and the alternate-space-race drama For All Mankind carried the form to a still wider audience.

Common themes and settings

Certain divergence points recur so often that they have become the subgenre's shared furniture. The two most worked veins are an Axis victory in the Second World War and a Confederate victory in the American Civil War; other favourites include the survival of the Roman Empire, a successful Spanish Armada, Napoleon triumphant at Waterloo, the survival of the Byzantine Empire, a different outcome to the Cuban Missile Crisis, and a world in which the Apollo programme never ended. A related family of stories turns on technology: gunpowder, printing, steam power, or antibiotics arriving centuries early — or never. Science-fictional mechanisms frequently frame the divergence: time travellers changing the past, as in de Camp and in Poul Anderson's Time Patrol stories; "timeslip" romances; or contact between parallel timelines, as in H. Beam Piper's Paratime series and, in a comic register, the many-worlds crossings of later authors. Historians of the genre note that the choice of hinge is rarely neutral: alternate histories tend to encode their authors' views of what drives history — great individuals, economic forces, technology, or chance.

Relationship to science fiction technique

Critics have long observed that alternate history is science fiction's method applied to the past: the writer selects a novum, a single difference, and works out its consequences with as much consistency as the material allows. This is why the form sits inside the genre despite often containing no futuristic technology at all, and why anthologies and awards treat it as science fiction rather than as historical fiction, whose contract with the reader is the opposite one. The best alternate histories are praised for the density and plausibility of their extrapolated worlds — the altered maps, currencies, slang, and literary canons of a timeline that never was — while weak examples are criticized for "divergence tourism," in which a changed past serves only as exotic backdrop. Debate also persists over the ethics of certain settings, particularly narratives that invite the reader to inhabit worlds built on slavery's survival or fascist victory; defenders argue that such worlds, handled seriously, can expose the contingency of the reader's own history and the fragility of its hard-won outcomes.

Awards and criticism

The Sidewise Award for Alternate History has been presented annually since 1996 for works published in the preceding year, in long-form and short-form categories; winners include works by Turtledove, Chabon, and Jo Walton, whose Farthing (2006) combined the country-house mystery with a Britain that made peace with Hitler. Academic study of the form has grown steadily, with journals of science fiction studies publishing symposia on uchronia and on the overlap between fictional alternate history and historians' counterfactuals. The subgenre remains commercially healthy in the twenty-first century, sustained by dedicated small presses, by military-history readerships, and by streaming television, where the visual realization of a divergent twentieth century has proved a durable draw.
