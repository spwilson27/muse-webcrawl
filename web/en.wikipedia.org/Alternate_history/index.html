Alternate history

Alternate history explores how the past might have unfolded differently.

Alternate history (also referred to as alternative history, allohistory, uchronia, or simply AH) is a genre of speculative fiction in which one or more historical events have occurred differently from how they did in reality. The genre typically presupposes a point of divergence — sometimes called a nexus point or POD — at which the fictional timeline branches from the known historical record, and explores the consequences of that change for individuals and societies.

History of the genre

Speculation about unrealised historical possibilities is ancient: the Roman historian Livy wondered how Rome might have fared against Alexander the Great, and Herodotus imagined alternative outcomes of the Persian Wars. Early modern examples include Joanot Martorell's Tirant lo Blanc (1490), in which Constantinople does not fall to the Ottomans. The first recognisable alternate-history novel is often taken to be Louis Geoffroy's Napoléon et la conquête du monde (1836), in which Napoleon conquers the world; Charles Renouvier coined the term uchronie ("uchronia") in 1857.

The genre flourished in the 20th century. Ward Moore's Bring the Jubilee (1953) depicts a world in which the Confederacy won the American Civil War. Philip K. Dick's The Man in the High Castle (1962), in which the Axis powers won the Second World War, won the Hugo Award and remains one of the genre's best-known works. Later prolific authors include Harry Turtledove, sometimes called the "master of alternate history", whose works include The Guns of the South (1992) and the Southern Victory series.

Characteristics

Alternate history is defined by its divergence from recorded history, after which events unfold according to the author's sense of historical plausibility. Works differ in how rigorously they follow the consequences of the divergence: some aim for strict causal realism, while others use the changed setting chiefly as a backdrop for adventure or commentary on the real world. Common subgenres and devices include:

  • Time travel intervention — a traveller deliberately or accidentally changes the past.
  • Secret history — real events are given hidden fictional explanations (a borderline form, when history itself is unchanged).
  • Parallel worlds — multiple timelines coexist and interact.
  • Counterfactual essays — non-fiction speculation by historians, such as the essays collected in Virtual History (1997), edited by Niall Ferguson.

The genre's premier honours include the Sidewise Award for Alternate History, established in 1995. Alternate history is also a popular setting in strategy video games, tabletop role-playing games, and online collaborative fiction communities.

Related concepts

Alternate history is closely related to, but distinct from, counterfactual history as practised by historians, and from historical fantasy, which inserts magical or supernatural elements into a recognisably real past. When set in the future rather than the past, similar speculation is a matter for future history.
