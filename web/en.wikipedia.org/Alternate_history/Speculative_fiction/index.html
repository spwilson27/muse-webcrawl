Speculative fiction


Speculative fiction encompasses science fiction, fantasy, horror and related genres.

Speculative fiction is an umbrella category of fiction that departs from realism by imagining worlds, histories or possibilities different from the known world. It broadly encompasses science fiction, fantasy and supernatural horror, and within the context of alternate history it covers stories built on a point of divergence — a moment where the fictional timeline splits from recorded history — as well as utopian and dystopian fiction, superhero fiction, apocalyptic and post-apocalyptic fiction, and cross-genre hybrids. The unifying question of speculative fiction is often summarised as "What if?"

Definition and usage

The term was popularised in the 1960s and 1970s, in part through its use by writers such as Robert A. Heinlein, who had used "speculative fiction" in the 1940s as a synonym for serious science fiction, and by critics associated with the New Wave. Some authors and academics prefer it to the older label "science fiction and fantasy" because it also covers horror, alternate history and slipstream fiction that blurs the line between the fantastic and the literary mainstream. Margaret Atwood's use of the term for her dystopian novels, in preference to "science fiction", sparked a well-known debate with Ursula K. Le Guin about the boundaries of the genres.

Alternate history as speculative fiction

Alternate history is one of the core branches of speculative fiction. In an alternate-history narrative, the author changes one or more past events — the South wins the American Civil War, the Axis powers win the Second World War — and explores the consequences. Because it requires no magic or advanced technology, alternate history is sometimes classed as the most "realistic" of the speculative genres, and it overlaps with historical fiction. Works such as Philip K. Dick's The Man in the High Castle and Harry Turtledove's extensive alternate-history novels exemplify the form, which is recognised by the Sidewise Award for Alternate History.

History

Speculative elements are as old as literature itself: ancient epics, medieval romances and works such as Gulliver's Travels and Frankenstein all speculate beyond the real. The modern genres crystallised in the pulp magazines of the early twentieth century, and the term "speculative fiction" gained ground as writers sought a label that included the growing variety of non-realist modes. Today the category is used by publishers, bookshops, conferences and academic programmes as a catch-all for fiction that explores imagined possibilities, whether through technology, magic, supernatural horror or divergent history.
