Dystopia

A dystopia is an imagined community or society that is frightening or undesirable, typically characterised by oppression, totalitarian control, environmental degradation or the loss of individual freedom. The word was formed as the opposite of utopia (from Greek dys-, "bad", and topos, "place") and came into common use in the 20th century.

In literature

Dystopian fiction is a major branch of speculative fiction. Landmark works include Yevgeny Zamyatin's We (1924), Aldous Huxley's Brave New World (1932), George Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four (1949), Ray Bradbury's Fahrenheit 451 (1953) and Margaret Atwood's The Handmaid's Tale (1985). Such works usually extrapolate contemporary trends — surveillance, propaganda, consumerism, ecological collapse — into warnings about possible futures.

Characteristics

Common features of fictional dystopias include a controlling ruling power, restricted information and independent thought, constant surveillance, dehumanising living conditions, and a protagonist who questions or resists the society. Dystopian themes are also widespread in film, television and video games, and the genre saw renewed popularity in the 21st century through young-adult series and streaming dramas.
