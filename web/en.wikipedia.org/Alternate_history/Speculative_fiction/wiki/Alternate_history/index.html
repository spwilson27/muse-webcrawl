Alternate history

Alternate history (also written alternative history, and sometimes called allohistory or uchronia) is a subgenre of speculative fiction in which one or more historical events occur differently from how they did in reality. Typically the story departs from documented history at a single point of divergence and explores the consequences of that change for individuals and societies.

Characteristics

Alternate history works often blend science fiction, historical fiction and social commentary. Common premises include an Axis victory in the Second World War, as in Philip K. Dick's The Man in the High Castle (1962), or a Confederate victory in the American Civil War, explored at length in Harry Turtledove's Southern Victory series. Some stories use time travel to change the past, while others simply posit a different outcome without explanation.

History of the genre

Counterfactual speculation appears as early as Livy's reflections on Alexander the Great; modern works include Murray Leinster's 1934 story "Sidewise in Time" and L. Sprague de Camp's Lest Darkness Fall (1939). The Sidewise Award for Alternate History, founded in 1995, honours the best work in the genre each year. Historians also use counterfactuals analytically, though academic "virtual history" differs from literary alternate history in intent.
