Horror fiction

Horror fiction is a genre of speculative fiction intended to frighten, scare or disgust its audience, drawing on supernatural threats, psychological dread and taboo subjects. It evolved from Gothic literature of the 18th and 19th centuries and encompasses novels, short stories, film, television and other media.

History

Precursors include folklore and religious texts about demons, ghosts and the afterlife. Horace Walpole's The Castle of Otranto (1764) launched the Gothic novel; Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (1818), Robert Louis Stevenson's Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde (1886) and Bram Stoker's Dracula (1897) remain foundational. In the 20th century, H. P. Lovecraft transformed horror with cosmic horror, and Stephen King became its bestselling modern practitioner from the 1970s onward.

Characteristics and subgenres

Horror explores themes such as death, the unknown, madness and the monstrous, often using monsters — vampires, ghosts, serial killers, cosmic entities — to embody cultural anxieties. Subgenres include Gothic horror, supernatural horror, psychological horror, body horror, folk horror, splatterpunk and weird fiction. Scholars trace the genre's appeal to catharsis: audiences safely experience fear and confront repressed ideas in fictional form.
