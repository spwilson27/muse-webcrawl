Counterfactual history

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Counterfactual history is a form of historiography that explores what might have happened if a historical event had turned out differently. It proceeds by positing a plausible alternative outcome — for example, a different result in a battle or election — and reasoning about its consequences. Historians use counterfactuals to weigh the importance of causes, though the method remains debated within the profession.

Debate

Supporters, such as Niall Ferguson, argue that all causal claims in history imply counterfactuals. Critics, including E. H. Carr, dismissed the exercise as a "parlour game" that distracts from real causes. The philosopher of history has also examined counterfactual reasoning in the work of Max Weber.

Relation to fiction

Counterfactual history is closely related to the literary genre of alternate history, though academic counterfactuals generally restrict themselves to minimal, plausible rewrites of events, while fiction may pursue large and fanciful divergences.
