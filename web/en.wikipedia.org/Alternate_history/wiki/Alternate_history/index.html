Alternate history (also known as alternative history, allohistory, althist, or by its French name uchronia) is a genre of speculative fiction in which one or more historical events occur differently than they did in reality, and the story explores the consequences of that divergence. The genre typically begins from a point of divergence (POD), a moment at which history branches from the recorded timeline, and then extrapolates a plausible alternative course of events. Common points of divergence include wars with different outcomes, assassinations that succeed or fail, inventions made earlier or later, and individuals making different decisions.

Alternate history is closely related to, but distinct from, counterfactual history, a form of historiography in which historians themselves speculate about "what might have happened" in order to test causal claims about actual events. It also overlaps with the secret history, in which documented history is presumed accurate but hidden causes or events are added behind the scenes, and with historical fantasy and time travel fiction, in which characters may actively change the past.

A point of divergence splits a timeline into the historical record and an alternative sequence of events.

Definition and terminology

The French term uchronie ("uchronia") was coined by the philosopher Charles Renouvier, whose novel Uchronie (l'Utopie dans l'Histoire) (1857, revised 1876) imagined a Roman Empire that survived and reshaped European history. The word is modelled on "utopia" (no-place), substituting chronos (time) for topos (place): a no-time, an era that never was. In English, "alternate history" and "alternative history" are used interchangeably. Writers and fans often distinguish between "hard" alternate history, which strives for rigorous plausibility and extensive research, and looser works that use the divergence mainly as a setting for adventure.

Works in the genre usually assume one of two models of how the divergence works. In the first, the change simply happens — the author rewrites a single event and explores the ripple effects. In the second, the change is caused within the story, most often by time travellers, which shades into the parallel universe and many-worlds frameworks of science fiction.

History of the genre

Early examples

Speculation about alternative outcomes is nearly as old as historical writing itself. The Roman historian Livy paused in his history of Rome to consider what might have happened had Alexander the Great turned west and invaded Italy. In the nineteenth century, the French writer Louis Geoffroy published Napoléon et la conquête du monde (1836), imagining a triumphant Napoleon conquering the world, and Sir George Trevelyan's essay "If Napoleon Had Won the Battle of Waterloo" (1907) became a famous early exercise by a professional historian. J. C. Squire's anthology If It Had Happened Otherwise (1931) collected counterfactual essays by writers including Hilaire Belloc, André Maurois, and Winston Churchill, whose contribution "If Lee Had Not Won the Battle of Gettysburg" was written wittily from inside the alternative timeline itself.

Growth and modern popularity

The genre flourished in pulp science fiction and after the Second World War. Ward Moore's Bring the Jubilee (1953) depicted a world in which the Confederacy won the American Civil War, told from the viewpoint of a historian who ultimately alters the past. Philip K. Dick's The Man in the High Castle (1962), in which the Axis powers won the Second World War and partitioned the United States, won the Hugo Award and remains one of the most celebrated works in the genre; it was later adapted into a television series. The question of a Nazi victory became one of the most frequently explored divergences, also treated in Robert Harris's Fatherland (1992) and Len Deighton's SS-GB (1978).

From the 1990s onward, alternate history became a major commercial genre. Harry Turtledove, often called the "master of alternate history", produced extensive series including the Southern Victory sequence (beginning with How Few Remain, 1997), which follows a timeline of Confederate victory across both World Wars, and the Worldwar series, in which alien invaders interrupt the Second World War. Other notable modern works include Kim Stanley Robinson's The Years of Rice and Salt (2002), in which the Black Death wipes out nearly all of Europe's population; Stephen Baxter's and Terry Pratchett's various ventures; S. M. Stirling's Draka series; and Eric Flint's 1632 (2000), which launched the long-running Ring of Fire shared universe in which a modern American town is transported to seventeenth-century Germany.

Common themes and divergences

Certain points of divergence recur throughout the genre because of their obvious historical weight:

  • The Axis wins the Second World War — perhaps the single most common scenario, used to explore fascism, occupation, and resistance.
  • The Confederacy wins the American Civil War — a staple of American alternate history, raising questions about slavery, race, and the division of North America.
  • The Roman Empire survives, or Rome falls earlier than it did.
  • Napoleon triumphs in the Napoleonic Wars.
  • The Spanish Armada succeeds, or the Norman Conquest fails.
  • The Mongol Empire continues its conquests into western Europe.
  • Technological divergence — gunpowder, printing, steam power, or computing arriving centuries early or late, the latter underpinning much steampunk.

Relationship to historiography

Professional historians are divided about counterfactual reasoning. Critics, notably E. H. Carr, dismissed "what-if" questions as a parlour game, while others argue that causal claims in history implicitly rest on counterfactuals — to say an event caused an outcome is to imply the outcome might have differed without it. Niall Ferguson's edited volume Virtual History (1997) defended disciplined counterfactual analysis restricted to alternatives that contemporaries actually considered. Academic interest in the genre's literary forms has also grown, with scholars examining how alternate histories comment on the politics and anxieties of the time in which they were written rather than the period they depict.

In other media

Alternate history is widespread in film, television, comics, and games. Strategy games such as the Hearts of Iron series allow players to steer nations away from their historical paths, while the Wolfenstein games depict a Nazi-dominated world and the Command & Conquer: Red Alert series builds an entire timeline from the removal of Hitler before his rise. Role-playing settings such as GURPS Alternate Earths systematised dozens of divergent timelines, each given a code name — a conceit echoing the many-worlds treatments of comics publishers, where variant Earths are a long-standing device.
