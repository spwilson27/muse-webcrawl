Galactic Center

Galactic Center
Constellation: Sagittarius
Distance: ~26,000 light-years (~8 kpc)
Central object: Sagittarius A* (supermassive black hole, ~4.3 million solar masses)

The Galactic Center is the rotational center, and barycenter, of the Milky Way galaxy. Its central massive object is a supermassive black hole of about 4.3 million solar masses, which powers the compact radio source Sagittarius A*. It is located roughly 26,000 light-years (about 8 kiloparsecs) away from Earth in the direction of the constellations Sagittarius, Ophiuchus, and Scorpius, where the Milky Way appears brightest.

Because interstellar dust along the line of sight obscures visible light, the Galactic Center is studied mainly at infrared, radio, submillimetre, and X-ray wavelengths, which penetrate the dust. Around the black hole lies a dense nuclear star cluster, including a population of young massive stars (the S-stars) on tight orbits, as well as the circumnuclear ring of gas and dust and large structures such as the Fermi bubbles extending above and below the plane.

Discovery and distance measurement

Harlow Shapley first placed the Sun far from the galactic center in 1918 using globular clusters. Karl Jansky detected radio emission from Sagittarius in the 1930s; the compact source Sagittarius A* was identified in 1974. Decades of observations of stellar orbits around Sgr A* — by teams led by Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez, who shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics — established the presence of the supermassive black hole and measured its distance and mass. In 2022 the Event Horizon Telescope released the first image of Sgr A*'s shadow and accretion flow.

Environment

The innermost parsecs host giant molecular clouds of the Central Molecular Zone, intense star formation, supernova remnants, and the magnetar and pulsar population near the center. Large-scale features include the Fermi and eROSITA bubbles, chimneys of hot gas venting from the center, and evidence of past energetic outbursts from Sgr A*.
