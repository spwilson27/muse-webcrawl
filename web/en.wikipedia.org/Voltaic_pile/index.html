Voltaic pile

The voltaic pile was the first electrical battery that could continuously provide an electric current to a circuit. It was invented by the Italian physicist Alessandro Volta, who published his experiments in 1799 and demonstrated the device in 1800. The pile consists of alternating discs of two dissimilar metals — typically copper and zinc — separated by pieces of cloth or cardboard soaked in brine or dilute acid.

Year1799–1800
TypePrimary (non-rechargeable) battery
Cell materialsCopper and zinc discs, brine electrolyte
Voltage per cellAbout 0.76 V

Background

Volta's invention arose from his dispute with Luigi Galvani, who had attributed the twitching of frogs' legs in contact with dissimilar metals to "animal electricity". Volta argued that the metals, not the animal tissue, generated the electricity, and set out to show that a purely inorganic arrangement could produce a continuous electric current — something electrostatic machines and Leyden jars could not do.

Construction and operation

Each copper–zinc pair separated by electrolyte forms a galvanic cell producing roughly 0.76 volts; stacking many pairs in series multiplies the voltage. Volta also described a "crown of cups" variant using cups of electrolyte linked by metal arcs. When the top and bottom of the pile are connected by a wire, current flows: zinc is oxidised at one electrode while hydrogen is evolved at the copper electrode, and the pile runs until the zinc is consumed or the electrolyte dries out.

Significance

The pile transformed electrical science by giving experimenters a steady current source. Within weeks of its announcement, William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle used one to perform the first electrolysis of water; Humphry Davy used large piles to isolate sodium, potassium and other elements; and the device laid the groundwork for Ørsted's and Ampère's electromagnetism and for the entire battery industry. The unit of electric potential, the volt, is named after Volta.
