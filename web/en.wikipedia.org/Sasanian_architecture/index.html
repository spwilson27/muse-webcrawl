Sasanian architecture refers to the building tradition of the Sasanian Empire (AD 224–651), the last pre-Islamic Persian empire. Developing earlier Parthian and Achaemenid precedents, it achieved a distinctive synthesis of vaulted and domed construction that profoundly influenced the architecture of the Islamic world and beyond. Its characteristic elements include the iwan — a vaulted hall open on one side — great barrel vaults, domes carried on squinches, and stucco and mosaic decoration.

Characteristic forms

The iwan, inherited from Parthian building, became the organizing element of palace plans, often arranged four around a court (four-iwan plan). The Zoroastrian fire temple type known as the chahar taq placed a dome over a square chamber open on four sides. Sasanian engineers mastered the dome on squinches, allowing circular domes over square rooms, and used fast-setting gypsum mortar to erect enormous brick vaults without centering.

Major monuments

The Palace of Ardashir at Firuzabad, founded by the dynasty's first king, used early domes on squinches in its audience halls. The royal city of Bishapur, built partly by Roman prisoners, combined Iranian plans with Greco-Roman mosaics. The supreme monument is the Taq Kasra at Ctesiphon in Mesopotamia: its iwan, some 37 metres high with a span of about 25 metres, remains the largest single-span unreinforced brick vault in the world, built under Khosrow I in the 6th century.

Legacy

After the Arab conquest, Sasanian forms — the iwan, the dome on squinches, stucco ornament — were absorbed into early Islamic architecture, from Abbasid palaces to the mosques and madrasas of later Persia, Central Asia and Mughal India.
