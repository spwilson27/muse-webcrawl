Proprioception

Proprioception is the sense of the position and movement of one's own body parts, mediated by receptors in muscles, tendons, and joints. Sometimes described as a "sixth sense," it allows a person to move accurately without looking — for example, touching the nose with the eyes closed — and is essential for balance, posture, and motor coordination.

Proprioceptors

The principal proprioceptors are muscle spindles, which sense muscle length and the rate of change of length; Golgi tendon organs, which sense muscle tension; and joint receptors, which respond to joint angle and pressure. Signals travel to the cerebellum and somatosensory cortex through pathways such as the dorsal columns.

Function

Proprioceptive feedback enables fine motor control, rapid postural adjustments, and the sense of effort and heaviness of held objects. It works together with the vestibular system and vision to maintain balance.

Impairment

Proprioception can be damaged by peripheral neuropathy (as in diabetes), spinal cord injury, stroke, or vitamin B12 deficiency. Affected individuals may develop sensory ataxia and an unsteady gait that worsens in the dark, when visual compensation is unavailable. The Romberg test assesses proprioceptive function by asking a patient to stand with feet together and eyes closed.

History of study

The concept was developed in the 19th century and the term was coined by Charles Scott Sherrington in 1906, who distinguished proprioceptors from exteroceptors and interoceptors.
