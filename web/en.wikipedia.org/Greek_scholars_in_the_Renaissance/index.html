Greek scholars in the Renaissance

Byzantine émigré scholars brought Greek manuscripts and learning to Renaissance Italy.

Greek scholars in the Renaissance were Byzantine intellectuals who emigrated to Western Europe — above all Italy — during the 14th and 15th centuries, as the Byzantine Empire contracted and finally fell to the Ottomans in 1453. They revived the study of Greek in the West, bringing manuscripts, grammatical knowledge and direct access to the classical and patristic texts that fuelled Renaissance humanism.

Early contacts

Manuel Chrysoloras, invited to teach Greek in Florence in 1397, is traditionally credited with inaugurating the revival; his grammar, the Erotemata, was the first Greek grammar used in the West. His students included Leonardo Bruni and Guarino Veronese. The Council of Ferrara-Florence (1438–39), convened to discuss union of the churches, brought a large Byzantine delegation to Italy, including the philosopher Gemistus Pletho, whose lectures on Plato in Florence inspired Cosimo de' Medici's interest in a Platonic academy, and Bessarion, who became a cardinal and donated his great library of Greek manuscripts to Venice.

After 1453

The fall of Constantinople accelerated emigration. Scholars such as John Argyropoulos, Demetrius Chalcondyles, Constantine Lascaris (author of the first book printed entirely in Greek, a grammar, in 1476), and Janus Lascaris staffed chairs at Florence, Padua, Rome and Milan. Marcus Musurus worked with the printer Aldus Manutius in Venice, whose Aldine Press issued the great editiones principes of Aristotle, Aristophanes, Thucydides and other Greek authors from 1495 onwards, making the texts widely available in portable, affordable editions.

Legacy

Through their teaching, translations and editions, the émigré scholars transformed European learning: Greek became, alongside Latin, a foundation of the humanist curriculum, and direct knowledge of Plato, Homer and the Greek Church Fathers in the original reshaped philosophy, theology and philology.
