The Battle of Hattin took place on 3–4 July 1187 between the Crusader states of the Levant and the forces of Saladin, sultan of Egypt and Syria. It was one of the most decisive battles of the Crusades: the field army of the Kingdom of Jerusalem was virtually annihilated, King Guy of Lusignan was captured, and within months Saladin had retaken Jerusalem itself, ending almost ninety years of Crusader rule over the holy city.

Background

The Crusader kingdom, weakened by succession disputes, advanced in July 1187 to relieve Tiberias, which Saladin had besieged, luring the Franks away from their water sources. Against wiser counsel, Guy marched east across the arid Galilean hills in midsummer heat. Saladin's forces harassed the column, blocked access to springs, and set fire to the dry brush so smoke and thirst tormented the Crusaders.

The battle

On 3 July the thirsty army was forced to camp near the Horns of Hattin, an extinct volcano with twin peaks, cut off from the Sea of Galilee's water by Saladin's troops. On 4 July the encircled Crusaders charged repeatedly toward the lake but were beaten back; the infantry broke toward the Horns and surrendered, and the mounted knights were overwhelmed. The True Cross, the kingdom's most sacred relic, was captured.

Aftermath

Saladin treated the captured king honourably but executed Raynald of Châtillon — whose raids on Muslim caravans and shipping had broken a truce — with his own hand, according to tradition, and ordered the execution of the captured Templar and Hospitaller knights. Deprived of its army, the kingdom's cities and castles surrendered one by one; Jerusalem fell on 2 October 1187. News of the disaster shocked Europe and led directly to the Third Crusade (1189–1192).
