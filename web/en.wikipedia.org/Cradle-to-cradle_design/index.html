Cradle-to-cradle design (also referred to as Cradle to Cradle, C2C, cradle 2 cradle, or regenerative design) is a biomimetic approach to the design of products and systems that models human industry on nature's processes, in which materials are viewed as nutrients circulating in healthy, continuous cycles. It contrasts with the conventional "cradle-to-grave" model of take-make-dispose linear production. The phrase was popularised by chemist Michael Braungart and architect William McDonough in their 2002 book Cradle to Cradle: Remaking the Way We Make Things.

Cradle-to-cradle's two metabolisms: biological nutrients safely return to the biosphere, while technical nutrients circulate within industry.

Principles

The framework rests on three principles: waste equals food — every output is designed as an input for another cycle; use current solar income — systems should run on renewable energy; and celebrate diversity — designs should fit local material, energy, and cultural conditions. Products are designed so that materials separate cleanly into biological nutrients, which can safely biodegrade, and technical nutrients — synthetic or mineral materials kept in closed industrial loops and never released into nature. The approach criticises conventional recycling as "downcycling" that merely slows, rather than eliminates, waste, and aims for eco-effectiveness rather than mere eco-efficiency ("less bad is not good").

Certification and implementation

Since 2005 products have been certified under the Cradle to Cradle Certified™ scheme (Basic through Platinum), assessing material health, material reutilisation, renewable energy, water stewardship, and social fairness; administration passed to the independent Cradle to Cradle Products Innovation Institute in 2010. Certified examples include office chairs, carpets designed for disassembly and take-back, and building materials; the concept influenced circular economy policy, including the city of Venlo's municipal building programme in the Netherlands.
