Bankruptcy in the United States

Bankruptcy cases are heard in federal bankruptcy courts. [Image placeholder]

Bankruptcy in the United States is governed by federal law, principally the United States Bankruptcy Code (Title 11 of the United States Code), as authorised by the Bankruptcy Clause of the Constitution. Cases are filed in the United States bankruptcy courts, a unit of the federal district courts. Filing a petition triggers an automatic stay that halts most collection actions by creditors, and the process aims both to give honest debtors a "fresh start" and to treat creditors equitably.

Chapters of the Bankruptcy Code

  • Chapter 7 — liquidation: a trustee collects the debtor's non-exempt assets, sells them and distributes proceeds to creditors; most remaining unsecured debts of individuals are discharged. This is the most common form for individuals.
  • Chapter 11 — reorganisation, used mainly by businesses (but available to individuals), allowing a debtor to propose a plan to restructure debts and continue operating. Subchapter V (added 2019) streamlines the process for small businesses.
  • Chapter 12 — designed for family farmers and fishermen, with a repayment plan.
  • Chapter 13 — wage-earner reorganisation for individuals with regular income, who repay some or all debts under a court-approved 3- to 5-year plan and receive a discharge on completion.
  • Chapter 9 — adjustment of debts of a municipality (used, for example, by Detroit in 2013, the largest municipal bankruptcy in US history).
  • Chapter 15 — cross-border insolvency cases, based on the UNCITRAL Model Law.

Key concepts

On filing, a bankruptcy estate is created comprising the debtor's property. State and federal exemptions determine what an individual debtor may keep. Some debts are non-dischargeable, including most taxes, child support and alimony, and (absent undue hardship) most student loans. Transfers made to defraud creditors, and preferential payments shortly before filing, may be avoided (reversed) by the trustee. The Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act of 2005 introduced a means test restricting Chapter 7 eligibility for higher-income debtors and added credit-counselling requirements.

History

Early federal bankruptcy laws (1800, 1841, 1867) were temporary responses to financial panics and were repealed. The Bankruptcy Act of 1898 established a permanent system, modernised by the Chandler Act of 1938 and comprehensively replaced by the Bankruptcy Reform Act of 1978, which created the modern Code. Large corporate Chapter 11 cases — such as Enron, Lehman Brothers, General Motors and Chrysler — have repeatedly drawn national attention.
