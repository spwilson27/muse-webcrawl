The term diabetes management refers to the ongoing care and self-care required by people with diabetes mellitus to keep blood glucose levels within a target range and to prevent or delay the disease's acute and long-term complications. Because diabetes is a chronic condition, effective management is a lifelong process combining medical treatment with education, monitoring, diet, and physical activity.

Goals

The primary goal is glycaemic control: avoiding both hyperglycaemia, which over time damages blood vessels, eyes, kidneys, and nerves, and hypoglycaemia, a potentially dangerous side effect of treatment. Control is commonly assessed with the HbA1c test, which reflects average blood glucose over the preceding two to three months; a target below 7% is recommended for many adults, adjusted for individual circumstances. Management also addresses blood pressure and lipids, which strongly influence cardiovascular risk.

Monitoring

People with diabetes may monitor glucose with fingerstick blood glucose meters or continuous glucose monitors (CGMs), which read interstitial glucose and can alert users to dangerous highs and lows. Regular clinical review screens for complications affecting the eyes, kidneys, feet, and cardiovascular system.

Treatment

Type 1 diabetes requires insulin, delivered by injections, insulin pumps, or increasingly by automated insulin-delivery ("closed-loop") systems. Type 2 diabetes is managed first with lifestyle measures and oral or injectable medications such as metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, and GLP-1 receptor agonists, with insulin added when needed. Large trials, notably the DCCT and UKPDS, established that tighter glycaemic control substantially reduces microvascular complications.

Self-management and education

Structured education programmes teach carbohydrate counting, sick-day rules, foot care, and recognition of hypoglycaemia. Diet and exercise improve insulin sensitivity, and weight loss can put type 2 diabetes into remission in some people.
