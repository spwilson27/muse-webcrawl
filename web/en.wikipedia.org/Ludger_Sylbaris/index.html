Ludger Sylbaris (1 June 1874 – c. 1929) was a Martiniquan sailor and labourer who became famous as one of the few survivors of the destruction of Saint-Pierre, Martinique, by the eruption of Mount Pelée on 8 May 1902. He is often described as "the man who lived through Doomsday".

The eruption

On the night of 7 May 1902, Sylbaris was arrested after a bar fight (in some accounts a brawl or drunken disturbance) and was locked overnight in a solitary confinement cell of the Saint-Pierre jail — a small stone cell, partially underground, with thick walls, a tiny slit window and a heavy door. The next morning, a pyroclastic surge (nuée ardente) from Mount Pelée destroyed the city in minutes, killing an estimated 28,000–30,000 people.

Sylbaris's cell protected him from the worst of the blast. He suffered severe burns but survived on dampened cloth and his own urine for moisture, and was rescued four days later, on 12 May, by searchers who heard his cries from the ruins.

Later life

Sylbaris was pardoned and later toured with the Barnum & Bailey circus in the United States, billed as the sole survivor of Saint-Pierre, sitting in a replica of his cell. Accounts of his later years vary; he is reported to have died in Panama around 1929.

Legacy

Sylbaris was not literally the only survivor — others, including the shoemaker Léon Compère-Léandre and the girl Havivra Da Ifrile, survived in the city — but his story became the most famous emblem of the disaster. His cell still stands in Saint-Pierre, known as the cachot de Sylbaris.
