Ross Ice Shelf

The front (Barrier) of the Ross Ice Shelf
Area:≈ 487,000 km²
Thickness:≈ 15–50 m at the front; up to ~700 m at grounding line
Location:Ross Sea, Antarctica

The Ross Ice Shelf is the largest ice shelf in Antarctica, with an area of roughly 487,000 square kilometres—about the size of France—and a length of some 800 kilometres. It is the floating extension of the West and East Antarctic ice sheets where they meet the Ross Sea, fed by great ice streams and outlet glaciers including the Beardmore and Shackleton glaciers from the east and the West Antarctic ice streams from the south.

Description

The shelf's seaward edge forms a nearly continuous ice cliff, the Ross Barrier, tens of metres high, first sighted in January 1841 by the expedition of Sir James Clark Ross, for whom the shelf, sea and island are named. Ice flows from the grounding line toward the front at speeds of hundreds of metres per year, and huge tabular icebergs periodically calve from the front—including iceberg B-15 in 2000, one of the largest ever recorded, at about 11,000 square kilometres.

Although largely afloat, the shelf buttresses the grounded ice streams behind it, slowing their discharge into the ocean; scientists therefore monitor it closely, since its collapse would accelerate sea-level contribution from West Antarctica. Drilling projects such as ANDRILL have sampled sediments beneath and beyond the shelf to reconstruct its past retreats, and oceanographic work explores the cavity beneath it, which hosts a distinctive ecosystem in permanent darkness.

Exploration history

The Barrier was the starting point for the heroic-age journeys to the South Pole: both Roald Amundsen (from the Bay of Whales) and Robert Falcon Scott (from Ross Island) based their 1911–12 polar expeditions at its edge. The United States' Operation Deep Freeze and New Zealand's Scott Base were later established nearby on Ross Island, and the shelf remains a logistics corridor for Antarctic research.
