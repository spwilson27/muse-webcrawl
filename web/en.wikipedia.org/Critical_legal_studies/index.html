Critical legal studies

CLS argues that law is intertwined with social issues and politics.

Critical legal studies (CLS) is a school of legal theory that first emerged as a movement in the United States during the 1970s. Critical legal studies adherents claim that laws are used to maintain the status quo of society's power structures; it is also their position that the law is a codified form of society's biases against groups of people. Despite wide variation in the opinions of critical legal scholars around the world, there is general consensus that the key goals of critical legal studies are to demonstrate the ambiguity, indeterminacy and possible contradictory results of legal doctrine.

Origins

The movement crystallised at the first Conference on Critical Legal Studies at the University of Wisconsin–Madison in 1977. Its scholars drew on American legal realism — which had argued in the 1920s–30s that judges decide cases on more than formal rules — as well as on the Frankfurt School's critical theory and the political ferment of the 1960s. Harvard Law School became a major centre, with figures such as Duncan Kennedy, Morton Horwitz and Roberto Mangabeira Unger, whose book The Critical Legal Studies Movement (1983) became a defining text.

Core claims

CLS advances an indeterminacy thesis: legal materials, it argues, rarely compel a single outcome, and judges' choices reflect ideology and policy preferences rather than neutral deduction. It also criticises legal formalism and the idea that legal reasoning is autonomous from politics, and it examines how legal categories — contract, property, rights — naturalise existing distributions of wealth and power.

Influence and criticism

CLS influenced successor movements including critical race theory, feminist legal theory and LatCrit. Critics argue that CLS is purely negative, undermining the rule of law without offering workable alternatives, and that radical indeterminacy overstates the freedom judges actually possess. By the 1990s CLS as an organised movement had largely dissolved, though its themes persist throughout legal academia.
