Genetic testing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genetic testing, also known as DNA testing, is used to identify changes in DNA sequence or chromosome structure. Genetic testing can also include measuring the results of genetic changes, such as RNA analysis as an output of gene expression, or through biochemical analysis to measure specific protein output. In a medical setting, genetic testing can be used to diagnose or rule out suspected genetic disorders, predict risks for specific conditions, and guide decisions about treatment and prevention.

Types

Diagnostic testing confirms or rules out a suspected condition in a symptomatic person. Predictive and presymptomatic testing estimates the risk of developing a condition later in life, such as Huntington's disease or hereditary cancers caused by BRCA1 and BRCA2 variants. Carrier testing identifies people who carry one copy of a recessive variant (for example for cystic fibrosis or Tay–Sachs disease). Prenatal testing and non-invasive prenatal screening detect chromosomal abnormalities such as Down syndrome before birth; newborn screening tests infants for treatable metabolic and genetic disorders. Pharmacogenomic testing predicts responses to particular drugs, and forensic and ancestry testing use DNA for identification and genealogy.

Methods

Methods range from karyotyping and fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) for large chromosomal changes, through targeted variant tests and gene panels, to whole-exome and whole-genome sequencing. Falling sequencing costs — from roughly a hundred million dollars for the first human genome to under a thousand dollars — have made broad testing routine, but interpretation of variants of uncertain significance remains a major challenge, and results are often accompanied by genetic counseling.

Ethical and legal issues

Testing raises questions about privacy, discrimination (addressed in the US by the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008), incidental findings unrelated to the reason for testing, implications for relatives who share DNA, and the marketing of direct-to-consumer tests. Consent, data storage and the possibility of re-identification from genomic data are ongoing policy concerns.
