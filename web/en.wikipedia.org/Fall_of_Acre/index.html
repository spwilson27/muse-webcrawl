Fall of Acre

Date6 April – 28 May 1291 (final assault)
LocationAcre, Kingdom of Jerusalem
AttackersMamluk Sultanate under Sultan al-Ashraf Khalil
DefendersCrusader garrison: Templars, Hospitallers, Teutonic Knights, royal and civic forces
ResultMamluk victory; end of the mainland Crusader states

The fall of Acre in 1291 was the capture of the Crusader port city of Acre, capital of the rump Kingdom of Jerusalem, by the Mamluk army of Sultan al-Ashraf Khalil. The siege, lasting from April to 28 May 1291, destroyed the last major Crusader stronghold in the Holy Land and is conventionally taken to mark the end of the Crusades in the Levant.

Background

After the fall of Jerusalem to Saladin in 1187 and the Third Crusade, the Kingdom of Jerusalem survived as a coastal strip centred on Acre, a wealthy trading port dominated by the military orders and Italian maritime communes. Sultan Qalawun's capture of Tripoli in 1289 left Acre exposed; his son Khalil, succeeding in 1290, assembled a huge army with massive siege engines — including great trebuchets nicknamed "the Victorious" and "the Furious" — and marched on the city in spring 1291, spurred by a massacre of Muslim merchants in Acre the previous year.

The siege

From 6 April the Mamluks bombarded the double walls while sappers mined the towers. The defenders, perhaps 15,000–17,000 troops including some 1,000 knights and sergeants of the orders, were reinforced too late and in too small numbers from Cyprus by King Henry II. On 18 May the Accursed Tower and the King's Tower fell; Khalil ordered a general assault on 28 May. The city was stormed amid heavy slaughter; the Templar master William of Beaujeu was killed, and the Hospitaller master Jean de Villiers was wounded and evacuated by sea. Thousands of civilians were killed or enslaved as the population fled to the harbour and overloaded ships.

Aftermath

Remaining Crusader positions — Tyre, Sidon, Beirut, Tortosa, and Athlit — were evacuated or surrendered by August 1291; the Templars held the island of Ruad until 1302. The Mamluks systematically demolished the coastal fortifications and harbours to prevent any Crusader return. Latin Christendom mounted no successful return expedition, and the fall of Acre closed two centuries of Crusader presence in the Levant.
