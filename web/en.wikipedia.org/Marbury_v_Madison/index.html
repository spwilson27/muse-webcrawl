Marbury v. Madison

CourtSupreme Court of the United States
Full case nameWilliam Marbury v. James Madison, Secretary of State of the United States
DecidedFebruary 24, 1803
Citation5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137
Chief JusticeJohn Marshall (unanimous opinion)
HoldingSection 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 conflicts with the Constitution and is void; the Supreme Court has the authority to review acts of Congress and declare them unconstitutional (judicial review).

Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803), is a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court that established the principle of judicial review — the power of the federal courts to declare an act of Congress void if it conflicts with the Constitution. Written by Chief Justice John Marshall, the unanimous opinion is regarded as one of the foundations of United States constitutional law.

Background

In the final days of his presidency, the Federalist John Adams appointed numerous judges and justices of the peace under the Judiciary Act of 1801 and the District of Columbia Organic Act — the so-called "Midnight Judges" — to keep Federalists in office after the Democratic-Republican Thomas Jefferson assumed the presidency. William Marbury was appointed a justice of the peace for the District of Columbia, but his commission, though signed and sealed, was not delivered before Adams left office. The new Secretary of State, James Madison, refused to deliver it. Marbury sued directly in the Supreme Court under Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, asking for a writ of mandamus ordering Madison to deliver the commission.

Decision

Marshall's opinion posed three questions: (1) Did Marbury have a right to the commission? Yes — the appointment was complete upon signing and sealing. (2) Did the laws afford him a remedy? Yes — the refusal was a violation of a legal right, and mandamus was an appropriate remedy in principle. (3) Was that remedy available from the Supreme Court? No — because Section 13 of the Judiciary Act purported to expand the Court's original jurisdiction beyond the limits in Article III of the Constitution, it was unconstitutional and therefore void. Marbury thus lost his case, but the Court asserted its far greater power to review legislation.

Marshall reasoned that a written constitution must be paramount law; that an act of the legislature repugnant to the Constitution is void; and that "it is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." By striking down a minor provision while denying itself the relief Marbury sought, the Court claimed the power of judicial review without provoking the Jefferson administration into a confrontation it could defy.

Significance

Marbury was the first time the Supreme Court invalidated an act of Congress, and it cemented the judiciary's role as a co-equal branch and final arbiter of constitutional meaning. The Court did not strike down another federal statute until Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857), but the principle was later extended to state laws and executive actions, and judicial review became central to landmark rulings from Brown v. Board of Education onward. The decision remains among the most studied in American law, and Marshall's opinion is often described as a masterpiece of legal reasoning and political statecraft.
