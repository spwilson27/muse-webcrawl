Congregational polity

Congregational polity, often called congregationalism as a system of church governance, is a form of ecclesiastical polity in which each local congregation is autonomous and self-governing, acknowledging no higher earthly authority than itself. It stands in contrast to episcopal polity, in which churches are governed by bishops, and presbyterian polity, in which congregations are accountable to assemblies of elders.

Principles

Under congregational polity, the local church meeting of members holds final authority in matters of doctrine, membership, discipline, and the calling of ministers. Congregations may voluntarily join associations or denominations for fellowship and cooperative work, but such bodies are advisory only and cannot impose decisions on a local church. The theological basis usually rests on the priesthood of all believers and on the belief that Christ alone is head of the church, with authority exercised through the gathered congregation.

Churches using congregational polity

The system is characteristic of Baptist churches, Congregational churches, Disciples of Christ congregations, many charismatic and independent evangelical churches, and the United Church of Christ, whose congregations retain historic congregational autonomy. It was practised by the English Separatists and by the Puritan settlers of New England, whose town churches shaped early American democracy; town meetings and congregational meetings developed side by side.
