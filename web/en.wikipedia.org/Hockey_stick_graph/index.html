Hockey stick graph

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The "hockey stick" shape: a long relatively flat shaft followed by a sharp recent rise.

A hockey stick graph, or hockey stick curve, is a graph whose shape resembles an ice hockey stick, with a long, relatively flat "shaft" followed by a sharp upward "blade". The term became prominent in climate science for reconstructions of global or hemispheric temperature over the past 1,000 to 2,000 years that show relative stability followed by a steep rise in the twentieth century.

Origin in climate science

The name derives from the reconstruction by climatologists Michael E. Mann, Raymond S. Bradley and Malcolm K. Hughes (1998, 1999), which reconstructed Northern Hemisphere temperatures back to AD 1000 using proxy data such as tree rings, ice cores and corals, combined with the instrumental record. Their graph, featured prominently in the IPCC Third Assessment Report (2001), showed the 1990s as likely the warmest decade of the millennium in the Northern Hemisphere.

Debate and subsequent research

The reconstruction became a focus of the climate change controversy, attracting criticism of its statistical methods (notably from Stephen McIntyre and Ross McKitrick) and congressional scrutiny. Several independent investigations — including the US National Academy of Sciences' North Report (2006) and numerous subsequent reconstructions using different data and methods — have broadly supported the central conclusion: recent decades have been warmer than any comparable period in at least the last several centuries to millennium, though uncertainty grows further back in time. The IPCC's later assessment reports present multiple reconstructions showing a similar shape.

Other uses

"Hockey stick" is also used in business and technology for charts of growth that remain flat for a long period before rising steeply, for example in startup revenue or adoption curves, and in discussions of phenomena such as population and resource use.
