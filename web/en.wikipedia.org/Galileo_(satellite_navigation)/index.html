Galileo is a global navigation satellite system (GNSS) created by the European Union through the European Space Agency (ESA), operated by the European Union Agency for the Space Programme (EUSPA) and headquartered in Prague, Czech Republic, with two ground operations centres in Oberpfaffenhofen, Germany, and Fucino, Italy. The €10 billion project is named after the Italian astronomer Galileo Galilei.

One of the aims of Galileo is to provide an independent high-precision positioning system so European political and military authorities do not have to rely on the US GPS or the Russian GLONASS systems, which could be disabled or degraded by their operators at any time. The use of basic (lower-precision) Galileo services is free and open to everyone. Higher-precision capabilities are available to paying commercial users.

History

The Galileo programme was formally launched in 2003, after years of study. The first test satellite, GIOVE-A, was launched on 28 December 2005, followed by GIOVE-B in 2008. The first two operational satellites were launched in October 2011, beginning the in-orbit validation phase. Initial services were declared operational on 15 December 2016. Batch launches aboard Ariane 5 rockets placed four satellites at a time, and the constellation approached completion in the early 2020s, with additional satellites launched on Falcon 9 rockets in 2024.

Services

Galileo provides several services: the Open Service (OS) for mass-market applications; the free High Accuracy Service (HAS), offering corrections for positioning accuracy down to about 20 centimetres; the Public Regulated Service (PRS), an encrypted service reserved for government users; and a Search and Rescue (SAR) service that contributes to the international Cospas-Sarsat system and returns an acknowledgement to the user that their distress signal has been received.

System description

The space segment consists of 30 satellites in three planes of medium Earth orbit at an altitude of 23,222 km, inclined at 56 degrees to the equator. Each satellite broadcasts on several frequencies (E1, E5a, E5b and E6), enabling dual-frequency receivers to achieve metre-level accuracy and to mitigate ionospheric errors. Galileo satellites carry passive hydrogen maser and rubidium atomic clocks.

Compatibility

Most modern smartphones and receivers combine Galileo with GPS, GLONASS and BeiDou signals, improving accuracy and availability, particularly in urban canyons. An intergovernmental agreement with the United States ensures Galileo and GPS use compatible radio standards.
