Scharnhorst was a German capital ship, alternatively described as a battleship or battlecruiser, of the Kriegsmarine in the Second World War. Named for the Prussian general and army reformer Gerhard von Scharnhorst, she was the lead ship of her class, which included her sister Gneisenau. Fast and heavily armoured but armed with relatively light 28 cm (11 in) main guns, she spent much of the war as a commerce raider in the Atlantic and Arctic.

Construction and design

Scharnhorst was laid down at Kriegsmarinewerft Wilhelmshaven in 1935, launched on 3 October 1936 and commissioned on 7 January 1939. She displaced about 32,000 tonnes standard and could exceed 31 knots. After early sea trials revealed severe flooding over the bow in heavy seas, she was rebuilt with a raked "Atlantic bow". Plans to rearm her with 38 cm guns, like Gneisenau was to receive, were never carried out on Scharnhorst.

War service

In November 1939 Scharnhorst and Gneisenau sank the British armed merchant cruiser HMS Rawalpindi. During the invasion of Norway in April 1940 the sisters fought off the battlecruiser HMS Renown, and on 8 June 1940 they sank the aircraft carrier HMS Glorious and her two escorting destroyers — Scharnhorst's gunnery hit, at roughly 24 km, is among the longest-range naval gunfire hits recorded. In Operation Berlin (January–March 1941) the pair raided Atlantic convoys, sinking some 115,000 tons of shipping. Based at Brest, they were repeatedly bombed, and in February 1942 made the famous "Channel Dash" (Operation Cerberus) back to Germany, though Scharnhorst struck two mines en route.

Final battle and sinking

From March 1943 Scharnhorst was based in northern Norway, threatening the Arctic convoys to the Soviet Union. On Christmas Day 1943 she sailed to attack convoy JW 55B. In the resulting Battle of the North Cape on 26 December 1943, she was intercepted by a Royal Navy force built around the battleship HMS Duke of York. Blinded by the loss of her radar and overwhelmed by gunfire and torpedoes, Scharnhorst capsized and sank with the loss of 1,932 of her crew of 1,968; only 36 men survived. Her wreck was located in 2000 by a Norwegian survey expedition at a depth of about 290 metres off North Cape.
