Western Interior Seaway

Western Interior Seaway
Also called: Cretaceous Seaway, Niobrara Sea
Existed: approx. 100–66 million years ago
Extent: Arctic Ocean to Gulf of Mexico
Width: up to about 1,000 km

The Western Interior Seaway (also called the Cretaceous Seaway or Niobrara Sea) was a vast inland sea that split the continent of North America into western Laramidia and eastern Appalachia during the middle and late Cretaceous Period, roughly 100 to 66 million years ago. At its maximum it stretched from the Arctic Ocean to the Gulf of Mexico and was up to a thousand kilometres wide.

Geology

The seaway formed as global sea levels rose and the foreland basin east of the rising Rocky Mountains subsided. Its deposits — chalk, shale and limestone such as the Niobrara Formation and Pierre Shale — blanket much of the Great Plains and are a rich source of fossils and of petroleum.

Fauna

The warm, shallow sea teemed with marine reptiles such as mosasaurs and plesiosaurs, giant fish such as Xiphactinus, ammonites, and sharks, while dinosaurs walked its shores. The seaway withdrew as the Laramide orogeny uplifted the continent and sea levels fell toward the end of the Cretaceous.
