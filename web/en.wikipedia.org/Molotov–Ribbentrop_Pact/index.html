The Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, officially the Treaty of Non-Aggression between Germany and the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics, was signed in Moscow on 23 August 1939 by Soviet foreign minister Vyacheslav Molotov and German foreign minister Joachim von Ribbentrop. Publicly a ten-year non-aggression treaty between Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union, it stunned the world because the two regimes had been ideological enemies. A secret protocol divided Eastern Europe into German and Soviet spheres of influence.

Secret protocol

The protocol assigned Finland, Estonia, Latvia, and Bessarabia to the Soviet sphere, Lithuania to the German sphere (transferred to the Soviet sphere by a later amendment of 28 September 1939 in exchange for part of Poland), and split Poland roughly along the Narew, Vistula, and San rivers. Acting on this understanding, Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939 and the USSR invaded from the east on 17 September; the two states subsequently partitioned Poland, and the Soviet Union annexed the Baltic states and Bessarabia in 1940 and attacked Finland in the Winter War.

Aftermath

Economic agreements accompanying the pact supplied Germany with Soviet raw materials until Germany broke the treaty by invading the Soviet Union in Operation Barbarossa on 22 June 1941. The Soviet Union officially denied the secret protocol's existence until 1989, when it was formally acknowledged and denounced by the Congress of People's Deputies. The pact remains a central and bitterly remembered event in Poland and the Baltic states, where 23 August is commemorated as the European Day of Remembrance for Victims of Stalinism and Nazism.
