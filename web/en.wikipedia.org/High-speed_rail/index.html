High-speed rail

Definition (UIC): ≥250 km/h on new lines; ≥200 km/h on upgraded lines
First system: Shinkansen, Japan (1964)
Largest network: China (>40,000 km)

High-speed rail (HSR) is a type of rail network that runs trains significantly faster than conventional rail, using an integrated system of specialised rolling stock and dedicated tracks. The International Union of Railways (UIC) generally defines it as at least 250 km/h (155 mph) on purpose-built lines and 200 km/h (124 mph) on upgraded lines. High-speed trains travel roughly two to three times faster than ordinary trains and compete with air travel on journeys of a few hours.

History

Japan's Tōkaidō Shinkansen, opened in 1964 for the Tokyo Olympics at 210 km/h, was the first high-speed railway. France followed with the TGV in 1981, Germany with the ICE in 1991, and Spain, Italy, South Korea, and Taiwan built networks in subsequent decades. China, beginning in 2007–2008, expanded at unprecedented speed and now operates by far the world's largest network — over 40,000 km — with Fuxing trains running at up to 350 km/h.

Technology

High-speed lines use gentle curves, no level crossings, in-cab signalling, and continuous welded rail on concrete slab or ballast. Trains are streamlined, usually electrically powered from overhead lines, with distributed traction in many designs. The absolute rail speed record, 574.8 km/h, was set by a modified French TGV in 2007; commercial magnetic levitation (the Shanghai maglev) reaches 431 km/h.

Impact

HSR has shifted passengers from air and road on corridors such as Tokyo–Osaka, Paris–Lyon, and Beijing–Shanghai, cutting emissions per passenger-kilometre. Critics note the high construction costs and long payback periods; several countries, including the United States, still lack true high-speed lines, though projects such as California High-Speed Rail are under construction.
