Major-General Charles George Gordon (28 January 1833 – 26 January 1885) was a British Army officer and administrator. He is remembered for his campaigns in China and northern Africa and for his death at the fall of Khartoum. An eccentric and deeply religious officer, Gordon became one of the most celebrated — and controversial — heroes of Victorian Britain.

Early career and China

Commissioned in the Royal Engineers, Gordon served in the Crimean War and was part of the international force that surveyed the Danube delta. Sent to China during the Second Opium War, he was present at the capture of Peking in 1860. He then took command of the Ever Victorious Army, a Chinese force officered by foreigners, and led it in a series of victories against the Taiping rebels in 1863–64 — earning the nickname "Chinese Gordon" and high honors from the Qing court, which he characteristically declined in monetary form.

Sudan and Khartoum

As Governor of Equatoria and then Governor-General of the Sudan (1877–1880) in Egyptian service, Gordon suppressed slave trading and mapped the upper Nile. In 1884, with the Mahdist revolt having destroyed Egyptian authority in the Sudan, the British government sent him to Khartoum to oversee an evacuation. Instead, Gordon organized the city's defense and resolved to hold it. Besieged for ten months, Khartoum fell on 26 January 1885, two days before a British relief expedition reached the city; Gordon was killed in the assault. His death provoked public outrage in Britain and contributed to the fall of Gladstone's government.

Character and legacy

Gordon combined intense evangelical Christianity with impulsive independence of command. Victorian hagiography made him a martyr-hero; later writers, notably Lytton Strachey in Eminent Victorians (1918), painted him as a reckless, self-destructive mystic. His memory remains contested in Britain and in Sudan, where the Mahdist state he fought founded modern national consciousness.
