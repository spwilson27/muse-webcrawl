The subsequent Nuremberg trials (formally the Trials of War Criminals before the Nuremberg Military Tribunals) were a series of twelve trials for war crimes against members of the leadership of Nazi Germany, held at the Palace of Justice in Nuremberg, Germany, from 1946 to 1949. They followed the better-known International Military Tribunal (IMT), the "Trial of the Major War Criminals", of 1945–46. Unlike the IMT, which was conducted by the four Allied powers, the subsequent trials were run solely by the United States, before American military tribunals, with cases prosecuted by Brigadier General Telford Taylor.

Background

The trials were authorized under Control Council Law No. 10 of 20 December 1945, which allowed each occupying power to try suspected war criminals in its own zone of occupation. Because the cooperation of the four powers had broken down, the American authorities organized the proceedings alone under the Office of Chief Counsel for War Crimes, initially headed by Telford Taylor following the resignation of Robert H. Jackson. The defendants were charged with crimes against peace, war crimes, crimes against humanity, and conspiracy, as set out in Law No. 10.

The twelve trials

  • Doctors' Trial (United States v. Karl Brandt et al.) — medical personnel who conducted experiments on concentration camp prisoners.
  • Milch Trial — Field Marshal Erhard Milch, for war crimes and crimes against humanity.
  • Judges' Trial — jurists who implemented the Nazi legal system.
  • Pohl Trial — Oswald Pohl and officials of the SS economic administration (WVHA).
  • Flick Trial — industrialist Friedrich Flick and associates.
  • IG Farben Trial — directors of the chemical conglomerate IG Farben.
  • Hostages Trial — generals commanding in south-eastern Europe.
  • RuSHA Trial — officials of the SS Race and Settlement Main Office.
  • Einsatzgruppen Trial — commanders of the mobile killing squads.
  • Krupp Trial — directors of the Krupp armaments firm.
  • Ministries Trial — officials of German government ministries.
  • High Command Trial — senior officers of the Wehrmacht.

Outcomes

Of the 185 individuals indicted, 142 were convicted, 25 were sentenced to death, and 20 received life imprisonment; others received terms of imprisonment. Many sentences were later reduced or commuted by the American High Commissioner John J. McCloy in 1951, a controversial act of clemency during the early Cold War.
