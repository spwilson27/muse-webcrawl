Matthew Calbraith Perry (10 April 1794 – 4 March 1858) was a commodore of the United States Navy who commanded ships in several wars and played a central role in the opening of Japan to trade with the Western world under the Convention of Kanagawa in 1854. Perry was also an influential naval reformer: he championed the transition from sail to steam power, helped shape United States naval education, and is sometimes called the "Father of the Steam Navy".

Perry came from a distinguished naval family of Newport, Rhode Island. His father, Christopher Raymond Perry, was a naval officer, and his elder brother, Oliver Hazard Perry, won fame at the Battle of Lake Erie in the War of 1812. Matthew Perry entered the navy as a midshipman in 1809, aged fifteen, and built a career spanning the suppression of piracy and the slave trade, service in African and Mediterranean waters, the Mexican–American War, and finally the celebrated expedition to Japan that made his name known worldwide.

Early life and career

Perry was born in Newport, Rhode Island, the younger son of Christopher Raymond Perry and Sarah Wallace Alexander. Appointed a midshipman in 1809, he first served aboard the frigate USS Revenge, commanded by his brother Oliver. During the War of 1812 he served in operations connected with the defence of Newport and, seeing little action, spent part of the war assigned to vessels blockaded in port — a frustration that shaped his later insistence on a modern, steam-powered battle fleet.

In the 1820s Perry commanded the schooner USS Shark in the Caribbean, suppressing piracy and the transatlantic slave trade, and in 1821 he carried a party of free Black colonists and agents to West Africa as part of the movement that founded Liberia, helping select the site of Monrovia. He later served in the Mediterranean, commanded the Navy's first steam warship, the experimental USS Fulton (Demologos's successor, often called Fulton II), in 1837, and used that command to train officers and men in steam engineering, an experience that led to his strong advocacy of a steam navy. Promoted to captain in 1837, he commanded the New York Navy Yard at Brooklyn from 1841 to 1843.

Africa Squadron and the Mexican–American War

In 1843–1844 Perry commanded the Africa Squadron, charged with enforcing the suppression of the slave trade off West Africa under the Webster–Ashburton Treaty. During the Mexican–American War he first served as second-in-command of the Home Squadron in the Gulf of Mexico and then, in March 1847, succeeded Commodore David Conner in command. Perry's forces captured the port of Frontera, took Tabasco after an expedition up the Grijalva River, and supported the coastal operations that complemented the army campaign against Veracruz and Mexico City. The war confirmed his reputation as an organiser of amphibious and riverine operations.

The opening of Japan

Background

Since the 1630s Japan had followed a policy of national seclusion (sakoku), permitting only limited Dutch and Chinese trade at Nagasaki and refusing contact with other foreign powers. By the early 1850s the United States had strong incentives to change this: American merchantmen and whalers in the North Pacific needed ports of refuge and supply, shipwrecked American sailors had been harshly treated in Japan, and the recent acquisition of California and the growth of the China trade made a coaling station on the route to Asia strategically attractive. President Millard Fillmore authorised an expedition to negotiate with Japan, and Perry — now the navy's senior commodore and commander of the East India Squadron — was chosen to lead it.

The 1853 visit

Perry's squadron of four ships — the steam frigates Susquehanna and Powhatan-class consorts alongside the sailing warships Plymouth and Saratoga — entered Edo Bay on 8 July 1853 and anchored off Uraga. The black-hulled steamers, belching smoke, made a deep impression and entered Japanese memory as the "black ships" (kurofune). Perry refused to be diverted to Nagasaki, insisted on delivering President Fillmore's letter directly to representatives of the Tokugawa shogunate, and backed his diplomacy with surveys of the bay and a show of disciplined force. The letter, formally received on 14 July, requested humane treatment of shipwrecked crews, permission to purchase coal and provisions, and the opening of ports to American commerce. Perry promised to return the following year with a larger fleet for the Japanese answer.

The Convention of Kanagawa

Perry returned in February 1854 with nine ships. Negotiations at Yokohama produced the Convention of Kanagawa, signed on 31 March 1854. The convention opened the ports of Shimoda and Hakodate to American vessels for supplies and protection of shipwrecked crews, permitted an American consul to reside at Shimoda, and set the stage for the fuller commercial treaty negotiated by Townsend Harris in 1858. On his return voyage Perry also concluded a compact with the Kingdom of Ryukyu at Naha, and he had earlier recommended the American acquisition or use of positions in the Ryukyus, Formosa and the Bonin Islands as stepping stones to Asia.

Later life and legacy

Perry returned to the United States in 1855 to public acclaim. Congress voted him a grant of $20,000, and he supervised the publication of the official Narrative of the Expedition of an American Squadron to the China Seas and Japan (1856), compiled from his papers by Francis L. Hawks. His health, worn by decades of service and by malaria contracted in the Caribbean, declined; he died in New York City on 4 March 1858 and was later reinterred in Island Cemetery, Newport.

Perry's expedition is regarded in Japan as one of the shocks that exposed the weakness of the Tokugawa order and hastened the Meiji Restoration; annual "Black Ships Festivals" are still held at Shimoda and Yokosuka, and a monument to Perry stands at Kurihama, where he first landed. In the United States he is remembered as a model of the professional naval officer: an educator who helped found the United States Naval Academy's curriculum tradition through his earlier apprentice system, an organiser of the navy's first corps of engineers, and a leading advocate of steam propulsion and shell guns. His younger contemporary standing has sometimes led to confusion with his more flamboyant brother Oliver, but historians generally credit Matthew Perry with the larger long-term influence on American naval policy and Pacific strategy.
