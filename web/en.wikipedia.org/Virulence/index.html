Virulence

Pathogens differ widely in virulence

Field: Microbiology, epidemiology

Related measures: Infectivity, pathogenicity, LD50

Virulence is a pathogen's ability to damage a host. In microbiology and epidemiology, it refers to the degree of damage caused by a microbe to its host, encompassing the severity of the disease the pathogen produces. Virulence is often quantified by measures such as the lethal dose (LD50) — the amount of pathogen required to kill half of infected hosts — or by case fatality rates.

Virulence factors

The molecular traits that enable a pathogen to cause damage are called virulence factors. In bacteria these include adhesive structures such as pili and fimbriae, capsules that resist phagocytosis, toxins (exotoxins and endotoxins), secretion systems that inject proteins into host cells, and enzymes that degrade tissue. Viruses employ factors such as surface proteins that bind host receptors and proteins that suppress the immune response.

Evolution

Virulence evolves under a trade-off: pathogens must reproduce and transmit, but killing the host too quickly can reduce transmission. Many pathogens evolve toward intermediate virulence, although outcomes depend on transmission mode; pathogens spread by vectors or water may remain highly virulent because transmission does not depend on host mobility. The attenuation of myxoma virus in Australian rabbits is a classic case study.

Attenuation

Deliberately reducing virulence, called attenuation, is a traditional method of producing live vaccines, as in the oral polio and yellow fever vaccines.
