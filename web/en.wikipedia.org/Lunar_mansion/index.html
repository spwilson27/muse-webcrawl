Lunar mansion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A lunar mansion is a segment of the ecliptic (the path the Sun follows across the sky, and approximately the Moon's path) through which the Moon passes on one night of its roughly 27.3-day sidereal cycle. Because the Moon travels about 13 degrees of arc per day, the ecliptic divides naturally into 27 or 28 mansions. Several pre-modern astronomical traditions independently developed such systems for calendar-keeping, astrology and navigation, each naming the mansions after the stars or asterisms within them.

Traditions

Indian: nakshatra

Indian astronomy divides the ecliptic into 27 nakṣatras, each of 13°20′, named for prominent stars or groups — Aśvinī, Bharaṇī, Kṛttikā (the Pleiades), Rohiṇī (Aldebaran), Mṛgaśīrṣa, Ārdrā (Betelgeuse), and so on through Revatī. The list is already given in the Taittirīya Saṁhitā and remains fundamental to Hindu calendrics and astrology (Jyotiṣa): the Moon's nakshatra at birth determines the starting period of the Vimśottarī daśā system.

Chinese: xiù

Chinese astronomy uses 28 xiù (宿, "lodges"), grouped into four palaces of seven, each associated with one of the Four Symbols: the Azure Dragon of the East, the Black Tortoise of the North, the White Tiger of the West and the Vermilion Bird of the South. Unlike the roughly equal Indian divisions, the Chinese lodges have unequal widths determined by their determinative stars, and they served to track the Moon and, by culmination observations, the seasons; the complete set is attested on a lacquer chest from the tomb of Marquis Yi of Zeng (433 BC).

Arabic: manāzil al-qamar

Arabic astronomy and astrology adopted 28 manāzil al-qamar ("mansions of the Moon"), with names such as Al Thurayya (the Pleiades), Aldebaran and Al Jabhah. Adapted partly from the nakshatra system via Persian and Indian sources, the manāzil were used in weather lore (the anwāʾ tradition of rain stars) and in medieval European astrology, where the "28 mansions of the Moon" appear in works attributed to Albumasar and in later grimoires and astrological medicine.
