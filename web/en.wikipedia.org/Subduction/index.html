Subduction

Subduction
ProcessOne tectonic plate sinks beneath another into the mantle
Typical rates2–8 cm per year
Associated featuresOceanic trenches, volcanic arcs, deep earthquakes

Subduction is a geological process in which an oceanic plate is forced down into the mantle under a second, overriding plate. Subduction zones, where this occurs, are convergent plate boundaries characterised by deep oceanic trenches, volcanic island arcs, and the largest earthquakes on Earth, including megathrust earthquakes capable of generating tsunamis.

Process

Subduction occurs because oceanic lithosphere, formed at mid-ocean ridges, cools and becomes denser than the underlying asthenosphere as it ages. When it meets a less dense plate, the older, denser plate bends and sinks at angles typically between 30 and 60 degrees. The descending slab carries water-rich sediments and hydrated minerals into the mantle; released water lowers the melting point of the mantle wedge above the slab, generating magma that rises to form volcanic arcs such as the Andes, Japan, and Indonesia.

Friction and deformation along the plate interface produce earthquakes along a dipping zone known as the Wadati–Benioff zone, which can extend to depths of nearly 670 km, the base of the upper mantle.

Significance

Subduction is the engine of plate tectonics' recycling system: oceanic crust, which is nowhere older than about 200 million years, is returned to the mantle and consumed, balancing the new crust created at spreading ridges. Subduction also plays a central role in the long-term carbon cycle, transporting carbonate sediments into the mantle and returning carbon dioxide to the atmosphere through arc volcanoes such as stratovolcanoes.

Examples

  • The Peru–Chile Trench, where the Nazca Plate subducts beneath South America, building the Andes.
  • The Japan Trench, source of the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami.
  • The Mariana Trench, the deepest oceanic trench on Earth.
  • The Cascadia subduction zone off the Pacific Northwest of North America.
