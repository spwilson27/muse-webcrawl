Hafsid dynasty

The Hafsids (Arabic: الحفصيون) were a Sunni Muslim dynasty of Berber descent who ruled Ifriqiya (modern-day Tunisia, western Libya, and eastern Algeria) from 1229 to 1574.

History

The dynasty takes its name from Muhammad ibn Abi Hafs, a Berber from the Hintata tribe of the High Atlas mountains of Morocco, whose descendants served as governors of Ifriqiya under the Almohad Caliphate. In 1229 Abu Zakariya Yahya declared independence from the weakening Almohads and established himself in Tunis, expanding his rule over Constantine, Béjaïa and Tripoli. Under his son Abu Abd Allah Muhammad al-Mustansir (r. 1249–1277), who assumed the title of caliph, the Hafsid state reached its height: it received the allegiance of Mecca and Medina, prospered from trans-Saharan and Mediterranean trade, and was wealthy enough that in 1270 it paid off the Crusade of Louis IX of France, who died of dysentery outside Tunis.

The later Middle Ages saw dynastic strife, pressure from the Marinids of Morocco — who twice occupied Tunis in the 1340s–50s — and the growing impact of plague. The dynasty revived under Ahmad II, Uthman (r. 1435–1488), whose long reign restored stability and made Tunis a centre of learning, and the early sixteenth century saw the Hafsids caught between the Ottoman Empire and Habsburg Spain. Spanish forces installed and supported Hafsid client rulers, but in 1574 the Ottomans definitively conquered Tunis, ending Hafsid rule.

Culture

Hafsid Tunis was a notable centre of scholarship; the historian Ibn Khaldun was born in Tunis in 1332 and served the Hafsid court. The dynasty patronized mosques, madrasas, and the manufacture of ceramics and textiles traded across the Mediterranean.
