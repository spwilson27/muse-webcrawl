Subarachnoid hemorrhage

CT scan showing subarachnoid blood (white) around the midbrain.

Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is bleeding into the subarachnoid space — the area between the arachnoid membrane and the pia mater surrounding the brain. Symptoms may include a severe headache of rapid onset (a "thunderclap headache"), vomiting, decreased level of consciousness, fever, and sometimes seizures. Neck stiffness or neck pain are also relatively common.

Causes

In general, head trauma is the most common cause. Spontaneous SAH is most often caused by rupture of a cerebral aneurysm, which accounts for about 85 percent of non-traumatic cases; other causes include arteriovenous malformations, perimesencephalic hemorrhage (which carries a better prognosis), vasculitis, tumours and cocaine use. Risk factors for aneurysmal rupture include hypertension, smoking and heavy alcohol use.

Diagnosis and treatment

The diagnosis is typically confirmed by a CT scan of the head, or occasionally by lumbar puncture showing blood or xanthochromia in the cerebrospinal fluid when CT is negative. CT angiography can locate the bleeding source. Treatment aims to secure the aneurysm as soon as possible by endovascular coiling or neurosurgical clipping, prevent rebleeding and manage complications such as vasospasm (treated with nimodipine and induced hypertension), hydrocephalus and seizures.

Prognosis

SAH is a medical emergency with high mortality: a substantial fraction of patients die before reaching hospital, and many survivors have lasting cognitive impairment. Outcomes have improved with modern neurocritical care and early aneurysm repair, but the condition remains one of the most lethal forms of stroke.
