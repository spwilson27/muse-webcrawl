Angiosarcoma

TypeSoft-tissue sarcoma of endothelial cells
SitesSkin, breast, liver, spleen, heart, deep soft tissue
Risk factorsChronic lymphedema; radiation; vinyl chloride, arsenic (liver)
TreatmentSurgery, chemotherapy, radiation

Angiosarcoma is a rare and aggressive cancer of the endothelial cells that line the inner surface of blood vessels and lymphatic vessels. It can arise in any part of the body but most commonly occurs in the skin (especially of the head and neck in older adults), the breast, the liver, the spleen and deep soft tissues.

Causes and associations

Most cases arise without a clear cause, but recognised associations include chronic lymphedema (Stewart–Treves syndrome), prior radiation therapy, and exposure to vinyl chloride, arsenic or thorium dioxide in the case of hepatic angiosarcoma. Some cases are linked to foreign-body material or pre-existing vascular lesions.

Signs and diagnosis

Cutaneous angiosarcoma often appears as a bruise-like patch or enlarging nodule that may ulcerate and bleed. Diagnosis rests on biopsy, with immunohistochemical staining for vascular markers such as CD31 and ERG. Prognosis is generally poor because the tumour spreads readily through vessels and often metastasizes early, commonly to the lungs.

Treatment

Management usually combines wide surgical excision with radiotherapy and chemotherapy (such as paclitaxel or anthracycline-based regimens); targeted agents and immunotherapy are under investigation.
