Typology (archaeology)

In archaeology, typology is the classification of artefacts according to their physical characteristics — form, size, material, decoration, and method of manufacture — grouping them into types. Typology is one of the foundations of archaeological analysis, used to organise finds, compare assemblages between sites, and build relative chronologies.

Seriation and chronology

The method was formalised in the 19th century, notably by Oscar Montelius, who arranged European Bronze Age objects into evolutionary sequences. Because artefact styles change over time, ordering types into series (seriation) allows archaeologists to date contexts relatively: types appear, rise in popularity, and disappear, producing characteristic "battleship curves" of frequency. Typological sequences must be checked against stratigraphy and absolute dating methods such as radiocarbon dating.

Criticism

Archaeologists debate whether types are real categories recognised by past makers or analytical constructs imposed by researchers, and typological dating can be misleading where styles persist, revive, or vary regionally.
