Thermosetting polymer

A thermosetting polymer, often called a thermoset, is a polymer that is irreversibly hardened by curing from a soft solid or viscous liquid prepolymer or resin. Curing is induced by heat or suitable radiation and may be promoted by high pressure or mixing with a catalyst. Heat is not necessarily applied externally, but is often generated by the reaction of the resin with a curing agent. Curing results in chemical reactions that create extensive cross-linking between polymer chains to produce an infusible and insoluble polymer network.

The starting material for making thermosets is usually malleable or liquid prior to curing, and is often designed to be moulded into its final shape, a process known as reaction injection moulding, or used as an adhesive or matrix for composite materials. Once hardened, a thermoset cannot be melted or reshaped, in contrast to thermoplastics, which can be repeatedly softened by heating.

Examples and applications

Common thermosets include epoxy resins, phenol-formaldehyde resins (such as Bakelite, the first fully synthetic plastic, patented by Leo Baekeland in 1907), urea-formaldehyde and melamine resins, unsaturated polyesters used in fibreglass composites, polyurethanes, silicone resins, and vulcanised rubber. Applications range from electrical insulators, circuit boards, adhesives and coatings to boat hulls, automotive body panels, aircraft composites, and kitchen worktops. Their cross-linked structure gives them high strength, rigidity, chemical resistance and dimensional stability at elevated temperatures.

Recycling

Because thermosets cannot be remelted, recycling is difficult. Ground thermoset waste can be used as filler, and chemical recycling methods that break cross-links are an active area of research; vitrimers, a newer class of polymers with exchangeable cross-links, aim to combine thermoset performance with reprocessability.
