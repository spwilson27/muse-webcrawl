Alkyd

An alkyd is a polyester resin modified by the addition of fatty acids and other components. Alkyds are derived from polyols and organic acids including dicarboxylic acids or carboxylic acid anhydride and triglyceride oils. The term alkyd is a modification of the original name alcid, reflecting the fact that they are derived from alcohol and organic acids. The inclusion of a fatty acid confers a tendency to form flexible coatings.

Alkyds are used in paints, varnishes and in moulds for casting. They have been the dominant resin for commercial oil-based paints. The original alkyds were compounds of glycerol and phthalic acid sold under the name Glyptal. These were sold as substitutes for the darker coloured copal resins, thus creating alkyd varnishes which were much paler in colour. From these, the alkyds that are known today were developed.

Manufacture

Alkyds can be prepared by two general processes. In the fatty acid process, the composition of the resin is controlled by the fatty acids used as raw material. In the alcoholysis (or monoglyceride) process, glycerol and a vegetable oil are reacted to form a monoglyceride intermediate, which is then reacted with a dibasic acid or anhydride such as phthalic anhydride. The degree of unsaturation in the fatty acid component determines the drying behaviour of the finished resin.

Types

Alkyd resins are commonly classified by their oil length, which is the percentage of fatty acid (or oil) in the finished resin. Short-oil alkyds contain less than about 40% oil and are typically used in industrial baking finishes. Medium-oil alkyds, with roughly 40–60% oil, are widely used in decorative and architectural paints. Long-oil alkyds, with more than 60% oil, dry in air at ambient temperature and are used in brush-applied gloss paints.

Applications

Because they cure by oxidative cross-linking of their unsaturated fatty acid chains, alkyds form hard, glossy, durable films. They remain in widespread use in solvent-borne decorative paints, protective coatings for metal, marine paints, printing inks, and foundry core binders, although water-borne acrylic emulsions have displaced them in many household paint markets.
