Complement system

Complement system
SystemImmune system (innate)
ComponentsOver 30 plasma and membrane proteins
PathwaysClassical, lectin, alternative
Main effectsOpsonisation, inflammation, cell lysis

The complement system, also known as the complement cascade, is a part of the humoral innate immune system. It consists of a number of small, inactive proteins (zymogens) made by the liver and circulating in the blood. When stimulated by one of several triggers, proteases in the system cleave specific proteins to release cytokines and initiate an amplifying cascade of further cleavages. The end result is cell killing by the membrane attack complex, phagocytosis of foreign cells carried out by specialised cells attracted to the site, and promotion of inflammation.

Complement was discovered in the 1890s by Jules Bordet, who found a heat-labile component of serum that "complemented" the action of antibodies in lysing bacteria.

Pathways

Classical pathway

The classical pathway is triggered when the C1 complex binds to antibody (IgM or IgG) already attached to an antigen on a pathogen surface, or directly to some pathogen surfaces and C-reactive protein. C1 cleaves C4 and C2, forming the C3 convertase C4b2a.

Lectin pathway

The lectin pathway begins when mannose-binding lectin (MBL) or ficolins bind carbohydrate patterns on microbial surfaces, activating MBL-associated serine proteases (MASPs) that likewise generate C4b2a.

Alternative pathway

The alternative pathway is continuously active at a low level through spontaneous hydrolysis ("tickover") of C3. On foreign surfaces lacking regulatory proteins, deposited C3b binds factor B, which is cleaved by factor D to form the alternative C3 convertase C3bBb, stabilised by properdin. This pathway also serves as an amplification loop for the other two.

Terminal pathway and functions

All three pathways converge on C3 convertases, which cleave C3 into C3a (an anaphylatoxin) and C3b (an opsonin). Addition of C3b creates C5 convertases, and C5 cleavage initiates assembly of the membrane attack complex (C5b–C9), which forms pores in target membranes and lyses the cell. Complement fragments also recruit phagocytes and mast cells, increasing vascular permeability and inflammation.

Regulation and clinical significance

Host cells are protected by regulators such as C1 inhibitor, factor H, CD55 (decay-accelerating factor) and CD59. Deficiencies can cause recurrent infections, hereditary angioedema, atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome, or the acquired disorder paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria (PNH), which is treated with the anti-C5 antibody eculizumab.
