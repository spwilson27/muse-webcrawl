Equalization (audio)

Equalization, or simply EQ, in recording and reproduction is the process of adjusting the volume of different frequency bands within an audio signal. The circuit or equipment used to achieve this is called an equalizer. Originally developed for telephone systems and film sound, equalization is now fundamental to music production, broadcasting and live sound.

Types of equalizer

Shelving equalizers boost or cut all frequencies above or below a cutoff. Peaking filters affect a band centred on a chosen frequency, with adjustable bandwidth (Q). Graphic equalizers provide fixed bands with slider controls — commonly 10 bands in consumer equipment and 31 (one-third octave) bands in professional use. Parametric equalizers allow continuous control of frequency, gain and Q for each band, and are standard in mixing consoles and digital audio workstations.

Uses

EQ is used correctively (to remove hum, reduce resonances, compensate for microphone or room characteristics) and creatively (to shape the timbre of instruments and balance a mix). Fixed equalization curves are also integral to recording standards, such as the RIAA equalization curve used for vinyl records, and de-emphasis in FM broadcasting.

History

The earliest equalizers were passive networks used by telephone engineers to compensate for line losses. John Volkman's work at RCA in the 1920s–30s brought equalization to film sound, and later engineers such as Peter Baxandall designed influential active tone-control circuits.
