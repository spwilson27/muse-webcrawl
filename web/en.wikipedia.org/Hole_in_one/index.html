Hole in one


A tee shot finding the cup on a par-3 hole

In golf, a hole in one (also called an ace) occurs when a golfer's ball, struck from the tee, finishes in the cup in a single stroke. Holes in one are most common on par-3 holes, where the green is reachable from the tee; they are rare on par-4 holes and exceptionally rare — sometimes called an albatross ace — on par-5 holes, a feat counted only a handful of times in recorded play, occasionally engineered on straight or dogleg holes cut across corners.

Odds and records

Actuarial estimates commonly cited by insurers put the odds for an average amateur at about 12,500 to 1 on a given par-3, and for a touring professional at roughly 2,500 to 1. Famous aces include Gene Sarazen's albatross at the 1935 Masters (a 4-wood second shot on a par 5 — strictly a double eagle rather than a tee-shot ace) and the youngest recorded holes in one by child prodigies aged four and five. Art Wall Jr. recorded 45 holes in one in his lifetime, among the highest verified totals. In professional tournaments, multiple aces in a single round and aces on par-4 holes (such as Andrew Magee's at the 2001 Phoenix Open, where the ball ricocheted off another player's putter) are celebrated rarities.

Customs

Tradition obliges the golfer who makes an ace to buy drinks for companions and often everyone in the clubhouse — a custom so costly that golfers and clubs commonly carry hole-in-one insurance, widespread in Japan where a lavish celebration is customary and insurance has been sold since the 1980s. Tournament sponsors frequently offer prizes, such as cars, for aces at designated holes. For an ace to count officially in many handicap and record systems, it must occur during a stipulated round of at least nine holes (or in a full round) and be witnessed.
