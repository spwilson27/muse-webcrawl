Crocus sativus

Crocus sativus, commonly known as saffron crocus, is a species of flowering plant in the iris family Iridaceae. It is cultivated for its crimson stigma and styles, called threads, which are collected and dried for use as the spice saffron — the world's most expensive spice by weight.

The plant is a sterile triploid that does not produce viable seed and must be propagated vegetatively by dividing its corms. Each purple flower bears three vivid crimson stigmas, which must be picked by hand; roughly 150,000 flowers are needed to produce one kilogram of dried saffron. The flavour and hay-like fragrance result from the chemicals picrocrocin and safranal, and the golden-yellow dye from crocin.

Cultivation

Saffron crocus flowers in autumn and grows in climates ranging from Mediterranean maquis to the dry continental plateaus of Iran, which produces the great majority of the world's crop; Kashmir, Greece, Spain, and Morocco are other traditional producers. The plant's origin is debated, with a wild ancestor likely in Crocus cartwrightianus of Greece.
