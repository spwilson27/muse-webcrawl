Prograde and retrograde motion

Prograde orbits follow the primary's rotation; retrograde orbits oppose it.

Prograde motion is motion in the same direction as the rotation of the central body, while retrograde motion is motion in the opposite direction. In the Solar System, most planets orbit the Sun prograde (counterclockwise as seen from above the Sun's north pole) and rotate prograde on their axes. Venus and Uranus are notable exceptions with retrograde rotation.

Apparent retrograde motion

The term also describes the apparent backward loop a superior planet such as Mars makes against the stars when Earth overtakes it in its orbit. This apparent retrograde motion was a major puzzle for geocentric astronomy and was explained naturally by the heliocentric model: the planet is not actually reversing, the effect is one of perspective as Earth passes it.

Retrograde satellites

Several moons orbit their planets retrograde, including Jupiter's outer irregular satellites and Neptune's moon Triton. Retrograde orbits are evidence that such bodies were captured rather than formed in place, since material in a protoplanetary disk normally orbits prograde.
