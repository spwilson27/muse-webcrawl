The Taiwanese indigenous peoples, also known as Taiwanese aborigines or Formosan peoples, are the Austronesian peoples native to the island of Taiwan. Their ancestors have lived on the island for at least 5,500 years, and linguistic evidence identifies Taiwan as the likely homeland from which the Austronesian peoples dispersed across the Pacific and Indian Oceans. Today they number roughly 580,000, about 2.5 percent of Taiwan's population.

Recognised peoples

The government of Taiwan officially recognises sixteen peoples, including the Amis (the largest group), Atayal, Paiwan, Bunun, Puyuma, Rukai, Tsou, Saisiyat, Yami (Tao) of Orchid Island, Thao, Kavalan, Truku, Sakizaya, Seediq, Hla'alua and Kanakanavu. Plains peoples such as the Ketagalan and Siraya, long partly assimilated, have sought recognition through the courts and legislature; in 2022 the Constitutional Court ruled in favour of recognising the Siraya.

History

Indigenous societies traded and fought with Dutch, Spanish, Qing and Japanese colonial regimes. Under Japanese rule (1895–1945) they were classified and administered as "Takasago" peoples; the 1930 Wushe (Musha) Incident, a Seediq uprising led by Mona Rudao, was the last major armed revolt. After 1945, Kuomintang policies promoted Mandarin-language assimilation. A democratic-era indigenous rights movement brought constitutional recognition, an official apology by President Tsai Ing-wen in 2016, and the Indigenous Languages Development Act of 2017.

Culture

Formosan languages belong to several primary branches of Austronesian—evidence that the family's deepest diversity is in Taiwan. Harvest festivals, millet and rice rituals, tattooing traditions (notably among the Atayal and Paiwan), woodcarving, and the Tao people's plank boats remain living expressions of these cultures.
