Clarified butter

Clarified butter
TypeCooking fat
Main ingredientButter
Smoke point~252 °C (486 °F)
Related formsGhee, beurre noisette, smen

Clarified butter is butter from which the water and milk solids have been removed, leaving almost pure butterfat. Because the milk solids — which burn at relatively low temperatures — are taken out, clarified butter has a much higher smoke point than ordinary butter, around 250 °C, making it well suited to frying and sautéing at high heat. It also keeps far longer than butter because the removed solids are what spoil first.

Production

Clarified butter is made by gently melting butter and allowing it to separate into three layers: foam of casein on top, clear butterfat in the middle, and water with dissolved solids at the bottom. The foam is skimmed and the fat is poured off, leaving the aqueous layer behind. Industrial production uses centrifuges or evaporation instead, and the milk solids may be browned first to give the fat a nutty flavour, as in beurre noisette.

Ghee

Ghee, the clarified butter of Indian cuisine, is simmered longer until the solids lightly brown and the water fully evaporates, giving a characteristic aroma. Ghee is central to cooking across South Asia and is also used in Ayurvedic practice and Hindu ritual. Related products include Moroccan smen, a fermented, aged clarified butter, and Ethiopian niter kibbeh, which is spiced.

Uses

In French cuisine, clarified butter is used for emulsified sauces such as hollandaise and béarnaise, and as the traditional dipping fat for lobster and other seafood. It is also used to seal potted meats and to fry foods where butter flavour is wanted without burning.
