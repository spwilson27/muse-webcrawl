Free-electron laser

A free-electron laser (FEL) is a type of laser whose lasing medium consists of very-high-speed electrons moving freely through a magnetic structure, hence the term free electron. Unlike conventional lasers, whose output wavelength is fixed by the atomic or molecular transitions of their gain medium, a free-electron laser is widely tunable: by adjusting the electron beam energy or the magnetic field, the same device can in principle produce radiation at any wavelength, from microwaves through the infrared and visible to ultraviolet, X-rays, and beyond.

Principle of operation

In an FEL, a relativistic electron beam is accelerated and passed through an undulator (or "wiggler"), a periodic array of magnets that forces the electrons onto a sinusoidal path. The transverse acceleration causes the electrons to emit electromagnetic radiation. As the radiation and electrons travel together, the radiation field modulates the electron energies; electrons bunch at the scale of the radiation wavelength and then emit coherently, amplifying the light exponentially. When this self-amplification arises from the noise of spontaneous emission, the process is called SASE (self-amplified spontaneous emission), the operating mode of most modern X-ray FELs.

The resonant wavelength depends on the undulator period, its magnetic field strength, and the relativistic Lorentz factor of the beam, so the output can be tuned continuously — a decisive advantage over fixed-line conventional lasers.

History

The underlying concept was developed by John Madey at Stanford University, who demonstrated the first free-electron laser operation in the infrared in 1976, following earlier work on the ubitron maser by Robert Phillips. Compact infrared FEL user facilities followed in the 1980s and 1990s. A major milestone came with the FLASH facility at DESY in Hamburg, which achieved lasing in the soft X-ray range, and with the Linac Coherent Light Source (LCLS) at SLAC in 2009, the first hard X-ray FEL, producing pulses billions of times brighter than synchrotron sources with femtosecond durations.

Applications

X-ray free-electron lasers enable "diffraction before destruction" imaging of single molecules and nanocrystals, ultrafast studies of chemical reactions, and investigations of matter under extreme conditions. Infrared FELs are used in spectroscopy, materials processing, and medical research. Major facilities include LCLS and LCLS-II in the United States, European XFEL in Germany, SACLA in Japan, SwissFEL in Switzerland, and PAL-XFEL in South Korea.
