Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu
Country of origin: Japan
Founder: Takeda Sokaku (1859–1943)
Focus: Joint locks, throws, pins
Parenthood: Said to descend from the Takeda clan's aiki traditions

Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu (大東流 合気柔術) is a Japanese martial art first widely taught in the early 20th century by Takeda Sokaku. It is a close-quarter grappling system emphasising joint locks, throws, pins and strikes, and is best known as the principal technical ancestor of aikido.

History

According to school tradition, the art's techniques were preserved within the Takeda family of the Aizu domain for centuries. Takeda Sokaku, after studying several martial arts, began teaching the system publicly, travelling throughout Japan and training thousands of students. His most famous pupil was Morihei Ueshiba, who went on to found aikido. Other students founded arts such as Yanagi-ryū and influenced the development of hapkido through Choi Yong-sool, who had lived in Takeda's household.

Techniques and curriculum

The classical curriculum is organised into scrolls (mokuroku) of techniques, beginning with the 118 techniques of the Hiden Mokuroku, progressing through aiki-no-jutsu and higher levels. Central to the art is the concept of aiki — unbalancing and controlling an opponent through subtle body mechanics rather than muscular force.

Modern branches

After Takeda's death in 1943, the art continued through several lines, including the mainline tradition of his son Takeda Tokimune in Hokkaido and the Takumakai organisation founded by students in Osaka.
