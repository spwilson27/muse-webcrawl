Tate is a British institution that houses the United Kingdom's national collection of British art and international modern and contemporary art. It is a network of four art museums: Tate Britain and Tate Modern in London, Tate Liverpool, and Tate St Ives in Cornwall.

History

The Tate was founded in 1897 as the National Gallery of British Art, funded by sugar magnate Sir Henry Tate, who also donated his collection of British art. The gallery on Millbank in London — now Tate Britain — was built on the site of the former Millbank Prison. Tate's remit expanded during the 20th century to include international modern art, and a separate gallery for that collection, Tate Modern, opened in 2000 in the converted Bankside Power Station. Tate Liverpool opened in 1988 in a converted dock warehouse at the Albert Dock, and Tate St Ives in 1993.

Collections and programmes

Tate holds the world's largest collection of British art from 1500 to the present, including the major holdings of J. M. W. Turner bequeathed to the nation, as well as canonical works of modern and contemporary art. It administers the Turner Prize, awarded annually to a British artist since 1984, and presents large-scale commissions in Tate Modern's Turbine Hall. Tate is an executive non-departmental public body sponsored by the Department for Culture, Media and Sport, and its permanent collections are free to visit.
