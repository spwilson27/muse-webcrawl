Vascular tissue

Vascular tissue
Found inVascular plants (tracheophytes)
ComponentsXylem, phloem, vascular cambium
FunctionLong-distance transport of water, minerals, and food

Vascular tissue is a complex conducting tissue found in vascular plants, formed of more than one cell type. Its primary components are the xylem and the phloem. Together these tissues transport water, minerals, and the products of photosynthesis throughout the plant, allowing vascular plants to grow far taller than non-vascular plants such as mosses.

Xylem

The xylem conducts water and dissolved minerals upward from the roots to the stems and leaves, driven mainly by transpiration pull and root pressure. Its water-conducting cells — tracheids and vessel elements — are dead and hollow at maturity, with lignified walls that also provide structural support.

Phloem

The phloem translocates sugars (chiefly sucrose) and other organic compounds from sources (usually leaves) to sinks (roots, fruits, growing tissues). It consists of living sieve elements accompanied by companion cells that load and unload sugars.

Arrangement and growth

Xylem and phloem are organised in vascular bundles; in stems of eudicots they form a ring, while in monocots they are scattered. In woody plants, the vascular cambium — a lateral meristem between xylem and phloem — produces secondary xylem (wood) inward and secondary phloem outward, thickening the stem each growing season and producing annual rings.
