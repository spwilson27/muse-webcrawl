Blood donation

A blood donation occurs when a person voluntarily has blood drawn to be used for transfusions or made into biopharmaceutical medications by fractionation. Donated blood is used for accident and surgical patients, people with anaemia and blood disorders such as thalassaemia and haemophilia, and patients undergoing cancer treatment. Donation may be of whole blood, or of specific components directly (apheresis).

Donor selection and process

Prospective donors are screened with a questionnaire and a brief health check covering haemoglobin level, pulse, blood pressure, and temperature. A whole-blood donation is about 450–500 mL, taken over roughly 10 minutes. Most healthy adults can donate every 8–16 weeks depending on local rules; the body replaces the fluid volume within about a day, while red cells take several weeks to replenish.

Types

Whole blood is usually separated into red cells, plasma, and platelets. Plasmapheresis and plateletpheresis collect a single component and return the rest to the donor, allowing more frequent donation. Autologous donation (for one's own later use) and directed donation (for a named patient) also occur.

Safety and testing

Every unit is tested for blood group and for transfusion-transmitted infections including HIV, hepatitis B and C, and syphilis. Donors in many countries are unpaid volunteers, a policy the WHO encourages; World Blood Donor Day is marked on 14 June, the birthday of Karl Landsteiner, who discovered the ABO blood groups.
