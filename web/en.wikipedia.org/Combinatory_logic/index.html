Combinatory logic

Combinatory logic is a notation to eliminate the need for quantified variables in mathematical logic. It was introduced by Moses Schönfinkel (1920) and Haskell Curry, and has more recently been used in computer science as a theoretical model of computation and as a basis for functional programming languages.

Combinators

Combinatory terms are built from primitive combinators by application. The best-known basis is S and K: K x y = x, and S x y z = x z (y z); the identity I can be defined as SKK. With S and K alone any computable function can be expressed, and bracket abstraction converts lambda terms into combinators, establishing equivalence with the lambda calculus.

Applications

Combinatory logic underlies the theory of functional programming, combinator graph reduction machines, and languages such as Miranda and Haskell (named for Curry). Typed combinatory logic connects to intuitionistic proof theory via the Curry–Howard correspondence.

History

Schönfinkel's 1924 paper, Curry's 1930s formalisation, and later work by Church, Rosser, and others shaped the study of variable-free logic and computability alongside Turing machines.
