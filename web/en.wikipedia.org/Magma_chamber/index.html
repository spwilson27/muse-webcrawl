Magma chamber

A magma chamber is a body of molten and partially molten rock (magma) stored beneath the Earth's surface within the crust. Magma accumulates in such reservoirs after rising from its source region in the mantle or lower crust; from the chamber it may erupt at a volcano or cool in place to form a pluton.

Formation and dynamics

Magma generated by partial melting is less dense than surrounding rock and ascends until it reaches a level of neutral buoyancy or is trapped beneath denser strata, where it pools. Modern geophysics suggests that many "chambers" are in fact vertically extensive, mostly crystalline "mush" zones containing a modest fraction of melt, which can be remobilised by fresh injections of hot magma. Within a chamber, magma evolves by fractional crystallisation, assimilation of wall rock, and mixing between batches — processes that diversify igneous rock compositions.

Eruption triggers

Eruptions may be triggered by injection of new magma, growth of gas bubbles as pressure falls or crystals form (increasing internal pressure), or collapse of the chamber roof. Very large chambers beneath caldera systems such as Yellowstone can feed super-eruptions; the chamber's roof collapse during eruption creates the caldera itself.

Detection

Magma chambers are imaged by seismic tomography (magma slows seismic waves), ground deformation measured by GPS and InSAR (inflation signals magma accumulation), and gas emissions. When magma chambers solidify underground they become batholiths and other intrusive bodies, often hosting economically important ore deposits.
