Foreign and Commonwealth Office

Formed: 1968 (as FCO); origins 1782

Headquarters: King Charles Street, Whitehall, London

Superseded by: Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (2020)

The Foreign and Commonwealth Office (FCO), commonly called the Foreign Office, was a department of the Government of the United Kingdom responsible for protecting and promoting British interests worldwide and for the country's foreign policy, diplomatic relations, and consular services. It was created in 1968 by the merger of the Foreign Office (established 1782) and the Commonwealth Office.

History

The Foreign Office was formed in March 1782 by combining the Southern and Northern Departments of the Secretary of State's office, and it has been headed by the Secretary of State for Foreign Affairs — the Foreign Secretary — ever since. In 1968 it merged with the Commonwealth Office to form the Foreign and Commonwealth Office. In September 2020, the FCO merged with the Department for International Development to become the Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO).

Headquarters

The department's main building, on King Charles Street off Whitehall in London, was designed by George Gilbert Scott and built between 1861 and 1868 in an Italianate style. Originally housing four separate departments, the building is famous for its grand interiors, including the Durbar Court and the Locarno Suite, named after the 1925 Locarno Treaties initialled there.

Functions

The Foreign Office ran the United Kingdom's network of embassies, high commissions, and consulates, provided travel advice and consular assistance to British nationals abroad, and coordinated policy on international security, trade, climate, and human rights. Its executive agencies included Wilton Park and FCO Services.
