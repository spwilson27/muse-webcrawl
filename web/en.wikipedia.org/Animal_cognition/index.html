Animal cognition

Animal cognition encompasses the mental capacities of non-human animals and the study of those capacities, including perception, learning, memory, decision-making, problem-solving, communication and, controversially, forms of awareness and reasoning. The field — closely linked to comparative psychology, ethology and cognitive ethology (a term promoted by Donald Griffin) — asks how animals represent and process information about their world, and whether capacities once thought uniquely human, such as tool manufacture, episodic-like memory, numeracy, metacognition and theory of mind, exist in other species. Findings have repeatedly documented sophisticated abilities in great apes, dolphins, elephants, corvids, parrots, dogs, rodents and even invertebrates such as octopuses and bees.

History

Darwin argued in The Descent of Man (1871) that mental differences between humans and other animals are of degree, not kind. Romanes's anecdotal comparative psychology gave way to behaviourism's caution after Lloyd Morgan's canon warned against over-attributing complex minds, and the 20th century saw landmark exceptions — Köhler's insight studies with chimpanzees at Tenerife (1913–17) being the most famous. The cognitive turn of the 1970s–80s, aided by Griffin's The Question of Animal Awareness (1976), made animal cognition a mainstream experimental science.

Domains of study

Learning and memory: beyond classical and operant conditioning, scrub-jays show episodic-like memory for what food was cached, where and when, and rats and pigeons display robust spatial maps. Tool use and problem-solving: chimpanzees fish for termites and crack nuts with stone hammers; New Caledonian crows manufacture hooked tools; Betty the crow famously bent wire into a hook. Number and quantity: many species discriminate numerosities, and Alex the grey parrot labelled quantities up to six. Communication and language: vervet monkeys give functionally referential alarm calls; apes such as Washoe and Kanzi learned elements of sign language and lexigram systems, though the extent of true syntax remains debated. Social cognition: dogs excel at reading human gestures; ravens and apes track others' knowledge states, with ongoing debate over full theory of mind. Metacognition and self-recognition: some dolphins, monkeys and rats choose "uncertain" responses adaptively; great apes, dolphins, elephants and magpies have passed versions of the mirror test.

Methods and criticisms

Researchers use controlled experiments, field observations and touchscreen tasks, guarding against Clever Hans effects and anthropomorphism on one side and "anthropodenial" (de Waal's term) on the other. The field increasingly studies animals in ecologically relevant settings and examines invertebrates, whose learning abilities — in bees navigating, counting and learning abstract concepts such as "same/different" — challenge assumptions that cognition requires a large brain.

Implications

Evidence for rich animal mental lives has influenced animal welfare law and ethics, contributing to protections for great apes and cephalopods in several jurisdictions, and informs debates about animal consciousness, notably the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness (2012).
