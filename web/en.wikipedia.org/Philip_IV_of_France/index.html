Philip IV of France

Philip IV (April–June 1268 – 29 November 1314), called Philip the Fair (Philippe le Bel) and the Iron King (le Roi de fer), was King of France from 1285 to 1314. By virtue of his marriage to Joan I of Navarre, he was also King of Navarre as Philip I. His reign is marked by the strengthening of royal power, conflict with the papacy, war with England and Flanders, and the suppression of the Knights Templar.

Reign

Philip, guided by able legist ministers such as Guillaume de Nogaret and Enguerran de Marigny, extended royal administration, reformed finances, debased the coinage, and summoned the first Estates General (1302) to rally support. His marriage brought Champagne and Navarre into the royal orbit.

Conflict with the papacy

Philip's taxation of the clergy provoked Pope Boniface VIII's bull Clericis laicos (1296) and a long struggle culminating in the bull Unam sanctam (1302). In 1303 Nogaret's agents seized the pope at Anagni; Boniface died shortly after — the "outrage of Anagni". Philip pressed his successor Clement V, a Frenchman who settled at Avignon (beginning the Avignon Papacy), to condemn Boniface posthumously and to destroy the Templars.

Wars and finance

War with Edward I of England over Gascony (1294–1303) ended in a settlement sealed by marriage alliances. In Flanders, French chivalry suffered a humiliating defeat by Flemish townsmen at the Battle of the Golden Spurs (1302), though Philip later restored his position. Fiscal pressure led him to expel the Jews from France (1306) and seize their assets, arrest Lombard bankers, and debase the currency, provoking unrest in Paris.

Suppression of the Templars

On Friday 13 October 1307, Philip had the Knights Templar in France arrested en masse on charges of heresy, blasphemy and idolatry — motivated, historians generally agree, by his debts to the order and desire for its wealth. Under torture many confessed; Pope Clement V dissolved the order in 1312 (Vox in excelso). Grand Master Jacques de Molay was burned at Paris in 1314, reportedly cursing king and pope to meet him before God's tribunal within the year — both died within months, feeding the legend of the curse.

Death and legacy

Philip died of a stroke after a hunt at Fontainebleau in November 1314. His three sons — Louis X, Philip V and Charles IV — each became king but left no surviving sons, ending the direct Capetian line in 1328 and opening the succession crisis behind the Hundred Years' War.
