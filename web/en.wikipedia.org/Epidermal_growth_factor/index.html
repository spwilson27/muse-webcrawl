Epidermal growth factor

GeneEGF (chromosome 4q25)
Protein size53 amino acids (mature form)
ReceptorEGFR (ErbB1/HER1)
DiscoveredStanley Cohen, 1962

Epidermal growth factor (EGF) is a protein that stimulates cell growth and differentiation by binding to its receptor, EGFR. Human EGF is a 6-kDa protein with 53 amino acid residues and three intramolecular disulfide bonds. Originally isolated from mouse submaxillary glands by Stanley Cohen, EGF is found in many human tissues including submandibular glands, parotid glands, urine, saliva, milk and plasma.

Discovery

Cohen identified EGF in the 1960s while working with Rita Levi-Montalcini on nerve growth factor; injections of gland extracts caused premature eyelid opening and tooth eruption in newborn mice through stimulation of epithelial growth. Cohen and Levi-Montalcini shared the 1986 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discoveries of growth factors.

Function and mechanism

EGF is synthesised as a large membrane-bound precursor that is cleaved to release the mature peptide. Binding to the extracellular domain of the epidermal growth factor receptor induces receptor dimerisation and autophosphorylation of intracellular tyrosines, activating signalling cascades including the Ras–MAPK (ERK), PI3K–Akt and PLCγ pathways. The result is stimulation of proliferation, survival and migration of epithelial and other cells; EGF is a potent mitogen for cultured cells and promotes wound healing in vivo.

Clinical significance

Dysregulation of EGF–EGFR signalling is a hallmark of many cancers: EGFR is overexpressed or mutated in lung, colorectal, head and neck, and other tumours. Drugs targeting the pathway — monoclonal antibodies such as cetuximab and small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as erlotinib and gefitinib — are standard cancer therapies. Recombinant EGF has been used topically for diabetic foot ulcers and burns in some countries.
