Oracle bone

A Shang-dynasty oracle bone bearing some of the earliest Chinese writing.

Oracle bones (Chinese: 甲骨; pinyin: jiǎgǔ) are pieces of ox scapula and turtle plastron used for pyromantic divination in ancient China, principally during the late Shang dynasty (c. 1250–1050 BCE). They bear the earliest known significant corpus of Chinese writing, called oracle bone script.

Divination practice

At the royal court at Anyang, diviners prepared the bones by boring hollows into them, then applied a heated bronze rod so that cracks formed. The king or diviner posed a question — about warfare, harvests, weather, childbirth, or the royal ancestors — and read the cracks as answers. The question, the prognostication, and sometimes the verified outcome were carved into the bone, creating a written archive of royal divination.

Discovery and significance

The bones were rediscovered in 1899 near Anyang in Henan province, having previously been ground up as "dragon bones" for traditional medicine. Their decipherment confirmed the historicity of the Shang dynasty, long doubted, and provided a king list matching that of the historian Sima Qian. Roughly 150,000 fragments have been excavated, of which the oldest Chinese characters — ancestors of the modern script — can be recognised.

Oracle bone script records a mature writing system with several thousand distinct graphs, evidence that Chinese writing had already undergone a long development before the Shang.
