Cloisonnism

Cloisonnism is a style of post-Impressionist painting characterised by bold, flat forms separated by dark contours. The term was coined by the critic Édouard Dujardin in 1888, by analogy with cloisonné enamelwork, in which metal wires (cloisons, "partitions") separate compartments of coloured enamel. The style was developed in Brittany in the late 1880s by Émile Bernard and Louis Anquetin, and famously adopted and transformed by Paul Gauguin, becoming the visual basis of the Pont-Aven School and of Synthetism.


The Cloisonnist technique: flat colour enclosed by dark outlines, recalling cloisonné enamel and stained glass.

Origins

Dujardin coined the term reviewing the Salon des Indépendants of 1888, singling out Anquetin's work. That summer at Pont-Aven in Brittany, Bernard painted Breton Girls Dancing and showed Gauguin the new manner; Gauguin's Vision of the Sermon (The Yellow Christ's precursor, the Jacob Wrestling with the Angel) of 1888 applied it to a religious subject with arbitrary colour and flattened space. Influences included Japanese woodblock prints (Japonisme), medieval stained glass and enamel, Breton folk art and the deliberate archaism of the Pont-Aven circle. By rejecting Impressionist broken brushwork and naturalistic modelling, Cloisonnism asserted the painting as a decorative, symbolic surface — a position Gauguin and Bernard elaborated as Synthetism.

Artists and influence

Besides Bernard, Anquetin and Gauguin, the manner influenced Paul Sérusier and the Nabis, the early work of Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec, and — through its poster graphics — Art Nouveau. Its flat, outlined shapes also anticipate the aesthetics of comic strips and modern illustration.
