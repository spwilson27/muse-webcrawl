The Cabinet of the United States is the principal advisory body to the president of the United States. It is composed of the vice president and the heads of the fifteen executive departments, each of whom (except the vice president) is appointed by the president and confirmed by the Senate. The members of the Cabinet are commonly referred to by their titles — for example, the secretary of state, the secretary of the treasury, and the secretary of defense — and collectively they stand near the top of the presidential line of succession.

The Cabinet's role is primarily advisory: under the Opinion Clause of Article II of the Constitution, the president "may require the Opinion, in writing, of the principal Officer in each of the executive Departments, upon any Subject relating to the Duties of their respective Offices." The institution developed by custom under George Washington, who initially relied on four officials: the secretary of state, the secretary of the treasury, the secretary of war, and the attorney general.

Executive departments

The fifteen executive departments, in order of creation, are:

  • Department of State (1789)
  • Department of the Treasury (1789)
  • Department of Defense (1949; originally War, 1789)
  • Department of Justice (1870; Attorney General 1789)
  • Department of the Interior (1849)
  • Department of Agriculture (1862)
  • Department of Commerce (1903)
  • Department of Labor (1888; independent in 1913)
  • Department of Health and Human Services (1953)
  • Department of Housing and Urban Development (1965)
  • Department of Transportation (1966)
  • Department of Energy (1977)
  • Department of Education (1979)
  • Department of Veterans Affairs (1988)
  • Department of Homeland Security (2002)

Cabinet-level officials

Presidents have extended cabinet rank to additional officials who do not head executive departments, a practice dating to the mid-20th century. Recent cabinet-level positions have included the White House chief of staff, the administrator of the Environmental Protection Agency, the director of the Office of Management and Budget, the United States trade representative, and the ambassador to the United Nations. These officials may attend Cabinet meetings but are not in the line of succession.

Confirmation and removal

Cabinet secretaries are nominated by the president and confirmed by the Senate by majority vote under the Appointments Clause. They serve at the pleasure of the president, who may dismiss them at will. Under the 25th Amendment, the vice president and a majority of the Cabinet may declare the president unable to discharge the powers and duties of the office.
