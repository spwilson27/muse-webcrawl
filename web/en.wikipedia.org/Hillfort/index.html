Hillfort


Maiden Castle, Dorset

A hillfort is a type of fortified refuge or defended settlement located on a rise of elevation which exploits a rise in elevation for defensive advantage. They are typically European and of the Bronze Age or Iron Age. Some were used in the post-Roman period. Hillforts follow the contours of a hill and consist of one or more lines of earthworks—ramparts, ditches, and entrances—often supplemented by timber or stone walls.

Types and distribution

Archaeologists distinguish univallate hillforts, with a single circuit of defences, from multivallate ones with multiple concentric lines, and classify them by situation: contour forts, promontory forts (defended on one side by cliffs or water), and lowland forts. Britain alone has around 3,300 hillforts; famous examples include Maiden Castle in Dorset—one of the largest in Europe—Old Oswestry, Danebury, and the Uffington White Horse hillfort in Oxfordshire. On the continent, hillfort traditions include the Celtic oppida of Gaul, the castros of Iberia, and Germanic and Slavic ring forts.

Function

Although long interpreted as purely military refuges, many hillforts contained dense permanent settlement, storage pits, shrines, and evidence of craft production and trade. Excavations at Danebury and elsewhere suggest they served as central places for tribal communities—markets, redistribution centres, and symbols of power—with defence only one function among several. Some fell to Roman assault, as described by Julius Caesar for Gaulish oppida; Maiden Castle's war cemetery was once attributed to resistance against Vespasian's legions, though its dating is debated.
