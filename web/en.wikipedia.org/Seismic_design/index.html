Seismic design

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Seismic base isolation allows a building to move independently of ground motion.

Seismic design is the branch of structural engineering concerned with designing buildings and other structures to withstand earthquakes. Its goals are expressed in performance terms: structures should resist minor earthquakes without damage, moderate earthquakes without significant structural damage, and major earthquakes without collapse, thereby protecting human life. Seismic design is governed by building codes such as the International Building Code, ASCE 7, and Eurocode 8.

Principles

Earthquakes load structures with inertial forces proportional to their mass and to ground acceleration. Key design principles include providing a continuous load path, redundancy, ductility — the capacity of reinforced concrete and steel members to deform and dissipate energy without brittle failure — and capacity design, in which selected members are designated to yield (forming "fuses") while others remain elastic. Detailing of reinforcement, especially confinement steel in columns and joints, is critical to ductile behavior.

Techniques

  • Shear walls and braced frames — stiff elements that resist lateral forces.
  • Moment-resisting frames — rigid beam-column connections that flex to absorb energy.
  • Base isolation — bearings of laminated rubber and steel, or friction pendulum systems, decouple the building from ground motion, lengthening its period.
  • Energy dissipation devices — viscous, friction, and yielding dampers supplement the structure's damping.
  • Seismic retrofit — strengthening existing vulnerable buildings, especially unreinforced masonry.

Analysis

Engineers assess seismic demand using response spectra derived from the site's seismic hazard, soil conditions, and the building's natural period. Methods range from the equivalent lateral force procedure to nonlinear time-history analysis using recorded ground motions.
