Plurality voting

A plurality ballot: the voter marks one candidate

Plurality voting is an electoral system in which the candidate who receives the most votes wins, with no requirement to obtain an absolute majority (more than half). When used to elect a single candidate from a single-member district it is commonly called first-past-the-post (FPTP). Each voter casts one vote for one candidate, and the candidate with the highest number of votes is declared elected, even if that total is well under 50 percent.

Use

First-past-the-post is used for national legislatures in the United Kingdom, Canada, India and (for the House of Representatives and most state offices) the United States, as well as in a number of other countries, many of them former British colonies. Plurality rules also decide the United States Electoral College in all but two states, and are common in corporate and organisational elections, where the candidates with the most votes take the available seats (plurality-at-large or block voting).

Effects

Supporters argue that plurality voting is simple to understand and count, tends to produce single-party majority governments able to implement a programme, and maintains a clear link between a district and its representative. Critics note that it can be highly disproportional: a party can win a majority of seats with a minority of votes, large numbers of votes are "wasted" on losing candidates or surplus majorities, and results are sensitive to how district boundaries are drawn (gerrymandering). Maurice Duverger observed that plurality elections in single-member districts tend toward two-party systems ("Duverger's law"), because voters and donors desert third parties that cannot win, and because similar parties split each other's vote — the "spoiler effect" — encouraging strategic voting for the lesser of two evils rather than a first preference.

Alternatives

Majoritarian alternatives such as the two-round system and instant-runoff (ranked-choice) voting ensure the winner commands broader support; proportional representation systems, including party-list PR and the single transferable vote, allocate seats in proportion to votes. Many electoral-reform movements in plurality countries advocate one or another of these replacements.
