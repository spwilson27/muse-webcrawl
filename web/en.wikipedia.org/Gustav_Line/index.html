Gustav Line

The Gustav Line, the principal German defensive line in Italy in 1943–44.

The Gustav Line was a series of German military fortifications in Italy during World War II, built by the Todt Organisation in late 1943. It ran across the Italian peninsula from the mouth of the Garigliano River on the Tyrrhenian coast, through the Apennine Mountains, to the Sangro River on the Adriatic coast.

Strategic position

The line's western anchor was dominated by Monte Cassino, a steep hill crowned by the ancient Benedictine abbey, overlooking the Liri valley and Highway 6, the main road to Rome. Field Marshal Albert Kesselring, the German commander in Italy, intended the position to delay the Allied advance northward after the landings at Salerno and the fall of Naples.

Battles of Monte Cassino and Anzio

Between January and May 1944 the Allies launched four major assaults against the Gustav Line around Cassino, involving American, British, Indian, New Zealand, Polish, French, and other forces. The abbey itself was destroyed by Allied bombing on 15 February 1944, controversially, as the Germans had not initially occupied the building itself. In January 1944 the Allies also landed at Anzio, behind the line, in an attempt to outflank it, but the beachhead was contained for months.

Breakthrough

The line was finally broken in May 1944 during Operation Diadem: French expeditionary forces crossed the Aurunci Mountains, long considered impassable, while Polish II Corps captured Monte Cassino on 18 May. The Germans withdrew northward to the Hitler (Senger) Line and then to the Gothic Line. Rome fell on 4 June 1944.
