Hebrew calendar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Hebrew calendar (Hebrew: הַלּוּחַ הָעִבְרִי, HaLuah HaIvri), also called the Jewish calendar, is a lunisolar calendar used today for Jewish religious observances and as an official calendar of Israel. It determines the dates of Jewish holidays, Torah readings, yahrzeits and birthdays, and appropriate daily psalms.

Structure

The calendar is lunisolar: months are based on lunar cycles, while years are adjusted to the solar year so that festivals fall in their proper seasons. An ordinary year has 12 months and 353–355 days; a leap year adds a thirteenth month, Adar I, before Adar (which becomes Adar II), and has 383–385 days. Leap years occur 7 times in each 19-year Metonic cycle, in years 3, 6, 8, 11, 14, 17 and 19.

Epoch and rules

The epoch of the calendar — year 1 — is traditionally reckoned from the creation of the world (Anno Mundi), corresponding to 3761 BCE in the proleptic Julian calendar. The rules for calculating the calendar were fixed by tradition attributed to Hillel II around 359 CE, replacing the earlier practice of declaring months by observation of the new moon and testimony before the Sanhedrin. Four postponement rules (dehiyyot) adjust the day of the week on which the new year can fall, so that certain holidays do not adjoin the Sabbath.
