A Fermi gas is an ideal gas composed of many non-interacting fermions — particles obeying Fermi–Dirac statistics and the Pauli exclusion principle. It is the quantum-mechanical counterpart of the classical ideal gas, and serves as a model for electrons in metals and semiconductors, nucleons in atomic nuclei, and matter in white dwarf and neutron stars.

Behaviour

Because no two fermions may occupy the same quantum state, even at absolute zero the particles fill up all energy states below the Fermi energy EF. The resulting zero-point motion exerts a degeneracy pressure that does not vanish at zero temperature — in stars this pressure supports white dwarfs against gravitational collapse up to the Chandrasekhar limit. At temperatures much below the Fermi temperature (over 10⁴ K for electrons in metals), only particles near the Fermi surface can be thermally excited, which explains why the electronic contribution to the heat capacity of metals is far smaller than classical theory predicted.

Applications

The free-electron model based on the Fermi gas accounts for the electrical and thermal conductivity, thermionic emission and magnetism of metals; with interactions added it develops into Fermi liquid theory. Ultracold atomic Fermi gases are used to study superfluidity and the BCS–BEC crossover.
