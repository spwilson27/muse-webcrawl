Idumea

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Idumea (Greek: Idoumaia) is the name given in the Hellenistic and Roman periods to the region of Edom after the Edomites migrated westward from their homeland east of the Arabah into the southern Judean hills and the northern Negev. Its principal towns included Maresha (Marisa) and Hebron, and its population—the Idumeans—was a mixture of Edomites, Arabs, Phoenicians, Greeks, and Jews.

History

The Edomite migration westward followed the Babylonian destruction of Judah in the 6th century BCE, when Edomites occupied southern Judah while their own territory was taken over by Arab tribes. During the Persian and Hellenistic periods Idumea formed a distinct administrative district under the Ptolemies and Seleucids; its city of Maresha, with its painted Sidonian tombs, flourished as a Hellenised commercial centre.

Around 113–110 BCE the Hasmonean ruler John Hyrcanus conquered Idumea and, according to Josephus, compelled the Idumeans to adopt Jewish customs, including circumcision, after which they were incorporated into the Jewish nation. The most famous Idumean descendant was Herod the Great, whose father Antipater was an Idumean noble and whose ancestry was used against him by his enemies. Idumeans participated in the Great Jewish Revolt against Rome (66–73 CE), during which the region suffered heavily, and Idumea thereafter fades as a distinct identity.
