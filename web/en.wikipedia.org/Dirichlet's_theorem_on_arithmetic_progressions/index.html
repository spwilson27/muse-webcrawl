Dirichlet's theorem on arithmetic progressions

Primes distributed among residue classes modulo a fixed integer

In number theory, Dirichlet's theorem, also called the Dirichlet prime number theorem, states that for any two positive coprime integers a and d, there are infinitely many primes of the form a + nd, where n is also a positive integer. In other words, there are infinitely many primes that are congruent to a modulo d, provided a and d are coprime.

History

The theorem was conjectured by Legendre and proved by Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet in 1837. Dirichlet's proof, which introduced Dirichlet characters and Dirichlet L-functions, is considered the founding result of analytic number theory. Elementary proofs for special cases exist, but the general theorem is known to require analysis in an essential way.

Distribution

The prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions strengthens the result: the primes are asymptotically equidistributed among the φ(d) reduced residue classes modulo d, each class containing a similar proportion of all primes. The Siegel–Walfisz theorem and the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem give quantitative forms of this equidistribution.

Generalizations

Analogous results hold in algebraic number theory, notably the Chebotarev density theorem, of which Dirichlet's theorem is a special case. Linnik's theorem bounds the size of the least prime in such a progression.
