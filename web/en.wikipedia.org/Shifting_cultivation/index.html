Shifting cultivation

Shifting cultivation is an agricultural system in which plots of land are cultivated temporarily, then abandoned while post-disturbance fallow vegetation is allowed to regenerate freely, as the cultivator moves on to a new plot. It is most widely known in its slash-and-burn form, in which forest is cut and burned to release nutrients as ash before planting. Regional names include jhum in north-east India and Bangladesh, milpa in Mesoamerica, conuco in Venezuela, swidden in Southeast Asia, and taungya in Myanmar (in a modified form combined with teak planting).

How it works

Farmers select a patch of secondary or primary forest, fell and dry the vegetation in the dry season, and burn it; the ash provides a pulse of phosphorus, potassium, calcium and magnesium and raises soil pH, fertilising one to three years of crops. Mixed cropping is the norm — dozens of species may share a field, mimicking forest structure and spreading risk. When yields fall and weeds and pests build up, the plot is left fallow; if the fallow is long enough (traditionally 10–25 years or more), forest regrowth restores soil fertility and the cycle can repeat sustainably at low population densities.

History

Shifting cultivation is among the oldest farming systems in the world, practised in Neolithic Europe (where it cleared much of the post-glacial forest) and independently developed across the tropics. It still supports an estimated 200–500 million people, mainly in the Amazon Basin, Central and West Africa, and Southeast Asia.

Environmental debate

With long fallows, shifting cultivation can maintain forest cover, biodiversity and near-closed nutrient cycles, and its critics in colonial and early development literature overstated its destructiveness. Under population pressure, land enclosure or market cropping, however, fallows shorten, soils exhaust, and the system degrades into repeated burning of Imperata grassland or deforestation. Burning also releases carbon dioxide, though long-fallow systems can be close to carbon-neutral over the full rotation. Governments have frequently tried to suppress the practice, with mixed results; alternatives promoted include agroforestry, improved fallows with nitrogen-fixing trees, and alley cropping.
