Mexican Texas is the historiographical term for the period of Texas history from 1821, when Mexico gained independence from Spain, until 1836, when the Anglo-American and Tejano settlers proclaimed the Republic of Texas during the Texas Revolution. Administratively, Texas was combined with Coahuila as the state of Coahuila y Tejas under the federal Constitution of 1824.

Immigration policy

Alarmed by the sparse population of its northern frontier and by threats from Indigenous peoples and possible American encroachment, Mexico encouraged immigration through the empresario system, granting large land contracts to agents such as Moses Austin and his son Stephen F. Austin, whose "Old Three Hundred" families founded San Felipe de Austin in 1823. Immigrants were required to become Mexican citizens, profess Catholicism, and observe Mexican law, though enforcement was lax. By 1834 the Anglo population of Texas was estimated at more than 20,000, outnumbering Tejanos several times over.

Growing conflict

Grievances accumulated over Mexico's abolition of slavery (1829), restrictions on further American immigration under the Law of April 6, 1830, customs enforcement, and disputes over political representation. The Fredonian Rebellion of 1826–27, an early Anglo bid for independence around Nacogdoches, heightened Mexican suspicion. Under President Antonio López de Santa Anna, Mexico's shift to a centralist regime in 1835, abrogating the federal constitution, pushed Texas settlers toward revolt, alongside federalists in other Mexican states.

Texas Revolution

Fighting began in October 1835 at the Battle of Gonzales. Texian forces captured San Antonio de Béxar in December, but Santa Anna's campaign in early 1836 retook it after the siege of the Alamo. Following the Goliad massacre, Sam Houston's army destroyed Santa Anna's force at the Battle of San Jacinto on April 21, 1836, securing de facto independence, which Mexico never formally recognized.
