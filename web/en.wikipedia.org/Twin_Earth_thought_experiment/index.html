Twin Earth thought experiment

Earth and Twin Earth: superficially identical, chemically different.

The Twin Earth thought experiment is an argument in the philosophy of language and philosophy of mind introduced by the philosopher Hilary Putnam, most famously in his 1975 paper "The Meaning of 'Meaning'". It is the classic defence of semantic externalism: the thesis that the meanings of a speaker's words, and the contents of their thoughts, are not determined solely by what is inside their head, but depend partly on the speaker's physical and social environment.

The experiment

Imagine a planet, Twin Earth, exactly like Earth in every observable respect, with one exception: the colourless, drinkable liquid that fills its lakes and falls as rain, and which its inhabitants call "water", is not H2O but a different chemical compound, abbreviated XYZ. Twin Earthlings are molecule-for-molecule duplicates of Earthlings, so an Earthling Oscar and his twin are in identical internal states whenever each thinks "water is wet".

Yet, Putnam argued, when Oscar says "water" he refers to H2O, while his twin refers to XYZ. Since their internal states are the same but their words refer to different substances, reference — and hence meaning — cannot be fixed by internal state alone. As Putnam's slogan puts it, meanings "just ain't in the head".

Significance and responses

Together with related arguments by Saul Kripke and Tyler Burge, Twin Earth reshaped analytic philosophy's understanding of natural-kind terms, reference and mental content. Critics have responded with two-dimensional semantics, internalist accounts of narrow content, and disputes about whether the intuition driving the case is semantic or merely pragmatic. The thought experiment remains a standard test case for theories of content.
