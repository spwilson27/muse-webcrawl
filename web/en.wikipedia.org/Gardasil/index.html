Gardasil is a vaccine for use in the prevention of certain strains of human papillomavirus (HPV). High-risk human papillomaviruses are sexually transmitted and can cause cervical, vulvar, vaginal and anal cancers, as well as precancerous lesions; low-risk types 6 and 11 cause most genital warts. The original Gardasil, approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in June 2006, protects against HPV types 6, 11, 16 and 18. Gardasil 9, approved in 2014, adds types 31, 33, 45, 52 and 58 and has replaced the original in many markets.

Medical uses

The vaccine is recommended for routine vaccination of adolescents — commonly at ages 11–12, and permitted from age 9 — before potential exposure to HPV, with catch-up vaccination recommended through age 26 and shared decision-making for adults up to 45. It prevents infection with the covered types but does not treat existing infection. Countries with high-coverage school-based programmes, such as Australia, have documented dramatic declines in genital warts, cervical precancers and, subsequently, cervical cancer rates.

Mechanism

Gardasil contains virus-like particles assembled from the L1 capsid protein of each included HPV type, produced in yeast. The particles contain no viral DNA and cannot cause infection, but elicit strong antibody responses that neutralise the virus at mucosal surfaces.

Safety

Extensive pre-licensure trials and post-marketing surveillance of hundreds of millions of doses have found the vaccine's safety profile favourable; common adverse effects are injection-site pain, swelling and fainting in adolescents, and large reviews have found no credible association with autoimmune or neurological conditions.
