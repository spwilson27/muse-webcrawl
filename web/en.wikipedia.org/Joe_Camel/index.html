Joe Camel

Joe Camel (also known as Old Joe) was an advertising mascot for the Camel brand of cigarettes, used by the R. J. Reynolds Tobacco Company in the United States from 1987 to 1997. The cartoon camel, depicted as a suave, sunglasses-wearing character in nightclubs and pool halls, was created by the Mezzina/Brown advertising agency to revive the brand's image.

Controversy

Joe Camel became the centre of a major public-health controversy after a series of studies published in the Journal of the American Medical Association in 1991 found that children recognised Joe Camel as readily as they recognised Mickey Mouse, and that Camel's share of the illegal underage cigarette market had risen dramatically since the campaign began. Critics argued that the cartoon character was a deliberate attempt to market cigarettes to children; R. J. Reynolds maintained that the campaign was aimed at adult smokers of competing brands.

Retirement

Under pressure from the Federal Trade Commission, the U.S. Congress and public-health organisations — and as part of the broader settlement negotiations between the states and the tobacco industry — R. J. Reynolds retired Joe Camel in 1997, replacing the campaign with one featuring only the brand's traditional camel illustration from the cigarette pack. The 1998 Master Settlement Agreement subsequently banned the use of cartoon characters in cigarette advertising in the United States.
