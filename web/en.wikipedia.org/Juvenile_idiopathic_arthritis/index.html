SpecialtyPaediatric rheumatology
OnsetBefore age 16
DurationAt least 6 weeks
TypesOligoarticular, polyarticular, systemic, enthesitis-related, psoriatic, undifferentiated

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA), formerly called juvenile rheumatoid arthritis, is the most common chronic rheumatic disease of childhood. It is an umbrella term for arthritis of unknown cause that begins before the age of 16 and persists for at least six weeks, after other causes have been excluded. It is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks the synovial lining of joints.

Subtypes

The International League of Associations for Rheumatology (ILAR) classification recognises seven categories, including oligoarticular JIA (four or fewer joints), rheumatoid-factor-positive and -negative polyarticular JIA (five or more joints), systemic JIA (formerly Still's disease, with fever and rash), enthesitis-related arthritis, and juvenile psoriatic arthritis. Subtypes differ in course, complications and treatment.

Symptoms and complications

Signs include joint swelling, morning stiffness, limp and reduced range of motion; young children may simply become irritable and avoid using a limb. A serious complication is chronic anterior uveitis, an inflammation of the eye that is often asymptomatic but can cause blindness, so regular slit-lamp screening is essential. Growth disturbance and leg-length discrepancy can occur.

Treatment

Management aims to suppress inflammation, preserve function and prevent joint damage. Treatments include non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, intra-articular corticosteroid injections, methotrexate, and biologic agents such as TNF inhibitors (e.g. etanercept, adalimumab) and interleukin inhibitors for systemic disease, alongside physiotherapy. Early aggressive treatment has greatly improved outcomes.
