Genetic algorithm

A genetic algorithm (GA) is a metaheuristic inspired by the process of natural selection that belongs to the larger class of evolutionary algorithms. Genetic algorithms are commonly used to generate high-quality solutions to optimization and search problems by relying on biologically inspired operators such as mutation, crossover, and selection.

Methodology

The algorithm maintains a population of candidate solutions, each encoded as a chromosome — often a bit string. In each generation, the fitness of every individual is evaluated; fitter individuals are stochastically selected to form a new generation, modified by crossover (recombining two parents' encodings) and mutation (randomly altering genes with a small probability). The process repeats until a termination condition is met, such as a fixed number of generations or a satisfactory fitness level.

History

The field was pioneered in the 1960s and 1970s by John Holland at the University of Michigan, whose 1975 book Adaptation in Natural and Artificial Systems introduced the schema theorem and remains a foundational reference. Holland's student David E. Goldberg's 1989 book Genetic Algorithms in Search, Optimization and Machine Learning helped popularize the technique.

Applications

Genetic algorithms have been applied to scheduling, timetabling, engineering design, circuit layout, antenna design — including an evolved antenna flown on NASA's ST5 spacecraft — game strategy, and hyperparameter optimization in machine learning. They are most useful when the search space is large, rugged, or poorly understood, and when exact methods are infeasible.
