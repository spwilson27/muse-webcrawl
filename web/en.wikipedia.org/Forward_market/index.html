Forward market

Forward contracts are traded over the counter rather than on centralised exchanges.

The forward market is the over-the-counter market in forward contracts—agreements to buy or sell an asset at a specified price on a future date. Unlike the futures market, whose contracts are standardised and traded on exchanges, the forward market deals in customised contracts negotiated directly between two parties, typically financial institutions or their corporate clients. Forward markets exist for currencies, interest rates, commodities, and other assets.

Forward contracts

A forward contract specifies the asset, quantity, delivery date, and the forward price agreed today. No cash changes hands at inception (in the standard case); instead, the contract's value fluctuates with the spot price of the underlying asset, and settlement occurs at maturity, either by physical delivery or in cash. The forward price is determined by the cost of carry—the spot price plus financing and storage costs, minus any income the asset yields—so that no arbitrage is possible.

Foreign exchange forwards

The largest forward market is in foreign exchange. Companies and investors use currency forwards to hedge exchange-rate risk on future receipts and payments, while banks use them to manage their own positions. The difference between the forward rate and the spot rate (the forward premium or discount) is governed by covered interest rate parity, reflecting interest-rate differentials between the two currencies.

Risks

Because forwards are private, bilateral contracts, participants bear counterparty (default) risk: if one side fails, the other may not be paid. There is also no daily marking-to-market or margining as on futures exchanges, although modern practice increasingly uses collateral agreements and central clearing for some products. The flexibility of forwards—their terms can be tailored exactly to a hedger's exposure—is their principal advantage over futures.
