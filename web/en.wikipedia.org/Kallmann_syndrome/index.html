Kallmann syndrome

Kallmann syndrome
SpecialtyEndocrinology, medical genetics
SymptomsAbsent or delayed puberty; absent sense of smell (anosmia)

Kallmann syndrome (KS) is a genetic disorder that causes hypogonadotropic hypogonadism — a failure to start or complete puberty — together with a partial or complete loss of the sense of smell (anosmia or hyposmia). It is a form of isolated GnRH deficiency.

Cause

During embryonic development, neurons producing gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) normally migrate from the nasal region to the hypothalamus, following the olfactory nerve fibres. In Kallmann syndrome this migration fails, so the hypothalamus cannot stimulate the pituitary to release LH and FSH, and the olfactory bulbs may be absent or underdeveloped. Mutations in more than 20 genes have been implicated, including ANOS1 (KAL1, X-linked), FGFR1, PROK2/PROKR2, and CHD7; inheritance may be X-linked, autosomal dominant, or autosomal recessive, and many cases remain genetically unexplained.

Features

The cardinal features are absent or incomplete puberty and infertility, with low sex steroids and low or inappropriately normal gonadotropins. Associated non-reproductive features can include mirror movements (bimanual synkinesis), unilateral renal agenesis, cleft lip/palate, hearing loss, and dental agenesis, depending on the causative gene.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis typically occurs in adolescence when puberty fails to appear; it is based on hormone profiles, smell testing, MRI of the olfactory bulbs, and genetic testing. Treatment is hormone replacement — testosterone in males, oestrogen/progesterone in females — to induce secondary sexual characteristics and maintain bone health. Fertility can usually be achieved with gonadotropin therapy or pulsatile GnRH. The condition is named after the German-American psychiatrist Franz Josef Kallmann, who described it in 1944.
