Lactase persistence

Lactase persistence
GeneLCT (control region in MCM6)
TraitContinued lactase production into adulthood

Lactase persistence is the continued activity of the enzyme lactase in adulthood, allowing adults to digest lactose, the sugar in milk. In most mammals, and in a majority of the world's humans, lactase production declines after weaning, leading to lactose intolerance; lactase persistence is a derived trait that evolved in populations with a long history of dairying.

Genetics

The trait is controlled by regulatory variants near the lactase gene (LCT) on chromosome 2, within an intron of the neighbouring MCM6 gene. The best-known variant, −13910*T, is common in Europe. Different variants arose independently in East African and Arabian pastoralist populations — a classic case of convergent evolution driven by gene–culture coevolution with milk drinking.

Distribution

Lactase persistence reaches frequencies above 90 percent in parts of northern Europe, is common among pastoralist groups in Africa and the Middle East, and is rare in East and Southeast Asia and among Indigenous peoples of the Americas and Oceania. Globally, roughly a third to two-fifths of adults are lactase persistent.

Evolutionary history

Ancient DNA shows the European variant was rare in early Neolithic farmers and rose in frequency within the last several thousand years, suggesting strong natural selection once milk became a reliable source of calories, fluid and calcium.
