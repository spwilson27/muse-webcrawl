Walk-off (baseball)


Sport: Baseball (and softball)
Occurs: Bottom of the final inning (or extra innings)
Forms: Walk-off hit, walk-off home run, walk-off walk, walk-off hit by pitch

A walk-off in baseball is any play by the home team in the bottom of the last inning of a game — the ninth inning of a regulation game, or any extra inning — that gives the home team the lead and thereby ends the game immediately, so that the losing team "walks off" the field. The term is most often attached to the decisive hit: a walk-off single, walk-off home run, or walk-off walk.

Mechanics

A walk-off can only happen for the home team, which always bats last. As soon as the go-ahead run scores, the game is over, regardless of how many outs have been made. Rules limits apply to the scoring: on a walk-off home run all runs count, but on other walk-off hits only the runs needed to win are normally credited, and a batter who fails to touch first base on the winning hit can be called out on appeal. A game can also end on a bases-loaded walk or hit by pitch, a sacrifice fly, a wild pitch, a balk, or an error.

Origin and famous examples

The term was popularised in the late 1980s and 1990s, attributed in part to Oakland Athletics reliever Dennis Eckersley, who used "walk-off piece" for a game-ending home run hit against him. Famous walk-offs include Bill Mazeroski's home run winning the 1960 World Series for Pittsburgh, Joe Carter's homer ending the 1993 World Series for Toronto, and Kirk Gibson's pinch-hit home run in Game 1 of the 1988 World Series — hit off Eckersley himself. Walk-off celebrations typically involve the home team mobbing the batter at home plate or first base.
