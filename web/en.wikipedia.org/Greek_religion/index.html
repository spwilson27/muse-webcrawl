Greek religion

Religion in Greece in the ancient world consisted of practices and beliefs that were largely polytheistic and centred on the Olympian gods, though there was no central authority, creed or sacred text binding all Greek speakers. This article summarises the religion of ancient Greece — its rituals, sanctuaries and festivals — as distinguished from the narrative corpus of Greek mythology, which supplied stories about those gods and heroes.

Characteristics

Greek religion was orthopraxic rather than orthodox: correct performance of ritual mattered far more than belief. There was no professional priestly caste; priests and priestesses were typically civic magistrates or hereditary functionaries who presided over sacrifices. Religion was inseparable from the life of the polis — assemblies, wars, colonisation and legislation were all framed by prayer, sacrifice, divination and oath.

Practices

The central rite was animal sacrifice (thysia), in which a victim was slaughtered at an open-air altar, portions burned for the gods and the meat shared in a communal feast, enacting reciprocity (charis) between mortals and gods. Other offerings included libations of wine, first-fruits, votive objects and hymns. Purification, processions and athletic or dramatic festivals — most famously the Olympic Games at Olympia and the City Dionysia at Athens — honoured particular deities. Oracles, above all Apollo's at Delphi, and seers who read entrails or bird-signs, mediated knowledge of divine will. Mystery cults such as the Eleusinian Mysteries and the worship of Dionysus promised initiates a more personal, emotional religious experience and hopes for the afterlife.

The gods

The principal gods were the twelve Olympians — Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Demeter, Athena, Apollo, Artemis, Ares, Aphrodite, Hephaestus, Hermes and Dionysus (or Hestia) — dwelling on Mount Olympus, alongside chthonic powers such as Hades and Persephone and a vast population of local heroes, nymphs and river gods. Each city had its patron deity and its own cult calendar and myths, so that "Greek religion" was in practice hundreds of local religions sharing a common grammar.

History

Greek religion descends in part from Minoan and Mycenaean religion of the Bronze Age: Linear B tablets record several Olympian names. It took classical shape in the Archaic period, when the poems of Homer and Hesiod gave the Greeks a shared mythological framework — Herodotus credited the two poets with giving the gods their names, epithets and forms. In the Hellenistic age, ruler cults and imported deities such as Isis and Serapis expanded the repertoire. Pagan practice declined under Christian Roman emperors in the fourth century CE, though the Olympic Games survived until 393 CE and rural paganism ('Hellenes') persisted for centuries longer.
