Thing (assembly)

A thing was the governing assembly of early Germanic societies, held at a designated outdoor site.

A thing (Old Norse: þing; Old English: þing; modern Scandinavian: ting) was the governing assembly of Germanic peoples, made up of the free people of the community, presided over by a lawspeaker or a chieftain. Things met at regular intervals, legislated, elected leaders, and judged disputes.

The word survives in the names of several modern parliaments and places: the Icelandic Althing, the Norwegian Storting, the Danish Folketing, the Faroese Løgting, the Manx Tynwald, and the English place-name element in names such as Thingwall. The English word "thing" itself descends from the assembly sense: originally "a meeting, a matter discussed at a meeting," it broadened to mean "an entity of any kind."

History

The Roman historian Tacitus describes Germanic assemblies in Germania (c. 98 AD): chiefs deliberated, but the assembled warriors approved proposals by brandishing their spears or rejected them by murmuring. In the Viking Age and medieval Scandinavia, local things were organised in hierarchies under regional law things. Free men of the district were obliged or entitled to attend; in Iceland, farmers owed attendance duty (þingför) to their chieftain, or goði.

Functions

Things combined legislative, judicial and political functions. The lawspeaker recited the law from memory; changes to the law and major decisions, such as choosing a king, required assembly approval. Feuds and compensation claims were settled at the thing, and outlawry was pronounced there. Assemblies also served as markets, social occasions and musters.

Notable things

The Icelandic Althing, founded in 930 at Þingvellir, is one of the oldest surviving parliamentary institutions in the world. The Manx Tynwald, of Norse origin, claims over a thousand years of continuous existence. In Anglo-Saxon England, local moots and the king's council, the witenagemot, performed comparable functions.
