Cosmic dust

Cosmic dust, also called extraterrestrial dust or space dust, is dust that occurs in outer space or has fallen to Earth. Most cosmic dust particles measure between a few molecules and about 0.1 millimetres. It includes interplanetary dust in the Solar System, interstellar dust between the stars, and intergalactic dust, and is composed largely of silicates, carbon compounds and ices.

Types

Interplanetary dust, produced by collisions among asteroids and by comets, causes the zodiacal light visible in the night sky. Interstellar dust makes up about 1% of the mass of the interstellar medium and is responsible for the extinction and reddening of starlight. Circumstellar dust surrounds young stars in protoplanetary disks, where it is the raw material of planets.

Study and collection

Tens of thousands of tonnes of cosmic dust reach Earth's surface each year, much of it as micrometeorites collected from deep-sea sediments, polar ice and the stratosphere. Spacecraft such as Stardust have returned comet dust samples to Earth, and missions like Cassini analysed interstellar grains directly.

Importance

Dust grains provide the surfaces on which molecules form in cold clouds, shield molecular gas from ultraviolet radiation, and seed the formation of stars and planets. Their infrared emission is a major tool for observing obscured regions of galaxies.
