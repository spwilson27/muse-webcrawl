Humane Methods of Livestock Slaughter Act

The Humane Methods of Livestock Slaughter Act, commonly known as the Humane Slaughter Act, is a United States federal law enacted in 1958 and amended in 1978, codified at 7 U.S.C. §§ 1901–1907. It requires that livestock — cattle, calves, horses, mules, sheep, swine, and other livestock — be rendered insensible to pain by a single blow, gunshot, or electrical, chemical, or other rapid and effective means before being shackled, hoisted, thrown, cast, or cut, and that livestock be handled in connection with slaughter so as to minimize excitement and discomfort.

History

Public concern over slaughterhouse conditions, stimulated in part by reports of inhumane practices, led Congress to pass the original Act in 1958 under President Dwight D. Eisenhower. The 1958 law applied mainly to federal purchasers; the 1978 amendments extended its requirements to all federally inspected slaughter establishments and to imports.

Exemptions and enforcement

The Act exempts ritual slaughter, such as kosher and halal slaughter, conducted in accordance with religious requirements, as well as slaughter on farms for personal use. Enforcement is carried out by the Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) of the U.S. Department of Agriculture, whose inspectors may halt slaughter operations for violations. Animal welfare organizations have periodically criticized the Act's enforcement and its exclusion of poultry, which account for the great majority of animals slaughtered in the United States.
