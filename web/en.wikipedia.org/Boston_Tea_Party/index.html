The Boston Tea Party was an American political and mercantile protest on 16 December 1773, in which members of the Sons of Liberty and other colonists boarded three ships of the British East India Company in Boston Harbor and threw 342 chests of tea overboard. It was a pivotal escalation in the conflict between Great Britain and its American colonies that led to the American Revolutionary War.

Background

The Tea Act of 1773 was not a new tax but a rescue measure for the financially troubled East India Company: it allowed the company to sell tea directly to the colonies, undercutting smugglers and colonial merchants, while retaining the Townshend duty on tea. Colonists saw acceptance of the cheaper tea as submission to Parliament's claim to tax them without representation — "no taxation without representation". In other ports, pressure forced tea ships to turn back; in Boston, Governor Thomas Hutchinson refused to let the ships leave without unloading.

The event

After a mass meeting at the Old South Meeting House on 16 December failed to win the governor's consent, Samuel Adams reportedly declared that the meeting could do nothing more to save the country. That night, between 30 and 130 men — thinly disguised as Mohawks — boarded the Dartmouth, Eleanor, and Beaver, hoisted out the tea chests, broke them open, and emptied roughly 340 chests (some 92,000 pounds, worth about £9,659, or around $1.7 million in modern terms) into the harbour. Nothing else was damaged and no one was injured; a padlock broken in the action was later replaced anonymously.

Consequences

Parliament responded in 1774 with the Coercive Acts — called the Intolerable Acts in America — closing Boston's port until the tea was paid for, curtailing Massachusetts self-government, and quartering troops. Rather than isolating Massachusetts, the acts united the colonies, leading to the First Continental Congress in September 1774 and, in April 1775, to open war. The name "Boston Tea Party" was not widely used until the 1830s; participants had called it "the destruction of the tea".
