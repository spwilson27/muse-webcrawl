The Cori cycle: lactate produced by muscle is converted back to glucose in the liver.

The Cori cycle (also known as the lactic acid cycle) is a metabolic pathway in which lactate, produced by anaerobic glycolysis in muscles, is transported to the liver and converted back to glucose, which then returns to the muscles and is metabolised back to lactate. It is named after its discoverers, Carl Ferdinand Cori and Gerty Cori, who shared the 1947 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Process

During vigorous exercise, when oxygen supply cannot meet demand, muscle cells convert glucose and glycogen to lactate, gaining a net yield of two ATP molecules per glucose. Lactate enters the bloodstream and is taken up by the liver, where gluconeogenesis rebuilds glucose at a cost of six ATP. The glucose is released into the blood and taken up again by muscle. The cycle thus shifts part of the metabolic burden of exercise from the muscle to the liver and prevents a dangerous build-up of lactate in the blood.

Energetics

The cycle is energetically expensive: muscle gains 2 ATP from glycolysis while the liver spends 6 ATP equivalents on gluconeogenesis, a net loss of 4 ATP per turn. This cost is acceptable because it sustains high rates of ATP production in muscle during intense effort and supplies energy later, when oxygen becomes available again — part of the "oxygen debt" repaid after exercise.

Significance

The Cori cycle complements the glucose–alanine cycle, another route for moving carbon and nitrogen from muscle to liver. An extended version of the cycle has been proposed in cancer metabolism, where lactate exchange between tumour cells and their surroundings may support growth.
