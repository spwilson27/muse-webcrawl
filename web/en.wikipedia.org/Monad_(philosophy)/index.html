Monad (philosophy)

The monad (from Greek monas, "unit") is a concept used by several philosophical traditions for the fundamental indivisible unit of reality. It appears in Pythagorean thought and later in Neoplatonism, but is most famous as the centrepiece of the metaphysics of Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, set out in his Monadology (1714).

Leibniz's monads

For Leibniz, monads are simple substances: they have no parts, cannot be created or destroyed naturally, and do not interact causally — in his phrase, they "have no windows". Each monad nevertheless mirrors the entire universe from its own point of view through its perceptions, and their apparent coordination is explained by a pre-established harmony ordained by God, the supreme monad. Monads differ in the clarity of their perceptions, forming a hierarchy from bare monads through animal souls to rational spirits.

Other uses

Giordano Bruno used monads for the minima of matter and spirit, and the term influenced later thinkers. In the 20th century the word was independently adopted in mathematics (category theory) and computer science for unrelated concepts of structured units of computation.
