A lamassu (Akkadian: lamassu; Sumerian: lamma) is a protective deity of ancient Mesopotamia, often depicted as a hybrid creature with the body of a bull or lion, the wings of an eagle, and the head of a human. In Assyrian sculpture of the early first millennium BCE, pairs of colossal lamassu — sometimes weighing 30–50 tonnes — were carved from single blocks of alabaster and set at the gateways of palaces and cities to ward off evil and overawe visitors.

Iconography

The hybrid form combined the strength of the bull, the courage of the lion, the swiftness of the eagle and the intelligence of man. Assyrian lamassu were famously carved with five legs, so that a viewer approaching from the front saw two legs standing still, while a side view showed four legs striding — resolving the figure from both viewpoints. The human head typically wears the horned cap of divinity and a stylised beard indicating royalty.

History

The lamma began in Sumerian religion as a female protective spirit and intercessor. In the Neo-Assyrian period (911–609 BCE) the motif was monumentalised: famous examples guarded the palaces of Ashurnasirpal II at Nimrud, Sargon II at Khorsabad (Dur-Sharrukin), and Sennacherib at Nineveh. Nineteenth-century excavations by Austen Henry Layard and Paul-Émile Botta shipped celebrated pairs to the British Museum, the Louvre and the Metropolitan Museum of Art. In February 2015, Islamic State militants destroyed an original lamassu at the Nergal Gate of Nineveh in Mosul, an act widely condemned as cultural vandalism; the site has since been partially reconstructed.

In modern culture

The lamassu has become a symbol of Iraqi and Assyrian heritage, appearing on the logo of Iraqi Airways and in the names of diaspora organisations. Comparable hybrid guardians — sphinxes, griffins and cherubim — populate the art of the wider ancient Near East.
