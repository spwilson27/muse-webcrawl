Fizeau–Foucault apparatus

PurposeMeasurement of the speed of light
InventorsHippolyte Fizeau; Léon Foucault
Years1849 (Fizeau); 1850–1862 (Foucault)

The Fizeau–Foucault apparatus refers to the terrestrial instruments used by the French physicists Hippolyte Fizeau and Léon Foucault to make the first accurate measurements of the speed of light on Earth, rather than by astronomical methods.

Fizeau's toothed wheel

In 1849 Fizeau shone a beam of light through the gaps of a rapidly rotating toothed wheel toward a mirror some 8.6 kilometres away. By adjusting the wheel's speed until the returning light was eclipsed by the next tooth, he timed the round trip and obtained a value of about 313,000 km/s.

Foucault's rotating mirror

Foucault replaced the wheel with a rotating mirror (1850, refined by 1862): light reflected from the spinning mirror to a fixed mirror and back is deflected by an angle depending on the travel time. His 1862 measurement gave about 298,000 km/s, within roughly 0.6% of the modern value. Foucault also used the apparatus to show that light travels slower in water than in air, a result supporting the wave theory of light.
