Planetary nebula

Planetary nebula
TypeEmission nebula; late stage of stellar evolution
Progenitor starsLow- to intermediate-mass (≈0.8–8 solar masses)
Lifetime~10,000–30,000 years
Famous examplesRing Nebula (M57), Helix Nebula, Cat's Eye Nebula, Dumbbell Nebula (M27)

A planetary nebula is an expanding, glowing shell of ionised gas ejected from a red giant star in the last stages of its evolution. Despite the name — given by William Herschel because their small discs resembled planets in early telescopes — planetary nebulae have nothing to do with planets. They are a brief but important phase in the lives of low- and intermediate-mass stars, including the Sun, and they return carbon, nitrogen, and other processed elements to the interstellar medium.

Formation

When a star of up to about eight solar masses exhausts hydrogen and helium in its core, it expands into a red giant and then an asymptotic giant branch (AGB) star, pulsating and shedding its outer layers in a slow, dense wind. The exposed hot core — contracting toward a white dwarf — emits intense ultraviolet radiation that ionises the expelled envelope, making it glow. Interactions between the fast wind from the core and the slower AGB wind, often shaped by magnetic fields or binary companions, sculpt the nebula into spheres, ellipses, and striking bipolar "butterfly" shapes.

Properties and lifetime

Typical planetary nebulae span about one light-year and expand at 20–30 km/s. The central star can exceed 100,000 K. Roughly 3,000 planetary nebulae are known in the Milky Way, out of an estimated total of 10,000–20,000; each remains visible for only a few tens of thousands of years before dispersing and fading as the white dwarf cools. Their emission lines — especially the green forbidden lines of doubly ionised oxygen once mistaken for a new element, "nebulium" — make them powerful probes of chemical abundances and distances, including in other galaxies.

Role in galactic evolution

Because successive generations of stars enrich their envelopes through fusion and dredge-up, planetary nebulae are a principal channel by which carbon and nitrogen produced in stars re-enter interstellar space, seeding future stars, planets, and — ultimately — chemistry relevant to life. The Sun is expected to form a modest planetary nebula in about five billion years.
