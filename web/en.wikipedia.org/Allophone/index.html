Allophone

The term allophone has distinct meanings in phonetics and in Canadian demography.

In phonetics

In phonetics, an allophone is one of a set of multiple possible spoken sounds (phones) used to pronounce a single phoneme in a particular language. For example, in English the voiceless plosives /p/, /t/, and /k/ are aspirated ([pʰ], [tʰ], [kʰ]) at the beginnings of stressed syllables but unaspirated after /s/, as in pin versus spin. These variants do not change word meaning and are therefore allophones of the same phoneme, unlike phones in a minimal pair, which distinguish words.

In Canadian usage

In Canada, an allophone (French: allophone) is a person whose mother tongue is neither English nor French, the country's two official languages. Statistics Canada uses the term in census reporting, in contrast to anglophone and francophone. The term is especially common in Quebec, where debates over immigration, language legislation such as the Charter of the French Language, and the linguistic integration of newcomers frequently refer to allophone communities.
