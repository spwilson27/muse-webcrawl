A nature documentary or wildlife documentary is a genre of documentary film or series about animals, plants, or other non-human living creatures, usually concentrating on video taken in their natural habitat, but also including footage shot in zoos and aquariums. Such programmes are most frequently made for television, particularly for public broadcasting, and some are made for theatrical release in cinemas.

History

Early wildlife films date to the beginning of cinema; big-game hunting films and expedition footage were popular in the 1900s–1920s. The genre matured with the work of the BBC Natural History Unit, founded in 1957, and presenters such as David Attenborough, whose Zoo Quest (1954) and later Life on Earth (1979) brought natural history to mass audiences. Jacques Cousteau's underwater films, beginning with The Silent World (1956), opened the oceans to viewers.

Notable series

The BBC's landmark series include The Blue Planet (2001), Planet Earth (2006), Life (2009), Frozen Planet (2011), Planet Earth II (2016) and Blue Planet II (2017). Streaming-era productions include Netflix's Our Planet (2019). High-definition and later ultra-high-definition photography, drones, remote cameras and extreme slow motion have become hallmarks of the modern genre.

Production and criticism

Nature documentaries often require years of filming in remote locations, with crews spending hundreds of days to capture rare behaviour. The genre has been criticised for staged sequences, misleading sound design (most wildlife sound is added in post-production), and for presenting an overly pristine view of nature that omits human environmental impact — a criticism recent series have increasingly addressed by covering conservation and climate change.
