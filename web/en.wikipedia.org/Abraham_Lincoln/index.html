Abraham Lincoln (12 February 1809 – 15 April 1865) was the 16th president of the United States. He led the Union to victory in the American Civil War, preserving the nation, abolishing slavery, and strengthening the federal government.

Early life and career

Born in a log cabin in Kentucky and raised in Indiana and Illinois, Lincoln was largely self-educated. He became a lawyer in Springfield, Illinois, served in the state legislature as a Whig, and was elected to the U.S. House of Representatives (1847–1849). His 1858 Senate campaign against Stephen A. Douglas, featuring the Lincoln–Douglas debates, made him a national figure, and he won the Republican presidential nomination and election in 1860.

Presidency and Civil War

His election triggered the secession of eleven Southern states. As commander-in-chief Lincoln directed the Union war effort, issued the Emancipation Proclamation (effective 1 January 1863) making abolition a war aim, and delivered the Gettysburg Address (19 November 1863), a defining statement of American democratic ideals. He secured passage of the Thirteenth Amendment abolishing slavery and was re-elected in 1864. Days after Lee's surrender at Appomattox, Lincoln was shot on 14 April 1865 by John Wilkes Booth at Ford's Theatre and died the next morning — the first American president to be assassinated. He is consistently ranked by historians among the greatest U.S. presidents.
