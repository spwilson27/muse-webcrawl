Concorde

Concorde was a supersonic passenger airliner jointly developed by Britain and France and operated from 1976 to 2003. Capable of cruising at Mach 2.02 — more than twice the speed of sound — at altitudes up to 60,000 feet, it could fly from London or Paris to New York in about three and a half hours, half the time of a conventional jet. With its slender delta wing and droop nose, it became one of the most recognisable aircraft ever built.

Development

The project grew out of a 1962 Anglo-French treaty and first flew in 1969. Twenty aircraft were built, including six prototypes and development aircraft; the fourteen production aircraft were divided between British Airways and Air France. High fuel burn, a small cabin of about 100 seats and restrictions on overland supersonic flight confined Concorde mainly to transatlantic routes.

Accident and retirement

On 25 July 2000, Air France Flight 4590 crashed shortly after takeoff from Paris Charles de Gaulle after debris on the runway ruptured a fuel tank, killing all 109 people on board and four on the ground. The fleet was grounded and modified, returning to service in 2001, but rising maintenance costs and falling passenger numbers after 2001 led both airlines to retire Concorde in 2003; the final flight took place on 26 November 2003.
