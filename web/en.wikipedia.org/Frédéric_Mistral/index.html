Frédéric Mistral

Frédéric Mistral (born Joseph Étienne Frédéric Mistral; 8 September 1830 – 25 March 1914) was a French writer of Occitan literature and lexicographer of the Provençal dialect. He received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1904, shared with José Echegaray. Mistral led the nineteenth-century revival of Occitan through the Félibrige movement, which he co-founded in 1854, and spent decades compiling his monumental Provençal dictionary, Lou Tresor dòu Felibrige.

Life and work

Born to a prosperous farming family at Maillane in Provence, Mistral studied law at Aix-en-Provence but resolved to devote his life to restoring Provençal as a literary language. His epic poem Mirèio (Mireille, 1859), a love story set among Provençal peasants, won acclaim across Europe and was later adapted into an opera by Charles Gounod. Later epics include Calendau (1867), Nerto (1884) and Lou Pouèmo dóu Rose (The Poem of the Rhône, 1897).

Félibrige and lexicography

With six fellow poets Mistral founded the Félibrige in 1854 to safeguard the language and culture of the south. His dictionary Lou Tresor dòu Felibrige (1878–1886) remains the standard lexicon of modern Occitan. He used his Nobel money to found the Museon Arlaten, an ethnographic museum of Provençal life in Arles.

Legacy

Mistral's championing of a "minority" language made him a model for regional and minority-language movements worldwide; the Chilean poet Lucila Godoy Alcayaga took her pen name Gabriela Mistral in his honour.
