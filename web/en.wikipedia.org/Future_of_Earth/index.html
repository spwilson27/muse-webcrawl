The biological and geological future of Earth can be extrapolated from the effects of long-term processes: the steadily brightening Sun, the cooling of Earth's core, plate tectonics, and interactions with other Solar System bodies. On timescales of billions of years these changes will end Earth's habitability long before the Sun becomes a red giant.

The brightening Sun

Solar luminosity increases about 10% every billion years. Within roughly 1 billion years the extra heat is expected to trigger a moist or runaway greenhouse, evaporating the oceans; falling CO₂ from accelerated weathering may end photosynthesis for most plants within 500–900 million years, collapsing complex ecosystems. Plate tectonics may cease as the mantle cools and the oceans vanish.

Red giant phase

In about 5 billion years the Sun will exhaust its core hydrogen and expand into a red giant. Whether Earth is engulfed depends on solar mass loss and tidal effects; many models suggest Earth will be consumed in roughly 7.5 billion years, though some calculations allow a scorched Earth to survive in a wider orbit. The Moon will continue receding, days lengthening, and any remnant Earth will eventually cool as the Sun becomes a white dwarf.

Other long-term events

Earlier milestones include the formation of a future supercontinent (e.g. Pangaea Ultima) in 250–300 million years, possible glaciation cycles, and a ~1% chance over the next billion years of orbital destabilisation involving Mercury.
