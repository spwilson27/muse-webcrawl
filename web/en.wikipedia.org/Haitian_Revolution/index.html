The Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) was a successful insurrection by self-liberated slaves against French colonial rule in Saint-Domingue, now the sovereign state of Haiti. It was the only slave uprising in history that led to the founding of a state, and it is widely considered the most successful slave rebellions of the modern era. The revolution abolished slavery in the colony, defeated armies sent by France, Spain, and Britain, and made Haiti the first independent nation of Latin America and the Caribbean, the second republic in the Americas, and the first country in the modern era led by people of African descent.

Background

Saint-Domingue, the western third of the island of Hispaniola, was France's richest colony, producing a huge share of the world's sugar and coffee on plantations worked by roughly half a million enslaved Africans, who vastly outnumbered some 40,000 whites and 28,000 free people of colour. Conditions were exceptionally brutal. Inspired in part by the French Revolution's Declaration of the Rights of Man, free people of colour demanded equal rights, while enslaved people planned revolt.

Course of the revolution

The uprising began in August 1791, traditionally linked to the Vodou ceremony at Bois Caïman led by Dutty Boukman and Cécile Fatiman; within weeks enslaved insurgents controlled much of the northern plain. The struggle became a multi-sided war involving French commissioners (who abolished slavery in the colony in 1793 and in all French colonies in 1794), Spanish and British invasions, and rivalries among Black and mixed-race leaders. Toussaint Louverture, a former slave turned brilliant general and statesman, rose to become lieutenant governor and then governor-general, issuing a 1801 constitution that abolished slavery forever while keeping the colony nominally French.

Napoleon sent a large expedition under General Charles Leclerc to restore French authority and, ultimately, slavery. Louverture was arrested by treachery in 1802 and died in prison in France in 1803, but his lieutenant Jean-Jacques Dessalines united the resistance. French forces, ravaged by yellow fever and defeated at the Battle of Vertières on 18 November 1803, surrendered. On 1 January 1804 Dessalines proclaimed the independence of Haiti — from an Indigenous Taíno word for the island — at Gonaïves.

Aftermath and significance

Haiti's independence was long unrecognised: France only acknowledged it in 1825 in exchange for a crushing indemnity (the "ransom of independence"), whose payments lasted until 1947, and the United States withheld recognition until 1862. The revolution terrified slaveholding societies, inspired abolitionists and enslaved people across the Americas, helped push Napoleon to sell Louisiana to the United States in 1803, and stands as a landmark in the history of human rights and decolonisation.
