Garifuna

Image placeholder: Garifuna drummers performing punta music

The Garifuna (singular and plural: Garifuna; also spelled Garínagu for the people collectively) are an Afro-Indigenous people of the Caribbean coast of Central America, descended from West and Central African people and Indigenous Arawak and Carib (Kalinago) peoples of the island of Saint Vincent. Their language, also called Garifuna, is an Arawakan language with Carib, French, English, and Spanish influences.

History

On Saint Vincent, Africans — some survivors of shipwrecked slave ships, others escaped from nearby plantations — intermarried with the Island Caribs and formed the people known to the British as the "Black Caribs." After conflict with British colonists, the Garifuna were defeated in 1796 and deported in 1797 to the island of Roatán off Honduras; from there they spread along the Caribbean coasts of Honduras, Guatemala, Nicaragua, and Belize. The arrival in Belize is commemorated each year on November 19 as Garifuna Settlement Day, a national holiday.

Culture

Garifuna culture is noted for its music — especially punta and punta rock, driven by primero and segunda drums — its cassava-based cuisine (including ereba, or cassava bread, and the fish stew hudut), and its spiritual practices honouring ancestors, such as the dügü healing ceremony. In 2001, UNESCO proclaimed the language, dance, and music of the Garifuna a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity.

Population

Significant Garifuna communities live in Honduras (the largest population), Belize, Guatemala, and Nicaragua, and a large diaspora exists in the United States, particularly in New York City, Los Angeles, Houston, and New Orleans. Population estimates for the Garifuna worldwide are commonly in the hundreds of thousands.
