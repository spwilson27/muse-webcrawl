Spatha
Type: Long sword
Used by: Roman cavalry, later infantry; Germanic peoples
Blade length: c. 60–100 cm
Period: 1st century BC – early Middle Ages

The spatha was a long, straight, double-edged sword used in the Roman Empire and the Migration Period, typically with a blade between 60 and 100 cm (24 and 39 in) long. Originally a cavalry weapon, it progressively replaced the shorter gladius as the standard sidearm of Roman infantry during the 2nd and 3rd centuries AD, and became the ancestor of the early medieval swords of Europe.

Origins

The spatha derived from the long swords of the Celts, whose cavalry traditions Rome absorbed. Roman horsemen — who needed reach from horseback — adopted the long blade by the 1st century BC, while the infantry retained the short gladius for close-order thrusting. By the later 2nd century AD, however, auxiliary and then legionary infantry increasingly carried the spatha, worn on a baldric over the shoulder rather than on the waist belt.

Design

Typical spathae had a parallel-sided or slightly tapering blade with a long fuller and a rounded or short triangular point — in contrast to the gladius's long thrusting point — suiting them to cutting as well as thrusting. Hilts followed Roman fashion with a bulbous pommel and grip of wood, bone or ivory. Finds such as those from Nydam Mose in Denmark preserve elaborate examples carried by Germanic warriors.

Legacy

As Roman frontiers dissolved, the spatha became the general-purpose sword of Franks, Alamanni, Saxons and other peoples of the Migration Period, evolving into the Viking Age and Carolingian swords and, ultimately, the medieval knightly sword. The word itself, from Greek spathē, survives in the Spanish espada and French épée.
