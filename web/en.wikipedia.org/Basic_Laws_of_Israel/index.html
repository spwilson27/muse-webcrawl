The Basic Laws of Israel are fourteen quasi-constitutional laws of the State of Israel, intended as chapters of a future constitution. Israel does not have a single constitutional document; instead, the Basic Laws together with Supreme Court rulings function as the country's constitution. Most of the Basic Laws deal with the workings of state institutions, while two of them — Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty and Basic Law: Freedom of Occupation, both enacted in 1992 — protect civil rights.

Background

The Declaration of Independence of 1948 promised that a Constituent Assembly would prepare a constitution by 1 October 1948. However, disagreements between religious and secular parties, and the ongoing Arab–Israeli war, delayed the process. The First Knesset converted itself from a Constituent Assembly into a regular legislature, and in June 1950 adopted the Harari Resolution, under which the constitution would be built up chapter by chapter, each chapter a separate Basic Law enacted by the Knesset.

The Basic Laws

The Basic Laws enacted include: The Knesset (1958); Israel Lands (1960); The President of the State (1964); The Government (1968, replaced 2001); The State Economy (1975); The Army (1976); Jerusalem, Capital of Israel (1980); The Judiciary (1984); The State Comptroller (1988); Human Dignity and Liberty (1992); Freedom of Occupation (1994); Referendum (2014); and Israel — The Nation-State of the Jewish People (2018). Most were passed as ordinary laws by simple majorities, though several contain entrenched provisions requiring special majorities to amend.

Judicial review

In the landmark 1995 case United Mizrahi Bank v. Migdal Cooperative Village, the Supreme Court, led by President Aharon Barak, asserted the power of judicial review over ordinary legislation that conflicted with the Basic Laws on human rights — a development Barak termed a "constitutional revolution". The Court has since struck down provisions of Knesset legislation on a number of occasions, generating recurring political debate over the balance of power between the legislature and judiciary.
