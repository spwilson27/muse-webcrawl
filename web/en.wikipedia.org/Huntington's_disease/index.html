Huntington's disease (HD), also known as Huntington's chorea, is an incurable neurodegenerative disease that is mostly inherited. It typically begins between the ages of 30 and 50 and progressively affects movement, cognition, and mental health. The disease is named after the American physician George Huntington, who described it in 1872.

Signs and symptoms

Early symptoms often include subtle changes in mood, irritability, depression, and clumsiness. As the disease advances, uncoordinated, jerky body movements known as chorea become apparent, along with declining mental abilities, dementia, and behavioural problems. Physical abilities gradually worsen until coordinated movement becomes difficult; death typically occurs 15–20 years after onset, often from complications such as pneumonia or falls.

Genetics

HD is caused by an expansion of a CAG trinucleotide repeat in the huntingtin gene (HTT) on chromosome 4, producing a mutant huntingtin protein that damages neurons, particularly in the striatum. It is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern: each child of an affected parent has a 50 per cent chance of inheriting the mutation. Repeats of 40 or more CAG units cause the disease with full penetrance, and longer repeats cause earlier onset, including rare juvenile cases. Genetic testing can identify carriers before symptoms appear, raising significant ethical issues.

Treatment and research

No treatment alters the course of the disease, though medications such as tetrabenazine can suppress chorea, and multidisciplinary care manages symptoms. Gene-silencing approaches, including antisense oligonucleotides and allele-selective therapies, are an active area of clinical research.
