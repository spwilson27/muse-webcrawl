Environmental DNA

Environmental DNA or eDNA is DNA shed by organisms into their environment — in skin cells, mucus, feces, gametes, hair, and decaying tissue — that can be collected from samples of water, soil, sediment, or air without capturing or even seeing the organism itself. Analyzing eDNA has become a powerful tool in ecology, conservation biology, and environmental monitoring.

Methods

A typical study filters water or collects soil, extracts the DNA, and amplifies target sequences by PCR or analyzes the whole mixture by high-throughput sequencing (metabarcoding). Species-specific assays (often qPCR) test for a single species, while metabarcoding with universal primers for marker genes such as mitochondrial 12S, 16S, or COI can inventory whole communities from one sample. Rigorous controls are needed because eDNA methods are highly sensitive and prone to contamination.

Applications

eDNA is used to detect rare, invasive, or elusive species — for example monitoring Asian carp in North American waterways, surveying amphibians and fish, and tracking species distributions for conservation. Sedimentary ancient DNA from lake and ocean cores, and DNA preserved in permafrost, allows reconstruction of past ecosystems over thousands to (in some claims) millions of years. Airborne eDNA has even been detected around zoos and in natural settings.

Limitations

DNA degrades in the environment at rates depending on temperature, UV exposure, and microbial activity, and it can be transported away from where the organism lives, so eDNA indicates recent presence in an area rather than exact location or abundance. Inferring population size from eDNA concentration remains an active research problem.
