Abu'l-Fath Jalal-ud-din Muhammad Akbar (15 October 1542 – 27 October 1605), popularly known as Akbar the Great, was the third Mughal emperor, reigning from 1556 until his death in 1605. Succeeding his father Humayun at the age of 13, he gradually expanded the Mughal Empire to include much of the Indian subcontinent, from Afghanistan to Bengal and south to the Deccan.

Reign and expansion

Akbar came to the throne under the regency of Bairam Khan, whose victory at the Second Battle of Panipat (1556) secured the empire. Akbar annexed Malwa, Gujarat, Bengal, Kabul and Kashmir, and won the submission of the Rajput kingdoms, partly through marital alliances. His victory over Rana Pratap's forces at Haldighati (1576) became legendary. By his death the empire was one of the richest states in the world.

Administration and policy

Akbar created a centralised administration with the mansabdari system of ranked officials, land-revenue reforms administered by Todar Mal, and a policy of religious tolerance (sulh-i-kul, "peace with all"). He abolished the jizya tax on non-Muslims (1564), debated scholars of many faiths in the Ibadat Khana at Fatehpur Sikri, and patronised translations of Hindu epics into Persian. His court included the "nine gems" (navaratnas), such as the musician Tansen and the courtier Abu'l-Fazl, author of the Akbarnama.

Legacy

Akbar is widely regarded as the greatest of the Mughal emperors, laying the administrative and cultural foundations that allowed the empire to endure for two more centuries. His tomb stands at Sikandra, near Agra.
