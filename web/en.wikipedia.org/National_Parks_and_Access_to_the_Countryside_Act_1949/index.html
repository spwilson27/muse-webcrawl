National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949

The National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949 (12, 13 & 14 Geo. 6. c. 97) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom which created the National Parks system in England and Wales, the National Nature Reserves, and the framework for public rights of way and access to open countryside. It is widely regarded as the founding charter of British nature conservation and countryside access.

History

The Act followed decades of campaigning by ramblers and conservationists, including the mass trespass of Kinder Scout in 1932, and the reports of John Dower (1945) and Sir Arthur Hobhouse (1947), which recommended the creation of national parks. The post-war Labour government of Clement Attlee enacted the legislation in 1949.

Provisions

The Act established the National Parks Commission (later the Countryside Commission) and empowered county councils to designate National Parks. The first to be designated was the Peak District in 1951, followed by the Lake District, Snowdonia and Dartmoor in the same year; there are now ten national parks in England and three in Wales. The Act also provided for Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty, long-distance footpaths (the first being the Pennine Way), the definitive mapping of public rights of way, and the creation of National Nature Reserves by the Nature Conservancy.

Legacy

Although later legislation — notably the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 — has extended public access rights, the 1949 Act remains the foundation of protected landscape law in England and Wales.
