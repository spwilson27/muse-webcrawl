Row echelon form

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In linear algebra, a matrix is in row echelon form if it has the shape resulting from a Gaussian elimination.

A matrix is in row echelon form if:

  • all rows consisting entirely of zeros are grouped at the bottom of the matrix;
  • the first nonzero entry (the leading entry, or pivot) of every nonzero row appears strictly to the right of the leading entry of the row above it.

Some texts add the condition that every leading entry must be 1. A matrix satisfying this extra condition, in which every pivot column contains zeros everywhere else, is said to be in reduced row echelon form (RREF). The reduced row echelon form of a matrix is unique: two matrices that are row-equivalent have the same reduced row echelon form.

Transformation to row echelon form

By means of a finite sequence of elementary row operations — swapping two rows, multiplying a row by a nonzero scalar, and adding a multiple of one row to another — any matrix can be transformed into row echelon form. This process is known as Gaussian elimination. Continuing the process to produce the reduced form is called Gauss–Jordan elimination.

Systems of linear equations

A system of linear equations is said to be in row echelon form if its augmented matrix is in row echelon form. Such a system can be solved by back-substitution: the last nonzero row determines one unknown directly, and substituting upward determines the rest. The system has a unique solution if the number of pivots equals the number of unknowns, infinitely many solutions if there are free variables, and no solution if a row of zeros in the coefficient part is paired with a nonzero constant.
