Fiscal federalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fiscal federalism is the study of how governmental functions and financial relations are vertically structured among different levels of government in a federal system. As a subfield of public economics, it examines the assignment of taxing and spending powers between central, regional, and local governments, and the design of intergovernmental transfers that fill the gaps between them.

The assignment problem

Following the framework of Richard Musgrave, fiscal federalism distinguishes the three functions of government — allocation, distribution, and stabilization — and argues that distribution and stabilization are best handled centrally, because local redistribution is undermined by migration and local stabilization by open regional economies, while allocation of local public goods can be decentralized. Wallace Oates's decentralization theorem (1972) holds that, absent spillovers and economies of scale, provision by the lowest level of government that internalizes a good's costs and benefits is more efficient, because local governments can match services to local preferences. Charles Tiebout's model of "voting with one's feet" suggests competition among local jurisdictions can reveal preferences for public goods.

Vertical fiscal imbalance and transfers

In most federations the central government raises more revenue than it spends directly, while subnational governments spend more than they raise — a "vertical fiscal gap." It is closed by intergovernmental grants, which may be unconditional (such as Canada's equalization payments, designed to give poorer provinces comparable fiscal capacity) or conditional and matching (such as many U.S. federal grants for highways, health care, and welfare). Grant design affects incentives: the "flypaper effect" describes the empirical finding that grant money tends to stick where it lands, raising local spending more than an equivalent rise in local income.

Issues

Recurring problems in fiscal federalism include tax competition and tax exporting between jurisdictions, unfunded mandates imposed by higher levels of government, soft budget constraints when subnational governments expect central bailouts, and horizontal imbalances between rich and poor regions. The field, founded as a distinct discipline by Oates's 1972 book Fiscal Federalism, now also studies federalism in the European Union and in developing countries undergoing decentralization.
