Niccolò Paganini

Niccolò (or Nicolò) Paganini (27 October 1782 – 27 May 1840) was an Italian violinist and composer. He was the most celebrated violin virtuoso of his time, and left his mark as one of the pillars of modern violin technique. His 24 Caprices for Solo Violin Op. 1 are among the best known of his compositions and remain a benchmark of violin virtuosity.

Life

Born in Genoa, Paganini was driven by his father to practise for punishing hours as a child prodigy. After study in Parma, he toured Italy to acclaim, served as a court musician in Lucca, and from 1828 embarked on international tours through Austria, Germany, France and England that made him wealthy and famous across Europe.

Legend and technique

Paganini's astonishing technique, showmanship, and gaunt appearance fed rumours that he had sold his soul to the Devil. He pioneered or popularised techniques including left-hand pizzicato, harmonics, scordatura and playing entire works on a single string — most famously variations on the G string. His performances inspired composers including Liszt, Schumann, Brahms and Rachmaninoff, whose Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini draws on the 24th Caprice.

Compositions and instruments

Besides the Caprices, he wrote six violin concertos, chamber music and guitar works. He owned numerous fine instruments, most famously the 1743 Guarneri del Gesù violin known as Il Cannone, bequeathed to the city of Genoa, where it is still preserved and occasionally played.

Death

Chronically ill for much of his life, Paganini died in Nice in 1840. Because of the diabolical legends, church authorities initially refused him burial in consecrated ground; his remains were eventually interred in Parma in 1876.
