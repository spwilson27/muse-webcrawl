Galaxy formation and evolution

The study of galaxy formation and evolution is concerned with the processes that formed a heterogeneous universe from a homogeneous beginning, the formation of the first galaxies, the way galaxies change over time, and the processes that have generated the variety of structures observed in nearby galaxies. Galaxy formation is hypothesized to occur from structure formation theories, as a result of tiny quantum fluctuations in the aftermath of the Big Bang.

In the standard ΛCDM model, small dark-matter halos collapsed first in the early universe, gathering gas that cooled, formed stars, and merged hierarchically into ever larger galaxies. Observations from the Hubble and James Webb space telescopes now probe galaxies within a few hundred million years of the Big Bang.

Formation of the first galaxies

After recombination, about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, the universe was a nearly uniform gas of hydrogen and helium imprinted with slight density variations (seen today in the cosmic microwave background). Gravity amplified these into dark-matter halos; gas falling into the smallest halos cooled by atomic hydrogen emission, and the first (Population III) stars ignited roughly 100–300 million years after the Big Bang, ending the cosmic dark ages and beginning reionization.

Evolution

Galaxies grow by star formation fuelled by accreting gas and by mergers. Interactions trigger starbursts and feed central supermassive black holes, whose feedback — winds, jets, and radiation from active galactic nuclei — can expel or heat gas and quench further star formation, turning blue star-forming disks into red, quiescent ellipticals. The observed "main sequence" of star-forming galaxies, the bimodality of galaxy colours, and the correlation between black hole mass and bulge properties are all explained in broad outline by this interplay of gas accretion, star formation, mergers, and feedback, though details such as the early appearance of massive galaxies seen by JWST remain active research questions.

Classification

Edwin Hubble's tuning-fork diagram divides galaxies into ellipticals, spirals, barred spirals, and irregulars; modern surveys relate this morphology to mass, environment, and star-formation history. Galaxies reside in groups and clusters threaded along the filaments of the cosmic web.
