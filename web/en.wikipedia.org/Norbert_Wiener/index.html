Norbert Wiener (November 26, 1894 – March 18, 1964) was an American computer scientist, mathematician and philosopher. He became a professor of mathematics at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). A child prodigy, Wiener graduated from Tufts College at age 14 and received his PhD from Harvard at 18. He is widely considered the originator of cybernetics, the study of control and communication in animals and machines, a field he defined in his 1948 book Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine.

Early life and education

Wiener was born in Columbia, Missouri, the son of Leo Wiener, a professor of Slavic languages. Educated at home and in brief stints at public school, he entered Tufts at age 11 and completed his doctorate at Harvard with a dissertation on mathematical logic. He then studied in Europe with Bertrand Russell and G. H. Hardy at Cambridge and with David Hilbert at Göttingen, an experience cut short by the First World War.

Work

During the 1920s and 1930s Wiener made fundamental contributions to harmonic analysis, Tauberian theorems, probability theory (including the rigorous theory of Brownian motion, the Wiener process) and ergodic theory. During the Second World War he worked on anti-aircraft fire control, developing statistical prediction theory and the Wiener filter, which led him to think generally about feedback, communication and information — themes that converged in cybernetics.

After the war, Wiener became a prominent public intellectual on the social consequences of automation, notably in The Human Use of Human Beings (1950), and he refused further military work, writing an open letter, "A Scientist Rebels" (1947). His ideas influenced computing, control engineering, systems theory and cognitive science.

Selected works

Cybernetics (1948); Extrapolation, Interpolation, and Smoothing of Stationary Time Series (1949); The Human Use of Human Beings (1950); I Am a Mathematician (1956, autobiography); God & Golem, Inc. (1964).
