Cerdanya (Catalan: Cerdanya; French: Cerdagne; Spanish: Cerdaña) is a natural comarca and historical region of the eastern Pyrenees, divided between Spain and France. The Cerdanya valley is a wide, high plain — one of the largest valley floors in the Pyrenees — watered by the upper course of the Segre River, and is noted for its sunny climate, with one of the highest numbers of sunshine hours in Europe.

History

The valley was inhabited in Roman times by the Ceretani people, from whom the region takes its name, and was later a county of the Spanish March under the Counts of Barcelona. The Counts of Cerdanya built the monastery of Sant Martí del Canigó and founded towns such as Llívia and Puigcerdà. Under the Treaty of the Pyrenees of 1659, which settled the Franco-Spanish War, the northern part of Cerdanya passed to France, dividing the historical county. The Spanish town of Llívia remains an exclave entirely surrounded by French territory, a curiosity preserved because the treaty ceded "villages" to France while Llívia held the status of a town.

Geography and economy

The valley lies at about 1,000–1,200 metres above sea level, ringed by peaks approaching 3,000 metres, including Carlit and Puigmal. Agriculture, livestock and tourism dominate the economy; the area is a popular destination for skiing (Font-Romeu, La Molina, Masella), hiking and second homes. Near Font-Romeu stands the Odeillo solar furnace, one of the largest solar furnaces in the world, a legacy of the region's exceptional sunshine. The division between the Spanish Baixa Cerdanya ("Lower Cerdanya") and the French Alta Cerdanya ("Upper Cerdanya") is geographical — the French part is actually the higher, upper valley.
