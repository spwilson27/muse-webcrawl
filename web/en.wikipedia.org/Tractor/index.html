Tractor

A tractor is an engineering vehicle specifically designed to deliver a high tractive effort (or torque) at slow speeds, for the purposes of hauling a trailer or machinery, most commonly in agriculture but also in construction and other industries. The word derives from the Latin trahere, "to pull". Most tractors also provide power to implements through a power take-off (PTO) shaft and hydraulic systems.

History

Steam traction engines were used on farms from the mid-nineteenth century. Internal-combustion tractors followed: the first commercially successful lightweight gasoline tractor was built by John Froelich in 1892, and firms such as Hart-Parr, Fordson (from 1917), and John Deere made tractors affordable and ubiquitous in the early twentieth century. Harry Ferguson's three-point linkage, introduced in the 1930s, became the standard method of attaching implements and dramatically improved safety and control. The tractor was a key driver of agricultural mechanization, replacing draft animals and greatly raising farm productivity.

Design

The classic farm tractor has two large driven wheels at the rear and smaller steerable wheels at the front; many modern machines have four-wheel drive with equal-sized wheels. Standard features include a drawbar and hitch, a PTO rotating at standardized speeds (540 or 1000 rpm), hydraulic remotes, and increasingly enclosed cabs with air conditioning, electronic controls, and GPS-based auto-steering for precision agriculture. Specialized types include row-crop, orchard, crawler (tracked), and compact utility tractors, as well as road tractors ("semi-tractors") that pull semi-trailers in trucking.
