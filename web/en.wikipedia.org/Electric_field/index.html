Electric field

An electric field is the physical field that surrounds electrically charged particles. It exerts a force on all other charged particles in the field, attracting them if the charges are opposite and repelling them if the charges are the same. The electric field is defined as the force per unit charge, measured in newtons per coulomb (equivalently volts per metre).

Electric field lines point from positive to negative charges.

Definition

If a small test charge q experiences a force F at a point in space, the electric field at that point is E = F/q. For a point charge Q, Coulomb's law gives a field of magnitude E = (1/4πε₀) · Q/r², directed radially. Fields from multiple charges add vectorially by the superposition principle.

Sources

Electric fields arise from electric charges and from time-varying magnetic fields, as described by Faraday's law of induction. Together with the magnetic field, the electric field forms the electromagnetic field, governed by Maxwell's equations; changes in the fields propagate as electromagnetic waves at the speed of light.

Field lines and potential

Field lines provide a visual representation: they begin on positive charges, end on negative charges, and their density indicates field strength. The electric field is the negative gradient of the electric potential, so it points from regions of high potential to low. Static electric fields are described by electrostatics and are central to the working of capacitors, cathode-ray tubes, and countless electronic devices.
