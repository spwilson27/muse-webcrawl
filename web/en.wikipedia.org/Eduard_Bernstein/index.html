Eduard Bernstein (6 January 1850 – 18 December 1932) was a German social-democratic Marxist theorist and politician. A member of the Social Democratic Party of Germany (SPD), Bernstein had held close association to Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, but he saw flaws in Marxist thinking and began to criticize views held by Marxism when he investigated and challenged the Marxist materialist theory of history. He rejected significant parts of Marxist theory that were based upon Hegelian metaphysics and he rejected the Marxist idea of imminent and inevitable working-class revolution in favour of gradual reform through parliamentary democracy.

Life

Born in Berlin to a Jewish family, Bernstein worked as a bank clerk before joining the socialist movement. Driven into exile by Bismarck's Anti-Socialist Laws, he edited the party newspaper Der Sozialdemokrat in Zurich and London, where he became close to Engels and was named literary executor of Marx and Engels. Exposure to the British Fabian Society influenced his thinking during the London years.

Revisionism

In articles of 1896–98 and his book Evolutionary Socialism (1899), Bernstein argued that capitalism was not collapsing as predicted, that living standards were rising, and that socialism should be achieved through trade unions, cooperatives, and democratic legislation rather than revolution — summarized in his famous maxim that the movement is everything, the final goal nothing. His "revisionism" was formally condemned by the SPD but in practice described what the party increasingly did; Rosa Luxemburg's Reform or Revolution (1899) was the classic orthodox reply. Bernstein sat in the Reichstag for most years from 1902 to 1928 and co-founded the anti-war USPD split during the First World War before rejoining the SPD.

Legacy

Bernstein is regarded as a founding thinker of modern social democracy and democratic socialism, whose influence extended to postwar European socialist parties.
