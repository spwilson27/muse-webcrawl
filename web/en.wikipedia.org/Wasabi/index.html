Wasabi (Japanese: ワサビ, わさび, or 山葵) or Japanese horseradish is a plant of the family Brassicaceae, Eutrema japonicum, whose rhizome is grated to produce a pungent green paste traditionally served with sushi and sashimi. Its sharp heat comes from allyl isothiocyanate, which stimulates the nasal passages rather than the tongue, so the sensation is brief and vapour-like compared with the heat of chilli peppers.

Cultivation

Wasabi grows naturally along cool mountain streams in Japan. Cultivated wasabi is typically grown in flooded gravel beds (sawa wasabi) fed by clear running water at temperatures around 8–20 °C, conditions that are difficult to reproduce commercially; field-grown (oka) wasabi is considered inferior for paste but provides leaves and stems used in other products. Because of its exacting requirements, fresh wasabi is rare and expensive even in Japan. Major growing areas include the Izu Peninsula in Shizuoka Prefecture, Nagano and Iwate.

Use and imitation

Traditionally the rhizome is grated on a sharkskin grater (oroshigane) just before serving, since the flavour compounds are volatile and fade within about fifteen minutes. Most "wasabi" served outside Japan — and much of that served in Japan — is an imitation made from horseradish, mustard and green food colouring, sold as powder or paste. Wasabi also exhibits antimicrobial properties, one reason it was historically paired with raw fish. The leaves and stems are used in pickles such as wasabi-zuke, and modern products include wasabi-flavoured snacks, peas and mayonnaise.
