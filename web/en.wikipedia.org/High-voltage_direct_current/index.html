High-voltage direct current (HVDC) is an electric power transmission system that uses direct current (DC) for the bulk transmission of electrical power, in contrast with the more common alternating current (AC) systems. Power is converted from AC to DC at a converter station, transmitted over a line or cable, and converted back to AC at the far end. Most HVDC links are used to interconnect separate AC networks or to transmit power over distances where DC is more economical.

Advantages and uses

For long overhead lines, HVDC becomes cheaper than AC beyond a break-even distance of roughly 500–800 km, because a DC line needs fewer conductors, narrower towers and suffers no reactive-power or skin-effect losses. For submarine cables the break-even distance is much shorter (around 50 km), since AC cables generate large charging currents; hence nearly all long undersea power links are HVDC. HVDC also connects grids that are asynchronous or run at different frequencies (for example Japan's 50/60 Hz regions), and its power flow can be precisely controlled, improving grid stability. "Back-to-back" stations connect neighbouring grids with no line at all.

Configurations and technology

Links may be monopolar (one conductor, with earth or a metallic return) or bipolar (two conductors at opposite polarity, able to run at half power if one pole fails). Classic converters use thyristors in line-commutated bridges requiring strong AC networks; newer voltage-sourced converters (VSC) based on IGBTs can feed weak or passive networks, control reactive power independently, and are standard for offshore wind connections. The world's most powerful links are China's ultra-high-voltage DC lines, reaching ±1,100 kV and capacities around 12 GW over distances exceeding 3,000 km.

History

Early DC transmission was demonstrated in the 1880s (the Miesbach–Munich line, and René Thury's systems), but AC won the "war of the currents" because transformers made voltage conversion easy. Practical HVDC awaited high-power electronic valves: the first modern commercial link, using mercury-arc valves, connected mainland Sweden to Gotland in 1954. Thyristor converters from the 1970s and VSC technology from the late 1990s have driven steady expansion, and multi-terminal DC grids are an active area of development.
