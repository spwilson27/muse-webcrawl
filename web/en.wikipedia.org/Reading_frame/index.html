Reading frame

The three forward reading frames of a nucleotide sequence

In molecular biology, a reading frame is a way of dividing the sequence of nucleotides in a nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) molecule into a set of consecutive, non-overlapping triplets called codons. Where these triplets equate to amino acids in a translated protein, a reading frame is a sequence that can be translated without interruption by a stop codon.

Frames and open reading frames

A given double-stranded DNA sequence can be read in six frames: three in the forward direction on one strand and three on the reverse strand. An open reading frame (ORF) is a reading frame that begins with a start codon (usually ATG in DNA, AUG in mRNA) and continues without a stop codon for a length typical of a protein-coding gene. Identifying ORFs is a basic step in gene prediction from genomic sequence.

Frameshift mutations

Insertions or deletions of nucleotides whose number is not a multiple of three cause a frameshift mutation, changing every codon downstream of the mutation and usually producing a non-functional protein. Such mutations underlie genetic diseases including some forms of Duchenne muscular dystrophy and Tay–Sachs disease.
