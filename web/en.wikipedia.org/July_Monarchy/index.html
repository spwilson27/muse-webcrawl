The July Monarchy (French: Monarchie de Juillet) was a liberal constitutional monarchy in France under Louis Philippe I, starting on 9 August 1830, following the July Revolution of the same year, and ending with the French Revolution of 1848. It marks a change of dynasty from the main Bourbon line to the cadet Orléans branch, and the replacement of the hereditary right of kings by popular sovereignty.

History

The July Revolution of 1830, known as the Trois Glorieuses ("Three Glorious Days"), toppled King Charles X after his reactionary July Ordinances suspended press freedom and dissolved the newly elected Chamber. Louis Philippe, Duke of Orléans, was proclaimed lieutenant general of the kingdom and then king "of the French" rather than "of France," signalling that his authority derived from the people.

The regime rested on an alliance with the upper bourgeoisie; the franchise, though doubled, still covered only about 170,000 voters. The king's domestic policy was dominated by the conservative resistance of figures such as Casimir Périer and later François Guizot, whose resistance party favoured the status quo, against the "movement" party that sought further reform.

Economy and society

The period saw industrial expansion, railway construction and the growth of banking under houses such as Rothschild and Laffitte. Yet the regime remained unpopular with both Legitimist royalists and republicans, surviving numerous assassination attempts, including Giuseppe Fieschi's "infernal machine" of 1835.

Fall

Economic crisis in 1846–47 and the banning of reformist banquets led to the February 1848 revolution. Louis Philippe abdicated on 24 February 1848 and fled to England, and the Second Republic was proclaimed.
