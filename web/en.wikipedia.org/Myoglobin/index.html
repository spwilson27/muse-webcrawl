Myoglobin

SymbolMB
TypeGlobin protein, single haem group
LocationMuscle tissue of vertebrates
FunctionOxygen storage and facilitated diffusion in muscle
Molecular massAbout 17 kDa; 153 amino acids

Myoglobin is an iron- and oxygen-binding protein found in the muscle tissue of vertebrates. It consists of a single polypeptide chain of about 153 amino acids folded around one haem group, whose iron atom reversibly binds a single oxygen molecule. Myoglobin stores oxygen within muscle and releases it when oxygen levels fall during contraction, and it helps shuttle oxygen from the cell surface to the mitochondria. Its high affinity for oxygen — higher than that of haemoglobin — lets it draw oxygen from the blood and hold it as a reserve.

The three-dimensional structure of myoglobin: eight α-helices cradling the haem group.

Structure and history

Myoglobin was the first protein to have its three-dimensional structure determined by X-ray crystallography, a feat accomplished by John Kendrew and colleagues in 1958 using sperm whale myoglobin; Kendrew shared the 1962 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Max Perutz, who solved haemoglobin. The molecule folds into eight α-helices forming a pocket for the haem, with the iron coordinated by a proximal histidine. Its oxygen-binding curve is hyperbolic rather than sigmoidal, reflecting the absence of the cooperative binding found in tetrameric haemoglobin.

Biology

Myoglobin concentration is especially high in the muscles of diving mammals such as whales and seals, giving their flesh its dark red colour and supporting long dives; it also colours red meat, where its oxidation states determine fresh (bright red oxymyoglobin), cooked (brown metmyoglobin) appearances. When muscle is severely damaged, as in crush injuries and rhabdomyolysis, myoglobin released into the blood can obstruct the kidneys and cause acute kidney injury; its presence in urine (myoglobinuria) turns it dark brown.
