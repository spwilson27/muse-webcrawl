Focus group

A focus group is a qualitative research method in which a small group of people — typically six to ten — are brought together to discuss a product, service, concept, advertisement, idea or issue, guided by a moderator. Their perceptions, opinions, beliefs and attitudes are explored through group interaction, and the discussion is recorded and analysed for themes.

History

The method grew out of "focused interviews" developed in the 1940s by sociologist Robert K. Merton and colleagues, who studied audience responses to radio broadcasts and wartime propaganda. Market researchers adopted the technique widely from the 1950s onward, and it became a staple of commercial research, political campaigning and product development.

Method

Participants are usually recruited to share relevant characteristics. A trained moderator follows a discussion guide, encourages quieter members to speak, and probes for reasons behind opinions. Sessions commonly last 60 to 90 minutes and may be observed by clients behind a one-way mirror or by video link. Online focus groups and bulletin-board variants have become common.

Criticism

Focus groups cannot support statistical generalisation, and results can be distorted by dominant personalities, groupthink and social-desirability bias. Critics also note that stated preferences in a group setting may not predict actual behaviour; for these reasons focus groups are usually combined with surveys, interviews or observational methods.
