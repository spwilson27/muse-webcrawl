The Umayyad invasion of Gaul followed the Umayyad conquest of Hispania (711–718) and was part of the wider expansion of the Umayyad Caliphate in the early 8th century. Beginning in 719 with the capture of Narbonne, Muslim forces from al-Andalus established control over Septimania, the old Visigothic province in southwestern Gaul, and launched repeated raids northward into Aquitaine and Burgundy. The invasion is best known for the Battle of Tours (also called the Battle of Poitiers) in 732, where the Frankish leader Charles Martel defeated a large army under Abd al-Rahman al-Ghafiqi, who was killed in the battle.

Background

After conquering the Visigothic Kingdom, Umayyad commanders crossed the Pyrenees. Narbonne fell in 719 and became the capital of a Muslim province. From there, campaigns were launched against Toulouse (besieged in 721, where the governor al-Samh ibn Malik was killed by Duke Odo of Aquitaine), Bordeaux, and Autun; Muslim raiders reached as far north as Sens and the Loire valley.

Battle of Tours

In 732 Abd al-Rahman al-Ghafiqi, governor of al-Andalus, defeated Odo at the Battle of Bordeaux and sacked Bordeaux, then marched north toward Tours, attracted by the wealth of the Abbey of Saint Martin. Between Poitiers and Tours his army met the Franks under Charles Martel. In the ensuing battle the Muslim army was defeated and Abd al-Rahman killed. The battle became a landmark in European historiography, traditionally portrayed as the moment that halted the Muslim advance into Western Europe, though modern historians emphasise that the campaigns were largely large-scale raids and that logistical and internal difficulties in al-Andalus also played a role.

Later campaigns and Frankish reconquest

Charles Martel campaigned south in 737, winning the Battle of the River Berre and destroying Avignon and Narbonne's relieving forces, though Narbonne itself held out. Muslim control of Septimania lasted until 759, when Pepin the Short completed the conquest of Narbonne after a seven-year siege, ending Umayyad rule north of the Pyrenees. Raiding across the frontier continued for decades afterwards.

Legacy

The campaigns set the Pyrenees as the effective frontier between al-Andalus and the Frankish world, later organised by Charlemagne as the Spanish March. The Battle of Tours acquired an outsized place in later European memory, celebrated by chroniclers and by historians such as Edward Gibbon as a decisive turning point.
