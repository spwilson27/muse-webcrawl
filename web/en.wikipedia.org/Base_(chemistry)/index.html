Base (chemistry)


Sodium hydroxide, a common base, turns universal indicator blue-violet.

In chemistry, a base is a substance that can accept hydrogen ions (protons) or, more generally, donate a pair of valence electrons. In aqueous solution, bases feel slippery, taste bitter, turn red litmus paper blue, and react with acids to form salts and water. Common examples include sodium hydroxide (caustic soda), potassium hydroxide, ammonia and calcium hydroxide (slaked lime). A base dissolved in water is called an alkali, and such solutions have a pH greater than 7 at 25 °C.

Definitions

Several definitions have been proposed as acid–base theory developed. The Arrhenius definition (1884) identifies a base as a substance that increases the concentration of hydroxide ions (OH−) in aqueous solution. The Brønsted–Lowry definition (1923) generalises this: a base is a proton acceptor, so ammonia is a base because it accepts a proton from water to form ammonium and hydroxide ions. The still more general Lewis definition (1923) defines a base as an electron-pair donor, covering substances such as amines and ethers that need not contain hydroxide or even accept protons in the Brønsted sense. In each acid–base reaction, a base and its conjugate acid form a conjugate pair differing by a single proton.

Properties and strength

The strength of a base is measured by its base dissociation constant, Kb, or equivalently the pKa of its conjugate acid. Strong bases, such as the hydroxides of the alkali metals and heavier alkaline-earth metals, dissociate essentially completely in water; weak bases such as ammonia and most organic amines dissociate only slightly. Extremely strong bases, including organolithium reagents and metal hydrides, are called superbases in organic synthesis contexts and react violently with water. Concentrated bases are corrosive: hydroxides saponify fats — the reaction at the heart of traditional soap-making — and attack proteins, which is why caustic soda is used as a drain cleaner and why base burns are dangerous.

Uses

Bases are indispensable in industry and daily life. Sodium hydroxide is used in soap and paper manufacture, alumina refining and chemical production; calcium hydroxide in construction (mortar, lime wash), agriculture (liming acidic soils) and food processing; ammonia in fertilisers and cleaning products; sodium bicarbonate (baking soda), a mild base, in cooking and antacid medicines. In the laboratory and in organic synthesis, bases neutralise acids, drive eliminations and deprotonations, and serve as catalysts. In biology, nitrogenous bases — adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine and uracil — are the information-carrying components of DNA and RNA, an unrelated sense of the word deriving from their role as the "base" constituents of nucleotides.

Neutralisation and titration

When a base reacts with an acid, the products are a salt and, where hydroxide is involved, water; this neutralisation reaction is exothermic. The stoichiometry of such reactions is exploited in acid–base titrations, where a solution of known concentration is used to determine the concentration of an unknown base or acid, with indicators such as phenolphthalein — colourless in acid, pink in base — signalling the endpoint.
