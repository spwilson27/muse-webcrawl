Acid


Common laboratory acids.

An acid is a molecule or ion capable of either donating a proton (i.e. hydrogen ion, H+), known as a Brønsted–Lowry acid, or forming a covalent bond with an electron pair, known as a Lewis acid. The first category of acids is the proton donors, or Brønsted–Lowry acids. In the special case of aqueous solutions, proton donors form the hydronium ion H3O+ and are known as Arrhenius acids.

Definitions

The Arrhenius definition describes an acid as a substance that increases the concentration of hydrogen ions in aqueous solution. The Brønsted–Lowry definition, proposed independently in 1923, describes an acid as a proton donor, and a base as a proton acceptor, united in conjugate acid–base pairs. The Lewis definition is broader: an acid is an electron-pair acceptor, so species without hydrogen, such as boron trifluoride or metal ions, qualify as acids.

Properties

Acids commonly taste sour, turn blue litmus red, react with bases to form salts and water, and react with many metals to release hydrogen gas. Strength is measured by the acid dissociation constant (pKa): strong acids such as hydrochloric and sulfuric acid dissociate nearly completely in water, while weak acids such as acetic acid dissociate only partially. The pH scale quantifies acidity in solution.

Examples and applications

Important acids include sulfuric acid (industrial manufacture), nitric acid (fertilizers and explosives), citric acid (in citrus fruits and food), and amino acids (the building blocks of proteins). Nucleic acids such as DNA and RNA, and fatty acids, are essential in biology.
