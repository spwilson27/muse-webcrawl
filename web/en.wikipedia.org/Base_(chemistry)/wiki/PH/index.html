pH


The pH scale, from acidic (low) to alkaline (high).

In chemistry, pH is a scale used to specify the acidity or basicity of an aqueous solution. Acidic solutions (with higher concentrations of hydrogen ions) are measured to have lower pH values than basic or alkaline solutions. The scale is logarithmic and inversely indicates the activity of hydrogen ions in the solution: pH = −log10(aH+).

History

The concept of pH was introduced by Danish chemist Søren Peder Lauritz Sørensen at the Carlsberg Laboratory in 1909 and was later revised to the modern pH in 1924 to accommodate definitions in terms of electrochemical cells. At 25 °C, pure water has a pH of 7 and is neutral; values below 7 are acidic and above 7 are alkaline, though strong acids and bases can produce values outside the nominal 0–14 range.

Measurement

pH is measured with indicators (dyes such as litmus or universal indicator paper) that change color with pH, or more accurately with a pH meter using a glass electrode. Many natural systems are sensitive to pH: blood is tightly buffered near 7.4, soils influence plant growth, and ocean acidification refers to the decline in seawater pH from absorbed carbon dioxide.

Related quantities

The analogous quantity pOH measures hydroxide ion concentration, and at 25 °C pH + pOH = 14. The pKa scale applies the same logarithmic idea to acid dissociation constants.
