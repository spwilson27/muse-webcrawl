High-altitude balloon

High-altitude balloons or stratostats, also known as high-altitude research balloons, are crewed or uncrewed balloons, usually filled with helium or hydrogen, that are released into the stratosphere, generally attaining between 18 and 37 km (11 and 23 mi; 59,000 and 121,000 ft) above sea level. In 2013, a balloon named BS 13-08 reached a record altitude of 53.7 km (33.4 mi; 176,000 ft).

History

Scientific ballooning dates to the 19th century; Auguste Piccard reached the stratosphere in a pressurised gondola in 1931. During the Cold War, the United States flew high-altitude balloons for reconnaissance (Project Mogul, Project Genetrix) and crewed altitude records were set by projects Manhigh and Excelsior, culminating in Joseph Kittinger's 1960 jump from 31 km. In 2012, Felix Baumgartner's Red Bull Stratos jump from about 39 km set new records, surpassed in 2014 by Alan Eustace's jump from 41.4 km.

Uses

The most common application is weather forecasting: weather balloons carrying radiosondes are launched twice daily from hundreds of sites worldwide. Other uses include atmospheric and astronomical research (balloon-borne telescopes such as BLAST), communications relay, near-space tourism, and amateur "pico balloon" and student experiments carrying cameras and GPS trackers. NASA's scientific ballooning program flies zero-pressure and superpressure balloons, including long-duration flights from Antarctica and the ULDB (ultra-long-duration balloon) concept.

Design

Zero-pressure balloons vent gas through ducts to equalise pressure and typically fly for hours to days; superpressure balloons are sealed, maintain a constant volume, and can fly for weeks or months. Latex weather balloons expand until they burst, after which the payload descends by parachute.
