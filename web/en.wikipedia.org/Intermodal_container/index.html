An intermodal container, often called a shipping container, is a large standardized container designed and built for intermodal freight transport — meaning it can be used across different modes of transport, from ship to rail to truck, without unloading and reloading its cargo. Containers are manufactured according to ISO specifications, principally in 20-foot and 40-foot lengths, and are counted in twenty-foot equivalent units (TEU).

History

Containerization is credited to American trucking entrepreneur Malcolm McLean, who in 1956 loaded the converted tanker Ideal X with 58 containers from Newark to Houston, slashing the cost of loading cargo from dollars to cents per ton. Sea-Land and Matson adopted containers in the late 1950s, and ISO standards for sizes and corner fittings were agreed in the 1960s. Dedicated container ships, ports with gantry cranes, and unit trains followed, transforming world trade by making transport costs so low that global supply chains became economical.

Design

Containers are built of corrugated weathering (Corten) steel with cast-steel corner fittings that allow them to be lifted by cranes and locked together or to vehicles with twistlocks. Types include dry freight boxes, refrigerated "reefers" with integral cooling units, open-top and flat-rack units for oversized loads, and tank containers for liquids. Each container carries a unique reporting mark and serial number under the ISO 6346 system.

Impact

By standardizing the unit of cargo, containers reduced theft, damage, and port labour, shortened ship turnaround from days to hours, and are widely credited with boosting global trade more than many trade agreements. Tens of millions of containers are in service, and the largest container ships now carry more than 24,000 TEU. Retired containers are also reused as modular building units.
