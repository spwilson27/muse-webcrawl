Harmonic oscillator

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In classical mechanics, a harmonic oscillator is a system that, when displaced from its equilibrium position, experiences a restoring force F proportional to the displacement x: F = −kx, where k is a positive constant. If the only force acting is this restoring force, the system is a simple harmonic oscillator and undergoes simple harmonic motion: sinusoidal oscillations about equilibrium at constant amplitude and a single constant frequency (independent of amplitude), described by the equation x(t) = A cos(ωt − φ), with ω = √(k/m).

Examples

Examples include a mass on a spring, a pendulum swinging through small angles, and an LC electrical circuit, in which charge plays the role of displacement. The harmonic oscillator model is fundamental because any system near a stable equilibrium behaves, to first approximation, like one: molecular vibrations, acoustic waves, and the quantum harmonic oscillator — whose energy levels are evenly spaced — are all treated with the same mathematics.

Damped and driven oscillators

Real oscillators lose energy: adding a friction force proportional to velocity gives the damped harmonic oscillator, which may be underdamped, critically damped or overdamped. Adding a periodic external force gives the driven oscillator, which exhibits resonance when driven near its natural frequency, with potentially large amplitudes limited only by damping.
