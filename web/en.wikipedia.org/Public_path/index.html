Public path

A public path (or public footpath) is a path over which the public has a legally protected right of passage. In England and Wales, public paths form the bulk of the rights of way network and fall into several legal categories, most importantly public footpaths (for pedestrians only), public bridleways (for pedestrians, horse riders, and cyclists), and byways open to all traffic.

Legal status

Public paths are recorded on "definitive maps" maintained by local surveying authorities under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981; once a way is shown on the definitive map, that is conclusive evidence of the public right. Rights can arise from statute, from express dedication by a landowner, or from presumed dedication after long public use — historically twenty years — under the Highways Act 1980. Landowners must keep paths unobstructed, maintain stiles and gates, and reinstate paths across ploughed fields. The Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 clarified procedures and set in motion the recording of historic paths before a cut-off date, a deadline later moved and then effectively removed in England by government decision.

Other jurisdictions

Scotland has a distinct system of recorded rights of way supplemented by the general statutory right of responsible access under the Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003. Many other countries recognize comparable institutions — such as the German Betretungsrecht and the Scandinavian allemansrätten — though the English model, in which specific routes are legally registered, is unusual in its detail. In the United States, public paths typically exist as easements or as trails on public land rather than as a registered network crossing private land.
