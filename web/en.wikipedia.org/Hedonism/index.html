Hedonism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hedonism is a family of views about pleasure and well-being holding that pleasure is the highest good and that a life should be organised around maximising pleasure and minimising pain. In ethics, axiological hedonism claims that pleasure is the only thing that is intrinsically good and pain the only thing intrinsically bad. Psychological hedonism is the descriptive thesis that human beings are in fact motivated solely by the pursuit of pleasure and avoidance of pain.

Ancient hedonism

The earliest systematic hedonists were the Cyrenaics, followers of Aristippus of Cyrene (c. 435–356 BCE), a student of Socrates. The Cyrenaics held that immediate bodily pleasures are more intense and more certain than mental or future pleasures, and advised seizing present enjoyment.

Epicurus (341–270 BCE) developed a subtler version. For Epicurus the goal was not indulgence but ataraxia (freedom from anxiety) and aponia (absence of pain), achieved through moderation, friendship, and philosophy. Epicureans classified desires as natural and necessary, natural but unnecessary, or vain, and taught that simple pleasures are easier to secure and less likely to produce painful consequences.

Utilitarianism

Jeremy Bentham built his moral philosophy of utilitarianism on a hedonistic foundation, proposing a "felicific calculus" by which pleasures and pains could be weighed by intensity, duration, certainty, and extent. John Stuart Mill modified Bentham's view by distinguishing higher (intellectual) from lower (sensual) pleasures, arguing that "it is better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied".

Criticisms

Critics have raised several objections. Robert Nozick's experience machine thought experiment asks whether people would choose a machine guaranteeing a lifetime of pleasurable experiences over real life; many would not, suggesting that truth, agency, and accomplishment matter beyond pleasure. G. E. Moore attacked the identification of goodness with pleasure as the naturalistic fallacy. Earlier critics noted the paradox of hedonism: pleasure is often most reliably attained when one aims at something other than pleasure itself.

Modern usage

In everyday speech, "hedonism" often connotes self-indulgent pursuit of sensual pleasure without regard for consequences, a sense closer to the Cyrenaic position than to Epicurus's teaching. Psychologists study related ideas such as the hedonic treadmill — the tendency for people to return to a baseline level of happiness despite major positive or negative life events.
