Workweek and weekend

The workweek is the span of days in a seven-day week devoted to work, and the weekend is the period of rest days, conventionally Saturday and Sunday in most Western and many other countries. Working time, and the length of the week, have been central issues in labour history.

History

The seven-day week derives from ancient Near Eastern and Jewish practice, with the Sabbath as a day of rest; Christian observance shifted the rest day to Sunday, and Islam marks Friday for congregational prayer. In industrialising Britain and America, factory workers commonly laboured six long days. Campaigns for shorter hours won the eight-hour day in many industries by the early 20th century, and the two-day weekend spread after Henry Ford adopted a five-day, 40-hour week in 1926; the US Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 entrenched the 40-hour standard. Many Muslim-majority countries historically used a Friday–Saturday weekend, though several, including the United Arab Emirates in 2022, have moved toward Saturday–Sunday to align with global markets.

Modern developments

Statutory maximum hours vary widely — the European Union averages a 48-hour cap including overtime, while some countries have no general statutory limit. Average actual hours have fallen in most rich countries over the past century, though professional and gig-economy work often blurs the boundary. Recent experiments with a four-day week, and the spread of remote work, continue to reshape the division between workweek and weekend. See also work–life balance.
