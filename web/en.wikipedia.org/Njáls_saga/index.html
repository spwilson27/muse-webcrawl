Njáls saga

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Njáls saga (modern Icelandic pronunciation: [ˈnjauls ˌsaːɣa]), also known as The Story of Burnt Njáll, is a thirteenth-century Icelandic saga, generally regarded as the greatest of the Sagas of Icelanders. Written by an unknown author, probably in the south of Iceland between about 1275 and 1290, it spans events from roughly 960 to 1020 and weaves together two great feud narratives: the career of the heroic Gunnar of Hlíðarendi and the fate of the wise lawyer Njáll Þorgeirsson, who is burned to death in his house.

Synopsis

The first half of the saga follows the friendship of Gunnar Hámundarson, Iceland's most accomplished warrior, and Njáll, a beardless sage unmatched in knowledge of the law. A feud kindled by their wives — the proud Hallgerðr and the equally formidable Bergþóra — escalates through killings of servants and kinsmen until Gunnar, outlawed for slayings, is killed defending his home at Hlíðarendi after his bowstring breaks and Hallgerðr refuses him a lock of her hair to restring it. The second half concerns the conversion of Iceland to Christianity (which the saga treats as a turning point), the career of Njáll's foster-son and the feud that culminates in the burning of Njáll, Bergþóra, and their sons at Bergþórshvoll around 1011. Njáll's son-in-law Kári survives the fire and pursues vengeance across the Norse world, to Orkney, Wales, and Rome, before a final reconciliation.

Themes and style

The saga is celebrated for its psychological depth, complex plotting, and exploration of law, honour, fate, and the transition from pagan to Christian values. Unlike many sagas, it ranges beyond Iceland to Ireland (including a partly historical account of the Battle of Clontarf, 1014), Orkney, and Scandinavia. It is the longest of the family sagas — over 150 chapters — and survives in more medieval and early-modern manuscripts than almost any other saga, testifying to its enduring popularity.

Legacy

The saga's events are anchored in real landscapes of southern Iceland; place names such as Hlíðarendi and Bergþórshvoll remain identifiable, and the saga has inspired trails, exhibitions, and numerous works of literature and art. Major English translations include those by George Webbe Dasent (1861), Magnus Magnusson and Hermann Pálsson (1960), and Robert Cook (2001).
