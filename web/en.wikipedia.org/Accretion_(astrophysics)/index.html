Accretion (astrophysics)

In astrophysics, accretion is the accumulation of particles into a massive object by gravitationally attracting more matter, typically gaseous matter, into an accretion disk. Most astronomical objects, such as galaxies, stars, and planets, are formed by accretion processes.

Accretion onto compact objects

When gas falls toward a compact object such as a white dwarf, neutron star, or black hole, it typically possesses angular momentum and so forms a rotating disk rather than falling directly inward. Friction within the disk — often modelled as turbulence driven by the magnetorotational instability — transports angular momentum outward and allows matter to spiral inward, converting gravitational potential energy into heat and radiation. Accretion onto compact objects is among the most efficient energy-release processes known: accretion onto a black hole can convert roughly 6–42% of rest-mass energy into radiation, far exceeding the ~0.7% yield of hydrogen fusion.

Eddington limit

The luminosity of an accreting object is limited by the Eddington luminosity, at which radiation pressure on infalling material balances gravity. Accretion at or above this limit occurs in some active galactic nuclei and in ultraluminous X-ray sources.

Planet formation

In the core-accretion model of planet formation, dust grains in a protoplanetary disk stick together to form planetesimals, which collide and merge into planetary embryos and eventually full planets. Gas giants are thought to accrete thick hydrogen-helium envelopes once their cores reach roughly ten Earth masses.
