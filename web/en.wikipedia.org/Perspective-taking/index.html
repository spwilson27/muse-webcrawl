Perspective-taking

Perspective-taking is the act of perceiving a situation or understanding a concept from an alternative point of view, such as that of another individual. In psychology it denotes the cognitive capacity to consider the world from another person's perspective — their thoughts, feelings, beliefs and perceptions — and is closely related to, but distinct from, empathy, which involves sharing or responding to another's emotional state.

Types

Researchers commonly distinguish visual perspective-taking (judging what another person can see; Level 1 concerns whether an object is visible, Level 2 how it appears from the other viewpoint), cognitive perspective-taking (inferring beliefs and thoughts, studied through theory of mind and false-belief tasks), and affective perspective-taking (imagining how another person feels).

Development

Jean Piaget described young children as egocentric, illustrated by his three-mountains task, though later research with simpler tasks showed earlier competence. Robert Selman proposed a stage model of social perspective-taking from undifferentiated childhood responses to mutual and societal perspectives in adolescence. Perspective-taking continues to develop through adolescence and is impaired in some clinical conditions, including autism spectrum conditions, though the picture is nuanced and task-dependent.

Effects and applications

Deliberately taking another's perspective has been shown to reduce stereotyping and prejudice, improve negotiation and conflict resolution, and increase helping behaviour. It is cultivated in education, design ("user perspective"), medicine and mediation. Adam Galinsky and colleagues demonstrated that active perspective-taking can decrease reliance on stereotypes and increase social coordination. Counterpoints include that perspective-taking can sometimes backfire, increasing stereotyping when the target appears to confirm a stereotype, and that "perspective-getting" (asking the other person directly) may be more accurate.
