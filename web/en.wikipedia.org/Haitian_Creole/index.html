Haitian Creole

Haitian Creole (Haitian Creole: kreyòl ayisyen), commonly referred to as simply Creole or Kreyòl, is a French-based creole language spoken by some 10–12 million people worldwide, principally in Haiti, where it is co-official with French. It is the most widely spoken creole language in the world and the only one whose speech community consists overwhelmingly of monolingual speakers.

Origins

Haitian Creole emerged in the French colony of Saint-Domingue during the late 17th and 18th centuries, through contact between French colonists and enslaved Africans drawn from many language communities, including speakers of Gbe languages such as Fon and Ewe. Its vocabulary is derived mainly from 18th-century French, while its grammar shows both French restructurings and influences debated between substrate-language transfer and universal processes of creole formation.

Features

Creole verbs are uninflected; tense, aspect and mood are marked by preverbal particles (such as te for past, ap for progressive, and pral/ava for future). Nouns take a postposed definite article (la, a, an, nan, or plural yo) whose form depends on the preceding sounds. Plural marking uses the particle yo after the noun phrase.

Status and orthography

Although spoken by virtually all Haitians, Creole long had low prestige beside French, the language of government and education. A phonemic orthography was standardised in the late 1970s (the IPN orthography), and the 1987 constitution made Creole an official language alongside French. Creole is now used in broadcasting, literature, parliament and increasingly in schooling, and significant diaspora communities speak it in the United States, Canada, France and elsewhere in the Caribbean.
