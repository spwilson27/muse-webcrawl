Wisdom of the crowd

The wisdom of the crowd is the phenomenon in which the aggregated judgements of a large group of people are often more accurate than the judgements of any single individual in the group, including experts. Averaging many independent estimates tends to cancel out individual errors, leaving a collective answer close to the truth.

Origins

The classic example comes from statistician Francis Galton, who reported in 1907 that the median of nearly 800 guesses at the weight of an ox at a county fair came within about one percent of the animal's true weight, beating most individual guesses. The idea was popularised in the modern era by James Surowiecki's 2004 book The Wisdom of Crowds, which argued that diverse groups of independent decision-makers can outperform elite panels under the right conditions.

Conditions

Surowiecki identified four conditions for wise crowds: diversity of opinion, independence of judgements, decentralisation of knowledge, and a mechanism for aggregating individual judgements into a collective decision. When these fail — for example, when people copy one another and information cascades form — crowds can instead produce herding, bubbles, and collective error.

Applications

The principle underlies prediction markets, forecasting tournaments, some forms of crowdsourcing, and ensemble methods in machine learning. It also has a statistical basis in results such as the Condorcet jury theorem, which shows that if each voter is independently more likely than not to be correct, a majority vote becomes increasingly reliable as the group grows.
