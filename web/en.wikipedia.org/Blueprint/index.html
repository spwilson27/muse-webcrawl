Blueprint

A blueprint is a reproduction of a technical drawing or engineering drawing using a contact print process on light-sensitive sheets introduced by Sir John Herschel in 1842. The traditional process, cyanotype, produced a negative: white lines on a dark blue background. The term has since become generic for any detailed plan or technical drawing, and "blueprint" metaphorically denotes a scheme or plan for the future.

Cyanotype process

Paper is coated with a solution of ferric ammonium citrate and potassium ferricyanide. An original drawing on translucent material is placed in contact with the sensitized sheet and exposed to ultraviolet light; exposed areas turn insoluble Prussian blue, while areas shielded by the drawing's lines remain soluble and wash away, leaving white lines on blue.

Later processes

The diazo (whiteprint) process, which produces blue or black lines on a white background, overtook cyanotype in the mid-20th century because it was cheaper and could be developed with ammonia vapour. From the 1970s onward, digital printing and computer-aided design largely displaced chemical reproduction, and modern "blueprints" are usually large-format digital prints or electronic files.

Usage

Blue-printing, or a blueprint, is still widely used in architecture, engineering, and construction to distribute plans among contractors and trades, though physical prints are increasingly supplemented by tablets and shared CAD/BIM models on site.
