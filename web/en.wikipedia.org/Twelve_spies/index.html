The Twelve Spies

The Twelve Spies, as recorded in the Book of Numbers (chapters 13–14), were a group of Israelite chieftains, one from each of the Twelve Tribes, sent by Moses from the wilderness of Paran to scout the land of Canaan ahead of the Israelites' entry.

Narrative

The spies spent forty days reconnoitring the land and returned carrying a huge cluster of grapes from the Valley of Eshcol. Ten of the spies reported that the land was fruitful but inhabited by powerful peoples and giants (the Anakim), discouraging the people. Only Joshua and Caleb urged trust in God and immediate conquest.

Consequences

According to the narrative, the Israelites' refusal to enter led God to condemn that generation to wander forty years in the wilderness — one year for each day of the mission — until only Joshua and Caleb remained of the adults who left Egypt.

In tradition

In Jewish tradition the spies' report is associated with the fast of Tisha B'Av, and the episode is a classic study in leadership, faith, and majority error.
