Freeze-drying

Freeze-drying, also known as lyophilisation, is a low-temperature dehydration process in which a product is frozen and the pressure reduced, allowing the frozen water in the material to sublimate — pass directly from solid ice to vapour — without first melting. Because it avoids liquid water and high temperatures, freeze-drying preserves structure, flavour, and biological activity better than most other drying methods.

Process

The process has three stages. In the freezing stage, the product is cooled below its freezing point so that most of its water forms ice. In primary drying, vacuum is applied and heat is supplied gently so the ice sublimates and the vapour is captured on a cold condenser. In secondary drying, residual unfrozen water bound to the material is removed by desorption at somewhat higher temperatures, typically leaving 1–4 percent residual moisture.

History and applications

Freeze-drying has roots in Andean preservation of potatoes (chuño) and was developed industrially in the 20th century — notably during the Second World War to transport blood plasma and penicillin. Today it is widely used for pharmaceuticals and vaccines that are unstable in solution, biological samples and reagents, instant coffee, astronaut and military rations, camping food, and freeze-dried fruits and confectionery. Its main disadvantages are high equipment and energy costs and long processing times.

Properties

Freeze-dried products are light, usually shelf-stable for long periods without refrigeration, and rehydrate quickly, recovering much of their original texture and nutritional content. They are typically packaged in moisture-proof, low-oxygen containers to prevent degradation.
