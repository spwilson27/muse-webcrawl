Browser wars

Browser market share has shifted dramatically over successive "wars". (Image placeholder)

The browser wars are the competitions for dominance between web browsers. The term is usually applied to two major contests: the first, in the late 1990s, between Microsoft's Internet Explorer and Netscape's Navigator; and the second, from the 2000s onward, involving Internet Explorer, Mozilla Firefox, Google Chrome, Safari and others.

First browser war (1995–2001)

Netscape Navigator was the dominant browser when Microsoft released Internet Explorer in 1995, bundling it with Windows. The companies raced to add proprietary features and HTML extensions, fragmenting web standards. By 2001 Internet Explorer had reached over 90% market share, Netscape had been acquired by AOL, and Microsoft's tactics became central to the United States v. Microsoft antitrust case.

Second browser war (2004–2017)

Mozilla Firefox, descended from the open-sourced Netscape code, launched in 2004 and eroded Internet Explorer's share by offering tabs, extensions and better standards support. Apple's Safari and Opera competed on desktop and mobile, and Google's Chrome, launched in 2008, overtook all rivals within a few years through speed, simplicity and Google's promotion. By the mid-2010s Chrome held a majority share worldwide, and Microsoft replaced Internet Explorer with Edge.

Legacy

The wars drove rapid innovation but also an era of incompatible web sites optimised for a single browser. They contributed to the creation and empowerment of web standards bodies and, in their second phase, to a standards-based web — though Chrome's dominance and its Chromium engine's adoption by rival browsers have raised new concerns about a single-vendor monoculture.
