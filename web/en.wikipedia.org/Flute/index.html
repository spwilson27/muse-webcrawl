The flute is a family of musical instruments in the woodwind group. Unlike woodwind instruments with reeds, a flute is a type of aerophone that produces its sound from the flow of air across an opening. In the Hornbostel–Sachs classification, flutes are edge-blown aerophones: the player's breath, or a channel of air, is directed against a sharp edge, setting the air column inside the tube vibrating.

Flutes are among the earliest known identifiable musical instruments. Bone flutes from the Swabian Jura in Germany, made from bird bone and mammoth ivory, date back some 40,000 years to the Upper Paleolithic; the Divje Babe flute from Slovenia may be older still, though its status is debated.

Types

The flute family is usually divided into two broad groups:

  • Transverse (side-blown) flutes, held horizontally and blown across an embouchure hole: the Western concert flute, piccolo, alto and bass flutes; the Indian bansuri; the Chinese dizi (with its buzzing membrane); the Japanese shinobue.
  • End-blown and duct flutes, held vertically: the Japanese shakuhachi and Middle Eastern ney (end-blown), and duct (fipple) flutes such as the recorder and tin whistle, in which a channel directs air onto the edge.

The Western concert flute is a transverse flute made of metal (silver, nickel silver, gold or platinum), about 67 cm long, with a cylindrical head joint and a conical or cylindrical body. Its modern design derives from the keywork system developed by Theobald Boehm in the nineteenth century (notably the model of 1847), which gave the instrument even intonation, greater volume and a fully chromatic range of about three octaves.

History and repertoire

Flutes appear in nearly every musical culture: the transverse flute was a military and court instrument in medieval and Renaissance Europe, a central solo and orchestral instrument in the Baroque era (when it was made of wood and called the flauto traverso), and, after Boehm's redesign, a standard member of the modern orchestra and concert band. Major repertoire includes works by J. S. Bach, Mozart (the flute concertos and the Concerto for Flute and Harp), Debussy (Syrinx for solo flute), and Poulenc. In non-Western traditions, flutes such as the bansuri (associated with the god Krishna) and the shakuhachi (used by Zen Buddhist monks) have deep spiritual associations.

Technique

The player directs a flattened stream of air across the embouchure hole; changes in air speed and angle, together with fingering, select the pitch. Overblowing at the octave gives the upper registers. Extended techniques used in contemporary music include flutter-tonguing, multiphonics, harmonics, key clicks and singing while playing.
