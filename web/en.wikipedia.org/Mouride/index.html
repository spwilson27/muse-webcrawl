The Mouride brotherhood (Wolof: yoonu murit; Arabic: الطريقة المريدية, aṭ-Ṭarīqat al-Murīdiyyah) is a large Sufi order (tariqa) most prominent in Senegal and The Gambia, with headquarters in the city of Touba, which is also the order's holy city. Followers are called Mourides, from the Arabic word murīd ("one who desires"), a term used generally in Sufism for an aspirant or disciple. The order was founded by Sheikh Amadou Bamba (1853–1927), a Muslim mystic and religious leader who was exiled by the French colonial administration to Gabon (1895–1902) and Mauritania (1903–1907).

Mouridism places a distinctive emphasis on the sanctification of work — often summarised as "work as worship" — which historically made its disciples central to Senegal's peanut-growing economy. Disciples (talibés) owe allegiance to a marabout (sheikh), and the order is headed by a Caliph-General drawn from Bamba's descendants.

Touba and the Grand Magal

Amadou Bamba founded Touba in 1887; its Great Mosque, completed in 1963, is one of the largest in Africa. Each year millions of Mourides make the Grand Magal pilgrimage to Touba to commemorate Bamba's exile, one of the largest religious gatherings in West Africa.

Influence

The brotherhood wields considerable economic and political influence in Senegal. Mouride traders and entrepreneurs are prominent in commerce in Dakar and in the Senegalese diaspora, including street traders in cities such as New York, where the community holds an annual Cheikh Amadou Bamba Day parade. Despite strict internal hierarchies, the order is admired for promoting peace, industriousness and self-reliance.
