Extended technique

Extended technique is a term used in music to describe unconventional, unorthodox, or non-traditional methods of singing or playing musical instruments in order to obtain unusual sounds or timbres. The term gained currency in twentieth-century art music, where composers and performers systematically expanded the sound palette of conventional instruments and the voice.

Examples

For the piano, Henry Cowell's tone clusters and string-piano effects — plucking, strumming, and stopping the strings — and John Cage's prepared piano, in which screws, bolts, and rubber are inserted between the strings, are foundational examples. String players employ col legno (striking with the wood of the bow), bowing behind the bridge, harmonics, and Bartók pizzicato; wind players use multiphonics, circular breathing, flutter-tonguing, and key clicks; vocalists use overtone singing, inhaled phonation, and the extended vocal techniques explored by performers such as Cathy Berberian and Joan La Barbara.

History and use

Many "extended" techniques have older folk or non-Western roots and were absorbed into the concert tradition from the early twentieth century onward, notably in experimental music and free improvisation. Because results can be idiosyncratic, composers developed specialized notations, and performers have produced manuals cataloguing the techniques available on each instrument. Extended techniques are now standard vocabulary in contemporary classical music, jazz, and rock.
