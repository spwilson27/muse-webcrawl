Exosphere

The exosphere is the outermost, extremely tenuous layer of Earth's atmosphere, fading into interplanetary space.

The exosphere is the uppermost layer of Earth's atmosphere. It starts at the exobase, at an altitude of roughly 500–1,000 km above the surface (varying with solar activity), and extends outward about halfway to the Moon, merging imperceptibly with the solar wind and interplanetary space.

Composition and behaviour

In the exosphere the air is so rarefied that molecules travel on ballistic trajectories, rarely colliding; fast-moving hydrogen and helium atoms can achieve escape velocity and leak into space, a process called Jeans escape. The layer is composed mainly of hydrogen, with traces of helium, carbon dioxide and atomic oxygen near its base. Temperatures inferred from molecular kinetic energies can exceed 1,000 °C, though the density is far too low to transfer appreciable heat.

Objects in the exosphere

Most artificial satellites, including the International Space Station and geostationary weather and communications satellites, orbit within the exosphere. Earth's faint hydrogen envelope, the geocorona, extends well beyond the Moon's orbit.
