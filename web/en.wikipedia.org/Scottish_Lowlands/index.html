Scottish Lowlands

The Scottish Lowlands (Scots: Lallans; Scottish Gaelic: a' Ghalldachd, "the place of the foreigner") are the southern and eastern parts of Scotland not forming part of the Highlands. The term is used culturally and historically as much as geographically, marking a contrast in language, landscape and society with the Gaelic-speaking Highlands to the north and west. The Highland Boundary Fault, running from Arran to Stonehaven, forms a rough geological dividing line.

Geography

The Lowlands comprise the Central Lowlands or Midland Valley — a rift valley between the Highland Boundary Fault and the Southern Uplands Fault containing the Forth and Clyde valleys — together with the Southern Uplands, a range of rolling hills along the English border, and the fertile coastal plains of the east. Although called "low", much of the region is hilly; the name contrasts mainly with the mountainous Highlands.

People and economy

The great majority of Scotland's population, around four-fifths, lives in the Lowlands, concentrated in the Central Belt between Glasgow and Edinburgh. The traditional language of the region is Scots, a Germanic tongue descended from Old English, together with Scottish English. The Lowlands were the heartland of feudal Scotland, of the burghs and the Reformation, and later of the Scottish Enlightenment and the Industrial Revolution, when coalfields, shipbuilding on the Clyde, textiles and heavy engineering transformed the landscape. Fertile Lowland farms, in contrast to Highland crofting, produced grain, potatoes and livestock for urban markets.

Culture

The Highland–Lowland divide shaped Scottish history for centuries: Lowlanders long regarded Highlanders as backward, while Highlanders saw Lowlanders as soft and foreign-influenced. The clearances, clan society and tartan belong to Highland history; Lowland identity has instead been associated with Presbyterianism, burgh democracy, and figures such as Robert Burns, who wrote in the Scots vernacular of Ayrshire.
