Oedipus Rex, also known by its Greek title Oedipus Tyrannus or in Latin as Oedipus the King, is an Athenian tragedy by Sophocles, first performed around 429 BC. It dramatises Oedipus's discovery that he has fulfilled the prophecy he sought to escape. Aristotle cited it in the Poetics as the supreme example of tragedy, and it has been called the perfect tragedy ever since.

Plot

Thebes is ravaged by plague. King Oedipus, who once saved the city from the Sphinx, learns from the oracle that the plague will end only when the murderer of the former king Laius is found and punished. His investigation — questioning the blind prophet Tiresias, his brother-in-law Creon, his wife Jocasta, a messenger from Corinth and an aged shepherd — steadily closes in on himself. A Corinthian messenger's news that Oedipus was adopted, and the shepherd's testimony that the infant he spared was Laius's own son, complete the revelation: Oedipus himself killed Laius, his father, and married Jocasta, his mother. Jocasta hangs herself; Oedipus blinds himself with the pins of her dress and begs to be exiled.

Themes and analysis

The play explores fate versus free will, the limits of human knowledge, and sight versus blindness — the blind Tiresias sees the truth, while the sharp-eyed Oedipus is blind to it until the end. Aristotle praised its plot for uniting peripeteia (reversal) and anagnorisis (recognition) in a single stroke. Freud's reading of the play underpinned his concept of the Oedipus complex.

Influence

Oedipus Rex has been continually adapted and reinterpreted, from Seneca's Roman version to Stravinsky's opera-oratorio Oedipus rex (1927) and Pier Paolo Pasolini's film Edipo Re (1967).
