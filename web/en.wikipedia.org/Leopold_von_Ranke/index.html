Leopold von Ranke (21 December 1795 – 23 May 1886) was a German historian widely regarded as the founder of modern source-based historical scholarship. His insistence on primary sources, archival research, and critical method — and his famous aim to show the past wie es eigentlich gewesen, "as it actually was" — transformed the writing of history into a professional academic discipline.

Life and career

Ranke was born in Wiehe in Thuringia and educated at the University of Leipzig, where he studied theology and philology. His first book, Histories of the Latin and Germanic Nations from 1494 to 1514 (1824), with its appendix subjecting earlier historians to rigorous source criticism, won him a professorship at the University of Berlin in 1825. There he taught for nearly half a century, training generations of historians in his seminar method. Ennobled in 1865, he continued dictating works almost until his death at ninety.

Works

Ranke's output was immense: histories of the popes (History of the Popes, 1834–36), of the Reformation in Germany, of Prussia, France, and England, and a nine-volume Universal History begun in his eighties. He pioneered the use of diplomatic dispatches — especially the reports of Venetian ambassadors — and of the great state archives of Europe, which were opened to him as few scholars before.

Method and influence

Ranke held that every epoch stands in an immediate relation to God and must be understood in its own terms rather than judged by later standards. Politically conservative and focused on states and great powers, he nonetheless set the methodological standard — archival evidence, impartial presentation, and criticism of sources — that professional history still invokes, even where his own political premises have been rejected.
