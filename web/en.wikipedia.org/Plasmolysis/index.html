Plasmolysis

During plasmolysis the protoplast shrinks away from the cell wall.

Plasmolysis is the process in which plant cells lose water in a hypertonic solution. The reverse process, deplasmolysis or cytolysis, can occur if the cell is in a hypotonic solution resulting in a lower external osmotic pressure and a net flow of water into the cell. As a general phenomenon, plasmolysis is the contraction of the protoplast of a plant cell as a result of loss of water from the cell when it is placed in a solution whose concentration is greater than that of the cell sap (a hypertonic solution).

Mechanism

In a hypertonic environment, water leaves the cell by osmosis, principally from the central vacuole. As the vacuole shrinks, the protoplast loses volume and the plasma membrane detaches from the rigid cell wall, remaining connected at points called Hechtian strands. The cell becomes flaccid, and in tissues this loss of turgor pressure causes wilting. Plasmolysis is generally reversible: if the cell is returned to a hypotonic or isotonic solution before damage occurs, water re-enters and the protoplast re-expands against the wall (deplasmolysis). Prolonged or severe plasmolysis can be lethal.

Types

Two forms are classically described. In concave plasmolysis, the protoplast pulls away unevenly, forming concave pockets; it is usually readily reversible. In convex plasmolysis, the protoplast detaches completely and rounds up into a spherical shape, a state from which recovery is unlikely. The point at which roughly half the cells in a tissue are plasmolysed — incipient plasmolysis — can be used experimentally to estimate the osmotic potential of cell sap.

Occurrence

Plasmolysis occurs naturally when plant roots encounter highly saline soils or concentrated fertiliser, and in food preservation, where salt or sugar draws water from both plant tissues and contaminating microbes. Animal cells, lacking walls, undergo the related process of crenation in hypertonic solutions.
