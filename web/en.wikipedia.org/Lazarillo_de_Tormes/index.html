La vida de Lazarillo de Tormes y de sus fortunas y adversidades ("The Life of Lazarillo de Tormes and of His Fortunes and Adversities"), usually shortened to Lazarillo de Tormes, is a Spanish novella published anonymously in 1554. It is credited with founding the picaresque genre: the episodic, first-person account of a low-born rogue — a pícaro — who survives by his wits while serving a succession of masters, through whom the book satirises the society of Golden Age Spain.

Plot

Lázaro, born in a mill on the river Tormes near Salamanca, is given by his widowed mother to a blind beggar, whose cruelty teaches him cunning — the boy tricks his master over grapes, sausages and wine, and finally takes revenge by guiding him into a stone post. He then serves a starving miserly priest at Maqueda, from whose locked bread chest he steals crumbs with a copied key; a destitute squire in Toledo, obsessed with honour yet so poor that Lázaro must beg food for him; a friar of the Order of Mercy; a pardoner who sells indulgences with a staged miracle; and others. The adult Lázaro, now a town crier in Toledo married — in an arrangement shaded by scandal — to the mistress of an archpriest, addresses his narrative to an unnamed "Vuestra Merced" to explain "the case": the ambiguous respectability he has finally, cynically, achieved.

Significance

Written in a plain, ironic style revolutionary beside the idealised chivalric romances it parodies, the book attacks clerical greed, false honour and hunger in a wealthy empire. Its anticlericalism led the Inquisition to ban it in 1559; an expurgated "Lazarillo castigado" appeared in 1573. The author remains unidentified — candidates proposed include the humanist Alfonso de Valdés and the Hieronymite friar Juan de Ortega. The picaresque form it created runs through Guzmán de Alfarache, Don Quixote, and ultimately to novels such as Moll Flanders and Huckleberry Finn.
