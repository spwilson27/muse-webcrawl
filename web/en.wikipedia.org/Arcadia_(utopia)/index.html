Arcadia (utopia)

Arcadia (utopia)
OriginArcadia, region of ancient Greece (Peloponnese)
ThemePastoral idyll, harmony with nature
Key worksVirgil's Eclogues; Sannazaro's Arcadia; Sidney's Arcadia; Poussin's Et in Arcadia ego

Arcadia is a poetic ideal of an unspoiled, harmonious wilderness, derived from the mountainous region of Arcadia in the Peloponnese of ancient Greece. In classical and Renaissance literature, Arcadia became a symbolic setting for the pastoral mode: a land of shepherds living in simplicity and contentment in nature, untouched by civilisation's corruption.

Development of the idea

The historical Arcadians worshipped Pan, god of the wild, and their rugged homeland was imagined by later writers as a primitive paradise. Virgil's Eclogues relocated the singing shepherds of Theocritus to Arcadia, fixing its literary image. Jacopo Sannazaro's prose romance Arcadia (1504) and Sir Philip Sidney's Arcadia spread the ideal through Renaissance Europe, influencing Shakespeare and later pastoral art.

Et in Arcadia ego

The phrase "Et in Arcadia ego"—"Even in Arcadia, there am I [Death]"—is associated with paintings by Guercino and Nicolas Poussin, in which shepherds contemplate a tomb. It introduces the theme of mortality into the idyll, a tension that has made Arcadia a durable symbol of both lost innocence and the inevitability of death. The word arcade and the Canadian region of Acadia are sometimes linked by folk association, though Acadia's name has separate origins.
