Evidence (law)

The law of evidence, also known as the rules of evidence, encompasses the rules and legal principles that govern the proof of facts in a legal proceeding. These rules determine what evidence must or must not be considered by the trier of fact in reaching its decision. The trier of fact is a judge in bench trials, or the jury in jury trials. Evidence law is especially significant in common law jurisdictions, where elaborate rules developed over centuries alongside the jury system; many are now codified, as in the United States Federal Rules of Evidence (1975).

A witness testifying — oral testimony is a central form of evidence.

Types of evidence

Evidence is commonly classified as direct (directly proving a fact, such as eyewitness testimony) or circumstantial (requiring an inference, such as fingerprint or motive evidence); and by form as testimonial (witness statements), documentary ( writings, records, and increasingly electronic data), real or physical evidence (objects themselves), and demonstrative evidence (diagrams, models, and reconstructions used to illustrate testimony). Scientific and expert evidence — from forensic DNA to economic modelling — is admitted where the witness is qualified and the methods reliable, under standards such as the Daubert test in US federal courts.

Admissibility

The central question is admissibility. Evidence must first be relevant — tending to make a fact of consequence more or less probable — and even relevant evidence may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice, confusion, or delay (Federal Rule 403). Major exclusionary rules include:

  • Hearsay: out-of-court statements offered for their truth are generally excluded, subject to numerous exceptions (dying declarations, excited utterances, business records, and others).
  • Character evidence: generally inadmissible to prove conduct, with exceptions and special rules in sexual offence cases.
  • Privileges: attorney–client, spousal, clergy, and other privileges shield certain communications.
  • Illegally obtained evidence: in criminal cases, evidence obtained in violation of constitutional rights is excluded under the exclusionary rule (Mapp v. Ohio), together with its "fruit of the poisonous tree".
  • Authentication and best evidence: documents and objects must be shown to be what they purport to be, and the original writing rule governs proof of a document's contents.

Burdens and standards of proof

Evidence law allocates the burden of production and the burden of persuasion. In criminal cases the prosecution must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt; in most civil cases the standard is the preponderance of the evidence (more likely than not), with an intermediate "clear and convincing evidence" standard for some claims. Presumptions and judicial notice manage facts that need not be formally proved.

Comparison across systems

Civil law systems typically admit evidence more freely and rely on professional judges to weigh it ("free evaluation of evidence"), with less elaborate exclusionary doctrine, though they maintain their own rules on, for example, illegally obtained proof. International courts and tribunals likewise operate flexible admissibility regimes. Modern pressures on all systems include digital evidence, metadata, deepfakes and AI-generated material, and the sheer volume of electronic discovery in civil litigation.
