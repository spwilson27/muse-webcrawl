Juan de Herrera (1530 – 1597) was a Spanish architect, mathematician and geometrician, regarded as one of the most important figures of Spanish Renaissance architecture. He was the principal designer of the monastery-palace of El Escorial and the namesake of the austere Herrerian style that dominated Spanish building in the late 16th and early 17th centuries.

Life and career

Herrera studied at the University of Valladolid and served in the retinue of the future Philip II on journeys through Italy, Germany and the Spanish Netherlands, where he absorbed Renaissance architectural theory. In 1563 he joined the works of El Escorial under Juan Bautista de Toledo; on Toledo's death in 1567 Herrera took charge, greatly expanding and regularizing the design. The vast granite complex of San Lorenzo de El Escorial — monastery, palace, basilica, library and royal pantheon — was completed under his direction in 1584.

Works and style

Herrera's other works include the Royal Alcázar works in Madrid, the Cathedral of Valladolid (begun 1589), the Casa de la Moneda in Segovia, the Lonja (exchange) of Seville, and the palace of Aranjuez. The Herrerian style is characterized by geometric rigor, bare stone surfaces, slate-roofed towers and an almost complete rejection of ornament, reflecting the Counter-Reformation austerity of Philip II's Spain. Herrera also founded the Academy of Mathematics in Madrid (1582) and wrote on geometry and the cube.

Legacy

The "estilo herreriano" became the official architecture of the Spanish Habsburg monarchy, and Herrera's influence extended across the Spanish Empire in churches, monasteries and civic buildings in Europe and the Americas.
