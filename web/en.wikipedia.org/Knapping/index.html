Knapping is the shaping of stone — such as flint, chert or obsidian — through the process of controlled fracturing known as lithic reduction. It was the primary technology by which humans and their ancestors made cutting tools for more than three million years, from the earliest Stone Age handaxes to projectile points, scrapers and, into the industrial era, gunflints.

Principles

Suitable stones fracture conchoidally: a precisely placed blow produces a predictable cone of force (a Hertzian cone) that detaches a flake from the core, leaving a sharp edge and a characteristic bulb of percussion. Because the fracture can be controlled by the angle, position and force of the strike, a skilled knapper can progressively shape a core into a desired tool. The waste flakes (debitage) left behind are a major source of information for archaeologists.

Techniques

  • Direct percussion — striking the core with a hard hammerstone or a softer hammer of antler, bone or wood.
  • Indirect percussion — using a punch placed on the core and struck with a hammer, for greater precision.
  • Pressure flaking — pressing a pointed tool of antler or copper against the edge to remove small flakes, used for fine retouch of arrowheads and spear points.
  • Prepared-core techniques — such as the Levallois method of the Middle Palaeolithic, in which the core is shaped so that a single blow detaches a flake of predetermined form.

History and modern practice

The earliest known knapped tools are the Lomekwian artefacts from Kenya, dated to about 3.3 million years ago, followed by the Oldowan industry at 2.6 million years. Knapping remained central worldwide until metal tools displaced stone; nevertheless flint knapping for gunflints continued commercially at Brandon in Suffolk, England, into the 20th century. Today experimental archaeologists knap to understand ancient technology, and hobbyist flintknappers reproduce prehistoric tool forms.
