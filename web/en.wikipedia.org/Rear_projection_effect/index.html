Rear projection effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The rear projection effect (also called process photography or back projection) is a classical in-camera film special effect in which actors perform on a studio set in front of a translucent screen onto which previously filmed background footage — called a "plate" — is projected from behind, while the camera films the combined image in a single exposure. Widely used from the 1930s through the 1960s, the technique allowed studios to place performers in distant, dangerous or nonexistent settings without leaving the lot: it is most familiar from countless driving scenes, where actors in a stationary prop car appear to travel along roads unrolling behind them.

Technique

The projector and camera are synchronised so that their shutters open simultaneously, avoiding flicker. The screen — often 5–15 metres wide — must be lit evenly and brightly, while foreground lighting is matched in colour and direction to the plate footage so the actors appear to inhabit the scene. Plates were shot by second units from moving vehicles, trains or aircraft, or filmed from miniature models. The method required skill: the foreground had to be brighter than the screen, and differences in grain, contrast and colour between plate and foreground often betray the trick to modern eyes, as does the absence of parallax when the camera moves.

History and decline

Experiments date to the 1910s, but the process was perfected around 1930 — Farciot Edouart at Paramount developed refined equipment and methods that made it reliable — and it became a mainstay of Golden Age Hollywood, used in films from King Kong (1933) to the works of Alfred Hitchcock, who employed it extensively (and conspicuously) in films such as North by Northwest. Stanley Kubrick's frustrations with it in 2001: A Space Odyssey led him to perfect front projection instead. From the 1970s, travelling mattes, blue screen and eventually digital compositing and LED-volume virtual production (as in The Mandalorian, a modern descendant of the idea) largely replaced rear projection, though filmmakers such as Quentin Tarantino and Christopher Nolan have revived it deliberately for period authenticity or in-camera realism.
