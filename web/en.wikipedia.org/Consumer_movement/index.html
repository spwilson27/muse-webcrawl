The consumer movement is an effort by individuals and organisations to promote the interests of buyers of goods and services, campaigning for consumer rights such as safety, accurate information, fair pricing and effective redress. It has been a significant force in shaping modern regulatory law in the United States, Europe and elsewhere.

Early history

Consumer activism grew with industrial mass production. In the United States, Upton Sinclair's 1906 novel The Jungle exposed conditions in meatpacking and helped spur the Pure Food and Drug Act. Consumers' Research (founded 1929) and Consumers Union (1936), publisher of Consumer Reports, pioneered independent product testing.

Post-war expansion

In 1962 President John F. Kennedy proclaimed a Consumer Bill of Rights — the rights to safety, to be informed, to choose and to be heard — later expanded by consumer advocates to include redress, education and a healthy environment. Ralph Nader's 1965 book Unsafe at Any Speed, attacking the safety record of the Chevrolet Corvair, made him the movement's most famous figure and led to automobile safety legislation and, indirectly, to the creation of agencies such as the Consumer Product Safety Commission (1972) and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (2010).

Internationally

Consumers International, founded in 1960, federates consumer groups worldwide, and World Consumer Rights Day is marked on 15 March. The movement today also overlaps with campaigns on data privacy, greenwashing, planned obsolescence and sustainable consumption.
