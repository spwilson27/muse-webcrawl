Panic attack

Panic attacks are sudden periods of intense fear and discomfort that may include palpitations, otherwise defined as a rapid, irregular heartbeat, sweating, chest pain, shaking, shortness of breath, numbness, or a feeling of impending doom or loss of control. Typically, symptoms reach a peak within ten minutes of onset, and last for roughly 30 minutes, but the duration can vary from seconds to hours. There may be a sensation of detachment, fear of losing one's mind, fear of dying, and an intense urge to escape or flee.

Causes and triggers

Panic attacks can occur in the context of panic disorder, other anxiety disorders, phobias, post-traumatic stress disorder, and substance use or withdrawal, or without any diagnosed disorder. They are thought to involve a misfiring of the body's fight-or-flight response, with hyperactivity in fear circuits such as the amygdala and sensitivity to bodily sensations. Triggers can include stress, caffeine, certain medications, and interoceptive cues such as a racing heart. Many first attacks appear "out of the blue".

Treatment

Education and reassurance are important, because sufferers often fear they are having a heart attack. Cognitive behavioural therapy — including interoceptive exposure, in which feared bodily sensations are deliberately induced and reinterpreted — is an effective treatment for recurrent panic attacks and panic disorder. Antidepressants such as SSRIs are commonly prescribed; benzodiazepines act quickly but carry dependence risks and are generally reserved for short-term use. Breathing retraining and lifestyle changes, including limiting caffeine and alcohol, may also help.
