Irish neutrality

Irish neutrality refers to Ireland's policy of military non-alignment, maintained since the 1930s. Ireland remained neutral during the Second World War — a period officially styled "the Emergency" — and did not join NATO during the Cold War or since, though it participates in EU defence cooperation and UN peacekeeping.

Second World War

Under Taoiseach Éamon de Valera, Ireland declared a state of emergency in September 1939 and stayed formally neutral for the war's duration. In practice, neutrality leaned toward the Allies in several respects: Allied airmen who came down in Ireland were generally repatriated while Axis airmen were interned, Irish weather reports assisted Allied planning (including for D-Day), and tens of thousands of Irish citizens served in British forces. The policy asserted Ireland's sovereignty and unity of public opinion so soon after independence and partition.

Cold War and after

Ireland declined NATO membership in 1949, citing partition — it would not join an alliance with the United Kingdom while Northern Ireland remained British. It later joined the European Community (1973) and the UN (1955), contributing troops to peacekeeping missions from the Congo (1960) onward. Irish governments describe the policy as "military neutrality" rather than political neutrality: Ireland takes positions on international conflicts, imposes sanctions, and participates in the EU's Common Security and Defence Policy, subject to the "triple lock" governing overseas troop deployments. Debates over the policy intensified after Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine.
