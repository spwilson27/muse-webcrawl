Blinded experiment

In a blinded experiment, information that may influence the participants or researchers is withheld (masked) until after the experiment is complete. Blinding is used to reduce bias arising from expectations — including the placebo effect, observer bias, and confirmation bias. The term "blind" in this context is a metaphor; the process is also called masking.

Types

In a single-blind experiment, the participants do not know whether they belong to the experimental or control group, while the researchers do. In a double-blind experiment, neither participants nor the researchers administering the intervention know the assignments until the study ends. Triple-blind studies additionally mask the analysts. Blinding is a standard feature of randomized controlled trials of medicines, where treatments and placebos are made indistinguishable.

History

Blinded assessment dates to the eighteenth century: the 1784 royal commission investigating mesmerism, which included Benjamin Franklin and Antoine Lavoisier, used blindfolds to test whether subjects could detect "magnetized" objects. Blinding became standard methodology in twentieth-century clinical research and is also used in physics (blind data analysis), forensics, and taste testing.

Limitations

Blinding is not always possible — for example, in trials of surgery or psychotherapy — and it can fail when treatments have distinctive side effects. Failed blinding can reintroduce bias, so trials often test whether participants guessed their assignment.
