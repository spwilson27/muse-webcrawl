Babylonian religion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Babylonian religion is the religious practice of the Babylonians of Mesopotamia, a development of the earlier Sumerian religion and closely related to Assyrian religion. Centred on the worship of many gods, each associated with a city, natural forces, and celestial bodies, it placed the god Marduk at the head of the pantheon as patron deity of Babylon, especially after the reign of Hammurabi (18th century BC).

Pantheon

The Babylonian pantheon inherited the Sumerian gods under Akkadian names: Anu (sky), Enlil (lord of the air, whose supremacy passed largely to Marduk), Ea/Enki (waters and wisdom), Shamash (sun and justice), Sin (moon), and Ishtar/Inanna (love and war). Marduk's rise is narrated in the creation epic Enūma Eliš, in which he defeats the primordial sea-goddess Tiamat and fashions the cosmos from her body, receiving the Tablet of Destinies and fifty names.

Practice

Each major city maintained a great temple — in Babylon, the Esagila complex and the ziggurat Etemenanki — staffed by priests who performed daily offerings, festivals, and divination. The New Year (Akitu) festival, in which the king was ritually humiliated before Marduk and reaffirmed in office, was the religious high point of the Babylonian year. Babylonians practised extispicy (reading omens in animal entrails), hemerology, incantations against demons and illness, and a sophisticated astral religion that developed into Babylonian astrology and influenced the later Hellenistic world. Ordinary people also worshipped personal gods and protective spirits such as the lamassu.

Myths and legacy

Babylonian literature preserved the Epic of Gilgamesh, the Atrahasis flood story, and the myth of Ishtar's descent to the underworld. Babylonian religion endured through the Neo-Babylonian Empire and into the Hellenistic period before fading under Parthian rule, while its astronomy, zodiac, and divinatory traditions left a lasting mark on Greek, Jewish, and later thought.
