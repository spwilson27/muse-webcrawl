Actuarial science

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Actuarial science is the discipline that applies mathematical and statistical methods to assess risk in the insurance, pension and financial industries. Practitioners, known as actuaries, use probability theory, statistics, economics and the theory of interest to model uncertain future events—such as death, illness, accidents and catastrophes—and to design and price financial protection against them.

History

The foundations were laid in the 17th century: John Graunt analysed mortality data in 1662, Christiaan Huygens and Johan de Witt developed early life-annuity mathematics, and Edmond Halley published the first modern life table in 1693. The profession organised in the 19th century with the founding of the Institute of Actuaries in London (1848) and the Faculty of Actuaries in Scotland (1856); professional bodies now exist worldwide and entry requires passing a demanding series of examinations in probability, financial mathematics, statistics, economics and risk modelling.

Practice areas

Actuaries work principally in life insurance (pricing policies and setting reserves using mortality tables), general/property-and-casualty insurance (estimating claim frequency and severity, reserving for claims incurred but not reported), pensions (valuing scheme liabilities and funding), and health insurance. Increasingly they also work in enterprise risk management, banking, investment, climate-risk analysis and data science.

Methods

Core tools include life tables and survival models; the present-value mathematics of annuities and contingent payments; credibility theory; ruin theory; and stochastic simulation. Modern actuarial work relies heavily on statistical software and generalised linear models for pricing, alongside professional standards governing the communication of uncertainty.
