In aeronautics, a balloon is an unpowered aerostat — a lighter-than-air aircraft that remains aloft because its lifting gas is less dense than the surrounding air. Unlike an airship, a balloon has no engine and no means of steering against the wind; it drifts with the air currents, and the pilot controls only altitude, by adjusting lift or ballast, in order to find winds blowing in desired directions.

Types

Hot air balloons carry an onboard burner, usually fuelled by propane, that heats the air inside a fabric envelope. Gas balloons are filled with a gas lighter than air — historically coal gas and hydrogen, today usually helium — sealed in an impermeable envelope. Hybrid designs such as the Rozière balloon combine a hot-air envelope with a separate gas cell and are used for long-distance record flights. Small uncrewed balloons include toy balloons, meteorological weather balloons, and high-altitude research balloons.

History

The first untethered crewed flight took place in Paris on 21 November 1783, when Jean-François Pilâtre de Rozier and the Marquis d'Arlandes flew in a hot air balloon built by Joseph-Michel and Jacques-Étienne Montgolfier. Days later, Jacques Charles and Nicolas-Louis Robert flew a hydrogen balloon. Balloons were soon used for military observation — the French Aerostatic Corps at the Battle of Fleurus in 1794 is usually cited as the first military use — and observation balloons saw extensive service in the American Civil War and the First World War.

In the twentieth century, ballooning achieved dramatic altitude and distance records, including Auguste Piccard's stratospheric ascents of the 1930s and the first non-stop circumnavigation of the Earth by balloon, completed by Bertrand Piccard and Brian Jones in Breitling Orbiter 3 in 1999. Recreational hot air ballooning became popular from the 1960s onward, following Ed Yost's development of the modern envelope and burner.
