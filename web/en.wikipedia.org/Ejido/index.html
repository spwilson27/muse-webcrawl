Ejido

An ejido (Spanish pronunciation: [eˈxiðo]) is an area of communal land used for agriculture in Mexico, on which community members individually farm designated parcels and collectively maintain communal holdings. Ejidos were created under the Mexican land reforms of the 20th century, when land was expropriated from large estates (haciendas) and redistributed to rural communities.

Ejido lands are farmed by individual households but held collectively by the community.

History

The ejido system has roots in both pre-Hispanic Aztec land tenure, in which the calpulli held land in common, and in Spanish colonial law. The modern ejido was established by Article 27 of the Mexican Constitution of 1917, adopted during the Mexican Revolution, which authorized the government to break up large estates and grant land to villages. President Lázaro Cárdenas (1934–1940) carried out the largest redistribution, transferring tens of millions of hectares to ejidos.

Organization

Each ejido is governed by an assembly of its members (ejidatarios), which elects a comisariado ejidal to administer day-to-day affairs. Ejidal land is typically divided into individually worked parcels, common-use lands such as pastures and forests, and plots reserved for schools and public buildings. Under the original system, ejidal land could not be sold, rented, or used as collateral.

Reform of 1992

In 1992, under President Carlos Salinas de Gortari, Article 27 was amended to end further land redistribution and to allow ejidatarios to certify their parcel rights, rent land, and—with the approval of the ejido assembly—convert parcels to full private ownership (dominio pleno). The reform was partly intended to prepare Mexico for the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA).
