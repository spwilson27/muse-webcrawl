Yupik peoples

The Yupik (also spelled Yup'ik) are a group of Indigenous peoples of the Arctic who inhabit western and southwestern Alaska and the Russian Far East (Chukotka), including St. Lawrence Island. They are closely related culturally and linguistically to the Inuit of northern Alaska, Canada, and Greenland, but form a distinct branch of the Eskimo peoples, speaking the Yupik languages. The name derives from yuk ("person") plus -pik ("real" or "genuine"), i.e. "real people".

Groups

The principal Yupik groups include the Central Alaskan Yup'ik of the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta and Bristol Bay region (including the Cup'ik of the Chevak area and the Cup'ig of Nunivak Island), the Sugpiaq or Alutiiq of the Alaska Peninsula and Kodiak Island, and the Siberian Yupik (Yuit) of St. Lawrence Island and the Chukotka coast. The total Yupik population is on the order of 35,000, the great majority in Alaska.

Culture

Traditionally, Yupik communities lived by hunting and fishing: seals, walrus, whales, salmon and other fish, waterfowl, caribou, and gathered berries and greens formed the basis of the diet and of material culture. Semi-subterranean sod houses, the communal men's house (qasgiq), kayaks (qayaq) and larger open boats (umiak), and elaborately carved masks used in winter ceremonial cycles are hallmarks of Yupik culture. Dance, storytelling, and shamanic ritual structured social and spiritual life, and these traditions remain vital today, alongside Christianity introduced from the 19th century onward.

History

The Yupik descend from the Thule and earlier Bering Sea cultures that spread along the coast roughly a thousand years and more ago. Sustained contact came with Russian traders in the 18th and 19th centuries and later with American whalers, missionaries, and schools, which brought epidemics, trade goods, and pressure toward assimilation. Under the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971, Alaska Native peoples including the Yup'ik organized into regional corporations. Today subsistence hunting and fishing remain central to Yupik identity, even as communities contend with language loss, climate change, and coastal erosion.
