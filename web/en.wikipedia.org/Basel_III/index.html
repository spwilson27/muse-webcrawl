Basel III is an international regulatory framework for banks developed by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision in response to the deficiencies in financial regulation revealed by the 2007–2008 financial crisis. It strengthens bank capital requirements, stress testing, and market liquidity risk standards, building on the earlier Basel I and Basel II accords.

Key requirements

RequirementMinimum
Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) ratio4.5% of risk-weighted assets
Capital conservation buffer2.5% (additional CET1)
Countercyclical buffer0–2.5%
Total capital ratio8% (plus buffers)
Leverage ratio3% (Tier 1 capital to total exposure)
Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)100% (30-day stressed outflows)
Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR)100%

History

The framework was agreed in 2010–2011 and phased in from 2013. In December 2017 the Basel Committee finalized the remaining reforms — often called the "Basel III endgame" or, unofficially, "Basel IV" — including a standardized "output floor" limiting the benefit banks can obtain from internal models (set at 72.5% of the standardized approach), revised credit and operational risk frameworks, and constraints on model parameters. Implementation dates have varied by jurisdiction, with many standards applying from 2023 onwards.

Criticism

Critics have argued the standards are still insufficient to prevent crises, that risk-weighting can be gamed, and that compliance costs fall unevenly on smaller banks. Supporters note that large banks' common-equity ratios roughly doubled in the decade after the crisis.
