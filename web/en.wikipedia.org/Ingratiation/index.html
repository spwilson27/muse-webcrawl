Ingratiation

Ingratiation is a psychological technique in which a person attempts to become more attractive or likable to another person — the target — in order to gain approval, influence, or benefits. The term and its first systematic analysis were introduced by social psychologist Edward E. Jones in his 1964 book Ingratiation: A Social Psychological Analysis.

Tactics

Jones identified several classic ingratiation tactics: opinion conformity (agreeing with the target's views), other enhancement (complimenting and flattering the target), self-presentation (describing oneself in favorable terms), and rendering favors. Later research added tactics such as expressing similarity, humor, and modesty, and has studied ingratiation extensively in workplaces, where employees use it with supervisors during performance appraisals and interviews.

The ingratiator's dilemma

Ingratiation carries an inherent risk known as the ingratiator's dilemma: tactics must appear sincere, for if the target suspects flattery is manipulative, the attempt backfires and the ingratiator is judged less trustworthy. Research suggests ingratiation is often effective when subtle, partly because targets are motivated to accept praise at face value (the "flattery law" of reciprocal liking).
