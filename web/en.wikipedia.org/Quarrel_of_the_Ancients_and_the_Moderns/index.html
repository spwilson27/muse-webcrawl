Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns

The Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns (French: Querelle des Anciens et des Modernes) was a literary and artistic debate in late 17th- and early 18th-century France over whether the artistic achievements of the present age could equal or surpass those of classical antiquity. The Ancients held that the authors of Greece and Rome were unsurpassable models who must be imitated, while the Moderns argued that writers of the age of Louis XIV were equal or superior, and that literature and learning progress with time.

Course of the debate

The quarrel formally opened in January 1687, when Charles Perrault read his poem Le Siècle de Louis le Grand before the Académie Française, praising the modern age above that of Augustus. Perrault developed his case in the four volumes of his Parallèle des Anciens et des Modernes (1688–1697), supported by Fontenelle, whose Digression on the Ancients and the Moderns (1688) argued for the progress of knowledge.

The leading champion of the Ancients was Nicolas Boileau, who defended the authority of Homer, Virgil and Longinus and attacked the moderns' taste. The dispute generated a flood of pamphlets and was provisionally settled by a reconciliation in 1694, though it flared again in the "Homer quarrel" of 1711–1716, when Anne Dacier's prose translation of the Iliad was abridged and adapted by Houdar de la Motte, who did not know Greek.

Significance

The quarrel marked a turning point in European thought: it weakened the doctrine of imitation, raised the idea of historical progress in the arts, and helped establish the modern period's confidence in itself. A related English episode, the "Battle of the Books", inspired Jonathan Swift's satire The Battle of the Books (1704).
