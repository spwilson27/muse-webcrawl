Bituminous coal, or black coal, is a relatively soft coal containing a tar-like substance called bitumen or asphalt. It is of higher quality than lignite and sub-bituminous coal, but poorer than anthracite. Bituminous coal is the most abundant rank of coal and is widely mined for electricity generation and for making coke used in steel production.

Properties and formation

Bituminous coal formed from peat subjected to greater heat and pressure than lignite over geological time; typical seams date from the Carboniferous and later periods. It is usually banded, with alternating bright and dull layers, and has a carbon content of roughly 45–86 percent and a heating value of about 24–35 megajoules per kilogram. On heating in the absence of air, suitable bituminous coals soften and resolidify into coke, a property essential for ironmaking.

Uses

The coal is classified by use as thermal (steam) coal, burned in power stations, and metallurgical (coking) coal, converted to coke for blast furnaces. Major producing regions include the Appalachian and Illinois basins in the United States, China, India, Australia and Russia. Bituminous coal was the principal fuel of the Industrial Revolution, powering steam engines, railways and factories.

Environmental issues

Burning bituminous coal releases carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides and particulates; some seams contain significant sulfur and trace elements such as mercury. Mining hazards include methane accumulation (firedamp) and coal dust, historically responsible for explosions and miners' lung disease.
