Distributed computing is a field of computer science that studies distributed systems, in which components located on networked computers communicate and coordinate their actions by passing messages in order to achieve a common goal. A distributed system is one in which the failure of a computer the user did not even know existed can render their own computer unusable — a wry formulation attributed to Leslie Lamport that captures the field's central difficulty.

Characteristics and challenges

Distributed systems must cope with concurrent execution, the absence of a global clock, partial failure of components, and unpredictable network delays. Programs that appear correct on a single machine may deadlock, lose data or behave inconsistently when spread across machines that can fail independently.

Fundamental results

The CAP theorem states that a distributed data store cannot simultaneously provide consistency, availability and partition tolerance. The FLP result shows that deterministic consensus is impossible in a fully asynchronous system with even one faulty process, driving practical systems toward randomised or partially synchronous protocols such as Paxos and Raft.

Applications

Distributed computing underlies telecommunications networks, the World Wide Web, search engines, multiplayer games, blockchain networks, volunteer computing projects such as SETI@home, and the cloud platforms on which most large-scale services now run.
