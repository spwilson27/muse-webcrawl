Celestial navigation

Celestial navigation, also known as astronavigation, is the practice of position fixing that enables a navigator to transition through a space without having to rely on estimated calculations, or dead reckoning, to know their position. Celestial navigation uses "sights", or angular measurements taken between a celestial body (such as the Sun, the Moon, a planet, or a star) and the visible horizon. The Sun is most commonly used, but navigators can also use the Moon, a planet, Polaris, or one of 57 other navigational stars whose coordinates are tabulated in nautical almanacs and air almanacs.

Principles

At any given moment, a celestial body lies directly over one point on the Earth's surface, known as its geographic position (GP). The navigator measures the body's altitude — its angle above the horizon — using a sextant. The difference between the observed altitude and 90 degrees gives the angular distance from the body's GP; the navigator therefore lies somewhere on a circle of position centred on the GP. By combining two or more such observations, ideally taken at different times or from different bodies, the navigator obtains intersecting lines of position whose crossing point is the fix.

The intercept method

The practical technique used at sea is the intercept method, devised by the French naval officer Adolphe Laurent Anatole Marcq de Blonde de Saint-Hilaire in 1875. The navigator computes the altitude and azimuth the body would have from an assumed position, compares the computed altitude with the observed altitude, and plots the difference — the intercept — along the azimuth line to draw a line of position. The method reduced laborious spherical trigonometry to a short table-driven calculation.

History

Polynesian navigators crossed vast Pacific distances using star paths, swell patterns, and bird flight long before European contact. In the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean, Arab sailors used the kamal to measure stellar altitudes. The astrolabe, quadrant, cross-staff, and backstaff were progressively refined in Europe, culminating in the octant (1731) and Hadley's sextant. The solution of the longitude problem by John Harrison's marine chronometers in the eighteenth century, together with published lunar distance tables, made celestial navigation a routine, reliable practice. It remained the primary method of oceanic navigation until satellite systems such as GPS displaced it in the late twentieth century, though it is still taught as a backup and is retained in naval training.

Modern use

Celestial navigation persists in military training, in ocean racing, and among traditional sailors. Modern almanacs, sight-reduction tables, and software have replaced much of the manual arithmetic, and automated celestial systems are used on some spacecraft and missiles for attitude and position determination independent of radio navigation aids.
