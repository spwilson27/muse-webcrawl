Appropriation (art)

Appropriation artists deliberately borrow existing images and objects to create new works.

In art, appropriation is the use of pre-existing objects or images with little or no transformation applied to them. The practice has roots in the readymades of Marcel Duchamp—most famously Fountain (1917), a porcelain urinal signed "R. Mutt"—and runs through Cubist collage, Dada, Pop art (Andy Warhol's Campbell's soup cans and Marilyn Monroe silkscreens), and the Pictures Generation of the late 1970s and 1980s.

Pictures Generation

Artists such as Sherrie Levine, who rephotographed Walker Evans's Depression-era photographs (After Walker Evans, 1981), and Richard Prince, who rephotographed Marlboro cigarette advertisements in his Cowboys series, made appropriation a central strategy of postmodern art. Their work interrogated authorship, originality, authenticity, and the circulation of images in mass media.

Copyright controversies

Appropriation has repeatedly collided with copyright law. In Rogers v. Koons (1992), Jeff Koons was found to have infringed a photograph he copied for a sculpture. In Cariou v. Prince (2013), the US Second Circuit held that most of Prince's contested works were fair use because they were transformative. In Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith (2023), the US Supreme Court narrowed that reasoning, ruling that Warhol's licensing of a Prince portrait based on Lynn Goldsmith's photograph was not fair use where it shared the photograph's commercial purpose.

Related practices

Related concepts include détournement (the Situationists' hijacking of existing cultural material), sampling in music, remix culture, and found object art. Critics debate whether appropriation critiques or merely exploits its sources, and the practice has also been discussed in relation to cultural appropriation.
