K-nearest neighbors algorithm

In statistics, the k-nearest neighbors algorithm (k-NN) is a non-parametric, supervised learning classifier first developed by Evelyn Fix and Joseph Hodges in 1951 and later expanded by Thomas Cover. It is used for classification and regression: a data point is classified by a plurality vote of its k nearest neighbours in feature space, or, in regression, assigned the average of the values of its k nearest neighbours.

Method

k-NN is a type of instance-based or "lazy" learning: it stores the training examples and defers computation until a query is made. Distance is usually measured with Euclidean distance, though other metrics (Manhattan, Minkowski, Hamming) are common. Features are normally normalised so that each contributes comparably. The parameter k is typically chosen by cross-validation; small k is sensitive to noise, while large k smooths decision boundaries.

Properties

Cover and Hart showed in 1967 that, with enough data, the error rate of the 1-NN classifier is at most twice the Bayes optimal error rate. Practical challenges include the cost of searching large training sets (mitigated by kd-trees, ball trees and approximate nearest-neighbour methods) and degradation in high dimensions, the "curse of dimensionality."

Applications

k-NN is used in pattern recognition, recommender systems, image and text classification, imputation of missing values, and as a baseline method in machine-learning practice.
