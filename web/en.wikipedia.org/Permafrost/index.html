Permafrost is ground — soil, rock or sediment — that remains at or below 0 °C for at least two consecutive years. It underlies about 15% of the exposed land area of the Northern Hemisphere, including large parts of Siberia, Alaska, northern Canada and Tibet, and can reach depths of roughly 1,500 metres. Above it lies an "active layer" that thaws and refreezes seasonally.

Formation and types

Permafrost forms where ground heat loss in winter exceeds heat gain in summer, over centuries to millennia. It is classified as continuous where it underlies more than 90% of the landscape, and discontinuous, sporadic or isolated at warmer margins. Subsea permafrost exists beneath Arctic continental shelves, relics of land exposed during glacial low sea levels.

Climate change

Warming is thawing permafrost across the Arctic, which stores roughly twice as much carbon as the atmosphere in frozen organic matter. Decomposition of thawed material releases carbon dioxide and methane, creating a positive climate feedback. Thaw also destabilises buildings, pipelines and roads built on frozen ground, causes thermokarst lakes and coastal erosion, and has revived preserved organisms and pathogens in rare cases. The 2016 Yamal anthrax outbreak was attributed to spores released from a thawed reindeer carcass.
