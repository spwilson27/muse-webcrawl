Natural theology

Natural theology
DomainPhilosophy of religion, theology
MethodReason and observation of nature, without revelation
Key argumentsCosmological, teleological, ontological
Key figuresThomas Aquinas, William Paley, David Hume (critic)

Natural theology is the branch of theology and philosophy that attempts to establish knowledge of God and religious truths through reason and observation of the natural world alone, without relying on revelation, scripture, or religious experience. It contrasts with revealed theology, which begins from texts and traditions held to be divinely disclosed.

History

Roots of natural theology appear in Greek philosophy: Plato and Aristotle argued for an unmoved mover, and the Stoics inferred divinity from cosmic order. In medieval Christianity, Thomas Aquinas framed his Five Ways — arguments from motion, causation, contingency, degrees of perfection, and governance — as demonstrations of God's existence accessible to reason. Natural theology flourished in early modern Britain, from John Ray's The Wisdom of God Manifested in the Works of the Creation (1691) to William Paley's Natural Theology (1802), famous for the watchmaker analogy: just as a watch implies a watchmaker, the complexity of organisms implies a designer.

Criticism

David Hume's Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (1779) attacked the design argument's inference from analogy and raised the problem of evil. Immanuel Kant argued that the classical proofs rest on the ontological argument and exceed the limits of theoretical reason. Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection (1859) offered a naturalistic explanation of biological adaptation, undercutting the design argument's chief evidence. In the twentieth century Karl Barth famously rejected natural theology within Protestant theology, while others have revived it in modified forms, including fine-tuning arguments drawn from cosmology.
