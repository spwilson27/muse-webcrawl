Hernán Cortés

Hernán Cortés de Monroy y Pizarro Altamirano (1485 – 2 December 1547) was a Spanish conquistador who led the expedition that caused the fall of the Aztec Empire and brought large portions of what is now mainland Mexico under Spanish rule in the early sixteenth century. Early life and Cuba

Cortés was born in Medellín, Extremadura, to a family of minor nobility. He briefly studied law at Salamanca before sailing to Hispaniola in 1504. He took part in the conquest of Cuba under Diego Velázquez, becoming a wealthy landowner and official there.

Conquest of Mexico

In 1519 Cortés sailed for the Mexican mainland against Velázquez's revoked orders, founding Veracruz and famously scuttling his ships. Aided by his interpreter Malinche (La Malinche) and by alliances with Indigenous peoples hostile to Aztec rule — above all the Tlaxcaltecs — he marched inland, survived the retreat known as La Noche Triste (1520), and after a long siege captured the Aztec capital Tenochtitlan on 13 August 1521. Smallpox, which devastated the city's defenders, was a decisive factor. Cortés's five letters to Emperor Charles V are a primary account of the campaign.

Later life

Cortés governed New Spain briefly before royal officials curtailed his authority; he was made Marquis of the Valley of Oaxaca in 1529. He explored the Pacific coast of Mexico and reached Baja California. After returning to Spain in 1540 and joining the ill-fated Algiers expedition, he died near Seville in 1547. His legacy remains intensely debated in Mexico and Spain.
