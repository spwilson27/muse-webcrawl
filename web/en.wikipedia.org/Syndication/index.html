Syndication

Syndication is the sale or licensing of the right to publish or broadcast material — such as newspaper columns, comic strips, radio and television programmes, photographs, or web content — to multiple outlets simultaneously. The organisation that brokers such material is a syndicate; historically important newspaper syndicates include King Features Syndicate (founded by William Randolph Hearst in 1914) and United Feature Syndicate.

Print syndication

From the late nineteenth century, syndicates supplied newspapers with comic strips, editorial cartoons, opinion columns, puzzles, and advice columns, letting small papers offer famous features cheaply and giving creators national reach. Iconic syndicated strips include Peanuts by Charles M. Schulz and Calvin and Hobbes by Bill Watterson, and columnists from Walter Winchell to Dear Abby (Abigail Van Buren) reached millions of readers through syndication.

Broadcast syndication

In broadcasting, syndication covers the sale of programmes to individual stations outside network schedules. First-run syndication consists of shows made for syndication, such as Jeopardy!, Wheel of Fortune, The Oprah Winfrey Show, and Star Trek: The Next Generation. Off-network syndication sells reruns of network series station by station, a famously lucrative market in which shows like Seinfeld and Friends earned billions; the traditional threshold for viability was about 100 episodes, roughly five seasons. Barter syndication, in which stations receive programmes in exchange for part of the advertising time, is also common.

Other uses

The term also applies to web syndication — the making of site content available to other sites, including through feeds such as RSS (RDF Site Summary / Really Simple Syndication) — and, in finance, to a syndicated loan, in which a group of lenders jointly funds a borrower.
