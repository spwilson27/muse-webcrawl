The Turkic languages are a language family of more than 35 documented languages spoken by the Turkic peoples across a vast area from Eastern Europe and the Mediterranean to Siberia and western China. Total native speakers number around 200 million. The language with the largest number of speakers is Turkish, spoken mainly in Turkey and Cyprus.

Characteristics

Turkic languages are strongly agglutinative, use vowel harmony, have subject–object–verb word order, lack grammatical gender and definite articles, and build words with extensive suffix chains. They were traditionally grouped with Mongolic and Tungusic in the proposed (now widely rejected as a family) "Altaic" grouping; shared features are now attributed largely to prolonged contact in the Central Asian sprachbund.

Branches

  • Oghuz (southwestern): Turkish, Azerbaijani, Turkmen, Gagauz, Qashqai.
  • Karluk (southeastern): Uzbek, Uyghur.
  • Kipchak (northwestern): Kazakh, Kyrgyz (often separately classified), Tatar, Bashkir, Crimean Tatar, Karakalpak.
  • Siberian (northeastern): Yakut (Sakha), Tuvan, Khakas, Altai.
  • Oghur: Chuvash, the family's sole surviving member of this early-diverging branch.

History

The earliest Turkic texts are the 8th-century Orkhon inscriptions of the Göktürks in Mongolia, written in a runic-like script. Turkic peoples spread westward with the Seljuks and later the Ottoman Empire. Writing systems varied historically — Old Turkic runes, Arabic, Cyrillic — and since the 1990s several states have shifted to Latin alphabets.
