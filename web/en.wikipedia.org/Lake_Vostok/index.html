Lake Vostok (Russian: озеро Восток, "Lake East") is the largest of Antarctica's almost 400 known subglacial lakes. It lies beneath some 4 kilometres of ice in East Antarctica, below Russia's Vostok Station, and is one of the largest lakes in the world by area. The freshwater lake has remained isolated from the surface for millions of years, sealed in total darkness under immense pressure.

Discovery

Soviet geographer Andrey Kapitsa inferred a great subglacial lake from seismic soundings in the 1960s. British airborne ice-penetrating radar in the 1970s confirmed it, and satellite altimetry in the 1990s mapped its extent: about 250 km long and 50 km wide, with two deep basins. The lake sits in a rift valley; geothermal heat and the insulating pressure of the ice keep its water liquid at around −3 °C.

Drilling

The Vostok ice core, drilled by Russian teams above the lake, yielded climate records spanning about 420,000 years before drilling was halted just short of the water to avoid contamination. In February 2012, Russian scientists reported penetrating the lake surface for the first time, allowing lake water to rise into the borehole and freeze for later sampling. Analysis of accretion ice and water samples has suggested the lake may contain a unique ecosystem of microbes adapted to extreme cold, darkness, high pressure, and high oxygen — an environment analogous to those believed to exist on Jupiter's moon Europa and Saturn's moon Enceladus.
