The Cathars (from Greek katharoi, "the pure ones") were adherents of a Christian dualist or Gnostic-revival movement that flourished in parts of Europe, especially the Languedoc region of southern France and northern Italy, between the 12th and 14th centuries. In France they were also called Albigensians, after the city of Albi. The Roman Catholic Church condemned Catharism as heresy and ultimately suppressed it through crusade and inquisition.

Beliefs and practices

Cathar theology was dualist: it held that the material world was evil — the work of a malevolent creator (identified by some with the God of the Old Testament, or with Satan) — while the good God of the New Testament was purely spiritual. Human souls were seen as sparks of light trapped in matter, subject to reincarnation until liberated.

The community was divided between the perfecti ("Perfects"), an ascetic elite who received the consolamentum — the spiritual baptism by laying on of hands that alone conferred salvation — and ordinary believers (credentes), who supported the Perfects and hoped to receive the consolamentum, often on their deathbeds. Perfects abstained from meat, eggs, cheese, sexual relations, oaths, and killing, and rejected Catholic sacraments, church buildings, and the cross as a symbol of worship.

Suppression

After the murder of the papal legate Pierre de Castelnau in 1208, Pope Innocent III proclaimed the Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229), in which northern French nobles conquered much of the Languedoc; the massacre at Béziers (1209) became infamous. The Treaty of Paris (1229) subordinated the region to the French crown, and the newly organized Inquisition hunted remaining Cathars. The last great stronghold, the mountain fortress of Montségur, fell in 1244, and over 200 Perfects were burned. Organized Catharism was effectively extinguished in France by the early 14th century; the last known Languedoc Perfect, Guillaume Bélibaste, was burned in 1321.

Historiography

Modern historians debate the degree to which "Catharism" was a coherent, organized church versus a label partly constructed by its persecutors. Cathar ideas likely drew on earlier dualist movements such as the Bogomils of the Balkans. The Cathars remain a powerful subject in the regional memory of southern France, where ruined "Cathar castles" dot the hills of the Aude and Ariège.
