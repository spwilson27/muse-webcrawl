Roscoe Conkling

Roscoe Conkling (1829–1888)
Born:October 30, 1829, Albany, New York
Died:April 18, 1888, New York City
Office:U.S. Senator from New York (1867–1881)

Roscoe Conkling (October 30, 1829 – April 18, 1888) was an American lawyer and Republican politician who represented New York in both the U.S. House of Representatives and the U.S. Senate. As leader of the "Stalwart" faction of the Republican Party, he controlled federal patronage in New York for more than a decade and was one of the most powerful figures in Gilded Age politics.

Early life and House career

Conkling was born in Albany, the son of Alfred Conkling, a congressman and federal judge. He studied law, was admitted to the bar, and settled in Utica, where he served as mayor in 1858. Elected to the House that year, he became a prominent War Republican during the Civil War and later a leading Radical Republican during Reconstruction, helping to draft the Fourteenth Amendment as a member of the Joint Committee on Reconstruction.

Senate career

Chosen by the state legislature for the Senate in 1867, Conkling built a formidable political machine anchored by the New York Custom House, whose collector he controlled. He championed hard money, opposed civil service reform as an invasion of senatorial prerogative, and was a key supporter of President Ulysses S. Grant, whose attempted third-term nomination Conkling backed at the 1880 Republican convention.

His feud with President James A. Garfield over control of New York patronage culminated in 1881, when Conkling and his junior colleague Thomas C. Platt resigned their seats in protest, expecting vindicating re-election by the legislature. The maneuver failed; neither was returned. Conkling declined a seat on the U.S. Supreme Court—he was the last person confirmed to the Court who declined to serve—and practiced law lucratively in New York City.

Death and legacy

During the Great Blizzard of 1888, Conkling insisted on walking home from his office through the storm, collapsed, and died of pneumonia weeks later. Remembered for his commanding oratory, imposing presence and imperious manner, he epitomized the spoils-system politics that reformers of his era sought to dismantle.
