FormulaF = −kx (springs)

Hooke's law is a law of physics stating that the force needed to extend or compress a spring by some distance is proportional to that distance, provided the elastic limit is not exceeded. It is named after the 17th-century British physicist Robert Hooke, who first stated it in 1676 as a Latin anagram and published the solution in 1678: ut tensio, sic vis — "as the extension, so the force."

Formal statement

For a spring, the law takes the form F = −kx, where x is the displacement from equilibrium, k is the spring constant, and the negative sign indicates that the restoring force opposes the displacement. In continuum mechanics the law generalises to the relation between stress and strain in elastic materials: stress is proportional to strain, with the constant of proportionality given by elastic moduli such as Young's modulus.

Applications and limits

Hooke's law underlies spring scales, balances, seismometers and the theory of elasticity used in engineering. It is an approximation valid only within the proportional limit of a material; beyond the elastic limit, materials deform permanently or fracture.
