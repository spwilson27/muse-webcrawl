The four Yamanaka factors can rewind a differentiated cell to pluripotency.

The Yamanaka factors are a set of four transcription factors — Oct4 (Pou5f1), Sox2, Klf4 and c-Myc, often abbreviated OSKM — whose forced expression can reprogram an ordinary adult (somatic) cell into an induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC), capable of developing into almost any cell type of the body.

Discovery

In 2006, Shinya Yamanaka and Kazutoshi Takahashi at Kyoto University screened 24 candidate genes associated with embryonic stem cells and found that just these four, introduced into mouse fibroblasts by retroviruses, produced pluripotent colonies. The feat was extended to human cells in 2007 by Yamanaka's group and, with a different factor set (OCT4, SOX2, NANOG, LIN28), by James Thomson's team. Yamanaka shared the 2012 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with John Gurdon for the discovery that mature cells can be reprogrammed to pluripotency.

Mechanism and use

The factors bind across the genome and progressively remodel chromatin, silencing the somatic programme and reactivating the pluripotency network centred on Oct4, Sox2 and Nanog. Because c-Myc can promote tumours, researchers have sought safer delivery methods — non-integrating viruses, mRNA, proteins and small molecules. iPSCs generated with the Yamanaka factors are now widely used for disease modelling, drug screening, and experimental regenerative therapies. Transient (partial) expression of the factors can also reset epigenetic marks of aging in cells and tissues without erasing cell identity, an approach known as partial reprogramming.
