Dhyana in Buddhism

In the oldest texts of Buddhism, dhyana (Pali: jhana) is the training of the mind, commonly translated as meditation, to withdraw the mind from automatic responses to sense impressions and attain a state of perfect equanimity and awareness. It is a central element of the Noble Eightfold Path as "right concentration" (samma samadhi).

The four jhanas

The early discourses describe four progressive states of meditative absorption. In the first jhana, the meditator, secluded from sensuality and unwholesome states, experiences rapture and pleasure born of seclusion, accompanied by thought. In the second, thought subsides and inner tranquillity and unification of mind arise. In the third, rapture fades, leaving equanimity and mindful contentment. In the fourth, even pleasure and pain are abandoned, leaving pure equanimity and mindfulness.

Formless attainments

Beyond the four material jhanas, texts describe four immaterial attainments: the base of boundless space, the base of boundless consciousness, the base of nothingness, and the base of neither-perception-nor-non-perception. These are sometimes counted as further jhanas.

Role in practice

The jhanas are cultivated as a basis for insight (vipassana) into the three marks of existence. Later traditions debated the depth of concentration required and developed varying interpretations of the jhana factors, a discussion that continues in modern Buddhism.
