Tragic hero

A tragic hero is the protagonist of a tragedy. In his Poetics, Aristotle records the descriptions of the tragic hero to the playmaker and lists the requirements for the protagonist. The tragic hero evokes in the audience feelings of pity and fear (catharsis) through a reversal of fortune brought about not by vice or depravity but by some error or frailty (hamartia).

Aristotle's criteria

According to Aristotle, the tragic hero should be a figure of high status whose fall is more affecting than that of an ordinary person; neither perfectly good nor wholly villainous; brought down by a mistake or judgment error — often compounded by hubris (excessive pride) — rather than by inherent evil; and should undergo a recognition (anagnorisis) of the truth, often at the moment of reversal (peripeteia). Classic examples include Oedipus in Sophocles' Oedipus Rex and Creon in Antigone.

Later conceptions

Shakespearean tragedy produced tragic heroes such as Hamlet, Othello, Macbeth, and King Lear, whose inner conflicts drive the catastrophe. Later critics, including A. C. Bradley, analyzed these characters' "fatal flaws." In modern drama, Arthur Miller's essay "Tragedy and the Common Man" argued that ordinary people — exemplified by Willy Loman in Death of a Salesman — could be tragic heroes. The concept overlaps with the antihero and the Byronic hero in later literature.
