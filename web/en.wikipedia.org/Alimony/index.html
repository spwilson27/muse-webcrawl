Alimony

AreaFamily law
Also known asSpousal support, spousal maintenance
Related conceptsChild support, division of property

Alimony, also called aliment (Scotland), maintenance (England, Ireland, Northern Ireland, Wales, Canada, New Zealand), spousal support (U.S., Canada) and spouse maintenance (Australia), is a legal obligation on a person to provide financial support to their spouse before or after marital separation or divorce. The obligation arises from the divorce law or family law of each country. In most jurisdictions, it is distinct from child support, where, after divorce, one parent is required to contribute to the support of their children by paying the other parent.

The term derives from the Latin alimōnia, meaning nourishment or sustenance. Historically, alimony reflected the husband's duty to support his wife; modern statutes are typically gender-neutral and base awards on need, ability to pay, the duration of the marriage, and the standard of living during it.

Types

Courts may award several kinds of alimony, including:

  • Temporary alimony — support during the divorce proceedings.
  • Rehabilitative alimony — support for a limited period while the recipient gains education or training to become self-supporting.
  • Permanent alimony — ongoing support, usually after a long marriage, until death or the recipient's remarriage.
  • Reimbursement alimony — repayment for contributions to the other spouse's education or career.
  • Lump-sum alimony — a fixed amount paid at once or in instalments instead of periodic payments.

United States

In the United States, alimony is governed by state law and varies widely between states. Factors commonly considered include the length of the marriage, each spouse's income and earning capacity, age and health, contributions to the marriage, and marital misconduct in some states. Beginning in the 2010s, several states reformed their alimony statutes to limit permanent awards. Since 2019, under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, alimony payments are no longer tax-deductible by the payer nor taxable income to the recipient for agreements executed after 2018.

History

The concept of alimony can be traced to the Code of Hammurabi and was developed in English ecclesiastical law, where it was awarded when a marriage was separated but not dissolved. With the introduction of no-fault divorce in the late 20th century, the rationale for alimony shifted toward economic fairness and the recipient's need rather than fault.
