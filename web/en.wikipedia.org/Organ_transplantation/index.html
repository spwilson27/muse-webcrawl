Organ transplantation is a medical procedure in which an organ is removed from one body and placed in the body of a recipient, to replace a damaged or missing organ. The donor and recipient may be at the same location, or organs may be transported from a donor site to another location. Organs and/or tissues that are transplanted within the same person's body are called autografts; transplants between two people are allografts.

Organs that have been successfully transplanted include the heart, kidneys, liver, lungs, pancreas, intestine, thymus and uterus. Tissues include bones, tendons, corneae, skin, heart valves, nerves and veins. Worldwide, the kidneys are the most commonly transplanted organs, followed by the liver and then the heart.

History

Early attempts at transplantation date to antiquity (Sushruta's skin grafts) and to 1900s vascular-suturing experiments by Alexis Carrel, but immune rejection defeated most efforts. The first successful solid-organ transplant was a kidney between identical twins performed by Joseph Murray in Boston in 1954. Christiaan Barnard performed the first heart transplant in Cape Town in 1967. The introduction of the immunosuppressant ciclosporin in the late 1970s dramatically improved outcomes and made transplantation routine.

Rejection and immunosuppression

The recipient's immune system recognises a graft as foreign and may destroy it (hyperacute, acute or chronic rejection). Matching of ABO blood groups and HLA tissue types, plus lifelong immunosuppressive drugs (e.g. ciclosporin, tacrolimus, mycophenolate), reduce this risk at the cost of increased susceptibility to infection and cancer.

Donation and ethics

Organs come from living donors (a kidney, a liver lobe) or deceased donors after brain death or circulatory death. Chronic global shortages drive waiting lists, allocation systems and debates over presumed consent ("opt-out") laws, organ trafficking and xenotransplantation — experimental transplants from animals, including genetically modified pig hearts and kidneys in the 2020s.
