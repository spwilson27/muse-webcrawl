Capsaicinoid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Capsaicinoids are a class of chemical compounds produced as secondary metabolites by chili peppers of the genus Capsicum. They are responsible for the pungent ("hot") sensation produced when the peppers are eaten, acting by stimulating the TRPV1 receptor in mammals, which ordinarily senses heat and physical abrasion.

Major capsaicinoids

The principal capsaicinoids are: capsaicin (typically about 69% of the total), dihydrocapsaicin (about 22%), nordihydrocapsaicin (about 7%), homocapsaicin, and homodihydrocapsaicin. Pure capsaicin rates about 16 million Scoville heat units (SHU), while nordihydrocapsaicin rates about 9.1 million SHU. Nonivamide (pelargonic acid vanillylamide, PAVA), a related compound used in pepper spray, is sometimes classed with the capsaicinoids.

Biosynthesis and distribution

Capsaicinoids are synthesized in the placental tissue of the fruit where seeds attach, by condensation of vanillylamine with fatty acids of differing chain lengths. They serve as a deterrent against mammalian herbivores—which grind seeds and whose digestion reduces germination—while not deterring birds, which disperse the seeds and whose TRPV1 receptors are insensitive to capsaicin.

Uses

Beyond culinary use, capsaicinoids are employed in topical analgesic creams for neuropathic pain, in self-defence sprays, and as anti-fouling agents and animal repellents. High-dose capsaicin patches (8%) are approved for treating certain chronic pain conditions, exploiting the desensitization of TRPV1-expressing nerve fibres after initial excitation.
