The Theogony (Greek: Θεογονία, Theogonía, "the genealogy or birth of the gods") is a poem by Hesiod (8th–7th century BC) describing the origins and genealogies of the Greek gods, composed c. 730–700 BC. It is written in the Epic dialect of Homeric Greek and comprises 1,022 lines. It is one of the most important sources for the understanding of early Greek cosmogony.

Contents and structure

The poem opens with an invocation to the Muses of Mount Helicon, who breathe into Hesiod a "divine voice" so that he may sing of things past and future. This hymnic proem (lines 1–115) is followed by the main body of the work: an account of the origin of the cosmos and the successive generations of gods, from the primordial entities Chaos, Gaia (Earth), Tartarus, and Eros, through the Titans, to the rule of Zeus and the Olympians.

Central episodes include the castration of Uranus by Cronus, the birth of Aphrodite from the sea foam, the Titanomachy (the war between the Titans and Olympians), the punishment of Prometheus, and Zeus's defeat of the monster Typhoeus, which secures his cosmic order. The poem closes with catalogues of goddesses who lay with mortal men and their offspring.

The succession myth

Scholars have long noted parallels between the Theogony's succession myth — Uranus, Cronus, Zeus — and Near Eastern myths such as the Hittite-Hurrian Kumarbi cycle, suggesting that Greek cosmogony adapted elements from the literatures of the Near East. The theme of a god who devours or confines his offspring, and of generational conflict among the gods, likewise has Near Eastern analogues.

Influence

Together with the Iliad and Odyssey of Homer and Hesiod's own Works and Days, the Theogony formed the foundation of the standard Greek account of the gods, shaping later literature from Greek tragedy to Apollodorus's Bibliotheca and Roman poets such as Ovid.
