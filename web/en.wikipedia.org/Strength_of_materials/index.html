Strength of materials

The strength of materials (also called mechanics of materials) is the behavior of solid objects subject to stresses and strains. The theory began with the consideration of the behavior of one- and two-dimensional members of structures, whose states of stress can be approximated as two dimensional, and was then generalized to three dimensions to develop a more complete theory of the elastic and plastic behavior of materials. An important founding pioneer in mechanics of materials was Stephen Timoshenko.

The strength of a material often refers to various modes of loading, such as tensile, compressive, and shear. Key concepts include the stress–strain curve, yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, elasticity (Young's modulus), and the behavior of beams in bending, torsion of shafts, buckling of columns, and deflection analysis.

Design considerations

In designing structures and machines, these considerations allow an engineer to select materials and dimensions so that a structure performs its function without failure. Design usually employs a factor of safety applied to allowable stress, and considers loading cases including fatigue, creep, and stress concentrations at holes and notches.

Loading types

Transverse loading produces bending moments and shear forces in beams; axial loading produces tensile or compressive stress; torsional loading twists prismatic elements. Combined loading is resolved using methods such as superposition and Mohr's circle, and failure is predicted with criteria such as the von Mises and Tresca theories.
