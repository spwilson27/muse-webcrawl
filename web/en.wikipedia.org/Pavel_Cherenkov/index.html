Pavel Cherenkov

Pavel Alekseyevich Cherenkov (1904–1990) was a Soviet physicist who discovered the electromagnetic radiation emitted when a charged particle passes through a medium faster than the phase velocity of light in that medium. Cherenkov radiation — the characteristic blue glow seen in nuclear reactor cores — became a fundamental tool of particle detection. He shared the 1958 Nobel Prize in Physics with Ilya Frank and Igor Tamm.

Discovery

Working at the Lebedev Physical Institute in Moscow under Sergey Vavilov, Cherenkov in 1934 observed a faint blue light emitted by a bottle of water subjected to radioactive bombardment. He carefully established that the glow was not fluorescence and that it appeared in many pure liquids. Frank and Tamm provided the theoretical explanation in 1937: a particle traveling faster than light in the medium emits a cone of radiation, analogous to a sonic boom.

Legacy

Cherenkov detectors, which record the light cone to measure a particle's velocity, are standard instruments in particle physics, neutrino observatories, and cosmic-ray research. Cherenkov spent his career at the Lebedev Institute, later working in cosmic-ray physics and on the synchrotron program.
