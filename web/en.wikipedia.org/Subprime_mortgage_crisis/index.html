Subprime mortgage crisis

Subprime mortgage crisis

The subprime mortgage crisis was a U.S. financial crisis that unfolded between 2007 and 2010, triggered by the collapse of a housing bubble and widespread defaults on subprime and adjustable-rate mortgages. Because risky mortgages had been bundled into mortgage-backed securities and collateralized debt obligations held worldwide, losses cascaded through the global financial system, producing the financial crisis of 2007–2008 and the Great Recession.

Background

During the early 2000s, low interest rates, strong investor demand for mortgage securities, and incentives throughout the originate-to-distribute lending chain fueled a boom in lending to borrowers with weak credit. Subprime lending grew from a small niche to roughly a fifth of U.S. mortgage originations by 2005–2006, often featuring teaser rates, interest-only periods, and little income documentation. Meanwhile house prices climbed far beyond historical norms relative to incomes and rents.

Unraveling

As the Federal Reserve raised rates and house prices stalled and fell from 2006, adjustable-rate loans reset to unaffordable payments and delinquencies surged. Subprime lenders such as New Century failed in early 2007. In August 2007 interbank lending froze as banks could not value mortgage-linked assets. The crisis intensified through 2008: Bear Stearns was sold under duress, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac entered conservatorship, Lehman Brothers filed the largest bankruptcy in U.S. history on September 15, 2008, and insurer AIG was rescued with massive federal support. The U.S. Congress authorized the $700 billion Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) to stabilize banks.

Consequences and response

Millions of households lost homes to foreclosure; unemployment reached 10 percent in October 2009. Policy responses included near-zero interest rates, quantitative easing, bank stress tests, and the Dodd–Frank Act of 2010, which created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and new rules for derivatives and systemically important institutions. Investigations, including the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, cited failures of corporate governance, risk management, credit-rating agencies, and regulation on all sides.
