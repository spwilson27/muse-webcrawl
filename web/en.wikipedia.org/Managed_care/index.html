Managed care

Managed care is a set of arrangements, most prominent in the United States, intended to reduce the cost of providing health care while improving its quality. Under managed care, insurers or dedicated managed care organisations contract with networks of physicians, hospitals and other providers, and use mechanisms such as utilisation review, prior authorisation, gatekeeping by primary-care physicians, capitation payments and formularies to control the volume and cost of services.

Forms

The main organisational forms are the health maintenance organisation (HMO), which typically requires members to use in-network providers and obtain referrals, and the preferred provider organisation (PPO), which offers more flexibility at higher cost. Point-of-service plans combine features of both. Managed care became widespread in the US after the Health Maintenance Organization Act of 1973 and dominated employer-sponsored insurance by the 1990s.

Criticism and backlash

Critics argued that managed care gave insurers incentives to deny necessary treatment and interfered with clinical judgement; a consumer and provider backlash in the late 1990s led to "patients' bill of rights" proposals, looser network restrictions, and state regulation of practices such as "drive-through deliveries". Supporters respond that fee-for-service payment rewards overuse and that managed care techniques can curb unnecessary care.
