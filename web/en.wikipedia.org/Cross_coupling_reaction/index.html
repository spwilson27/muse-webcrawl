Cross-coupling reaction

A cross-coupling reaction in organic chemistry is a reaction in which two different fragments are joined together with the aid of a metal catalyst, typically forming a new carbon–carbon or carbon–heteroatom bond. Cross-couplings are among the most important methods in modern synthetic chemistry, used extensively in the pharmaceutical and materials industries to assemble complex molecules from simpler building blocks.

Types

Major named reactions include the Suzuki–Miyaura coupling (aryl halides with boronic acids), the Heck reaction (aryl halides with alkenes), the Sonogashira coupling (aryl halides with terminal alkynes), the Negishi coupling (organozinc reagents), the Stille coupling (organotin reagents), the Kumada coupling (Grignard reagents), and the Buchwald–Hartwig amination for carbon–nitrogen bonds. Most proceed through a catalytic cycle of oxidative addition, transmetalation or migratory insertion, and reductive elimination at a palladium or nickel centre.

Recognition

The 2010 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded to Richard F. Heck, Ei-ichi Negishi, and Akira Suzuki "for palladium-catalyzed cross couplings in organic synthesis," recognising the transformative impact of these methods on the synthesis of medicines, agrochemicals, and organic electronic materials.
