Contrapositive

In logic and mathematics, the contrapositive of a conditional statement “if P then Q” is the statement “if not Q then not P”. A statement and its contrapositive are logically equivalent — each is true if and only if the other is — so proving the contrapositive is a valid way to prove the original.

Form

For the implication P → Q: the converse is Q → P; the inverse is ¬P → ¬Q; and the contrapositive is ¬Q → ¬P. The converse and inverse are equivalent to each other but not to the original; confusing a statement with its converse is the fallacy of affirming the consequent.

Example

“If it is raining, the ground is wet” has the contrapositive “If the ground is not wet, it is not raining.” In mathematics: “If n2 is even, then n is even” is readily proved via its contrapositive — if n is odd, then n = 2k + 1 and n2 = 4k2 + 4k + 1 is odd.

Proof by contraposition

Proof by contraposition is an inference rule of classical logic and a standard proof technique; unlike proof by contradiction, it does not assume the negation of the conclusion together with the hypothesis, but directly establishes the contrapositive implication.
