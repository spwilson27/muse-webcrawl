Westerlies

The westerlies are the prevailing winds between roughly 30° and 60° latitude in each hemisphere, blowing from west to east.

The westerlies, also called the prevailing westerlies, are prevailing winds from the west toward the east in the middle latitudes between 30 and 60 degrees latitude. They originate from the high-pressure areas in the horse latitudes and trend towards the poles and steer extratropical cyclones in this general manner. Tropical cyclones which cross the subtropical ridge axis into the westerlies recurve due to the increased westerly flow.

Behaviour

The westerlies are strongest in the winter hemisphere, when pressure over the poles is lower, and weakest in summer. They are especially strong in the Southern Hemisphere, where there is less land to cause friction and slow them down; the band of roaring forties, furious fifties and screaming sixties between 40° and 60° S, over the open Southern Ocean, experiences some of the strongest sustained winds on Earth.

The winds are not steady but meander in great waves (Rossby waves) around the globe, and the jet streams embedded within them guide the movement of low-pressure systems that bring much of the weather of the mid-latitudes — to North America, Europe, temperate Asia, southern South America, South Africa, Australia and New Zealand.

Role in exploration and trade

Together with the trade winds, the westerlies made possible efficient sailing routes that European ships used on return voyages, and in the age of the clipper ships the "Clipper Route" exploited the Roaring Forties to run from Europe to Australia and back. Aircraft flying eastward in the mid-latitudes also exploit the associated jet streams to save time and fuel.
