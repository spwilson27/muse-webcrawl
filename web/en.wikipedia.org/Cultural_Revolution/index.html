The Cultural Revolution, formally the Great Proletarian Cultural Revolution, was a sociopolitical movement in the People's Republic of China launched by Mao Zedong in 1966 and lasting until his death in 1976. Its stated goal was to preserve Chinese communism by purging remnants of capitalist and traditional elements from society; in practice Mao used it to reassert control over the Communist Party after the disaster of the Great Leap Forward, mobilising urban youth against his rivals and the party bureaucracy itself.

Course of the movement

Students organised as Red Guards attacked the "Four Olds" — old ideas, culture, customs and habits — destroying temples, books and artefacts, and subjecting teachers, officials and intellectuals to humiliation, struggle sessions, imprisonment and violence. Senior leaders including Liu Shaoqi, the head of state, and Deng Xiaoping were purged; Liu died in prison in 1969. By 1967–68 factional fighting among Red Guard groups approached civil war in several provinces, prompting Mao to deploy the People's Liberation Army, demobilise the Red Guards, and send some 17 million urban youth "up to the mountains and down to the countryside". The Lin Biao affair of 1971 — Mao's designated successor died fleeing after an alleged coup plot — further disillusioned the public.

Human and cultural cost

Historians estimate deaths ranging from hundreds of thousands to several million, with tens of millions persecuted. Education was disrupted for a decade: universities largely closed, then reopened for politically selected "worker-peasant-soldier students". Countless cultural relics were destroyed despite Premier Zhou Enlai's protection of sites such as the Forbidden City.

Aftermath

Weeks after Mao's death on 9 September 1976, the Gang of Four — including his widow Jiang Qing — were arrested. The Party's 1981 resolution judged the Cultural Revolution "a severe setback" and "internal chaos" wrongly launched by Mao and exploited by counter-revolutionary cliques. Deng Xiaoping's subsequent reform and opening era repudiated its economics while the Party retains tight control of its public memory.
