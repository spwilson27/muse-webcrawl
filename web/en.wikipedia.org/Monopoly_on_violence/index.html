Monopoly on violence

The monopoly on violence, or monopoly on the legitimate use of physical force, is a core concept of the modern state in political science and sociology. It holds that within a given territory, the state alone claims the right to use or authorise physical force, and that all other actors may use violence only with the state's permission.

Weber's formulation

The concept is most closely associated with the sociologist Max Weber, who in his 1919 lecture Politics as a Vocation defined the state as "a human community that (successfully) claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory." For Weber, legitimacy is essential: the monopoly rests not merely on the state's superior capacity for force but on the population's belief that its use of force is rightful.

Historical development

The consolidation of the monopoly on violence was a long historical process, traced by historians such as Norbert Elias in his account of the "civilising process" and by Charles Tilly, who summarised state formation as "war made the state, and the state made war." Feudal levies, private armies, feuds, duelling, and mercenary companies were progressively suppressed or absorbed into standing armies and professional police forces under central authority.

Challenges and criticism

The monopoly is never absolute in practice. Organised crime, insurgency, terrorism, and paramilitary groups contest it; its breakdown is a hallmark of the failed state. Critics from anarchist and libertarian traditions question the legitimacy of the monopoly itself, while others note that states routinely delegate violence to private actors such as security companies and military contractors.
