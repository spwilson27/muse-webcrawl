Referendum

A referendum is a direct vote by the electorate on a particular proposal or issue. This is in contrast to an issue being voted on by a representative assembly. Referendums can be binding, requiring a government to implement the result, or advisory/consultative. They form one of the principal instruments of direct democracy, alongside initiatives and recalls.

Types

Referendums may be mandatory, when a constitution requires them for certain changes, or optional, when a legislature or a qualifying petition of citizens triggers the vote. A plebiscite is sometimes distinguished as a vote on a question of sovereignty or a vote of confidence in a government, though the terms are often used interchangeably.

History and use

Switzerland holds referendums more frequently than any other country, with votes at federal, cantonal and municipal level several times a year. Ireland requires referendums for constitutional amendments. Nationwide referendums decided British membership of the European Union in 1975 and its withdrawal ("Brexit") in 2016. In the United States there is no national referendum, but many states provide for ballot initiatives and referendums.

Criticism

Critics argue referendums can oversimplify complex issues, be swung by short-term sentiment or campaign spending, and allow majorities to override minority rights. Supporters counter that they confer legitimacy on major decisions and check unresponsive legislatures.
