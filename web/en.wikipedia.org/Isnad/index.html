Isnad

A hadith manuscript with its isnad

In hadith studies, the isnad (Arabic: إسناد, "support" or "attribution") is the chain of narrators through which a report (hadith) about the words or actions of the Prophet Muhammad — or of his companions — was transmitted. Together with the text itself (the matn), the isnad forms the two-part structure of a hadith.

Function

Because hadith were transmitted orally and in writing over generations, Muslim scholars developed the isnad as a means of verification: each report was expected to be traced through a continuous, unbroken chain of named transmitters back to its source. The saying attributed to Ibn al-Mubarak captures its importance: "The isnad is part of the religion; were it not for the isnad, anyone could say whatever he wished."

Science of narrators

The evaluation of isnads generated the discipline of 'ilm al-rijal ("science of the men"), biographical dictionaries assessing each narrator's memory, character, reliability and contemporaneity with those they claimed to hear from. Chains could be graded continuous (muttasil), broken (mursal, munqati'), or fabricated, and reports classified accordingly as sound (sahih), good (hasan) or weak (da'if).

Scholarly debate

Western academic scholarship since Ignác Goldziher and Joseph Schacht has debated the historical reliability of isnads, with some arguing that chains grew backwards over time; other scholars, including specialists in isnad-cum-matn analysis, have argued for earlier dating of many traditions.
