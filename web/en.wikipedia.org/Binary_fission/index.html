Binary fission

Binary fission is a form of asexual reproduction and cell division employed by prokaryotes and some single-celled eukaryotes. A parent cell replicates its DNA and divides into two roughly equal daughter cells, each a genetic copy of the parent.

Process in bacteria

Replication begins at the origin of replication on the circular chromosome. As DNA is copied, the origins move apart; the cell elongates, a septum forms at mid-cell guided by the FtsZ ring, and the cell splits. Under ideal conditions some bacteria divide every 20 minutes, though growth depends on nutrients and environment.

Types

Fission may be irregular, longitudinal, transverse or oblique depending on the organism. Related forms include multiple fission, fragmentation and budding. In organelles such as mitochondria and chloroplasts—descendants of bacteria—division by fission also occurs inside eukaryotic cells.

Comparison with mitosis

Binary fission is simpler than mitosis: there is no spindle apparatus or nuclear envelope breakdown because prokaryotes lack a membrane-bound nucleus. The result is rapid population growth but limited genetic diversity except through mutation and horizontal gene transfer.
