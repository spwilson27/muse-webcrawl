Military rank

A military rank is a position in the hierarchy of an armed force, denoting a member's authority, seniority, pay grade and responsibilities relative to others. Rank systems divide personnel into commissioned officers, non-commissioned officers (NCOs) and enlisted ranks, and are displayed through insignia — stripes, stars, bars, pips and crowns — worn on uniforms.

Structure

Commissioned officers hold authority derived from a commission granted by the head of state, progressing in most land forces from second lieutenant through captain (company command), major and colonel (battalion and regimental command), to general officers (brigadier, major general, lieutenant general, general) commanding brigades, divisions, corps and armies. Naval ranks run from ensign/midshipman to admiral, with captain both a rank and a ship command. Warrant officers occupy a specialist tier between NCOs and commissioned officers in some countries. Enlisted ranks progress from private (or seaman/airman) through lance corporal and corporal to sergeant grades, culminating in senior appointments such as sergeant major or chief petty officer.

Standardisation

NATO assigns rank codes (OF-1 to OF-10 for officers, OR-1 to OR-9 for other ranks) so that equivalent grades across allied nations can be compared for protocol, command and pay purposes. Rank also interacts with time-in-grade, merit promotion boards and, historically, purchase of commissions.

History

Roman legions had a recognisable hierarchy from legionary to legate via centurions. Modern rank structures crystallised in the standing armies of the 17th and 18th centuries. Revolutionary states sometimes abolished rank titles — the early Red Army used functional titles instead — before reinstating them as professionalisation prevailed.
