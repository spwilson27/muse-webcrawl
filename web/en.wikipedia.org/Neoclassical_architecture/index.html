Neoclassical architecture
Active periodc. 1750 – 1850 (revivals into 20th c.)
OriginsReaction against Baroque and Rococo; renewed study of Greek and Roman antiquity

Neoclassical architecture is an architectural style produced by the Neoclassical movement that began in the mid-18th century in France and England, manifesting a reaction against the ornate Rococo style and a return to the perceived purity of the arts of Rome and, later, ancient Greece. It drew on renewed archaeological study — notably the excavations at Pompeii and Herculaneum — and on the writings of Johann Joachim Winckelmann.

Characteristics

Neoclassical buildings are characterized by grandeur of scale, simplicity of geometric forms, dramatic use of columns, a preference for blank walls, and sharp contrasts of volume and light. Public buildings — museums, libraries, capitols, banks and churches — adopted temple fronts and porticoes to associate civic institutions with the virtues of antiquity.

Regional developments

In France, architects such as Jacques-Germain Soufflot (Sainte-Geneviève, now the Panthéon, Paris) and Claude Nicolas Ledoux developed a stripped, monumental classicism. In Britain, Robert Adam popularized a refined decorative classicism in country houses and urban terraces. Karl Friedrich Schinkel shaped Berlin with works such as the Altes Museum. In the United States, Thomas Jefferson championed classical forms for the new republic, designing Monticello and the University of Virginia, while Benjamin Henry Latrobe introduced the Greek Revival style in buildings such as the Bank of Pennsylvania and the United States Capitol.

Later influence

By the mid-19th century Neoclassicism was challenged by the Gothic Revival and other historicisms, yet it persisted in academic and civic architecture well into the 20th century — visible in Washington, D.C.'s monumental core — and has influenced later "New Classical" architecture.
