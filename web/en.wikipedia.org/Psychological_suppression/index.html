Psychological suppression

Suppression is a psychological defence mechanism in which a person consciously pushes unwanted thoughts, feelings or impulses out of awareness, in contrast to repression, which is held to operate unconsciously. In Anna Freud's classification of defence mechanisms, suppression is considered a mature defence because it involves deliberate postponement of attention to a distressing content until it can be dealt with.

Research

Experimental work on thought suppression, notably Daniel Wegner's "white bear" studies, showed that deliberately trying not to think of something can paradoxically increase its later accessibility — the rebound effect explained by ironic process theory. Chronic suppression of emotional expression has been linked in some studies to increased physiological arousal and worse social outcomes, while short-term suppression can be adaptive in emergencies or professional settings.

Clinical significance

In psychotherapy, distinguishing suppression from repression and avoidance matters for treatment planning. Suppression can be a healthy coping strategy when temporary and flexible, but rigid reliance on it may contribute to anxiety, intrusive thoughts and emotional numbing.
