Max Horkheimer

Max Horkheimer (14 February 1895 – 7 July 1973) was a German philosopher and sociologist who was famous for his work in critical theory as a member of the "Frankfurt School" of social research. Horkheimer addressed authoritarianism, militarism, economic disruption, environmental crisis, and the poverty of mass culture using the philosophy of history and social theory.

His major works include Eclipse of Reason (1947), Between Philosophy and Social Science (1930–1938), and, in collaboration with Theodor W. Adorno, Dialectic of Enlightenment (1947). Through the Institute for Social Research (Institut für Sozialforschung), Horkheimer planned, supported, and made other significant works possible.

Life

Born in Stuttgart to a wealthy Jewish textile manufacturer, Horkheimer left school to work in the family business before studying philosophy and psychology at Munich, Freiburg, and Frankfurt. He earned his doctorate in 1922 under Hans Cornelius. In 1930 he became director of the Institute for Social Research at the University of Frankfurt. After the Nazi seizure of power in 1933, the Institute emigrated first to Geneva and then to New York, where it affiliated with Columbia University; Horkheimer became a US citizen in 1940. He returned to Frankfurt in 1949, re-established the Institute, and served as rector of the University of Frankfurt from 1951 to 1953.

Thought

Horkheimer coined the distinction between "traditional theory" and "critical theory", arguing that theory should aim at the emancipation of human beings from domination, not merely the description of society. In Dialectic of Enlightenment, written with Adorno during their exile in California, he argued that the Enlightenment project of rational liberation had turned into new forms of domination — through instrumental reason and the "culture industry" of mass entertainment.
