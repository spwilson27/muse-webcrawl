Benjamin Mendelsohn

Benjamin Mendelsohn (19 May 1900 – 22 February 1998) was a Romanian-born Israeli criminologist and lawyer, regarded along with Hans von Hentig and Stephen Schafer as one of the founders of victimology, the study of crime victims. As a defence attorney, Mendelsohn interviewed his clients and their victims and concluded that victims often share some degree of responsibility for their victimisation. From this he developed the first typology of victims, and in 1947 he proposed "victimology" as a new scientific discipline, presented in his paper "A New Branch of Bio-Psycho-Social Science: Victimology".

Victim typology

Mendelsohn classified victims by their degree of culpability in the crime: the completely innocent victim (such as a child); the victim with minor guilt; the victim as guilty as the offender (voluntary victim); the victim more guilty than the offender; the most guilty victim (who initiates the offence and is harmed in self-defence by the other party); and the imaginary or pseudo-victim (such as those who falsely report crimes). Although influential, the typology — especially its element of victim-blaming — has been widely criticised by later scholars and by advocates for victims' rights.

Career

Mendelsohn practised law in Bucharest before emigrating to Israel, where he continued his legal and academic work. He advocated state compensation for victims of violent crime and the systematic study of victim–offender relationships. His work, together with von Hentig's The Criminal and His Victim (1948), laid the groundwork for modern victim surveys, restitution schemes, and victim support services.

Legacy

The World Society of Victimology awards work in his spirit of advancing victims' interests, and his writings remain foundational texts in criminology courses. Critics distinguish his pioneering empirical interest in victims from the moral judgments embedded in his typology, much of which modern victimology has abandoned while retaining the discipline he named.
