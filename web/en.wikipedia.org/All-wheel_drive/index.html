All-wheel drive

All-wheel drive (AWD) is a drivetrain configuration in which a vehicle's engine (or motors) drives all four wheels, with torque distributed between the front and rear axles either continuously or on demand. The term is often used for systems fitted to road-going cars and crossovers, while "four-wheel drive" (4WD or 4×4) is conventionally reserved for off-road vehicles with a driver-selectable transfer case and low-range gearing — though usage varies by manufacturer and market.

Schematic of a typical AWD drivetrain with a centre differential.

Designs

Full-time AWD systems drive all wheels continuously, using a centre differential or viscous coupling to allow the front and rear axles to rotate at different speeds in corners. Part-time or "on-demand" systems normally drive one axle and engage the other via a clutch pack when slip is detected. Notable implementations include Audi's quattro, Subaru's Symmetrical All-Wheel Drive, and Mitsubishi's Super All Wheel Control. Electric vehicles can achieve AWD simply by fitting a motor to each axle, eliminating mechanical linkages.

Advantages and disadvantages

AWD improves traction when accelerating on slippery surfaces and can enhance stability, which is why it is popular in snowy climates and in high-performance cars. Against this, the additional hardware adds weight, cost, and mechanical losses, typically increasing fuel consumption, and it does not improve braking or cornering grip beyond the limits of the tyres.

History

Early four-wheel-drive cars included the Dutch Spyker of 1903. The concept reached prominence in rallying with the Audi Quattro of 1980, whose dominance in the World Rally Championship popularized AWD for road cars.
