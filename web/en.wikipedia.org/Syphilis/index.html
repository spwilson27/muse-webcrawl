Syphilis

Syphilis is a sexually transmitted infection caused by the bacterium Treponema pallidum subspecies pallidum. The disease usually begins as a painless sore (chancre) on the genitals, rectum, or mouth, and progresses through characteristic stages if untreated. Syphilis can also be transmitted from mother to unborn child (congenital syphilis), causing stillbirth or serious disease in newborns. The disease has been called "the great imitator" because its symptoms often resemble those of other conditions.

Stages and signs

Primary syphilis presents with a single, painless chancre at the site of infection, appearing about three weeks (range 3–90 days) after exposure; it heals without treatment in three to six weeks. Secondary syphilis follows with a diffuse rash—often involving the palms and soles—plus mucous membrane lesions, condylomata lata, hair loss, sore throat, and generalised lymphadenopathy. After a latent period in which the person is asymptomatic, untreated infection may progress in about a third of cases to tertiary syphilis, years or decades later, with destructive gummas, cardiovascular disease (notably aortitis), and neurosyphilis, including tabes dorsalis and general paresis.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis relies on serologic blood tests (non-treponemal tests such as VDRL and RPR, confirmed by treponemal tests) and direct detection of spirochetes in lesion fluid by darkfield microscopy or PCR. Syphilis is readily cured in its early stages with a single intramuscular dose of long-acting benzathine penicillin G; penicillin remains universally effective, and doxycycline is an alternative for those allergic. Sexual partners must be treated, and follow-up titres confirm cure.

History and epidemiology

The origin of syphilis is debated: under the Columbian hypothesis it arrived in Europe from the Americas in the 1490s and spread virulently after the 1495 siege of Naples; the pre-Columbian hypothesis argues for an Old World presence. For centuries, treatment relied on mercury—"a night with Venus, a lifetime with Mercury"—before Ehrlich's arsphenamine (Salvarsan, 1910) and then penicillin transformed therapy. The unethical Tuskegee study (1932–1972) in the United States, in which treatment was withheld from infected men, remains a landmark scandal in research ethics.

Rates fell sharply with antibiotics but have risen again in many countries since 2000; the World Health Organization estimates several million new cases annually worldwide, with congenital syphilis a resurgent problem.
