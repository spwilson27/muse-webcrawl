Spiritualism is a religious and social movement that emerged in the United States in the 1840s, based on the belief that the spirits of the dead exist and can communicate with the living, typically through a medium. It reached its peak of popularity from the 1840s to the 1920s, attracting millions of adherents in the English-speaking world.

Origins

The movement is usually dated to 31 March 1848, when sisters Kate and Margaret Fox of Hydesville, New York, reported communicating with a spirit through mysterious rapping sounds. Public demonstrations, séances and "spirit photography" spread rapidly; the movement drew on earlier ideas from Emanuel Swedenborg and Franz Mesmer, and found fertile ground amid religious revivalism and grief following the American Civil War.

Beliefs and practice

Spiritualists hold that the soul survives death and continues to evolve in a spirit world, and that communication through mediums can provide moral guidance and evidence of the afterlife. In Britain, Spiritualist churches were organised under bodies such as the Spiritualists' National Union, with seven principles received through the mediumship of Emma Hardinge Britten. Allan Kardec's codification in France developed into Spiritism, influential in Brazil and elsewhere.

Decline and legacy

Repeated exposures of fraudulent mediums damaged the movement's credibility, though it survives today in Spiritualist churches and camps such as Lily Dale in New York. It also played a notable role in nineteenth-century reform movements, including abolitionism and women's suffrage.
