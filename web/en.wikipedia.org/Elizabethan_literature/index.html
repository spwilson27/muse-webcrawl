Elizabethan literature


The First Folio (1623), preserving much Elizabethan drama

Elizabethan literature refers to bodies of work produced during the reign of Queen Elizabeth I (1558–1603) — one of the most splendid ages of English literature, when the language itself was rapidly expanding and absorbing new vocabulary. Though the "Elizabethan era" in literature is sometimes extended into the Jacobean period, its core flowering spans the 1580s and 1590s.

Drama

The era's supreme achievement was drama. London's purpose-built playhouses — the Theatre (1576), the Rose, the Globe (1599) — supported professional companies performing works by the "University Wits" (Christopher Marlowe, Thomas Kyd, John Lyly, Robert Greene), by Ben Jonson, and above all by William Shakespeare, whose plays from the histories and romantic comedies of the 1590s to the great tragedies straddle the Elizabethan–Jacobean boundary. Marlowe's Doctor Faustus and Kyd's The Spanish Tragedy pioneered blank-verse tragedy and the revenge play.

Poetry

Poetry flourished in the sonnet sequences of Philip Sidney (Astrophel and Stella), Edmund Spenser (Amoretti) and Shakespeare, in Spenser's allegorical epic The Faerie Queene (1590–96), and in the plays' songs and the madrigal collections of the English Madrigal School. Sidney's Defence of Poesy is the period's key work of criticism.

Prose

Prose achievements include John Lyly's Euphues, Sidney's romance Arcadia, Thomas Nashe's picaresque The Unfortunate Traveller, Richard Hooker's Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity, and Francis Bacon's essays (1597 onwards). Translation enriched the language: the Geneva Bible, North's Plutarch, and Florio's Montaigne (1603) all fed the drama of the period.
