Hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathy

Hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathy (HSAN) or hereditary sensory neuropathy (HSN) is a condition used to describe any of the types of this disease which inhibit sensation. They are less common than Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease and other hereditary motor and sensory neuropathies.

SpecialtyNeurology
TypesHSAN types I–VIII
CausesGenetic mutations

The HSANs are a genetically heterogeneous group of inherited disorders of the peripheral nervous system characterised by loss of large myelinated and unmyelinated fibres, predominantly affecting sensory and autonomic neurons. Patients typically present with sensory loss — particularly to pain and temperature — leading to painless injuries, chronic ulceration, fractures, and self-mutilation, accompanied in many types by autonomic dysfunction such as impaired sweating and blood-pressure instability.

Classification

Eight types (HSAN I–VIII) have been described, with distinct genes, inheritance patterns, and ages of onset:

  • Type I — the most common adult-onset form; autosomal dominant; associated with mutations in SPTLC1 and SPTLC2, genes encoding subunits of serine palmitoyltransferase. Onset typically in the second to fourth decades with distal sensory loss, ulcers, and lancinating pains.
  • Type II — congenital sensory neuropathy; autosomal recessive; onset in infancy with severe sensory loss affecting all modalities; associated genes include WNK1, FAM134B, and KIF1A.
  • Type III — familial dysautonomia (Riley–Day syndrome), caused by mutations in ELP1 (formerly IKBKAP); particularly prevalent among people of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry; prominent autonomic instability.
  • Type IV — congenital insensitivity to pain with anhidrosis (CIPA), caused by mutations in NTRK1, encoding the receptor for nerve growth factor; marked by inability to feel pain, inability to sweat, and often intellectual disability.
  • Type V — congenital insensitivity to pain with partial anhidrosis, associated with NGF mutations.
  • Types VI–VIII — rarer, severe early-onset forms associated with genes including DST, SCN9A, and PRDM12.

Signs and symptoms

Common features include loss of pain and temperature sensation in the hands and feet, painless fractures and wounds, chronic skin and bone infections (osteomyelitis), Charcot joints, and in some types episodes of unexplained fever from impaired sweating. Autonomic features may include postural hypotension, abnormal sweating, and gastrointestinal dysmotility.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis rests on clinical examination, nerve conduction studies (which may be normal in small-fibre predominant forms), quantitative sensory testing, skin biopsy for intraepidermal nerve fibre density, and genetic testing. There is no curative treatment; management is supportive — meticulous foot care, protective footwear, prompt treatment of injuries and infections, and avoidance of unnoticed trauma — aimed at preventing ulcers and amputations.
