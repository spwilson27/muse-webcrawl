A massive open online course (MOOC /muːk/) is an online course aimed at unlimited participation and open access via the Web. In addition to traditional course materials, such as filmed lectures, readings, and problem sets, many MOOCs provide interactive courses with user forums or social media discussions to support community interactions among students, professors, and teaching assistants, as well as immediate feedback through quick quizzes and assignments.

History

The term was coined in 2008 by Dave Cormier of the University of Prince Edward Island in response to a course called Connectivism and Connective Knowledge, run by Stephen Downes and George Siemens, in which 25 fee-paying students were joined by some 2,200 online participants. These early courses, later called cMOOCs, emphasized networked, connectivist learning. A second wave, the xMOOCs, followed in 2011–2012 when Stanford courses by Sebastian Thrun, Peter Norvig, Andrew Ng and Daphne Koller drew enrollments in the hundreds of thousands and led to the founding of Udacity, Coursera and edX. The year 2012 was declared "The Year of the MOOC" by The New York Times.

Features and pedagogy

MOOCs typically consist of short pre-recorded video lectures segmented into weekly modules, auto-graded quizzes, peer-reviewed assignments and discussion forums. Because enrollments can reach tens or hundreds of thousands, direct instructor feedback is minimal, and assessment relies heavily on automated grading and peer review. Completion rates are low relative to enrollment—often below ten percent—though many registrants intend only to sample material.

Business models and criticism

Providers have experimented with verified certificates, proctored exams, subscriptions, micro-credentials such as Coursera's Specializations and edX's MicroMasters, and full online degrees. Critics have questioned MOOCs' completion rates, their tendency to serve already-educated learners, academic-integrity problems, and early predictions that they would displace traditional universities, which largely did not materialize; instead, MOOCs settled into professional development and lifelong-learning markets.
