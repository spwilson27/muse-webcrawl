Notochord

Notochord
Present inAll chordate embryos and some adult chordates
Derives fromMesoderm (chordamesoderm)
Gives rise toNucleus pulposus of intervertebral discs (in vertebrates)

The notochord is a flexible, rod-shaped structure composed of cells derived from the mesoderm that is the defining characteristic of the phylum Chordata. Lying along the anteroposterior axis, ventral to the central nervous system and dorsal to the gut, it provides skeletal support and acts as a key signalling centre in embryonic development.

Development

In vertebrate embryos the notochord forms during gastrulation from cells that ingress through the primitive node. It secretes signalling molecules such as Sonic hedgehog that pattern the surrounding tissues, inducing the floor plate of the neural tube and guiding the formation of the vertebral column. In most vertebrates it is largely replaced by the vertebrae during development, persisting in adults only as the nucleus pulposus at the centre of each intervertebral disc.

In other chordates

In cephalochordates such as the lancelet (amphioxus), the notochord extends the length of the body and is retained throughout life as the main axial support. In tunicates it is present only in the larval tail and is resorbed at metamorphosis. Fossil and genetic evidence indicates the notochord evolved in the earliest chordates more than 520 million years ago; the gene Brachyury, essential for its formation, is found across chordates.

Pathology

Remnants of notochordal tissue can give rise to chordomas, rare slow-growing tumours most often found at the base of the skull or in the sacrum. Persistence of notochordal tissue within a vertebra is known as a benign notochordal cell tumour.
