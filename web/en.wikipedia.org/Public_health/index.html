Public health

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Public health is "the science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life and promoting health through the organized efforts and informed choices of society, organizations, public and private, communities and individuals" (C. E. A. Winslow, 1920). It is concerned with the health of entire populations rather than with the treatment of individual patients, and is implemented through government policy, community action and health systems.

Public health practice rests on disciplines including epidemiology, biostatistics, environmental health, health economics and the social and behavioural sciences. Its characteristic interventions — sanitation, vaccination, disease surveillance, health education and the regulation of hazards — are preventive and population-level rather than clinical.

History

Modern public health emerged in the nineteenth century with urbanisation and the sanitary movement. Edwin Chadwick's 1842 report on the sanitary condition of the labouring population in Britain, John Snow's investigation of the 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak in London, and the development of germ theory by Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch laid the scientific foundations of the field. The twentieth century saw the creation of national and international institutions, culminating in the World Health Organization (WHO) in 1948.

Core functions

The Institute of Medicine identified three core functions of public health: assessment (monitoring health status and diagnosing community problems), policy development, and assurance (ensuring the provision of needed services). Commonly cited essential services include disease surveillance, outbreak investigation, immunisation, water and food safety, and health promotion.

Major achievements

Public health measures are credited with much of the increase in life expectancy during the twentieth century, including the control of infectious diseases through vaccination and sanitation, safer workplaces, motor-vehicle safety, and declines in smoking. The eradication of smallpox in 1980, coordinated by the WHO, is regarded as one of the field's greatest accomplishments.

Contemporary challenges

Current priorities include pandemic preparedness (sharpened by the COVID-19 pandemic), antimicrobial resistance, the global burden of non-communicable diseases such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes, air pollution, and health inequalities within and between countries.
