Vision quest

A vision quest is a rite of passage in some Native American cultures, especially among peoples of the Great Plains. It generally involves a period — often several days — of fasting, isolation and prayer in a remote place, undertaken to seek a vision from the spirit world. The vision may reveal a guardian spirit, guidance for the individual's life, or a new name, and the experience traditionally marked the transition from adolescence to adulthood.

TypeRite of passage
PeoplesPlains peoples and others, incl. Lakota, Crow, Blackfoot
ElementsFasting, isolation, prayer, guidance from an elder
Lakota termHanblecheya ("crying for a vision")

Practice

Details vary between nations. In the Lakota practice of hanblecheya, the seeker is prepared and guided by a medicine person, purified in a sweat lodge, and taken to an isolated spot — often a hilltop — where they remain for one to four days without food or water, praying with a sacred pipe. On their return, the vision is related to the elder, who helps interpret it. The guardian spirit encountered, frequently an animal, may guide the person for life.

Significance

The quest serves spiritual, social and personal purposes: it connects the individual with the sacred, confirms adult status within the community, and can direct decisions about vocation, healing or warfare. Vision quests were also undertaken by adults at times of crisis or before major undertakings, not only by adolescents.

Modern context

The concept has been widely borrowed by New Age and wilderness-therapy movements, a practice many Native Americans regard as cultural appropriation, particularly where commercialized "quests" imitate sacred ceremonies. Fatal incidents at imitation sweat-lodge ceremonies have intensified criticism. Traditional communities continue the practice under tribal religious leadership.
