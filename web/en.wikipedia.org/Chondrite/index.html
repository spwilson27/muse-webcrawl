Chondrite

A chondrite is a stony (non-metallic) meteorite that has not been modified by melting or differentiation of its parent body. Chondrites are named for the chondrules they contain — small, round grains of silicate minerals that formed as molten droplets in the early Solar System. They are among the most primitive materials known, with ages of about 4.56 billion years, and make up roughly 86 percent of all meteorites that fall to Earth.

Characteristics

Chondrites consist of chondrules embedded in a fine-grained matrix, together with refractory inclusions such as calcium–aluminium-rich inclusions (CAIs) — the oldest dated solids in the Solar System — and grains of metal and sulfide. Because their parent asteroids never melted, chondrites preserve a nearly unchanged record of the solar nebula from which the planets formed. Their elemental composition, apart from volatile elements, closely matches that of the Sun's photosphere.

Classification

  • Ordinary chondrites (H, L, LL) — by far the most common meteorites to fall.
  • Carbonaceous chondrites (CI, CM, CV, and others) — rich in water-bearing minerals and organic compounds; the CI group matches solar abundances most closely.
  • Enstatite chondrites — highly reduced, rich in the mineral enstatite, formed in the inner Solar System.
  • Rumuruti and Kakangari types — rare, oxidized groups.

A petrologic scale from 1 to 6 further records the degree of aqueous alteration (low numbers) or thermal metamorphism (high numbers) experienced by the rock.

Scientific importance

Carbonaceous chondrites such as the Murchison meteorite (1969) contain amino acids and other organic molecules, informing theories about the origin of life. Sample-return missions to asteroids Ryugu and Bennu have confirmed the close link between carbonaceous chondrites and their parent bodies.
