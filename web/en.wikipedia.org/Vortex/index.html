Vortex

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In fluid dynamics, a vortex (plural vortices or vortexes) is a region in a fluid in which the flow revolves around an axis line, which may be straight or curved. Vortices form in stirred fluids, including liquids, gases and plasmas, and can be seen in smoke rings, whirlpools, waterspouts, tornadoes and hurricanes, in the wakes of boats and aircraft, and in the atmosphere of Jupiter — whose Great Red Spot is a giant anticyclonic vortex.

Properties

The strength of a vortex is described by its vorticity, the curl of the velocity field, and its circulation. Two idealised models are used: the forced (rotational) vortex, in which the fluid rotates like a solid body, and the free (irrotational) vortex, in which tangential velocity increases towards the axis in inverse proportion to radius, as in a draining bathtub. Real vortices, such as the Rankine vortex, combine a rotating core with an irrotational exterior. By Helmholtz's theorems, vortex lines move with the fluid and their strength is conserved in an inviscid flow; Kármán vortex streets are the alternating patterns of vortices shed behind bluff bodies.

Examples

Wingtip vortices generated by aircraft lift are a major factor in air-traffic separation. In weather, vortices range from dust devils to tropical cyclones. In the oceans, mesoscale eddies transport heat and nutrients over basin scales. Superfluids and superconductors support quantised vortices, where circulation takes only discrete values.
