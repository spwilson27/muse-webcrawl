Hypersaline lake

Lake Hillier, Australia, coloured pink by halophilic microorganisms.

A hypersaline lake is a landlocked body of water that contains significant concentrations of sodium chloride, brines, and other salts, with saline levels surpassing that of ocean water (3.5%, i.e. 35 grams per litre). Most hypersaline lakes are endorheic: they have no outflow, so evaporation concentrates the dissolved salts delivered by inflowing streams over thousands of years.

Examples

The Dead Sea, between Israel and Jordan, with salinity around 34%, is among the saltiest large lakes on Earth. The Great Salt Lake in Utah is the largest saltwater lake in the Western Hemisphere. Small Antarctic lakes such as Don Juan Pond in the McMurdo Dry Valleys are so saline (over 40%) that they remain unfrozen despite temperatures far below 0 °C; Gaet'ale Pond in Ethiopia has been measured at about 43% salinity, among the highest of any natural water body.

Biology

Despite the hostile conditions, certain species of bacteria, algae, and archaea thrive in hypersaline lakes. The alga Dunaliella salina and various halophilic archaea produce carotenoid pigments that turn many salt lakes orange or pink. Brine shrimp (Artemia) in lakes such as the Great Salt Lake support large migratory bird populations and a commercial harvest of brine-shrimp cysts.

Properties and uses

The high dissolved-salt content makes the water exceptionally dense and buoyant. Hypersaline lakes are mined for potash, lithium, magnesium, and table salt, and their brines are increasingly important sources of lithium for batteries.
