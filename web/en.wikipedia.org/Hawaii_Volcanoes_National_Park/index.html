Hawaii Volcanoes National Park

Hawaii Volcanoes National Park is an American national park on the island of Hawaii in the state of Hawaii. Established in 1916 as part of Hawaii National Park and made a separate park in 1961, it encompasses two of the world's most active volcanoes, Kīlauea and Mauna Loa, and protects landscapes shaped by at least 70 million years of volcanism. The park covers about 335,000 acres (1,356 km²) and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and International Biosphere Reserve.

Geology

The Hawaiian Islands were built by a mantle hotspot as the Pacific Plate moves northwest over it. Kīlauea, a shield volcano on Mauna Loa's southeastern flank, erupted almost continuously from 1983 to 2018 at the Puʻu ʻŌʻō vent, adding new land to the island. A major eruption in 2018 drained the summit lava lake at Halemaʻumaʻu crater, caused the caldera floor to collapse, and sent flows through the lower East Rift Zone that destroyed hundreds of homes outside the park. Eruptions resumed in the summit crater in December 2020 and have recurred episodically since. Mauna Loa, the largest subaerial volcano on Earth, erupted in November–December 2022 for the first time in 38 years.

Features

The Kīlauea summit district, at about 4,000 feet elevation, includes the Kīlauea Visitor Center, the Jaggar Museum site, steam vents, and the rim trails around Halemaʻumaʻu. Crater Rim Drive and the Chain of Craters Road descend 3,700 feet to the coast, crossing lava flows of many ages to the Hōlei Sea Arch. The Kīlauea Iki Trail crosses a crater floor that was a molten lava lake in 1959. The Kahuku Unit, added in 2003 on Mauna Loa's southwest rift, preserves ranch lands and native forest.

Ecology and culture

The park's elevation range from sea level to Mauna Loa's 13,681-foot summit spans seven ecological zones, sheltering endangered species such as the nene (Hawaiian goose), hawksbill sea turtle and Mauna Loa silversword. In Hawaiian culture the volcanoes are the home of the goddess Pele, and the park protects numerous archaeological sites, petroglyph fields such as Puʻu Loa, and places of continuing spiritual significance to Native Hawaiians.

History

The park owes much to volcanologist Thomas Jaggar, who founded the Hawaiian Volcano Observatory in 1912, and to territorial governor Lucius Pinkham and businessman Lorrin Thurston, who championed its creation. The park was split from Haleakalā in 1961. It was designated an International Biosphere Reserve in 1980 and a World Heritage Site in 1987.
