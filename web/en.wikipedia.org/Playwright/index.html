Playwright

The playwright is the author of plays for the stage

A playwright or dramatist is a person who writes plays. The word wright is an archaic English term for a craftsman or builder (as in shipwright or wheelwright), so a playwright is literally a "maker of plays". Playwrights are also often known by the French term dramaturge in some contexts, though dramaturgy more specifically refers to the study and practice of dramatic structure.

Role and craft

The playwright composes the script — dialogue, characters, plot and stage directions — that actors, directors and designers bring to life in performance. Unlike a novelist, the playwright writes words meant to be spoken and actions meant to be seen, working within constraints of casting, staging and running time. Playwrights commonly develop new work through readings, workshops and rehearsals, revising in collaboration with a company; organisations such as the Dramatists Guild of America represent their professional interests, including copyright in the script, which the playwright (rather than the producing theatre) ordinarily retains.

History

The profession is nearly as old as theatre itself: named author-playwrights begin with the tragedians of classical Athens — Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides — and the comic writer Aristophanes. The tradition continued through Roman comedy, medieval mystery cycles, and the professional playwrights of early modern Europe, of whom William Shakespeare is the most famous, alongside figures such as Molière and Lope de Vega. In the modern era major playwrights include Anton Chekhov, Henrik Ibsen, August Strindberg, Bertolt Brecht, Samuel Beckett, Tennessee Williams, Arthur Miller and Caryl Churchill. Literary managers, new-writing theatres and prizes such as the Pulitzer Prize for Drama sustain contemporary playwriting.
