Inverse problem

An inverse problem in science is the process of calculating from a set of observations the causal factors that produced them: for example, calculating an image in X-ray computed tomography, source reconstructing in acoustics, or calculating the density of the Earth from measurements of its gravity field. It is called an inverse problem because it starts with the effects and then calculates the causes. It is the inverse of a forward problem, which starts with the causes and then calculates the effects.

Ill-posedness

Many inverse problems are ill-posed in the sense of Hadamard: a solution may not exist, may not be unique, or may not depend continuously on the data, so small measurement errors can produce large errors in the estimate. Classical examples are the inverse heat conduction problem, deconvolution, and seismic inversion.

Methods

The dominant remedy is regularization, which adds prior assumptions to stabilize the solution. Tikhonov regularization penalises rough or large solutions; Bayesian methods express the prior as a probability distribution and produce a posterior distribution over the unknown parameters. Iterative optimization, least squares, and maximum likelihood methods are widely used.

Applications

Inverse problems arise in medical imaging (CT, MRI, ultrasound), geophysics, astronomy, nondestructive testing, remote sensing, and machine learning, where parameter estimation for a model fitted to data is an inverse problem. The field's foundations were laid by Andrey Tikhonov, and dedicated journals such as Inverse Problems have published research since the 1980s.
