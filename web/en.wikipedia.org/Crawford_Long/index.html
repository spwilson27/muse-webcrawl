Crawford Long

Born: November 1, 1815, Danielsville, Georgia
Died: June 16, 1878, Athens, Georgia
Known for: First use of ether in surgery (1842)

Crawford Williamson Long (November 1, 1815 – June 16, 1878) was an American surgeon and pharmacist best known for his first use of inhaled sulfuric ether as an anesthetic in a surgical operation. On March 30, 1842, in Jefferson, Georgia, Long removed a tumour from the neck of James Venable, who inhaled ether from a towel and felt no pain — the first documented surgical anesthesia.

Discovery

Long had observed that participants in "ether frolics", social gatherings where the gas was inhaled for amusement, felt no pain from bumps and bruises. He inferred ether could abolish surgical pain and used it in several operations, including amputations and childbirth, during the 1840s. However, he did not publish his results until 1849, after Boston dentist William T. G. Morton's public demonstration of ether anesthesia in 1846 had already won Morton fame.

The anesthesia controversy

Long's priority was championed by Georgia physicians and later recognized by medical historians: he was chronologically first, though Morton's publicity is credited with spreading the practice to the world. Georgia honoured Long with a statue in the U.S. Capitol's National Statuary Hall, and March 30 is celebrated as Doctors' Day in the United States in commemoration of his 1842 operation.

Later life

Long practised medicine in Athens, Georgia, for the rest of his life, serving as a surgeon during the Civil War. He died in 1878, collapsing shortly after delivering a baby — reputedly asking, as his final concern, for the welfare of mother and child.
