8 mm film is a motion picture film format in which the film strip is eight millimetres wide. Introduced by Kodak in 1932 as an economical alternative to 16 mm, it became the standard medium for home movies for half a century.

Regular 8

The original format, called Regular 8 or Double 8, is actually exposed as 16 mm film with twice the usual number of perforations: the camera runs the roll through twice, exposing one half each time, and after processing the film is slit lengthwise into two 8 mm strips spliced end to end. A standard 25-foot roll thus yields about four minutes of footage.

Super 8 and later use

In 1965 Kodak introduced Super 8, which used true 8 mm stock in a drop-in cartridge with smaller perforations and a roughly 40 percent larger image area; a sound version followed in 1973. Fujifilm sold a rival Single-8 system. Home video displaced 8 mm in the 1980s, but Super 8 survives in niche use by filmmakers, art schools, and the film-preservation community, and Kodak has continued to manufacture stock and even announced new cameras in the digital era.
