Brothers Grimm

The Brothers Grimm, Jacob Ludwig Carl Grimm (1785–1863) and Wilhelm Carl Grimm (1786–1859), were German academics and folklorists who together collected and published German folklore. They are best known for Kinder- und Hausmärchen ("Children's and Household Tales"), first published in 1812, which contains such stories as "Cinderella", "Hansel and Gretel", "Little Red Riding Hood", "Rapunzel", "Rumpelstiltskin", "Sleeping Beauty", and "Snow White".

Biography

The brothers were born in Hanau, in Hesse, the eldest surviving sons of a lawyer, Philipp Wilhelm Grimm, whose death in 1796 left the family in reduced circumstances. They studied law at the University of Marburg, where the lectures of Friedrich Carl von Savigny awakened their interest in the historical development of language and literature. Jacob worked as a librarian in Kassel and later accompanied diplomatic missions to Paris and Vienna; both brothers held posts at the University of Göttingen, but were dismissed in 1837 as two of the "Göttingen Seven" who protested the King of Hanover's abrogation of the constitution. They spent their later years in Berlin, working on a monumental German dictionary.

The fairy tales

The first edition of the tales (1812–1815) drew on oral informants, notably Dorothea Viehmann, and on middle-class and aristocratic acquaintances in Kassel as well as older printed sources. Across seven editions, Wilhelm increasingly edited the tales for a child audience, softening sexual content while often retaining or even adding violence and Christian moralizing. The collection has been translated into more than 160 languages and is among the most widely read works of German literature; Walt Disney's animated films drew heavily on it.

Scholarly work

Jacob's Deutsche Grammatik (1819–1837) founded Germanic philology, and his formulation of the consonant shift in Germanic languages is known as Grimm's law. Together the brothers also produced the Deutsches Wörterbuch, a historical dictionary of German begun in 1838 and completed by successors in 1961, as well as editions of medieval texts and the Deutsche Sagen, a collection of German legends.
