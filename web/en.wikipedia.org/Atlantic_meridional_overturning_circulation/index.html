Atlantic meridional overturning circulation

The Atlantic meridional overturning circulation (AMOC) is the main ocean current system in the Atlantic Ocean. It is a component of Earth's ocean circulation system and plays an important role in the climate system. The AMOC includes Atlantic currents at the surface and at great depths that are driven by changes in weather, temperature and salinity. Those currents comprise about half of the global thermohaline circulation that includes currents in several other oceans.

The AMOC carries warm surface water northward and cold deep water southward.

Structure

The AMOC is characterized by a northward flow of warm, salty water in the upper layers of the Atlantic, and a southward flow of colder water in the deep Atlantic. The northward branch includes the Gulf Stream and the North Atlantic Current, which transport heat from the tropics toward the North Atlantic, moderating the climate of western Europe. In the Nordic Seas and the Labrador Sea, surface waters cool, become denser and sink to great depth in a process called deep water formation.

Deep water formation

The sinking limbs of the circulation produce North Atlantic Deep Water, which fills the deep basin of the Atlantic and eventually upwells in other ocean basins, particularly the Southern Ocean. The strength of the overturning is commonly estimated at around 15 to 20 sverdrups (millions of cubic metres per second).

Observations

Continuous monitoring of the AMOC began in 2004 with the RAPID array of moorings across the Atlantic at 26.5°N, supplemented later by the OSNAP array in the subpolar North Atlantic. These observations have revealed substantial variability on timescales from weeks to years.

Stability and climate change

Palaeoclimate records suggest the AMOC has weakened or shifted modes abruptly in the past, for example during the Younger Dryas cold interval. Climate models project that the AMOC will weaken over the 21st century in response to greenhouse gas warming and freshening of the North Atlantic from melting ice and increased precipitation, though a complete collapse before 2100 is considered unlikely in most assessments. A substantial weakening would affect sea level along the North American east coast, European weather, and tropical rainfall patterns.
