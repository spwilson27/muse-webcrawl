Refined grain

Refined grains are grain products that have been significantly modified from their natural composition through milling that removes the bran and germ from the grain kernel, leaving mainly the starchy endosperm. The process extends shelf life and produces a finer texture, but removes dietary fibre, iron, and many B vitamins. Examples include white flour, white rice, white bread, and degermed cornmeal.

Milling removes the bran and germ, leaving the endosperm.

Comparison with whole grains

A whole grain contains all three parts of the kernel — bran, germ, and endosperm — in their original proportions. Refining removes the bran (the fibre-rich outer layer) and the germ (the nutrient-dense embryo), which together contain most of the grain's fibre, minerals, and phytochemicals. Studies associate higher whole-grain intake with lower risks of cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes, while high refined-grain intake is linked to higher glycaemic load.

Enrichment

Since the 1940s, many countries have required or encouraged the enrichment of refined flour with some of the nutrients lost in milling — typically iron and the B vitamins thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, and folic acid. Enrichment restores only a subset of the lost nutrients and little of the fibre. Mandatory folic acid fortification of enriched grain products in the United States (from 1998) substantially reduced neural tube defects.

Public health guidance

Dietary guidelines in many countries recommend making at least half of grain intake whole grains. Refined grains feature prominently in ultra-processed foods, and their consumption patterns are a recurring topic in research on obesity and metabolic disease.
