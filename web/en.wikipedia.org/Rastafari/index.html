Rastafari

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rastafari, sometimes called Rastafarianism, is an Abrahamic religion and social movement that developed in Jamaica during the 1930s. Its adherents, known as Rastafari, Rastas or Rastafarians, worship Haile Selassie I, the emperor of Ethiopia from 1930 to 1974, as God incarnate and the returned messiah, and regard Ethiopia (Zion) as the promised land to which people of the African diaspora will be repatriated from exile in "Babylon" — the oppressive Western world. Drawing on the Bible, Ethiopian history, the back-to-Africa ideas of Marcus Garvey and Afrocentric thought, the movement spread worldwide largely through reggae music, most famously the work of Bob Marley.

Beliefs and practices

Rastafari is decentralised, with no single authority; adherents gather in groups called "mansions", the largest being the Nyabinghi Order, the Bobo Ashanti and the Twelve Tribes of Israel. Common beliefs include the divinity of Haile Selassie (whose pre-coronation title, Ras Tafari Makonnen, gives the movement its name), the spiritual significance of Ethiopia, and the hope of repatriation to Africa. Practice centres on livity — righteous, natural living — expressed through an ital diet (often vegetarian, avoiding pork, alcohol and added salt), abstinence from cutting the hair, which is worn in dreadlocks, groundation ceremonies of drumming, chanting and reasoning, and the sacramental use of cannabis (ganja), understood as a holy herb supporting meditation. Many Rastas use a distinctive vocabulary ("I and I", "overstand") reflecting the movement's philosophy of the divine self.

History

The movement crystallised after the coronation of Haile Selassie in 1930, widely reported in Jamaica, where preachers such as Leonard Howell, Joseph Hibbert, Archibald Dunkley and Robert Hinds proclaimed his divinity, some interpreting Garvey's words as prophecy. Howell founded the Pinnacle commune in 1940, which was repeatedly raided and eventually destroyed by colonial authorities. Persecuted for decades, Rastafari gained respectability from the 1960s: Haile Selassie's state visit to Jamaica in 1966 was a landmark, and reggae's global success in the 1970s carried Rasta symbols — the colours red, gold and green, the Lion of Judah, and dreadlocks — around the world. A Rasta community established at Shashamane, Ethiopia, on land granted by Haile Selassie, remains a focus of repatriation.
