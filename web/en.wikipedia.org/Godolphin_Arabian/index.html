Godolphin Arabian

The Godolphin Arabian, one of three foundation sires of the Thoroughbred.

The Godolphin Arabian (c. 1724 – 1753), also known as the Godolphin Barb, was an Arabian horse who was one of three stallions that founded the modern Thoroughbred breed. Along with the Darley Arabian and the Byerley Turk, nearly all modern Thoroughbreds trace their male line to one of these three sires, and the Godolphin Arabian's line — through his great-grandson Matchem — is one of the most influential in racing history.

History

The horse was foaled in Yemen and was imported first to Syria and then to Tunisia before being given to Louis XV of France. Unimpressed, the French reportedly used him as a cart horse before he was purchased by Edward Coke of Derbyshire and brought to England around 1729. On Coke's death he passed to Francis Godolphin, 2nd Earl of Godolphin, from whom he takes his name, and stood at stud at Gog Magog, Cambridgeshire.

Stud career

At first used as a teaser to test mares' readiness for another stallion, the Godolphin Arabian famously won the right to cover the mare Roxana when the intended sire, Hobgall, refused her; the resulting foal, Lath, became one of the finest racehorses of the era. Other notable offspring included Cade (sire of Matchem) and Regulus. He died in 1753, reputedly aged 29, and was buried at Wandlebury Park, where his grave can still be seen.
