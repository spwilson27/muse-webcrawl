Yucca brevifolia, commonly known as the Joshua tree, is a tree-like plant species of the genus Yucca in the asparagus family, native to the arid southwestern United States, primarily the Mojave Desert. Its distinctive silhouette — a thick, fibrous trunk branching into spiky rosettes of dagger-like leaves — has made it an emblem of the desert Southwest.

Description

Joshua trees grow slowly, about 7.6 cm (3 in) per year in their first decade, and can reach 15 m (49 ft) in height over lifespans that may exceed 150 years. The trunk is made of thousands of small fibres without growth rings, making aging difficult. The specific epithet brevifolia, "short leaves", distinguishes it from other yuccas. Creamy white flowers bloom in spring in panicles, pollinated almost exclusively by the yucca moth Tegeticula synthetica, in a classic obligate mutualism.

Distribution and conservation

The species occurs in California, Nevada, Utah, and Arizona at elevations of roughly 400–1,800 m. Extensive stands are protected in Joshua Tree National Park. Mormon settlers are said to have given the tree its common name, likening its branches to the biblical Joshua raising his arms in prayer. Climate change, drought, and wildfire — notably the 2020 Dome Fire, which killed over a million trees in Mojave National Preserve — have raised concern for the species; California granted it interim protection under its endangered species law in the 2020s.
