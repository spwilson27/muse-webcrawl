The qibla (Arabic: قِبْلَة, romanized: qiblah, lit. 'direction') is the direction towards the Kaaba in the Sacred Mosque in Mecca, Saudi Arabia, which Muslims face when performing ritual prayer (salah). It is fixed as the direction of the Kaaba relative to a person standing on Earth, conventionally computed along the shortest path over the surface of a sphere — a great circle route.

History

According to Islamic tradition, during the first sixteen or seventeen months after the Hijra to Medina in 622 CE, Muhammad and his followers prayed facing Jerusalem. The direction was then changed to Mecca following a revelation recorded in the Quran (Surah Al-Baqarah, verse 144). The event is commemorated by the Mosque of the Two Qiblas (Masjid al-Qiblatayn) in Medina.

Because the qibla had to be determined across an expanding Islamic world, Muslim astronomers and mathematicians — including al-Khwarizmi, al-Biruni and Ibn al-Shatir — developed sophisticated spherical-trigonometry methods for computing it from geographical coordinates, making qibla determination a major driver of medieval mathematical astronomy.

Determination

The qibla bearing from any point on Earth can be calculated from the latitude and longitude of the location and of the Kaaba (21.4225° N, 39.8262° E). Twice a year, around 27–28 May and 15–16 July, the Sun passes directly over the Kaaba, allowing the qibla to be found simply by observing the direction of the Sun at local noon in Mecca. Traditional instruments include the qibla compass, often decorated and combined with a prayer rug, and the mihrab, a niche in the mosque wall indicating the direction.

Significance

Beyond prayer, the qibla orients mosques, the ritual slaughter of animals, Muslim burials (with the deceased laid on the right side facing Mecca), and historically served as a unifying spatial reference across the Islamic world. Debates over exact calculation — great-circle versus rhumb-line routes — arose notably in North America in the twentieth century.
