Lindisfarne, also called Holy Island, is a tidal island off the northeast coast of England, which constitutes the civil parish of Holy Island in Northumberland. The island is cut off from the mainland by the tide twice a day, and is connected by a causeway that is submerged at high tide.

The island is famous for its medieval religious heritage. A monastery was founded there in AD 635 by the Irish monk Saint Aidan, who came from Iona at the invitation of King Oswald of Northumbria. Saint Cuthbert, later the patron saint of northern England, was a monk and eventually abbot and bishop of the community.

History

The monastery

The monastery of Lindisfarne became a major centre of early English Christianity and learning. Around AD 700 the monks produced the Lindisfarne Gospels, an illuminated Latin manuscript of the Gospels of Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, considered one of the finest works of Insular art.

The Viking raid of 793

On 8 June 793, Vikings raided the monastery, an event recorded in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and in letters by Alcuin of York. The attack is conventionally taken to mark the beginning of the Viking Age in England. The monastic community eventually abandoned the island in the 9th century, carrying Cuthbert's relics to what became Durham.

Later history

A Benedictine priory was re-established in Norman times; its ruins survive today. In the 16th century Lindisfarne Castle was built on the island's highest point using stone from the dissolved priory, to defend against Scottish raids. The castle was later remodelled by the architect Edwin Lutyens for Edward Hudson, founder of Country Life magazine, and is now cared for by the National Trust.

Geography and nature

Much of the island, together with the surrounding intertidal area, is protected as the Lindisfarne National Nature Reserve, important for wintering wildfowl and waders including light-bellied brent geese, as well as grey seals. The island has a small permanent population, a village with a harbour, and is a popular site for pilgrims and tourists, who must time crossings carefully using published safe-crossing times for the causeway.
