Bill of Rights 1689

The Bill of Rights 1689 is an Act of the Parliament of England that set out certain basic civil rights and clarified who would be next in line for the English throne. It laid down limits on the powers of the monarch and set out the rights of Parliament, including the requirement for regular parliaments, free elections, and freedom of speech in Parliament. It forms a crucial part of the constitutional law of the United Kingdom.

Background

After the Glorious Revolution of 1688, in which James II was deposed and William III and Mary II were invited to take the throne, the Convention Parliament drafted a Declaration of Rights listing James's abuses of power and asserting ancient rights and liberties. The declaration was presented to William and Mary in February 1689 and was enacted into law as the Bill of Rights in December of that year.

Provisions

The Act declared illegal, among other things:

  • The suspension or execution of laws by royal authority without Parliament's consent.
  • Levying taxes by royal prerogative without grant of Parliament.
  • Maintaining a standing army in peacetime without Parliament's consent.
  • Interfering with the freedom of election of members of Parliament.

It also affirmed that subjects could petition the king, that Protestant subjects might bear arms for their defence, that excessive bail ought not to be required nor cruel and unusual punishments inflicted, and that parliaments ought to be held frequently. The Act settled the succession on William and Mary and their heirs, and excluded Roman Catholics from the throne.

Legacy

The Bill of Rights influenced constitutional documents elsewhere, notably the United States Bill of Rights of 1791. Together with Magna Carta, the Petition of Right, and the Habeas Corpus Act, it is one of the foundational documents of English constitutional law, and it remains in force in the United Kingdom and several Commonwealth realms.
