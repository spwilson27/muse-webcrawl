Staffordshire Potteries

The Staffordshire Potteries is the industrial area encompassing the six towns Burslem, Fenton, Hanley, Longton, Stoke and Tunstall, which now make up the city of Stoke-on-Trent in Staffordshire, England. North Staffordshire became the centre of ceramics production in Britain in the 17th century, because of the local availability of clay, salt, lead and coal, and the area is commonly called the Potteries.

History

Pottery had been made in the area since at least the medieval period, but production expanded dramatically during the Industrial Revolution. Hundreds of companies produced decorative and industrial ceramic wares ranging from the humblest earthenware to fine bone china; famous firms included Wedgwood, Spode, Minton, Royal Doulton and Moorcroft. Josiah Wedgwood's Etruria works and his marketing innovations made Staffordshire pottery known worldwide.

The landscape of the Potteries was famously marked by bottle kilns (bottle ovens) and canals used to carry raw materials and finished goods. Arnold Bennett immortalized the "Five Towns" in his novels. Through the twentieth century the industry contracted in the face of foreign competition and changing markets, though a number of historic firms and potteries survive, and the area's heritage is preserved in museums such as the Gladstone Pottery Museum and the Potteries Museum & Art Gallery.

Production

The region produced a wide range of wares: creamware, pearlware, stoneware, earthenware, bone china and porcelain, as well as tiles, sanitary ware and electrical porcelain. Transfer printing, pioneered locally in the eighteenth century, allowed decorated wares to be mass-produced. Clay came initially from local seams and later from Cornwall and Devon, transported by canal and rail.
