Circumventricular organs

The circumventricular organs (CVOs) are small structures in the brain located around the third and fourth ventricles. Unusually for brain tissue, their capillaries are fenestrated and they lack a complete blood–brain barrier, allowing them to sense substances in the blood and, in some cases, to secrete hormones and other signals into the circulation.

The organs

The principal circumventricular organs are the area postrema, the subfornical organ, the organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis (OVLT), the median eminence, the neurohypophysis (posterior pituitary), the pineal gland, and the subcommissural organ. They are conventionally divided into sensory organs — the area postrema, subfornical organ and OVLT, which detect blood-borne signals such as osmolality, sodium and circulating hormones and relay them to autonomic control centres — and secretory organs, such as the median eminence and posterior pituitary, which release substances including hypothalamic releasing hormones and vasopressin into the blood.

Function

Through the CVOs the brain monitors and regulates body fluid balance, blood pressure, feeding, fever and vomiting; the area postrema acts as a chemoreceptor trigger zone for emesis. Because they lie outside the blood–brain barrier, the CVOs are also a route by which blood-borne agents can affect the brain.
