Glaucoma

Glaucoma is a group of eye diseases characterised by damage to the optic nerve, with loss of retinal ganglion cells and a characteristic pattern of visual field loss. It is most commonly associated with raised intraocular pressure, although it can occur with statistically normal pressure. Glaucoma is a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, affecting tens of millions of people; because the commonest form progresses slowly and without symptoms until advanced, it is sometimes called the "silent thief of sight".

Types and mechanism

In primary open-angle glaucoma, aqueous humour drains too slowly through the trabecular meshwork, often raising pressure; in angle-closure glaucoma, the iris physically blocks the drainage angle, which can produce a sudden, painful attack requiring emergency treatment. Normal-tension glaucoma damages the nerve despite normal pressures, implicating vascular and structural factors.

Diagnosis and treatment

Detection relies on measuring intraocular pressure, inspecting the optic disc, assessing the drainage angle (gonioscopy) and testing the visual field, increasingly supplemented by optical coherence tomography of the nerve fibre layer. Treatment cannot restore lost vision but lowers pressure — usually by 20–30% — using eye drops, laser trabeculoplasty, or surgical procedures such as trabeculectomy and drainage implants, which slows or halts progression in most patients.
