Parliament of France

The Parliament of France (French: Parlement français) is the bicameral legislature of the French Republic, consisting of the Senate (upper house) and the National Assembly (lower house). Each assembly conducts legislative sessions at separate locations in Paris: the Senate meets in the Luxembourg Palace in the 6th arrondissement, and the National Assembly in the Palais Bourbon in the 7th. Together, sitting as the Congress of the French Parliament at Versailles, they can revise the Constitution.

Organisation

The National Assembly's 577 deputies are elected by direct universal suffrage in single-member constituencies using a two-round system, for five-year terms, and the Assembly can be dissolved by the President. The Senate's 348 senators are elected indirectly by an electoral college of local and national elected officials for six-year terms, with half renewed every three years. The President of the Senate is second in the order of precedence of the Republic and succeeds provisionally to the presidency if that office falls vacant.

Powers

Parliament votes laws, authorises taxes, declares war, and oversees the government. In the legislative shuttle (navette), bills pass between the houses; if disagreement persists, the government may give the National Assembly the final word — except for constitutional and certain organic laws, where the Senate's agreement is required or a joint Congress must approve by a three-fifths majority. The Assembly alone can dismiss the government by a motion of censure. Under Article 49.3 of the Constitution, the government may, on limited occasions, pass certain bills without a vote unless censured — a much-debated feature of the Fifth Republic.

History

The name parlement descends from the judicial parlements of the Ancien Régime, but the modern institution traces to the Revolution of 1789 and France's successive constitutions. The bicameral pattern settled under the Third Republic (1870–1940), with a powerful Senate balancing a directly elected chamber. The Constitution of 1958 deliberately rationalised parliamentarism to curb the assembly-dominated instability of the Fourth Republic, strengthening the executive while retaining parliamentary oversight.

The Congress

When both houses meet together at the Palace of Versailles as the Congress, they vote on constitutional revisions proposed by the President (unless submitted to referendum) and hear addresses by the President of the Republic, a practice used by every president since 2009.
