Primogeniture

Primogeniture is the right, by law or custom, of the firstborn child to inherit the parent's entire or main estate, in preference to shared inheritance among all or some children, any illegitimate child, or any collateral relative. In most modern contexts, this means the inheritance of the deceased's estate by the eldest surviving legitimate child; earlier it typically meant the eldest surviving legitimate son. Primogeniture kept large estates intact across generations and determined succession to thrones throughout Europe.

Primogeniture preserved landed estates by passing them whole to the eldest son.

Forms

Male-preference primogeniture places sons before daughters in birth order but lets daughters inherit if there are no sons; it governed the British succession until the Succession to the Crown Act 2013 introduced absolute primogeniture, in which the eldest child inherits regardless of sex. Agnatic primogeniture (as under Salic law) excludes women entirely. Spain, Monaco, and (formerly) several other monarchies used male-preference systems; Sweden (1980), Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium, Denmark, and Luxembourg have adopted absolute primogeniture.

Historical role

In feudal England, primogeniture in land inheritance became standard after the Norman Conquest, ensuring that a manor passed undivided to one heir who could perform the associated military service. Younger sons were pushed toward careers in the church, the military, law, or trade, and the system is often cited as a driver of younger sons' emigration and colonisation. Entails and strict settlements reinforced the pattern.

Decline

Most legal systems have abolished primogeniture for ordinary inheritance in favour of partible inheritance or testamentary freedom; in the United States it was abolished state by state beginning in the revolutionary era. It survives chiefly in monarchical succession laws, and even there has been widely reformed toward gender neutrality.
