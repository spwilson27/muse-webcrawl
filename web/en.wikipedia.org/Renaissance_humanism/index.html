Renaissance humanism

Renaissance humanism was an intellectual movement of the European Renaissance that emphasised the study of classical antiquity and the humanities — grammar, rhetoric, history, poetry and moral philosophy — as the foundation of education and civic life. Originating in 14th-century Italy, it spread across Europe over the following two centuries and reshaped learning, literature, politics and art.

Origins

The poet Francesco Petrarch (1304–1374) is often called the father of humanism for his recovery and imitation of classical Latin authors such as Cicero and his insistence on studying ancient texts in their original form. Humanists promoted the studia humanitatis against what they saw as the arid logic-chopping of medieval scholasticism, and their motto might be summed up in the call ad fontes — "back to the sources".

Ideas and practices

Humanists developed philology and textual criticism: Lorenzo Valla used them to expose the Donation of Constantine as a forgery in 1440. They placed new emphasis on rhetoric and eloquence as civic skills, on human dignity and potential — celebrated in Giovanni Pico della Mirandola's Oration on the Dignity of Man (1486) — and on education for active citizenship. Figures such as Coluccio Salutati and Leonardo Bruni linked classical learning to republican government in Florence.

Spread and influence

Greek scholarship, boosted by Byzantine émigrés before and after the fall of Constantinople (1453) and by Manuel Chrysoloras's teaching in Florence, added Plato and the Greek classics to the curriculum. Printing spread humanist texts rapidly: Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam became Europe's leading intellectual, editing the New Testament and satirising abuses in In Praise of Folly. In England, Thomas More's Utopia (1516) exemplified northern humanism. Humanist education dominated European grammar schools and universities for centuries.

Relationship to religion and science

Most humanists were sincere Christians who sought to reconcile classical wisdom with faith; their biblical scholarship nonetheless helped prepare the ground for the Reformation. Their recovery of ancient science and insistence on direct observation also contributed to the Scientific Revolution.
