Conservation genetics

Genetic sampling helps monitor the health of endangered populations.

Conservation genetics is an interdisciplinary subfield of population genetics that aims to understand the dynamics of genes in populations in order to avoid extinction. It applies genetic methods to the conservation of biological diversity and the recovery of endangered species. The field draws on principles from evolutionary biology, molecular ecology, and quantitative genetics, and has roots in the work of scientists such as O. Frankel, whose 1974 papers helped define the discipline.

Genetic diversity

Genetic diversity is the raw material that allows populations to adapt to changing environments. Small populations lose genetic variation through genetic drift and inbreeding, which can lead to inbreeding depression — a reduction in fitness caused by mating between related individuals. Conservation geneticists measure diversity using markers such as microsatellites and single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), and estimate effective population size to gauge how much variation a population can retain over time.

Applications

Key applications include identifying management units and evolutionarily significant units, resolving taxonomic uncertainties, detecting illegal trade in wildlife products through DNA forensics, and guiding captive breeding programmes. Genetic rescue — introducing individuals from another population to increase diversity — has been used successfully, for example in the Florida panther.
