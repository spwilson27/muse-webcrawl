Tulip mania

A "broken" tulip, whose rare colouring drove prices to extraordinary heights

Tulip mania (Dutch: tulpenmanie) was a period during the Dutch Golden Age when contract prices for some bulbs of the recently introduced and fashionable tulip reached extraordinarily high levels. The period is generally considered to have begun in 1634 and to have peaked in the winter of 1636–37, before abruptly collapsing in February 1637. It is generally considered to have been the first recorded speculative bubble, or asset bubble, in history.

Background

The tulip was introduced to Europe from the Ottoman Empire in the mid-16th century. In the Dutch Republic, tulips became a coveted luxury item and a status symbol. Particularly prized were "broken" tulips, whose petals showed vivid streaks of colour caused by a mosaic virus. Because broken patterns could not be reliably reproduced from seed, bulbs of rare varieties commanded high prices.

The bubble

Traditionally, tulips were bought and sold among growers during the summer flowering season. As demand grew, a futures market developed in which contracts to buy bulbs at the end of the season were traded in taverns — a form of trading derided as windhandel ("wind trade"). At the peak of the mania, a single bulb of the rare Semper Augustus variety was said to have been valued at many thousands of guilders, more than the price of a grand Amsterdam townhouse. In February 1637, at an auction in Haarlem, buyers failed to appear, and contract prices collapsed within days.

Historical interpretation

The story was popularised by Charles Mackay's Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds (1841). Modern historians and economists have debated how widespread the mania truly was, with some arguing the social and economic damage was far more limited than the popular account suggests, while others defend the traditional view of an irrational speculative episode.
