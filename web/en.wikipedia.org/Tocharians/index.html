Tocharians

The Tocharians were speakers of the Tocharian languages, a branch of the Indo-European language family attested in manuscripts from oasis towns around the northern rim of the Tarim Basin in present-day Xinjiang, China, mainly from the 6th to 9th centuries CE.

Languages and texts

Two related but distinct languages are known: Tocharian A, found around Turpan and Karashahr, and Tocharian B, centred on Kucha. Written in a form of the Indian Brahmi script, the surviving texts are largely Buddhist translations and monastic records recovered by expeditions in the early 20th century. Tocharian was remarkable as the easternmost known Indo-European language family and, unexpectedly, a centum rather than satem branch.

Identity and history

The Tocharians have been tentatively connected with peoples known from Chinese sources, such as the Yuezhi, and with the earlier Afanasievo culture of the Eurasian steppe; the well-preserved Tarim mummies have also been linked to them, though ancient DNA studies suggest a more complex picture. Tocharian culture declined as the Uyghur Khaganate's successors and Turkic languages spread through the region.
