Bill of rights

A bill of rights, sometimes called a declaration of rights or a charter of rights, is a list of the most important rights to the citizens of a country. The purpose is to protect those rights against infringement from public officials and private citizens. Bills of rights may be entrenched or unentrenched; an entrenched bill cannot be amended by a legislature through its normal procedure, typically requiring a supermajority or referendum.

History

The concept descends from documents such as Magna Carta (1215), the English Bill of Rights 1689, and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789). The United States Bill of Rights, the first ten amendments to the US Constitution, was ratified in 1791 and guaranteed freedoms of speech, religion, and the press, the right to bear arms, protection against unreasonable searches, and due process, among others. Its guarantees were originally applied only against the federal government but were largely extended to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment.

Around the world

Many modern constitutions contain bills of rights, such as the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982) and Chapter 2 of the South African Constitution. International instruments, including the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) and the European Convention on Human Rights (1950), function similarly at the supranational level. The United Kingdom historically relied on common law and statute; the Human Rights Act 1998 incorporated the European Convention into domestic law.
