Tennessee Williams

Williams in 1965

Tennessee Williams (born Thomas Lanier Williams III; 26 March 1911 – 25 February 1983) was an American playwright and screenwriter, widely regarded as one of the foremost dramatists of 20th-century American theatre. His plays, including The Glass Menagerie (1944), A Streetcar Named Desire (1947), and Cat on a Hot Tin Roof (1955), drew on his Southern upbringing and troubled family life.

Early life

Williams was born in Columbus, Mississippi, and raised largely in St. Louis, Missouri. His father was a travelling shoe salesman; his mother, Edwina, was a Southern belle whose mannerisms informed several of his characters. His beloved sister Rose, who suffered from mental illness and underwent a lobotomy in 1943, haunted his work—most directly in the character of Laura in The Glass Menagerie. He adopted the pen name "Tennessee," after his father's home state.

Career

After years of obscurity and itinerant jobs, Williams achieved a breakthrough with The Glass Menagerie in 1944. A Streetcar Named Desire won the Pulitzer Prize for Drama in 1948 and introduced Stanley Kowalski and Blanche DuBois to the stage; the 1951 film adaptation starred Marlon Brando and Vivien Leigh. Cat on a Hot Tin Roof won a second Pulitzer in 1955. Other works include Summer and Smoke (1948), The Rose Tattoo (1951), Suddenly Last Summer (1958), and The Night of the Iguana (1961).

Later years and legacy

Williams struggled with depression and substance abuse, and his later plays met with diminishing critical success. He died in New York City in 1983. His lyric, memory-haunted dramas remain staples of the repertoire, and his hometown of New Orleans holds an annual Tennessee Williams Literary Festival.
