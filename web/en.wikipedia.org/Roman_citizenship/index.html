Roman citizenship (Latin: civitas Romana) was a privileged political and legal status afforded to free individuals in the Roman Republic and Roman Empire. It carried a bundle of rights under Roman law — including the rights to vote, to hold office, to make contracts and own property, to contract legal marriage, and to appeal against capital sentences — together with obligations such as taxation and military service. Unlike most ancient states, Rome extended citizenship progressively to conquered peoples, and in AD 212 the emperor Caracalla granted it to nearly all free inhabitants of the empire, making it a key instrument of Roman integration.

Rights and status

Full citizenship (civitas optimo iure) combined public rights — suffragium (the vote in assemblies), honores (eligibility for magistracies), and provocatio (appeal to the people, and under the Empire to the emperor, against coercion or execution, the right famously invoked by St Paul) — with private rights: commercium, the capacity to hold Roman title to property and make enforceable contracts; conubium, legal marriage with another citizen, which made children citizens; and testamenti factio, the right to make and inherit under a Roman will. Citizens were in principle exempt from degrading punishments such as crucifixion and flogging.

Citizenship came in gradations, including the Latin rights (ius Latii) — commercium and conubium without the vote — held by many Italian and provincial communities as a halfway status, and citizenship without the vote (civitas sine suffragio) granted to some early Italian allies.

Acquisition

Citizenship passed by birth to the children of citizen parents in a legal marriage. Freed slaves (liberti) were normally manumitted directly into citizenship by a citizen master, though with some disabilities, and their freeborn children were full citizens — a remarkably open practice that astonished Greek observers. Entire communities received grants for loyalty or service; auxiliary soldiers earned citizenship on honourable discharge, documented by surviving bronze military diplomas. Emperors and magistrates also made individual grants, sometimes purchased — the tribune in Acts 22 remarks that he bought his citizenship "for a large sum."

Expansion through Italy and the Social War

Rome's willingness to share citizenship underpinned its conquest of Italy, but by the 1st century BC the Italian allies (socii) who supplied half its armies demanded full citizenship. Its refusal sparked the Social War (91–88 BC); Rome conceded by the Lex Julia (90 BC) and Lex Plautia Papiria (89 BC), extending citizenship to all free Italians south of the Po, and later to Cisalpine Gaul. Under the Empire, citizenship spread steadily through the provinces, especially in the West, via colonies of veterans, municipal promotions, and military service.

The Antonine Constitution

In AD 212, the Constitutio Antoniniana of the emperor Caracalla extended citizenship to almost all free men and women in the empire (the dediticii, probably certain surrendered peoples, being excluded). Ancient authors attributed the edict to fiscal motives — broadening the tax base for inheritance and manumission taxes — while modern historians also stress administrative unification and imperial ideology. Afterwards, legal distinctions shifted from citizen/non-citizen to the class divide between honestiores and humiliores, and citizenship lost much of its former exclusivity even as Roman law became universal.
