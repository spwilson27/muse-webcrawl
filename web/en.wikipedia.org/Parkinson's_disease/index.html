Parkinson's disease

Parkinson's disease (PD), or simply Parkinson's, is a long-term neurodegenerative disease of mainly the central nervous system that affects both the motor system and non-motor systems. The symptoms usually emerge slowly, and as the disease progresses, non-motor symptoms become more common. Early symptoms are tremor, rigidity, slowness of movement, and difficulty with walking. Problems may also arise with cognition, behaviour, sleep, and sensory systems. Dementia becomes common in advanced stages.

The disease is named after English doctor James Parkinson, who first described it in An Essay on the Shaking Palsy (1817). Its pathology is characterised by the loss of neurons in the substantia nigra of the midbrain and the accumulation of misfolded alpha-synuclein in Lewy bodies, leading to dopamine deficiency.

Causes and risk factors

The cause is unknown; risk is higher in those with a family history, in those exposed to certain pesticides, and in those who have had a head injury. Mutations in genes such as SNCA, LRRK2, and GBA contribute in a minority of cases. Smoking and coffee consumption appear to modestly reduce risk. Age is the strongest risk factor, with onset typically after 60.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is based mainly on medical history and neurological examination; imaging such as DaT-SPECT can support it. There is no cure, but medications and surgery can relieve symptoms. The mainstay is levodopa (L-DOPA), often combined with carbidopa; dopamine agonists and MAO-B inhibitors are also used. Deep brain stimulation is an option in advanced disease with motor complications. Exercise and physical therapy help maintain mobility.

Epidemiology

Parkinson's disease is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder after Alzheimer's disease, affecting more than 10 million people worldwide, and its prevalence is rising with population ageing. Awareness campaigns use the red tulip as a symbol, and 11 April, Parkinson's birthday, as World Parkinson's Day.
