Andesite is a volcanic rock of intermediate composition, named after the Andes mountain range of South America, where it is abundant. In a general sense, it is the intermediate type between silica-rich rhyolite and silica-poor basalt. Andesite is a common rock of subduction zones and oceanic island arcs.

Composition

Andesite typically contains 57% to 63% silicon dioxide (SiO2). Its mineralogy is dominated by plagioclase feldspar (usually andesine or oligoclase), with lesser amounts of pyroxene (augite or orthopyroxene), hornblende, and biotite. The groundmass is fine-grained or glassy, and crystals of plagioclase and mafic minerals commonly occur as phenocrysts, giving the rock a porphyritic texture.

Occurrence

Andesite is characteristic of volcanic arcs above subduction zones, including the Andes, the Cascades of North America (Mount St. Helens, Mount Rainier), Japan, Indonesia, and the Caribbean island arcs. Mount Rinjani and Mount Merapi in Indonesia, and Popocatépetl in Mexico, are examples of andesitic stratovolcanoes.

Formation

Andesitic magmas form through partial melting of the mantle wedge above a subducting slab, modified by fluids released from the slab, and by fractional crystallisation and crustal assimilation during ascent. The relatively high water content and viscosity of andesitic magma compared with basalt often produces explosive eruptions.
