Robert Koch

Heinrich Hermann Robert Koch (11 December 1843 – 27 May 1910) was a German physician and microbiologist who is regarded as one of the founders of modern bacteriology. He identified the causative agents of tuberculosis, cholera, and anthrax, and formulated the criteria — Koch's postulates — still used to establish that a microorganism causes a disease. He received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1905 for his work on tuberculosis.

Early life and education

Koch was born in Clausthal in the Harz Mountains, one of thirteen children of a mining engineer. He showed an early aptitude for science and studied medicine at the University of Göttingen, where he was influenced by the anatomist Jacob Henle, who had speculated that infectious diseases were caused by living organisms. After graduating in 1866, Koch worked as a hospital physician and served as a military surgeon in the Franco-Prussian War before settling into practice as a district physician in Wollstein (now Wolsztyn, Poland).

Anthrax and the germ theory

Working in a small home laboratory in Wollstein, Koch studied anthrax, a major disease of livestock. In 1876 he demonstrated the complete life cycle of the bacillus, including its ability to form resistant spores that could survive in soil for long periods and later germinate — explaining how pastures could remain infective for years. The work, published with the support of the botanist Ferdinand Cohn at Breslau, made Koch's reputation. In 1878 he published a landmark study of the bacterial causes of wound infections.

Berlin and technical innovations

In 1880 Koch was appointed to the Imperial Health Office in Berlin, where, with assistants including Friedrich Loeffler, Georg Gaffky, and Walther Hesse, he developed methods that became the foundation of laboratory bacteriology: growing bacteria on solid culture media (first potato slices, then agar, suggested by Fanny Hesse), the Petri dish (introduced by his assistant Julius Richard Petri), staining techniques, and microphotography. These methods made it possible to isolate pure cultures of individual bacterial species.

Tuberculosis and cholera

On 24 March 1882 Koch announced to the Berlin Physiological Society that he had identified the bacterium that causes tuberculosis, Mycobacterium tuberculosis — one of the most celebrated lectures in the history of medicine. The date is now commemorated as World Tuberculosis Day. The following year, leading a German expedition to Egypt and India during a cholera epidemic, he isolated the comma-shaped bacillus Vibrio cholerae and showed its presence in contaminated water. In 1890 Koch announced tuberculin as a supposed cure for tuberculosis; it failed as a therapy, amid considerable controversy, though it later proved valuable as a diagnostic test.

Koch's postulates

Building on ideas of Henle, Koch and his colleague Friedrich Loeffler formalised the criteria for proving that a specific microorganism causes a specific disease: the organism must be found in all cases of the disease; it must be isolated and grown in pure culture; the culture must reproduce the disease in a susceptible host; and the organism must be re-isolated from that host. The postulates became the standard framework of medical microbiology, though later knowledge of viruses, carriers, and unculturable organisms required their modification.

Later work and rivalry

Koch became professor of hygiene at the University of Berlin in 1885 and director of the new Institute for Infectious Diseases in 1891. He undertook expeditions to study malaria, sleeping sickness, rinderpest, and bubonic plague in Africa and Asia. His career overlapped and competed with that of Louis Pasteur in France, a rivalry coloured by national tensions after the Franco-Prussian War. Koch's school trained a generation of leading bacteriologists, including Emil von Behring, Paul Ehrlich, and Shibasaburo Kitasato.

Death and legacy

Koch died of a heart attack in Baden-Baden on 27 May 1910. The Institute for Infectious Diseases in Berlin was renamed the Robert Koch Institute in his honour; it remains Germany's national public health institute. Together with Pasteur, Koch is considered a principal founder of microbiology and of the germ theory of disease in its experimental form.
