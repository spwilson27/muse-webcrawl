Separable space


The rational numbers form a countable dense subset of the real line.

In mathematics, a topological space is called separable if it contains a countable, dense subset; that is, there exists a sequence of points of the space such that every nonempty open subset of the space contains at least one point of the sequence. Separability is a "size" restriction on a space that is often convenient in analysis: although it does not limit cardinality as strongly as compactness or second-countability, it guarantees that points and continuous functions can be approximated by sequences from a small, manageable set.

Examples

The real line R is separable because the rational numbers Q form a countable dense subset. More generally, Euclidean space Rn is separable, as is every subset of it with the subspace topology. Any countable space is separable. By contrast, an uncountable set with the discrete topology is not separable, and the function space L∞ over an interval is a standard example of a non-separable normed space, whereas Lp for finite p is separable.

Properties

  • A continuous image of a separable space is separable.
  • Every subspace of a separable metric space is separable, and for metric spaces separability is equivalent to second-countability and to the Lindelöf property.
  • A separable Hilbert space is precisely one possessing a countable orthonormal basis; all infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert spaces are unitarily isomorphic to the sequence space ℓ2.
  • The cardinality of a separable Hausdorff space is at most that of the continuum raised in a controlled way; separable spaces cannot be "too large".

History

The term was introduced by Maurice Fréchet in 1906. Separability should not be confused with the unrelated notion of a "separated space", a synonym sometimes used for a Hausdorff space.
