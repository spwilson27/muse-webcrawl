Lyapunov stability

A stable equilibrium: nearby trajectories stay nearby

Field: Dynamical systems, control theory

Introduced by: Aleksandr Lyapunov (1892)

Lyapunov stability is a concept in the theory of dynamical systems, introduced by the Russian mathematician Aleksandr Mikhailovich Lyapunov in his 1892 doctoral thesis The General Problem of the Stability of Motion. An equilibrium point of a dynamical system is said to be stable in the sense of Lyapunov if trajectories that begin sufficiently close to it remain arbitrarily close to it for all future time. If, in addition, nearby trajectories converge to the equilibrium, it is asymptotically stable; if the convergence is exponential, it is exponentially stable.

Lyapunov's methods

Two approaches descend from Lyapunov's work. The indirect method (first method) infers local stability from the linearisation of the system: if all eigenvalues of the Jacobian at the equilibrium have negative real parts, the equilibrium is asymptotically stable; if any has a positive real part, it is unstable. The celebrated direct method (second method) constructs a Lyapunov function — a positive-definite, energy-like scalar function of the state whose value decreases along trajectories — proving stability without solving the equations of motion.

Applications

Lyapunov theory is the principal tool for stability analysis in nonlinear control theory, robot control, adaptive control, and the analysis of equilibria in mechanics and electrical networks. Related quantities, the Lyapunov exponents, measure average rates of divergence of nearby trajectories and characterise chaotic behaviour.
