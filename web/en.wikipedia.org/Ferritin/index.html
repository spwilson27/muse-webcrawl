Ferritin

Ferritin is a universal intracellular protein that stores iron and releases it in a controlled fashion. It is produced by almost all living organisms, including archaea, bacteria, algae, higher plants, and animals. In humans it acts as a buffer against iron deficiency and iron overload, and serum ferritin measurement is the most widely used blood test for estimating body iron stores.

Structure

Ferritin consists of a hollow protein shell (apoferritin) roughly 12 nanometres across, assembled from 24 subunits of two types: heavy (H) and light (L) chains. The H chain has ferroxidase activity, converting soluble ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) into the ferric form (Fe³⁺) for storage. The interior cavity can hold up to about 4,500 iron atoms as a ferrihydrite mineral core, together with phosphate.

Function

Free iron is toxic because it catalyses the formation of reactive oxygen species through the Fenton reaction. Ferritin sequesters iron safely while keeping it available for processes such as haemoglobin synthesis. Its production is regulated by cellular iron levels through iron-responsive elements in its messenger RNA, which are bound by iron regulatory proteins when iron is scarce.

Clinical significance

Low serum ferritin is a sensitive indicator of iron deficiency and, in its absence of inflammation, essentially diagnostic of it. High ferritin occurs in iron overload disorders such as hereditary haemochromatosis, but also rises as an acute-phase reactant in infection, inflammation, liver disease, and some cancers; extremely high values are seen in haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis and adult-onset Still's disease.
