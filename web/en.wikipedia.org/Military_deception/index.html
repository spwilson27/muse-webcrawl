Military deception

Military deception (MILDEC) is an attempt by a military unit to gain an advantage during warfare by misleading adversary decision makers into taking actions detrimental to the adversary. It is usually achieved by creating or amplifying an artificial fog of war via psychological operations, information warfare, visual deception, or other methods. Deception is a core element of military planning, described by Sun Tzu in The Art of War with the maxim that "all warfare is based on deception."

Dummy and decoy equipment, such as inflatable tanks, are classic tools of visual deception.

Principles

Deception operations are typically designed to cause the adversary to misallocate forces, misjudge the timing or location of an attack, or fail to perceive a real threat. Effective deception requires centralized control, security, plausible storytelling consistent with the adversary's preconceptions, and feedback channels (often signals intelligence) to confirm the deception is being believed. Because deception targets the enemy commander rather than his forces, planners speak of manipulating the adversary's decision cycle or OODA loop.

Types

  • Strategic deception — directed at national-level decision makers, e.g. convincing an enemy of an invasion location.
  • Operational deception — supports a campaign or major operation.
  • Tactical deception — supports a specific battle or engagement, including feints, demonstrations, ruses and displays.
  • Physical deception — decoys, dummy equipment, camouflage and false radio traffic.

History

Ancient and early examples

Deception is as old as warfare: the Trojan Horse of legend, Hannibal's ambush at Lake Trasimene, and Mongol feigned retreats all relied on misleading the enemy about intentions or strength.

Second World War

The Second World War saw deception practised on an unprecedented scale. The British-led Double Cross System turned German agents in Britain and fed controlled information to Berlin. Operation Fortitude, part of the wider Operation Bodyguard, used the fictitious First United States Army Group, dummy aircraft and inflatable vehicles, and double agents to convince Germany that the 1944 Normandy landings would instead fall at the Pas-de-Calais and Norway. Operation Mincemeat in 1943 planted false documents on a corpse washed ashore in Spain to disguise the invasion of Sicily.

Cold War and modern era

During the Cold War, both superpowers maintained deception (maskirovka in Soviet doctrine) as a formal element of operations. Soviet maskirovka encompassed concealment, imitation, disinformation and demonstrative actions. In the 1991 Gulf War, Coalition forces used amphibious feints and media-visible threats to fix Iraqi divisions in Kuwait while the main ground attack swung far to the west, the famous "left hook". Modern deception must contend with satellite reconnaissance, open-source intelligence and social media, but decoys, electronic warfare and cyber deception remain standard tools.

Legal status

Under the laws of armed conflict, ruses of war are permitted: camouflage, decoys, mock operations and misinformation are lawful. However, perfidy — feigning protected status (such as surrender, medical or civilian status) in order to kill or wound — is prohibited by the Geneva Conventions.
