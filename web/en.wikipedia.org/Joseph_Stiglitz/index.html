Joseph Eugene Stiglitz (born 9 February 1943) is an American New Keynesian economist, public policy analyst, political activist, and professor at Columbia University. He is a recipient of the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences (2001) and the John Bates Clark Medal (1979), and a former senior vice president and chief economist of the World Bank, as well as a former member and chairman of the U.S. Council of Economic Advisers. He is known for his pioneering work on information asymmetry and for his outspoken criticism of globalisation, laissez-faire economists, and institutions such as the International Monetary Fund.

Academic work

Stiglitz's signature contribution is the economics of information. With Michael Rothschild he showed how insurance markets are shaped by screening and adverse selection; with Andrew Weiss he demonstrated credit rationing in loan markets; and the Shapiro–Stiglitz model explained unemployment through efficiency wages. His 2001 Nobel Prize, shared with George Akerlof and Michael Spence, was awarded "for analyses of markets with asymmetric information". His work with Bruce Greenwald argued that market economies with imperfect information are generally not Pareto efficient, providing a theoretical basis for government intervention. Earlier contributions include the Henry George theorem, work on local public goods and taxation with Anthony Atkinson, and the Dixit–Stiglitz model of monopolistic competition used throughout trade and growth theory.

Policy career

Stiglitz chaired President Bill Clinton's Council of Economic Advisers from 1995 to 1997. As chief economist of the World Bank (1997–2000) he publicly clashed with the IMF and the U.S. Treasury over the handling of the Asian financial crisis and the transition of post-communist economies, criticising austerity and rapid liberalisation — disputes documented in his bestseller Globalization and Its Discontents (2002). He left the Bank amid pressure from the Treasury.

Later work and advocacy

Stiglitz co-founded the Initiative for Policy Dialogue at Columbia and has written widely read books including Making Globalization Work (2006), The Price of Inequality (2012), and People, Power, and Profits (2019), advocating "progressive capitalism". He chaired a French government commission on the measurement of economic performance (the Stiglitz–Sen–Fitoussi commission, 2009) and has advised governments and international bodies on inequality, climate policy, and reform of the international monetary system. His works consistently argue that unfettered markets are neither efficient nor stable and that democratic politics must shape market rules.
