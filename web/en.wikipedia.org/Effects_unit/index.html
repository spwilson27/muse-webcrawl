Effects unit

An effects unit, effects processor, or effects pedal is an electronic device that alters the sound of a musical instrument or other audio source through audio signal processing. Effects units are used by guitarists, bassists, keyboard players, vocalists, and recording engineers, both live and in the studio.

Types of effects

Common categories include:

  • Dynamics — compressors and limiters that even out volume; noise gates.
  • Gain — overdrive, distortion, and fuzz, from mild breakup to heavy saturation.
  • Filter/EQ — equalizers and wah-wah pedals.
  • Modulation — chorus, flanger, phaser, and tremolo, which vary the signal cyclically.
  • Time-based — delay/echo and reverb, simulating spaces and repetitions.
  • Pitch — harmonizers, pitch shifters, and octave effects.

Formats and history

Effects began as studio techniques and built-in amplifier features (spring reverb, tremolo) in the 1940s–50s. The first standalone transistorised pedals appeared in the 1960s — the Maestro FZ-1 Fuzz-Tone (1962), popularised by the Rolling Stones' "(I Can't Get No) Satisfaction," is an early landmark. The 1970s saw mass-produced compact pedals from companies such as Electro-Harmonix, MXR, and Boss, whose compact pedal format became an industry standard.

Rack-mounted studio processors dominated professional studios in the 1980s; multi-effects units combined many effects in one box from the late 1980s onward; and modern digital modelling units and software plug-ins now emulate whole collections of classic pedals, amplifiers, and studio gear. Guitarists typically chain several pedals on a pedalboard, and the order of effects in the signal chain significantly affects the result.
