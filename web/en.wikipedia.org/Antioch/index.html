Antioch on the Orontes was an ancient Greek city founded in 300 BC by Seleucus I Nicator, one of Alexander the Great's successors, and named after his father Antiochus. Situated on the eastern bank of the Orontes River near the modern city of Antakya in Turkey, it became the capital of the Seleucid Empire and later one of the largest cities of the Roman Empire.

History

Antioch grew into a great Hellenistic metropolis and, after Rome annexed Syria in 64 BC, a major provincial capital and commercial hub on the routes between the Mediterranean and Mesopotamia. It was famous for its cosmopolitan population, its school of philosophy and rhetoric, and its luxury suburb of Daphne. The city suffered repeated Persian invasions (notably in 256 and 540 AD) and destructive earthquakes, including those of 526 and 528.

Christianity

Antioch holds a central place in early Christianity: according to the Acts of the Apostles, the disciples were first called "Christians" there, and it was the base of Paul's missionary journeys. Its patriarchate was one of the five great sees (the Pentarchy) of the early Church, and the city gave its name to the "Antiochene" school of biblical interpretation and theology associated with figures such as John Chrysostom.

Crusades and decline

The city fell to the Arabs in 637, was retaken by Byzantium in 969, taken by the Seljuk Turks in 1084, and captured by Crusaders in 1098 after a long siege, becoming the capital of the Principality of Antioch until its fall to the Mamluk sultan Baibars in 1268. Thereafter Antioch declined into a small town; the modern city of Antakya stands near, but not exactly on, the ancient site.
