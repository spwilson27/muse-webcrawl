Anaesthesia

Anaesthesia allows surgery to be performed without pain or awareness.

Anaesthesia (or anesthesia in American English) is a state of controlled, temporary loss of sensation or awareness that is induced for medical or veterinary purposes. It may include some or all of analgesia (relief from or prevention of pain), paralysis (muscle relaxation), amnesia (loss of memory), and unconsciousness. An individual under the effects of anaesthetic drugs is referred to as being anaesthetized.

Types

Anaesthesia falls broadly into three categories: general anaesthesia, which suppresses central nervous system activity and results in complete unconsciousness; sedation, which depresses the central nervous system to a lesser degree, inhibiting anxiety and creation of long-term memories without resulting in unconsciousness; and regional and local anaesthesia, which block transmission of nerve impulses from a specific part of the body — examples include spinal and epidural anaesthesia and peripheral nerve blocks.

History

Modern anaesthesia began in the 1840s with the public demonstration of ether by William Morton at the Massachusetts General Hospital in 1846, followed by chloroform and, in the 20th century, safer inhaled agents such as sevoflurane and intravenous drugs like propofol. Anaesthetists (anesthesiologists) manage the patient's physiology throughout surgery, monitoring airway, breathing, circulation, temperature, and depth of anaesthesia. When properly administered, modern anaesthesia carries a very low risk of serious harm.
