Shark fin soup

Shark fin soup, a traditional Chinese banquet dish.

Shark fin soup is a traditional soup or stewed dish served in Chinese and Vietnamese cuisine. The shark fins provide texture—as thin, chewy, needle-like strands—while the taste comes primarily from the other soup ingredients, typically a rich broth flavoured with ham, chicken, dried scallops, and mushrooms. The dish has been a symbol of wealth, prestige, and hospitality since the Ming dynasty and is traditionally served at banquets, weddings, and celebrations.

Preparation involves drying, skinning, and trimming the fins, then cooking them to produce gelatinous strands. Because the fin itself is nearly tasteless, imitation shark fin—made from mung bean vermicelli, konjac, or other materials—is widely used as a substitute.

Controversy and conservation

The dish is controversial because of the practice of shark finning—cutting off a shark's fins, often at sea, and discarding the body—and because of the steep declines in many shark populations driven partly by fin demand. Shark finning is banned or restricted in many countries, and several jurisdictions, including a number of US states and the European Union (which requires fins to be landed naturally attached), restrict the fin trade. The Chinese government banned shark fin soup from official banquets in 2012, and numerous airlines, hotel chains, and shipping companies have adopted no-shark-fin policies. Public campaigns in China, associated with figures such as former NBA player Yao Ming, are credited with significantly reducing consumption since the 2010s.
