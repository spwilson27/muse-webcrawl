Seven Countries Study

Heart disease rates and dietary fat across cohorts

The Seven Countries Study was a pioneering epidemiological investigation of the relationships among diet, lifestyle, and cardiovascular disease across contrasting populations. Begun in 1956 and directed by American physiologist Ancel Keys of the University of Minnesota, it was the first major study to systematically examine diet and heart disease risk factors in contrasting populations using standardised methods.

Design

The study enrolled 12,763 men aged 40–59 in 16 cohorts across seven countries: the United States, Finland, the Netherlands, Italy, Yugoslavia (Serbia and Croatia), Greece, and Japan. Participants received standardised examinations — including measurements of blood pressure, serum cholesterol, smoking, and diet — and were followed for decades for cardiovascular events and mortality. The main findings were published in 1970 and in the 1980 monograph Seven Countries: A Multivariate Analysis of Death and Coronary Heart Disease.

Findings

The study found that coronary heart disease mortality varied more than tenfold between cohorts: it was highest in eastern Finland and the United States and lowest in Japan and Mediterranean cohorts such as Crete. Average serum cholesterol, largely explained by saturated fat intake, strongly predicted population rates of coronary death. The findings helped establish the diet-heart hypothesis, promoted the Mediterranean diet, and influenced dietary guidelines worldwide. Individual-level analyses also identified blood pressure and smoking as major risk factors.

Criticisms

The study has been criticised for the selection of countries — Keys had data from more countries than he included — and for ecological correlations that might not reflect individual causation. Critics, beginning with Yerushalmy and Hilleboe in 1957, argued that other dietary factors such as sugar were insufficiently considered. Defenders respond that the cohorts were chosen for contrasting, stable diets and reliable records, and that within-cohort analyses confirmed the cholesterol and saturated fat relationships.
