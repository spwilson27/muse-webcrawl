Form of address

A form of address, in sociolinguistics and etiquette, is a word or phrase used to refer to or address another person, expressing levels of respect, familiarity, social distance, and relative status. Languages and cultures encode these distinctions in varied ways: through titles and honorifics, through pronoun choice, and through the use of given names, surnames, or kinship terms.

T–V distinction

Many languages distinguish a familiar second-person pronoun (T) from a formal or plural-derived one (V), as in French tu/vous, Spanish tú/usted, and German du/Sie. This is known as the T–V distinction. Choosing the wrong form can convey unintended intimacy or disrespect. English largely lost the distinction when thou fell out of general use.

Titles and honorifics

Forms of address include courtesy titles (Mr., Mrs., Ms., Miss, Mx.), professional and academic titles (Dr., Professor), religious styles, and the formal styles used for royalty, nobility, and officials, such as "His Majesty," "The Right Honourable," or "Your Excellency." In Japanese and Korean, elaborate systems of honorific speech (such as Japanese keigo) grade sentences by the status of the addressee.

Usage

Correct usage is prescribed in etiquette manuals and diplomatic protocol; foreign ministries maintain protocol services to ensure dignitaries are addressed according to their rank and local custom.
