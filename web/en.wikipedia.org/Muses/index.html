The Muses (Ancient Greek: Μοῦσαι, Mousai) are the inspirational goddesses of literature, science, and the arts in Greek mythology. They were considered the source of the knowledge embodied in the poetry, lyric songs, and myths that were orally related for centuries in ancient Greek culture.

The nine Olympian Muses

According to Hesiod's Theogony, the Muses were the nine daughters of Zeus and Mnemosyne (Memory), born over nine consecutive nights near the foot of Mount Olympus. In the standard later categorization, each Muse presided over a particular art or science:

  • Calliope — epic poetry
  • Clio — history
  • Erato — love poetry and lyric poetry
  • Euterpe — music and song
  • Melpomene — tragedy
  • Polyhymnia — sacred poetry and hymn
  • Terpsichore — dance and chorus
  • Thalia — comedy and idyllic poetry
  • Urania — astronomy

Cult and worship

The Muses were venerated especially at Mount Helicon in Boeotia and at Pieria near Mount Olympus, their mythical birthplace. The word "museum" derives from the Greek mouseion, a shrine or temple dedicated to the Muses. Poets traditionally invoked the Muse at the opening of an epic, a practice found in Homer's Iliad and Odyssey and echoed by Virgil and Dante.

Influence

Singers and poets believed the Muses granted the gift of song and authoritative speech. Their association with memory — through their mother Mnemosyne — reflects the oral nature of early Greek poetry. The Muses remain a lasting symbol of artistic inspiration in Western culture.
