Arecibo Observatory

Location: Arecibo, Puerto Rico

Built: 1960–1963

Telescope: 305 m (1,000 ft) spherical reflector

Demolished: Collapse, 1 December 2020

The Arecibo Observatory, also known as the National Astronomy and Ionosphere Center, was an astronomical observatory in Puerto Rico centred on a 305-metre (1,000 ft) spherical reflector dish built into a natural limestone sinkhole. Completed in 1963, it was the world's largest single-aperture telescope for over five decades, until China's FAST telescope surpassed it in 2016.

Science

Arecibo served radio astronomy, atmospheric science and planetary radar. It produced the first radar images of Venus and Mercury, confirmed Mercury's 59-day rotation, mapped near-Earth asteroids, and detected the first exoplanets, around pulsar PSR B1257+12 (announced 1992). In 1974 Russell Hulse and Joseph Taylor discovered the binary pulsar PSR B1913+21 there, work that won the Nobel Prize and provided indirect evidence for gravitational waves. That year the observatory transmitted the Arecibo message, a pictorial radio broadcast aimed at the globular cluster M13.

Collapse

After years of hurricane damage and funding strain, two support cables failed in August and November 2020. Before a planned controlled demolition, the suspended instrument platform collapsed onto the dish on 1 December 2020, destroying the telescope. Smaller instruments at the site continue atmospheric research, and the site now hosts an educational facility.
