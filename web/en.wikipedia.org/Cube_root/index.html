Cube root

In mathematics, a cube root of a number x is a number y such that y3 = x. All nonzero real numbers have exactly one real cube root and a pair of complex conjugate cube roots, and all nonzero complex numbers have three distinct complex cube roots. The cube root is denoted ∛x or x1/3.

Real cube roots

Unlike the square root, the cube root of a negative number is a well-defined negative real number: ∛(−8) = −2, because (−2)3 = −8. The function y = ∛x is an odd function defined on the whole real line, and its graph passes through the origin with a vertical tangent.

History

Methods for computing cube roots appear in ancient Chinese and Greek mathematics. Hero of Alexandria described an iterative approximation, and Indian mathematicians such as Aryabhata gave rules for extracting cube roots of large numbers digit by digit. The problem of doubling the cube — constructing ∛2 with compass and straightedge — was one of the classical problems of antiquity and was proved impossible in the 19th century.

Computation

Cube roots can be computed by Newton's method applied to y3 − x = 0, or via the identity ∛x = e(ln x)/3. In floating-point arithmetic, care is needed to preserve the sign for negative inputs.
