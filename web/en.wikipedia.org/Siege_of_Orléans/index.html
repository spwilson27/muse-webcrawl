The Siege of Orléans (12 October 1428 – 8 May 1429) was the watershed of the Lancer phase of the Hundred Years' War between England and France. The English besieged the strategically crucial city of Orléans, the gateway to the Loire valley and the remaining territories of the Dauphin Charles, but were driven off after Joan of Arc arrived to lead the relief.

The siege

English forces under the Earl of Salisbury, and after his death the Earl of Suffolk, invested Orléans and built a ring of fortified bastilles around it, though they never fully closed the city's river and eastern approaches. Through the winter the siege settled into stalemate.

Joan of Arc and the relief

On 29 April 1429 Joan of Arc entered the city with supplies and reinforcements, dramatically raising French morale. Over the following days the French stormed the English bastilles one by one — Saint-Loup, the Augustins, and finally Les Tourelles guarding the bridge, in whose assault Joan was wounded. On 8 May the English abandoned the siege and withdrew.

Aftermath

The victory reversed the momentum of the war. French forces cleared the Loire valley at Jargeau, Meung-sur-Loire, Beaugency, and Patay, enabling Charles VII's coronation at Reims in July 1429.
