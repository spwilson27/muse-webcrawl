Global South

For the academic journal, see Global South (journal).


Countries frequently classified as part of the Global South

The Global South is a term often used to identify regions within Latin America, Africa, Asia, and Oceania. It is one of a family of terms, including "Third World" and "the periphery," that denote regions outside Europe and North America, referring mostly to low-income and often politically or culturally marginalised countries as well as former colonial territories. The Global South refers to countries classified by the World Bank as low- or middle-income, in contrast to the wealthier nations of the Global North, which include Europe, North America, Australia, New Zealand, and developed parts of East Asia.

The term was first used in 1969 by political activist Carl Oglesby, who argued that the Vietnam War was the culmination of a history of northern domination of the south. Popularised in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, it has largely displaced the Cold War-era term "Third World" in academic and policy discourse, partly because it avoids the connotations of backwardness and partly because it captures forms of inequality that persist within wealthy countries as well as between them.

Definitions and usage

There is no single agreed definition of the Global South. The United Nations Development Programme and other bodies generally treat it as encompassing the developing countries of Africa, Latin America and the Caribbean, Asia (including the Middle East), and the Pacific. Geographically the term is imprecise: Australia and New Zealand lie in the Southern Hemisphere but are considered part of the Global North, while much of the "South"—including China and India—lies entirely north of the equator. Scholars therefore tend to treat the term as a political and economic category rather than a geographic one, connected to shared histories of colonialism, uneven development, and marginalisation in global governance.

History of the concept

The roots of the concept lie in the Bandung Conference of 1955, at which 29 Asian and African states asserted a common position outside the Cold War blocs, and in the Non-Aligned Movement founded in 1961. The Brandt Report of 1980 popularised a "North–South divide" drawn on maps of the world, with a line separating the wealthy industrialised states from the poorer, largely tropical world. The phrase "Global South" gained prominence through the work of the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD), the Group of 77, and South–South cooperation initiatives, and entered wide academic use after the Cold War ended and "Third World" fell out of favour.

Development and inequality

Countries of the Global South contain the majority of the world's population and a rapidly growing share of its economic output. The rise of large emerging economies—China, India, Brazil, and others grouped in forums such as BRICS—has complicated the picture of a uniformly poor South. Nevertheless, disparities in income, infrastructure, health outcomes, and exposure to climate change remain stark, and many of the least developed countries, landlocked developing countries, and small island developing states are located in the Global South.

Criticism

Critics note that the term lumps together extremely diverse societies, obscures inequality within countries of both North and South, and can function as a euphemism that depoliticises the relationships it names. Others defend it as a useful expression of political solidarity among postcolonial states.
