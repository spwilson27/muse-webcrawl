Rock crawling

Rock crawling is an extreme form of off-road driving using specialised vehicles ranging from stock SUVs to purpose-built buggies. Unlike high-speed desert racing or rallying, rock crawling emphasises slow, careful, low-speed manoeuvring over extremely rough terrain, including large boulders, rock piles, mountain trails, and other obstacles that appear impassable. Drivers are often guided by a spotter standing outside the vehicle.

Vehicles

Rock crawlers are typically heavily modified. Common modifications include lift kits to raise the suspension and chassis, very large off-road tyres, locking differentials, low-range transfer cases with extremely low gearing ("crawl ratios"), upgraded axles and driveshafts, roll cages, and protective skid plates. Purpose-built competition "moon buggies" use tube-frame chassis, rear steering, and hydraulic suspension systems that allow individual wheels to be raised or lowered.

Technique

Speeds are often below 5 km/h. The driver creeps over obstacles, allowing the tyres to deform and grip the rock surface. Air pressure in the tyres is frequently lowered drastically to increase the contact patch. A spotter directs the driver, helping choose lines and avoid rollovers, which are a constant risk on steep and off-camber terrain.

Competitions and popular areas

Organised competitions are held in the United States and elsewhere, with courses marked by cones over natural or artificial rock obstacles and points deducted for touching cones, reversing, or using winches. Famous recreational crawling areas include the Rubicon Trail in California and the slickrock trails around Moab, Utah, where events such as the Easter Jeep Safari draw thousands of participants.

Environmental concerns

Because crawling takes place in fragile desert and mountain environments, enthusiasts promote "tread lightly" ethics — staying on established trails, avoiding damage to vegetation and cryptobiotic soils, and packing out waste.
