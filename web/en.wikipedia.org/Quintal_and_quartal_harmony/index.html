Quintal and quartal harmony

In music, quartal harmony is the building of harmonic structures built from the intervals of the fourth (and often perfect fourths), and quintal harmony is built from the intervals of the fifth. This contrasts with traditional tertian harmony, in which chords are built by stacking thirds. Because a chord built in fourths can be inverted to give a chord built in fifths, the two concepts are closely related and are often discussed together.

History

Voicings built on fourths appear in medieval organum, but systematic quartal writing emerged in the early 20th century in the work of composers such as Alexander Scriabin — whose "mystic chord" is quartal in construction — Arnold Schoenberg, Paul Hindemith, and Charles Ives. In jazz, quartal voicings became a hallmark of modal playing: McCoy Tyner's piano voicings with the John Coltrane Quartet and Chick Corea's writing made stacked fourths a standard device, and the "So What" chord from Miles Davis's Kind of Blue (1959) is a famous quartal voicing.

Characteristics

Because the perfect fourth is ambiguous in tonal function (it can sound stable or like a suspension), quartal chords tend to sound open and modern, and they fit readily over modal scales. Quartal voicings leave the mode-defining third out of the chord, letting melody determine major or minor colour.
