Gadsden Purchase

The Gadsden Purchase (Spanish: La Venta de La Mesilla, "The Sale of La Mesilla") was an agreement between the United States and Mexico, finalized in 1854, in which the United States agreed to pay Mexico $10 million for a 29,640-square-mile (76,800 km²) portion of Mexico that later became part of Arizona and southwestern New Mexico. It was the last major territorial acquisition in the contiguous United States, completing the modern border between the two countries.

Background

After the Mexican–American War and the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848), disputes remained over the boundary in the Mesilla Valley, and American railroad interests sought a southern route for a transcontinental railroad that would avoid snow and difficult mountain passes. Secretary of War Jefferson Davis, a proponent of a southern railroad, supported acquiring the strip of land. James Gadsden, the American minister to Mexico and a railroad promoter from South Carolina, negotiated with Mexican president Antonio López de Santa Anna, whose government needed money.

Terms and ratification

The treaty was signed in Mexico City on December 30, 1853. The original draft envisioned a larger purchase, but the United States Senate reduced the territory and the price to $10 million, and added protections; the amended treaty was ratified and proclaimed on June 30, 1854. The purchased land lies south of the Gila River and west of the Rio Grande, encompassing the present-day cities of Tucson and Yuma, Arizona.

Legacy

The acquisition fulfilled the goal of securing a practicable southern railroad route; the Southern Pacific Railroad later built through the region in the 1880s. The purchase also provoked controversy in Mexico, contributing to Santa Anna's overthrow, and in the United States it intensified sectional debates over the expansion of slavery into new territories.
