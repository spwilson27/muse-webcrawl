Cultural assimilation

Cultural assimilation typically proceeds over generations as minority groups adopt the language and customs of a dominant culture.

Cultural assimilation is the process in which a minority group or culture comes to resemble a society's majority group or assimilates the values, behaviors, and beliefs of another group, whether fully or partially. The different types of cultural assimilation include full assimilation and forced assimilation; full assimilation is the more prevalent of the two, as it occurs spontaneously. When used as a political policy, assimilation may be pursued voluntarily or imposed coercively on ethnic, religious, or indigenous minorities.

Overview

Assimilation is generally understood as a multi-stage process. Sociologist Milton Gordon identified dimensions including cultural or behavioral assimilation (acculturation), structural assimilation (entry into the institutions and social circles of the host society), marital assimilation (intermarriage), and identificational assimilation (adopting the host identity). The concept became central to the study of immigration in the United States in the early 20th century, where the "melting pot" metaphor described the blending of immigrant cultures into a common American culture.

Forced assimilation

Forced assimilation has been imposed by many states on indigenous peoples and minorities. Examples include the residential school systems for indigenous children in Canada, the United States, and Australia; the Russification policies of the Russian Empire and Soviet Union; and prohibitions on minority languages in various countries. Such policies are now widely regarded as violations of human and cultural rights and, in some analyses, as elements of cultural genocide.

Criticism and alternatives

Critics argue that assimilationist expectations can erase valuable cultural diversity and impose unequal burdens on minorities. Alternative models include multiculturalism, integration (adaptation without loss of heritage culture), and cultural pluralism. Empirical research finds that assimilation outcomes vary widely by group, generation, and host-country institutions; "segmented assimilation" theory notes that some immigrants integrate into disadvantaged segments of the host society rather than its mainstream.
