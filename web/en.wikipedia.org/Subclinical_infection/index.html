Subclinical infection

A subclinical infection — sometimes called a silent infection or an asymptomatic infection — is an infection by a pathogen that causes few or no signs or symptoms of illness in the infected host. A subclinical infection differs from a clinical infection, in which symptoms are apparent. The classic example is the "Typhoid Mary" case: Mary Mallon, a cook in New York in the early 20th century, carried Salmonella typhi and spread typhoid fever to many people while remaining healthy herself.

Transmission

Because subclinically infected individuals feel well, they often do not know they are infected and may continue normal activities, making them important sources of transmission. Such asymptomatic carriers can sustain chains of infection in a population and complicate control measures aimed only at symptomatic cases.

Examples

Many common pathogens produce subclinical infections in a substantial fraction of cases. A large proportion of chlamydia infections, herpes simplex infections, and poliovirus infections are subclinical; in poliomyelitis, roughly 90–95% of infections produce no paralysis and often no symptoms at all. Subclinical infection is also documented for tuberculosis (latent tuberculosis infection), Helicobacter pylori, and many respiratory viruses.

Detection

Subclinical infections are typically detected through serological testing, culture, or molecular methods such as PCR rather than through clinical presentation. Population-level screening programmes are often designed to find subclinical cases before complications or further transmission occur.
