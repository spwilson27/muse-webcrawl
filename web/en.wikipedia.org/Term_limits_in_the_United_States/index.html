Term limits in the United States restrict the number of terms that officeholders may serve. The most prominent is the two-term limit on the presidency, established by the Twenty-second Amendment (ratified 1951) after Franklin D. Roosevelt was elected to four terms. No term limits apply to members of Congress, but many states impose them on governors, state legislators, and local officials.

Presidential term limits

George Washington's voluntary retirement after two terms set a precedent followed until 1940. The 22nd Amendment provides that no person may be elected president more than twice, and a person who has served more than two years of another's term may be elected only once.

Congressional term limits

During the 1990s, numerous states adopted limits on their congressional delegations, but in U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton (1995) the Supreme Court held 5–4 that states may not add qualifications for federal office beyond those in the Constitution; congressional term limits would require a constitutional amendment. Proposed amendments have repeatedly failed in Congress.

State and local limits

Most states limit their governors to two terms (Virginia uniquely limits governors to a single consecutive term, though they may return after leaving office). Fifteen states have term limits for state legislators, adopted mostly by ballot initiative in the 1990s. Many cities, including New York and Los Angeles, limit mayors and council members. Supporters argue limits curb incumbency advantage and encourage turnover; critics contend they shift power to lobbyists and staff and remove experienced legislators against voters' wishes.
