The Araneomorphae (also called the Labidognatha or "true spiders") are an infraorder of spiders. They are distinguished by having fangs that oppose each other and cross in a pinching action, in contrast to the Mygalomorphae (tarantulas and their kin), whose fangs are nearly parallel and point downward. Araneomorphs comprise the overwhelming majority of living spider species — roughly 93 per cent of the more than 50,000 described species — including orb-weavers, jumping spiders, wolf spiders, sheet-weavers, and house spiders.

Characteristics

In addition to their opposing, pincer-like cheliceral fangs, most araneomorphs have only one pair of book lungs (the second pair being modified into tracheae), and most have six spinnerets, though some groups have fewer. Many araneomorphs produce cribellate or ecribellate silk webs of great variety, and the group includes the most visually acute spiders (the jumping spiders, family Salticidae) as well as the spitting spiders, which eject a silk-and-venom mixture onto prey. Araneomorph lifespans are typically short compared to mygalomorphs; most live only one or two years.

Taxonomy

The Araneomorphae are divided into a number of superfamilies and families, with relationships revised by molecular phylogenetic studies in the 2010s and 2020s. Major lineages include the Hypochilidae (lampshade weavers, often treated as the sister group to the rest), the Synspermiata, the entelegyne spiders — whose females possess a complex sclerotised genital plate, the epigyne — and within them great radiations such as the RTA clade (named for a retrolateral tibial apophysis on the male pedipalp) and the orb-weaving Araneoidea. Familiar families include Araneidae (orb-weavers), Salticidae (jumping spiders, the largest spider family), Lycosidae (wolf spiders), Theridiidae (cobweb spiders, including widow spiders), and Linyphiidae (sheetweb and money spiders).

Evolution

The split between araneomorphs and mygalomorphs is ancient, dating to the Triassic or earlier. Araneomorphs diversified dramatically in the Cretaceous alongside flowering plants and insect prey. Amber deposits from Myanmar and the Baltic preserve diverse Cretaceous and Eocene araneomorph faunas, including extinct families.
