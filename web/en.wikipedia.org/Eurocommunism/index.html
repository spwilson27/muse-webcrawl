Eurocommunism

Eurocommunism was a trend among Western European communist parties in the 1970s that sought a democratic, nationally specific road to socialism, independent of the Soviet Union. It was associated above all with the Italian Communist Party (PCI) under Enrico Berlinguer, the Spanish Communist Party under Santiago Carrillo, and — more briefly and ambivalently — the French Communist Party.

Programme

Eurocommunist parties accepted parliamentary democracy, political pluralism and civil liberties as permanent features of a future socialist society rather than transitional concessions; rejected the Soviet model and the "dictatorship of the proletariat" in its Leninist form; and criticised Soviet interventions, from the 1968 invasion of Czechoslovakia onward. Carrillo's book Eurocommunism and the State (1977) systematised the position. Berlinguer's "historic compromise" strategy sought cooperation with Italy's Christian Democrats.

Decline

The trend faded in the 1980s: the French party retreated after its government participation ended, the Spanish party split and declined, and although the PCI remained Italy's second party it never entered national government. After 1989–91 the very framework of communism collapsed, but Eurocommunism is often seen as anticipating later democratic-left reinventions.
