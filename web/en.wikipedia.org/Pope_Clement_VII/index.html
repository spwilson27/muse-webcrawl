Pope Clement VII

Pope Clement VII (born Giulio de' Medici; 26 May 1478 – 25 September 1534) was head of the Catholic Church and ruler of the Papal States from 19 November 1523 to his death in 1534. His pontificate, one of the most turbulent of the Renaissance, encompassed the Sack of Rome (1527), the beginning of the English Reformation through his refusal to annul the marriage of Henry VIII and Catherine of Aragon, and the spread of Protestantism in Germany and Switzerland.

Early life

Giulio was the posthumous son of Giuliano de' Medici, murdered in the Pazzi conspiracy, and was raised by his uncle Lorenzo the Magnificent in Florence. His cousin, Pope Leo X, made him Archbishop of Florence and a cardinal in 1513, and as vice-chancellor he became one of the most powerful men in Rome. He also effectively governed Florence on behalf of the Medici.

Pontificate

Elected after a long conclave, Clement faced the Habsburg–Valois struggle for Italy. His shifting alliances — first with Emperor Charles V, then with France in the League of Cognac — ended in catastrophe when mutinous Imperial troops, many of them Lutheran Landsknechte, sacked Rome in May 1527; Clement took refuge in the Castel Sant'Angelo and escaped to Orvieto, while thousands of Romans were killed and the city looted. Reconciled with Charles V, he crowned him emperor at Bologna in 1530. Meanwhile his refusal — influenced by Charles V, Catherine of Aragon's nephew — to grant Henry VIII an annulment led to the English break with Rome: Henry's marriage to Anne Boleyn, Clement's excommunication of Henry (1533), and ultimately the English Reformation. Clement commissioned Michelangelo's Last Judgment and the Medici Chapel, and in 1533 arranged the marriage of his great-niece Catherine de' Medici to the future Henry II of France.

Death and assessment

Clement died in September 1534, reportedly after eating poisonous mushrooms. Historians often judge him an intelligent patron of the arts who was indecisive and unlucky as a politician, presiding over the greatest disasters to befall the Renaissance papacy.
