The October Crisis (French: Crise d'Octobre) was a chain of political events in Canada in the autumn of 1970, triggered by the kidnapping of two officials by the Front de libération du Québec (FLQ), a militant Quebec separatist group. It remains the only peacetime use of the War Measures Act in Canadian history and a defining moment in the relationship between Quebec and the federal government.

Background

Throughout the 1960s the FLQ carried out a campaign of bombings, robberies and propaganda in support of an independent, socialist Quebec. The Quiet Revolution had rapidly modernised Quebec society, heightening nationalist sentiment. In February 1970 two FLQ cells, later known as the Liberation cell and the Chénier cell, were formed with plans to kidnap foreign officials.

The kidnappings

On 5 October 1970, members of the Liberation cell kidnapped British trade commissioner James Cross from his Montreal home. Five days later, on 10 October, the Chénier cell kidnapped Quebec's minister of labour and deputy premier, Pierre Laporte, while he was playing football with his nephew. The FLQ issued demands including the release of imprisoned members, a ransom, and the broadcast of its manifesto, which was read on Radio-Canada.

War Measures Act

At the request of Quebec premier Robert Bourassa and Montreal mayor Jean Drapeau, Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau invoked the War Measures Act on 16 October 1970, suspending civil liberties and outlawing the FLQ. Hundreds of people were arrested and detained without charge; troops were deployed in Ottawa and Quebec. When asked how far he was prepared to go, Trudeau famously replied, "Just watch me."

Murder of Pierre Laporte

On 17 October, Laporte's body was found in the trunk of a car near Saint-Hubert airport; he had been strangled. His killing shocked the country and turned public opinion sharply against the FLQ.

Resolution and aftermath

James Cross was released on 3 December 1970 after police located his captors, who were exiled to Cuba. The Chénier cell members were captured on 28 December; Paul Rose and others were convicted of Laporte's murder. The crisis discredited violent separatism, and support shifted toward the democratic sovereigntist movement led by René Lévesque's Parti Québécois. The War Measures Act was later replaced by the Emergencies Act of 1988.
