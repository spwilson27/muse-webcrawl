Barnum effect

The Barnum effect, also called the Forer effect, is a psychological phenomenon whereby individuals give high accuracy ratings to descriptions of their personality that are supposedly tailored specifically to them, yet are in fact vague and general enough to apply to a wide range of people. It helps explain the popular acceptance of astrology, fortune telling, graphology, and some types of personality tests.

Research

The effect is named after a phrase often attributed to showman P. T. Barnum — that his attractions had "a little something for everybody". In 1948 psychologist Bertram R. Forer gave his students a personality test, then handed each an identical generic description assembled from horoscope statements; students rated its accuracy at an average of 4.3 out of 5. The study has been replicated many times. The effect is stronger when the description is perceived as personalised, comes from a high-status source, and emphasises positive traits.

Applications

Researchers and mentalists use Barnum statements in cold reading. Skeptics cite the effect in critiques of paranormal and pseudoscientific personality assessments, and psychologists note the importance of controlling for it in validating legitimate psychological tests.
