Seagrass

Seagrasses are flowering plants (angiosperms) that live fully submerged in shallow marine and estuarine waters. Despite the name they are not true grasses, though several have long, grass-like leaves; they evolved from land plants that returned to the sea, and they retain roots, rhizomes, flowers and seeds, pollinating underwater. Around 60–70 species in about a dozen genera — including Zostera (eelgrass), Posidonia and Thalassia (turtle grass) — form extensive underwater meadows on all continents except Antarctica.

Ecology

Seagrass meadows are among the most productive and valuable coastal ecosystems. They shelter juvenile fish, crustaceans and shellfish, provide food for grazers such as green turtles, dugongs and manatees, stabilise sediments with their rhizome mats, filter nutrients and pathogens from the water, and protect shorelines from erosion. Seagrass beds are also significant stores of "blue carbon": their sediments bury organic carbon for millennia, and although seagrasses occupy a small fraction of the ocean floor they account for a disproportionate share of the carbon buried in marine sediments.

Threats and conservation

Globally, seagrass meadows have declined steeply since the late nineteenth century, with losses commonly estimated at around a third of their historical extent. Causes include nutrient pollution and algal shading, dredging and coastal development, boating damage, warming waters and disease — a wasting disease devastated Atlantic eelgrass in the 1930s. Because recovery is slow, conservation focuses on protecting surviving meadows and improving water quality, while restoration projects transplant shoots or sow seeds; some large-scale efforts, such as those in Virginia's coastal bays, have re-established thousands of hectares. Neptune grass (Posidonia oceanica) meadows in the Mediterranean, which may be thousands of years old as single clones, are protected under European law.
