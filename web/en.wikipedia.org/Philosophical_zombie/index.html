Philosophical zombie

FieldPhilosophy of mind
Key proponentsRobert Kirk (coined the term); David Chalmers
Used to argue againstPhysicalism / materialism about consciousness
Related conceptsQualia, hard problem of consciousness, inverted spectrum

A philosophical zombie (or p-zombie) is a hypothetical being that is physically and behaviourally identical to a normal human yet lacks conscious experience (qualia). A zombie would react to stimuli, report pain, and insist it is conscious, while — by stipulation — there is "nothing it is like" to be the zombie. The concept, coined by philosopher Robert Kirk in the 1970s, was popularised by David Chalmers in The Conscious Mind (1996).

The zombie argument

Chalmers argues that zombies are conceivable — describable without contradiction — and that under a widely held link between conceivability and metaphysical possibility, their possibility shows that consciousness does not logically supervene on the physical facts. If a complete physical duplicate of our world could lack experience, then facts about experience are further facts beyond the physical, and physicalism is false. The argument is a descendant of Descartes's conceivability reasoning about mind and body.

Criticisms

Physicalists typically respond by denying one of the premises. Some, such as Daniel Dennett, hold that zombies are not genuinely conceivable once all the functional detail is filled in — that a perfect physical duplicate would necessarily be conscious, and the intuition otherwise rests on "intuition pumps" and impoverished imagination. Others distinguish senses of conceivability and deny that ideal conceivability entails metaphysical possibility (the "type-B materialist" response). The debate is a central front in contemporary philosophy of mind and closely tied to the hard problem of consciousness and the explanatory gap.
