Blueschist

Blueschist, also called glaucophane schist, is a metavolcanic rock that forms by the metamorphism of basalt and rocks with similar composition at high pressures and low temperatures (200–500 °C), corresponding to a depth of some 15–30 km. The blue color of the rock comes from the presence of the amphibole mineral glaucophane and the pyroxene-related mineral lawsonite. Blueschists are schists typically found within orogenic belts as terranes of metamorphosed rocks, or mélanges, which record the conditions of subduction zones.

Glaucophane gives blueschist its characteristic blue colour.

Formation

Blueschist facies metamorphism requires an unusually low geothermal gradient: rocks are buried rapidly to great depth while remaining relatively cold. Such conditions occur almost exclusively in subduction zones, where a cold oceanic slab descends into the mantle faster than it can heat up. The "blueschist facies" was defined as a distinct pressure–temperature regime of regional metamorphism, and its recognition provided key evidence supporting the plate tectonics revolution, because it explained how oceanic rocks could be metamorphosed at high pressure and later returned to the surface.

Mineralogy

The characteristic mineral assemblage includes glaucophane (a blue sodium amphibole), lawsonite or epidote, and commonly chlorite, albite, titanite, and sometimes garnet or jadeitic pyroxene. With increasing temperature at similar pressures, blueschists grade into eclogites or epidote-amphibolites. Lawsonite blueschists form at the coldest, highest-pressure end of the facies and are important reservoirs of water transported into the deep mantle, since lawsonite can carry significant water to depths exceeding 100 km.

Occurrences

Classic blueschist localities include the Franciscan Complex of California, the island of Syros and Sifnos in Greece, the Sanbagawa belt of Japan, New Caledonia, and the schistes lustrés of the western Alps. Because blueschist requires rapid burial and exhumation, most exposed blueschists are geologically young—few are older than the Jurassic—which has been interpreted as evidence that modern-style cold subduction became widespread relatively late in Earth history, though older examples, such as Neoproterozoic blueschists in central Africa and China, are known.
