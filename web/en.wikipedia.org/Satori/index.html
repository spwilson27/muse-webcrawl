Satori

Satori (Japanese: 悟り) is a Japanese Buddhist term for awakening, "comprehension; understanding." In the Zen tradition, satori refers to the experience of kenshō, "seeing into one's true nature." It is commonly translated as enlightenment — a sudden, intuitive insight into the nature of existence.

In Zen Buddhism

Satori is considered a decisive goal of Zen practice, reached through meditation (zazen), the study of kōans, and the guidance of a teacher. The Rinzai school emphasises sudden breakthrough experiences, while the Sōtō school tends to stress gradual cultivation, though both recognise awakening experiences. Satori is not regarded as a permanent final state: it is a glimpse of truth that must be deepened and integrated through continued practice.

Distinction from kenshō

The terms satori and kenshō are often used interchangeably, but kenshō is sometimes understood as a first insight, whereas satori denotes a deeper and more enduring realisation. Both stand in contrast to the Pāli Buddhist concept of nibbāna as presented in Theravāda, though they express related soteriological aims within Mahāyāna.

In popular culture

The concept entered Western awareness in the mid-20th century through the writings of D. T. Suzuki and the works of the Beat Generation, notably Jack Kerouac's novel The Dharma Bums, and has since been widely used in Western spiritual and literary contexts.
