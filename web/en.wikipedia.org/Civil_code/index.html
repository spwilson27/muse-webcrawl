A civil code is a codification of private law relating to property, family, and obligations. A jurisdiction that has a civil code generally also has a code of civil procedure. Civil codes are the central source of law in civil law jurisdictions, in contrast to common law systems, which rely primarily on precedent and case law.

The archetypal civil code is the French Code civil des Français of 1804, usually called the Code Napoléon. It was drafted by a commission of four jurists and strongly influenced by Roman law and revolutionary ideals of equality before the law, freedom of contract, and the protection of private property. Through conquest and imitation, the French model spread across Europe, Latin America, and beyond.

Contents

Civil codes typically begin with general provisions on persons and legal capacity, then treat family law, property (ownership and other real rights), obligations and contracts, delict (tort), and succession (inheritance). The German Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch (BGB), in force from 1900, adopted a more abstract structure, beginning with a "general part" (Allgemeiner Teil) governing all areas of private law.

History

Codification has ancient roots: the Code of Hammurabi and Roman law compilations such as Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis (6th century AD) systematised law in writing. After the Napoleonic Code, major codifications followed in Austria (ABGB, 1811), Germany (BGB, 1896/1900), Switzerland (ZGB, 1907/1912), and many other countries; Japan's civil code (1896–1898) blended French and German influences. China adopted its first comprehensive civil code in 2020, in force from 1 January 2021.

Role in civil law systems

In civil law countries, the code is intended to be a complete, systematic, and accessible statement of private law that courts apply deductively. Scholars and commentaries play an important interpretive role, though modern codes are supplemented by case law and special statutes.
