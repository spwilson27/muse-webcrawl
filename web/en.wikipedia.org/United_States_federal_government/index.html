The federal government of the United States of America (US federal government) is the national government of the United States, a federation of 50 states, a federal district, and various territories. Established by the Constitution in 1789, it is organized on the principle of separation of powers into three branches — legislative, executive and judicial — each checking the others, and shares sovereignty with the state governments under the system of federalism.

Legislative branch

Legislative power is vested in the bicameral Congress: the Senate (100 members, two per state) and the House of Representatives (435 members, apportioned by population). Congress enacts statutes, controls appropriations, declares war, and provides oversight of the executive.

Executive branch

The President, elected every four years through the Electoral College, is head of state and government, commander-in-chief of the armed forces and chief executive of the federal administration. The Vice President, 15 executive departments headed by Cabinet secretaries (State, Treasury, Defense, Justice, Interior, Agriculture, Commerce, Labor, Health and Human Services, Housing and Urban Development, Transportation, Energy, Education, Veterans Affairs, Homeland Security) and numerous independent agencies execute and enforce the laws.

Judicial branch

Judicial power is vested in the Supreme Court — nine justices appointed for life by the President with Senate confirmation — and in lower federal courts (94 district courts and 13 courts of appeals) created by Congress. Since Marbury v. Madison (1803) the courts exercise judicial review over the constitutionality of laws and executive actions.

Federalism and history

The Constitution replaced the Articles of Confederation, whose weak central government had proved unworkable. The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states or the people all powers not delegated to the federal government, though the commerce and spending powers, the Fourteenth Amendment, and two centuries of case law have greatly expanded federal reach — notably during the Civil War, the New Deal and the Great Society.

Scale

The federal government is the nation's largest employer, with about 2.1 million civilian employees plus roughly 1.3 million active-duty military personnel (postal workers included in civilian counts depending on measure); annual federal spending is on the order of US$6–7 trillion, around a quarter of GDP.
