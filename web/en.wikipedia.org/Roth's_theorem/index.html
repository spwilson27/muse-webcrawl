Roth's theorem

Roth's theorem is a result in Diophantine approximation proved by Klaus Roth in 1955: for any algebraic number α and any ε > 0, there are only finitely many rational numbers p/q with |α − p/q| < 1/q2+ε. In other words, algebraic numbers cannot be approximated by rationals much better than the generic rate given by Dirichlet's theorem.

Significance

The theorem completed a line of results by Thue and Siegel (the Thue–Siegel–Roth theorem) and showed that Liouville-type transcendence arguments could be sharpened dramatically; Roth received the Fields Medal in 1958. A corollary is that numbers with very good rational approximations — such as Liouville numbers — must be transcendental.

Related results

The proof is ineffective: it does not bound the size of the exceptional approximations. Generalisations include Schmidt's subspace theorem. The name "Roth's theorem" is also used in additive combinatorics for Klaus Roth's 1953 theorem that sets of integers of positive density contain three-term arithmetic progressions — a foundation of modern additive number theory.
