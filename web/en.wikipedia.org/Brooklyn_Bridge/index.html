The Brooklyn Bridge is a hybrid cable-stayed/suspension bridge in New York City, spanning the East River between Manhattan and Brooklyn. Opened on 24 May 1883, it was the first fixed crossing of the East River and, at the time, the longest suspension bridge in the world. It has become an iconic symbol of New York City and was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1964.

Construction

The bridge was designed by John Augustus Roebling, who died of tetanus following a site accident in 1869; his son Washington Roebling directed construction, later working from home after suffering decompression sickness ("the bends") from the pressurized pneumatic caissons used to found the two Gothic-arched granite towers. His wife, Emily Warren Roebling, served as his on-site liaison for over a decade. At least 20 workers died during construction. The bridge's four main cables contain 5,434 steel wires each spun in place, and its stiffening trusses made it exceptionally rigid.

History

On opening day 150,000 people crossed; six days later, a stampede sparked by a rumor of collapse killed 12. P. T. Barnum famously marched 21 elephants across in 1884 to prove its stability. Originally carrying horse-drawn vehicles and elevated trains, it now carries six lanes of motor traffic plus a celebrated elevated promenade for pedestrians and cyclists, used by over 100,000 vehicles and thousands of walkers daily.
