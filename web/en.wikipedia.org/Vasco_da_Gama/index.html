Vasco da Gama (c. 1460s – 24 December 1524), 1st Count of Vidigueira, was a Portuguese explorer and nobleman who was the first European to reach India by sea. His initial voyage to India by way of Cape of Good Hope (1497–1499) was the first to link Europe and Asia by an ocean route, connecting the Atlantic and the Indian oceans. This is widely considered a milestone in world history, as it marked the beginning of a sea-based phase of globalization.

First voyage

Da Gama's voyage succeeded in reaching India after decades of Portuguese attempts to find a sea route around Africa, following Bartolomeu Dias's rounding of the Cape of Good Hope in 1488. The fleet of four ships — São Gabriel, São Rafael, Berrio and a store ship — left Lisbon on 8 July 1497. After stops at Mozambique, Mombasa and Malindi, where a local pilot guided them across the Indian Ocean, the fleet reached Calicut (Kozhikode) on 20 May 1498. Trade negotiations with the Zamorin were frustrated by the poor quality of the Portuguese goods offered, but da Gama returned to Lisbon in 1499 with a cargo and the route established, having lost more than half his men, mostly to scurvy.

Second voyage

In 1502 da Gama sailed again with a fleet of 20 warships, the Fourth India Armada, tasked with enforcing Portuguese interests by force. He bombarded Kilwa, demanded tribute, and at Calicut notoriously attacked a ship returning from Mecca carrying hundreds of pilgrims, an act condemned by contemporaries. He established trading posts at Mozambique and Sofala and a factory at Cannanore, returning to Portugal in 1503.

Viceroy and death

After two decades in semi-retirement, da Gama was named Governor (Viceroy) of India in 1524 and returned with a mandate to reform the administration. He died of malaria at Kochi on Christmas Eve 1524, three months after arriving. His remains were returned to Portugal in 1539 and now rest in the Jerónimos Monastery in Lisbon. The Portuguese national epic, Luís de Camões's Os Lusíadas (1572), celebrates his voyage.
