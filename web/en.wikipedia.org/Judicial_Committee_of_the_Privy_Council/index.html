Judicial Committee of the Privy Council

Established: 1833 (Judicial Committee Act)
Location: Middlesex Guildhall, Parliament Square, London
Role: Final court of appeal for certain Commonwealth jurisdictions, Crown Dependencies, and UK overseas territories
Members: Predominantly Justices of the UK Supreme Court

The Judicial Committee of the Privy Council (JCPC) is the highest court of appeal for the Crown Dependencies, the British Overseas Territories, and some independent Commonwealth states. Established in 1833, it is one of the oldest judicial institutions in the world and historically served as the final appellate court for the entire British Empire.

History

The committee grew out of the medieval practice of petitioning the King in Council for redress. The Judicial Committee Act 1833 put it on a statutory footing as a standing tribunal of senior judges. At the height of empire it heard appeals from courts across a quarter of the globe, shaping the common law in fields from constitutional law to commercial law. Decisions were delivered as a single advice to the sovereign — technically, the committee "advises" the monarch, who gives effect to its judgment by Order in Council — a practice maintained today, although dissenting opinions have been permitted since 1966.

Jurisdiction today

Most large Commonwealth countries have abolished appeals to the Privy Council: Canada in 1949, India in 1949, Australia finally in 1986, and New Zealand in 2003. The committee remains the final court for Caribbean states such as Jamaica and the Bahamas (though several have transferred their final appeals to the Caribbean Court of Justice), for Mauritius, Brunei, and Pacific nations including Tuvalu and Kiribati, and for the Crown Dependencies of Jersey, Guernsey, and the Isle of Man. It also hears devolution and ecclesiastical cases and appeals from certain professional disciplinary bodies within the United Kingdom itself.

Composition and procedure

The panel is drawn mainly from the Justices of the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom, supplemented by senior judges from Commonwealth jurisdictions. Since 2009 it has sat in the Middlesex Guildhall on Parliament Square, sharing a building with the UK Supreme Court. Its judgments, though formally advice, are treated as authoritative statements of the common law and are widely cited.
