Diverticulosis

Diverticulosis

Specialty: Gastroenterology

Symptoms: Usually none

Complications: Diverticulitis, bleeding, perforation

Risk factors: Age, low-fibre diet, obesity

Diverticulosis is the condition of having multiple pouches (diverticula) in the colon wall. Diverticula are small outpouchings that form where the inner lining of the intestine pushes through weak spots in the muscular wall, often where blood vessels enter. The condition is extremely common in older adults in Western countries and usually causes no symptoms.

Signs and symptoms

Most people with diverticulosis are asymptomatic, and the condition is often discovered incidentally during colonoscopy or imaging done for other reasons. Some people experience cramping, bloating, or changes in bowel habit. Complications occur in a minority: inflammation of a diverticulum causes diverticulitis, with pain (typically in the left lower abdomen), fever, and nausea; diverticula can also bleed, sometimes heavily, or perforate and cause abscess or peritonitis.

Causes

Diverticulosis has traditionally been attributed to a low-fibre diet, which produces small, hard stools and raises pressure inside the colon, promoting herniation of the lining. More recent research also implicates genetics, ageing-related changes in the colonic wall, obesity, smoking, and lack of exercise. Prevalence rises steeply with age, exceeding 50 percent in people over 60 in Western populations, while remaining less common in rural Africa and Asia.

Diagnosis and management

Diverticulosis is diagnosed by colonoscopy, CT scan, or barium enema. Uncomplicated, asymptomatic diverticulosis needs no treatment beyond a fibre-rich diet and healthy lifestyle; contrary to older advice, nuts, seeds, and popcorn do not appear to increase the risk of complications. Diverticulitis is treated with antibiotics, bowel rest, and in severe or recurrent cases, surgery.
