Animal echolocation, also called biosonar, is the biological sonar used by several animal species to navigate and hunt. An echolocating animal emits sounds — usually high-frequency pulses or clicks — and listens to the echoes returning from nearby objects. From the delay, direction and spectral character of the echoes the animal derives the distance, size, shape, texture and even motion of objects, allowing it to perceive its environment in complete darkness.

History of discovery

In the 1790s Lazzaro Spallanzani showed that bats navigate by hearing rather than sight, an effect explained only in 1938 when Donald Griffin and Robert Galambos demonstrated, using early ultrasonic microphones, that bats emit sounds far above the range of human hearing and steer by their echoes. Griffin coined the term "echolocation" in 1944. Sonar-like behaviour in dolphins was demonstrated experimentally in the 1950s.

Bats

Most microchiropteran bats echolocate with calls produced in the larynx, typically between about 20 and 200 kHz. Calls may be frequency-modulated (FM) sweeps, good for resolving fine detail at short range, or constant-frequency (CF) components, used by horseshoe and mustached bats to detect the flutter of insect wings through Doppler shifts — with an "acoustic fovea" in the cochlea tuned to the echo frequency. As a bat closes on prey its call rate rises into a terminal "feeding buzz". Some moths and other prey have evolved ears sensitive to bat ultrasound and jamming or evasive responses, an ongoing evolutionary arms race.

Toothed whales

Dolphins, porpoises, sperm whales and other odontocetes generate clicks in specialised nasal structures; the fatty melon on the forehead focuses the sound into a beam, and echoes are received chiefly through the lower jaw, which conducts them to the ear. Dolphin biosonar can distinguish objects differing by millimetres and detect buried or camouflaged targets, and it operates effectively in turbid water where vision fails. Trained and wild dolphins alike adjust click rate and intensity to the task, a flexibility that has inspired engineered sonar systems.

Other animals

Oilbirds and some swiftlets echolocate with audible clicks to navigate dark caves, though with much coarser resolution than bats. Some shrews and the Madagascan tenrec use simple ultrasonic emissions for orientation, and there is evidence that a few other mammals use rudimentary forms of the sense. A small number of blind humans have independently learned active echolocation using tongue clicks, demonstrating the plasticity of the auditory system.
