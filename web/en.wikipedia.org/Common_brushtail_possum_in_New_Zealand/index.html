Common brushtail possum in New Zealand

Brushtail possum in New Zealand
Species: Trichosurus vulpecula
Introduced: from 1837 (from Australia)
Status: Major invasive pest
Estimated population: tens of millions (declining under control)

The common brushtail possum (Trichosurus vulpecula) was introduced to New Zealand from Australia beginning in 1837 to establish a fur trade. Lacking natural predators, the nocturnal marsupial multiplied to occupy nearly all of the country's forests and farmland, becoming one of New Zealand's most damaging invasive pests.

Ecological impact

Possums browse native trees such as rātā, tōtara, and kāmahi, defoliating and sometimes killing them, and they compete with native birds for food and nest sites. They also prey on the eggs and chicks of native birds and on native invertebrates such as wētā and snails. In agriculture, possums are the main wildlife vector of bovine tuberculosis, threatening the cattle and deer industries.

Control

Control methods include trapping, hunting for fur and "possum wool" blends, and aerial distribution of 1080 (sodium fluoroacetate) poison, a practice that is effective but controversial. National programs such as TBfree and the Predator Free 2050 initiative aim to suppress or eradicate possums alongside rats and stoats.

Fur industry

Possum fur, marketed as a luxury fiber blended with merino wool, provides some economic return from the pest, and possum trapping remains a small rural industry.
