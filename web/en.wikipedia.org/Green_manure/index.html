Green manure

In agriculture, a green manure is a crop specifically cultivated to be incorporated into the soil while still green. Traditionally, green manure was incorporated by ploughing, but in no-till systems it may be cut and left as a mulch. Green manures are usually legumes or other plants grown primarily to add nutrients and organic matter to the soil, improve its structure, suppress weeds, and reduce erosion and nutrient leaching.

Functions

Green manures serve several purposes. Leguminous species such as clover, vetch, alfalfa and beans host nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) in root nodules and so add plant-available nitrogen to the soil. Deep-rooted species draw nutrients up from the subsoil and, when incorporated, release them in the topsoil. Rapidly growing cover prevents wind and water erosion, shades out weeds, and adds humus as it decomposes. Some species, such as certain mustards, are grown as biofumigants whose tissues release compounds that suppress soil-borne pests and diseases.

History

The practice is ancient: Chinese agricultural texts describe the use of grasses and weeds as fertilisers over 3,000 years ago, and the Romans grew lupins and beans to enrich fields, as noted by Pliny and Columella. Green manuring was a mainstay of fertility management in European rotations before the arrival of synthetic fertilisers, and it remains central to organic farming today.

Common species

  • Legumes: clovers (crimson, red, white), vetches, alfalfa (lucerne), fenugreek, sunn hemp, cowpea
  • Grasses and cereals: rye, oats, buckwheat, sorghum-sudangrass
  • Others: phacelia, mustards, tillage radish, marigold (Tagetes)

Management

Crops are usually incorporated or terminated at flowering, before stems become woody and before the plant sets seed, to maximise nitrogen content and avoid volunteer weeds. In rotations, a green manure may occupy a field for a few weeks between cash crops or for a full season to restore exhausted soil.
