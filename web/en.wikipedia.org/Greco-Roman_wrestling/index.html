Greco-Roman wrestling

A Greco-Roman wrestling bout. Holds below the waist are forbidden.

Greco-Roman wrestling (French: lutte gréco-romaine) is a style of wrestling practised worldwide and contested at the Olympic Games. Unlike freestyle wrestling, Greco-Roman rules forbid holds below the waist: wrestlers may not trip, grab, or use their legs to attack or defend, and all holds must be applied with the upper body. The style emphasises throws, particularly spectacular high-amplitude lifts and throws in which the opponent is raised off the mat.

History

The style was developed in Europe in the 19th century, drawing loosely on classical ideals of ancient Greek and Roman wrestling; French soldier Jean Exbroyat is often credited with codifying the rules in the mid-1800s under the name lutte à mains plates. Greco-Roman wrestling has been part of the Olympic programme since the first modern Games at Athens in 1896 and is governed internationally by United World Wrestling.

Scoring and rules

A match consists of two three-minute periods. Points are awarded for takedowns, exposures (turning an opponent's back toward the mat), reversals, and penalties, with high-scoring throws earning up to five points. A wrestler can win by fall (pinning both of the opponent's shoulders), by technical superiority (an eight-point lead), or on points. When a bout goes to the ground, the bottom wrestler defends in the par terre position, and ordered par terre restarts punish passivity. Historically the sport was restricted to men at the Olympics, with women's competition held in freestyle only.
