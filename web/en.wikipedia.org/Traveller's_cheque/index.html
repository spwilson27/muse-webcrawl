Traveller's cheque

Traveller's cheques are signed at purchase and countersigned when cashed.

Introduced1891 (American Express)
Major issuersAmerican Express, Thomas Cook, Visa, Mastercard
DenominationsFixed sums in major currencies
StatusLargely superseded by payment cards

A traveller's cheque (also spelled traveler's check) is a medium of exchange that can be used in place of hard currency. They are preprinted cheques for fixed amounts, issued by financial institutions, that allow the person who has purchased them to exchange them for cash or pay an unconditional payment order on a payee. Because purchasers were effectively lending the issuer the money interest-free until redemption, issuers profited from the "float".

Security features

The cheque's security rests on two signatures. The purchaser signs each cheque immediately on receipt, in front of the issuing agent; when cashing it, the purchaser countersigns in front of the accepting cashier, who compares the signatures. Each cheque bears a serial number, and purchasers were advised to record the numbers separately so that lost or stolen cheques could be refunded or replaced — the feature that made them attractive compared with cash.

History

The first traveller's cheques were issued in London in 1772 by the London Credit Exchange Company. American Express popularised the modern product from 1891, when its employee Marcellus Fleming Berry devised a system during a European trip on which his letters of credit had been refused. Thomas Cook also built a worldwide network for its "circular notes" and cheques. At their peak in the mid-20th century, traveller's cheques were the standard way for tourists to carry funds safely abroad.

Decline

From the 1990s the spread of ATMs, credit and debit cards — and later prepaid travel money cards — sharply reduced demand, and many merchants stopped accepting cheques. American Express remains the best-known issuer; its cheques traditionally do not expire and unspent cheques may still be redeemed.
