Authoritarianism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Regimes on the democracy–authoritarianism spectrum

Authoritarianism is a political system characterized by the rejection of political plurality, the use of a strong central power to preserve the political status quo, and reductions in democracy, separation of powers, civil liberties, and the rule of law. Authoritarian regimes may be either autocratic or oligarchic in nature and may be based upon the rule of a party or the military.

Characteristics

Political scientist Juan Linz defined authoritarianism as possessing four qualities: limited political pluralism; legitimacy based on appeals to emotion and the regime's claimed necessity; minimal political mobilization of the population; and formally ill-defined executive authority, though real limits on executive power usually exist. This contrasts with totalitarianism, which seeks to mobilize and control all aspects of society.

Subtypes

Scholars distinguish military regimes, single-party regimes, personalist regimes, and monarchies, as well as hybrid forms such as "competitive authoritarianism", in which formal democratic institutions exist but incumbents systematically tilt the field against opponents.

Psychological usage

In psychology, "authoritarianism" also denotes a personality pattern — studied since Adorno's The Authoritarian Personality (1950) and later through the right-wing authoritarianism scale — marked by submission to authority, aggression toward outgroups, and conventionalism.
