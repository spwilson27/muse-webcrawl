Misericord

A misericord (also spelled misericorde; from Latin misericordia, "mercy") is a small wooden ledge or shelf on the underside of a folding choir-stall seat in a medieval church or cathedral. When the seat was tipped up, the misericord provided a discreet support on which a monk or cleric required to stand through long services could partially rest, appearing to stand while actually being supported — an act of mercy toward the elderly or infirm.

Carvings

Because the misericord was hidden beneath the seat when in use, its carvings were among the least supervised decoration in a church, and they form one of the richest bodies of medieval secular sculpture. Subjects range from biblical scenes and saints to everyday labour, games, proverbs, fantastic beasts, green men, and frequently bawdy or scatological humour. Thousands survive in Britain, France, Spain and the Low Countries, and catalogues of them are a major resource for historians of everyday medieval life.

Terminology

The same word was used for a room in a monastery where meat could be eaten by those exempt from the usual diet, and, in another sense, for a thin-bladed dagger used to deliver a mercy stroke to a wounded knight — the misericorde — though the choir-stall fitting is the sense most commonly encountered today.
