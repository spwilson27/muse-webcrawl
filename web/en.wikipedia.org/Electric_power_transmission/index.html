Electric power transmission

Electric power transmission is the bulk movement of electrical energy from a generating site, such as a power plant, to an electrical substation where it is stepped down for distribution to consumers. The interconnected lines that carry this power form a transmission network, or grid.

High-voltage lines minimise energy losses over long distances.

High voltage

Power is transmitted at high voltages—commonly 110 kV to 765 kV for alternating current—because for a given power, raising the voltage lowers the current, and resistive losses grow with the square of the current. Transformers make it possible to step AC voltage up and down efficiently, a decisive advantage that won alternating current the "war of the currents" in the 1890s. High-voltage direct current (HVDC) is preferred for very long distances, undersea cables, and links between asynchronous grids.

The grid

Transmission networks are interconnected into wide-area synchronous grids, such as those of continental Europe and North America, so that generation and load can be balanced across regions. System operators monitor flows in real time and must keep frequency within tight limits; failures can cascade, as in the Northeast blackout of 2003 in North America and the European disturbance of 2006.

Lines and losses

Most transmission uses overhead lines on steel lattice towers, with aluminium conductors; underground cables are used in cities and under water at much higher cost. A few percent of generated energy is lost as heat in transmission and distribution, and congestion on limited lines is a growing issue as remote renewable generation expands.
