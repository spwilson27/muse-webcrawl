Stygofauna

Stygofauna are any fauna that live in groundwater systems or aquifers, including caves, fissures and pore spaces saturated with water. The name derives from the river Styx of Greek mythology. Stygofauna are found on all continents except Antarctica, are often endemic to a single aquifer, and are studied as part of the broader field of subterranean biology. Animals fully adapted to groundwater life are called stygobites (or stygobionts); those tolerant of but not restricted to it are stygophiles.

Adaptations

Life in permanent darkness and scarce food has produced characteristic traits, a pattern called troglomorphism: loss of eyes and pigmentation, elongated bodies and appendages, enhanced senses of touch and smell, slow metabolism, long life, and reproduction with few, large offspring. Most stygofauna are small invertebrates — crustaceans such as amphipods, isopods, copepods and syncarids predominate, along with worms, mites, snails and beetles.

Vertebrates and notable regions

Rare vertebrate stygofauna include blind cave fish such as the Mexican cavefish and the Texas blind salamander, and most remarkably the olm, a wholly aquatic salamander of Dinaric karst caves. Australia's arid-zone calcrete aquifers and the Pilbara and Yilgarn regions are global hotspots of stygofauna diversity, as are the Edwards Aquifer in Texas and the karst of Slovenia and Croatia. Some lineages are "living fossils" — relicts of ancient surface fauna that retreated underground as climates dried.

Conservation

Because many species occupy a single aquifer and depend on stable water quality and level, stygofauna are vulnerable to groundwater extraction, mining dewatering and pollution, and they are increasingly assessed in environmental impact studies.
