Seal (emblem)

The obverse of the Great Seal of the United States.

A seal is a device for making an impression in wax, clay, paper, or some other medium, including an embossment on paper, and is also the impression thus made. The original purpose was to authenticate a document, a wrapper for one such as a modern envelope, or the cover of a container or package holding valuables or other objects.

History

Seals were used in the earliest civilisations and are of considerable importance in archaeology and art history. In Mesopotamia, cylinder seals were rolled across wet clay to mark ownership and prevent tampering. In China, seals carved from jade, wood or stone and stamped with red paste became an essential tool of officials and artists; a similar tradition persists in East Asia, where personal seals (dojang, hanko, yinzhang) are still used in place of signatures.

Use in government

Most governments possess a great seal, normally used only once at the head of the document to which it is applied. The Great Seal of the United States, adopted in 1782, bears the bald eagle and the mottos E pluribus unum and Novus ordo seclorum. Individual states, cities, courts and corporations also maintain official seals.

Sealing wax

In medieval and early modern Europe, documents were authenticated with impressions in beeswax or in "sealing wax", a mixture of shellac, turpentine and pigments. The matrix was engraved in metal or hard stone with a design in intaglio, so that the impression stood out in relief. Breaking the seal demonstrated interference, a practice that survives in the phrase "signed, sealed, and delivered".

Signet rings

A signet ring is a ring bearing a seal device on its face, worn since antiquity — Pharaohs, Roman senators and medieval nobles used them to seal correspondence and decrees.
