Hashimoto's thyroiditis

Other namesChronic lymphocytic thyroiditis, autoimmune thyroiditis
SpecialtyEndocrinology
SymptomsFatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, goitre
CausesAutoimmune attack on the thyroid gland
TreatmentLevothyroxine replacement

Hashimoto's thyroiditis, also known as chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis, is an autoimmune disease in which the thyroid gland is gradually destroyed by the body's own immune system. It is the most common cause of hypothyroidism in developed countries and was first described by the Japanese physician Hakaru Hashimoto in 1912.

Signs and symptoms

Early in the disease the thyroid may enlarge (goitre) and hormone levels may remain normal, but over years the gland loses its ability to produce thyroid hormone. Symptoms of hypothyroidism then develop, including fatigue, weight gain, sensitivity to cold, constipation, dry skin, hair loss, muscle weakness and depression. Some people experience a brief phase of hyperthyroidism (hashitoxicosis) as stored hormone is released from the damaged gland.

Cause and mechanism

The condition arises from a combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental factors. The immune system produces antibodies against thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) and thyroglobulin, and lymphocytes infiltrate the gland, progressively destroying follicular cells. It is more common in women than men and often occurs alongside other autoimmune disorders such as type 1 diabetes and coeliac disease.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on blood tests showing elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) with low or normal free thyroxine (T4), together with positive anti-TPO antibodies. Ultrasound may show a heterogeneous, hypoechoic gland.

Treatment

Hypothyroidism is treated with daily levothyroxine, a synthetic form of thyroxine, usually taken for life with periodic monitoring of TSH. There is no cure for the underlying autoimmune process.
