Adenosine deaminase deficiency

Adenosine deaminase deficiency (ADA deficiency) is a metabolic disorder that causes immunodeficiency. It is a major cause of severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID), accounting for about 10–20% of cases. It is inherited in an autosomal recessive manner and results from mutations in the gene encoding adenosine deaminase, an enzyme of purine metabolism.

Pathophysiology

Without ADA, the substrates adenosine and deoxyadenosine accumulate. Deoxyadenosine is phosphorylated to dATP, which inhibits ribonucleotide reductase and is toxic to developing lymphocytes, destroying both T cells and B cells and thereby crippling cellular and humoral immunity. Affected infants suffer recurrent, severe and opportunistic infections, chronic diarrhoea and failure to thrive, and will die in the first years of life without treatment.

Diagnosis

ADA deficiency can be detected by measuring enzyme activity in red blood cells or by genetic testing, and is included in newborn screening programmes for SCID in many countries, typically via the T-cell receptor excision circle (TREC) assay.

Treatment

Treatments include hematopoietic stem cell transplantation from a matched donor; enzyme replacement therapy with pegylated adenosine deaminase (pegademase bovine, and the recombinant elapegademase); and gene therapy. ADA-SCID was the target of the first successful human gene therapy trials in 1990, and an ex vivo retroviral gene therapy product, Strimvelis, was approved in Europe in 2016 — the first corrective gene therapy for a genetic disease to reach the market there.
