History of bread

The history of bread reaches back at least 14,000 years, to flatbreads baked by Natufian hunter-gatherers in Jordan. Bread became a staple of agricultural civilisations and a central symbol in religion, politics and daily life.

Prehistory

Charred crumbs found at Shubayqa in Jordan, dating to around 14,400 years ago, show that wild grains were ground and baked into flatbreads millennia before farming. With the Neolithic domestication of wheat and barley, bread became a dietary foundation across the Fertile Crescent.

Ancient world

The Egyptians developed leavened bread, using wild yeast and sourdough starters, and built beehive ovens. Bread and beer, both made from grain, underpinned the Egyptian economy and were paid as wages. In Greece and Rome bread diversified by grain and grade; Rome created professional bakers' guilds and state grain distributions (annona), making "bread and circuses" a byword for public provisioning.

Medieval and early modern

In medieval Europe, dark rye and barley breads fed the peasantry while fine white wheat bread marked status. Watermills and windmills mechanised grinding, and laws such as England's Assize of Bread regulated weight and price. Bread prices remained politically explosive — a factor in the French Revolution's October Days of 1789.

Industrial era

Roller milling (1870s) produced cheap white flour; commercial yeast, and later the Chorleywood process (1961), transformed baking. Otto Rohwedder's slicing machine brought pre-sliced loaves in 1928. In the 20th century bread also became a focus of nutritional debate over refined grains and whole grains.
