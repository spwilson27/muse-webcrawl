Usage-based models of language

Usage-based models of language are approaches in linguistics holding that the grammatical structure of language emerges from language use. Rather than positing an innate, autonomous grammar, usage-based theories — associated with scholars such as Joan Bybee, Michael Tomasello, Ronald Langacker and Adele Goldberg — argue that speakers build linguistic knowledge from repeated encounters with concrete utterances, storing both specific expressions and increasingly abstract patterns generalised over them. Frequency of use is central: high-frequency forms become entrenched and resistant to change, while patterns recur and are extended by analogy.

Key concepts

Central notions include entrenchment (the strengthening of representations through repetition), chunking (sequences processed as single units), schematisation (abstraction over similar instances), and construction — a learned pairing of form and meaning at any level of complexity, from morphemes to idioms and abstract sentence patterns. Usage-based phonology (Bybee) explains sound change and morphological irregularity through token frequency effects, and usage-based acquisition research (Tomasello) shows children learning language item by item before generalising.

Relation to other theories

Usage-based models are closely allied with cognitive linguistics and construction grammar, and are commonly contrasted with generative grammar, whose proponents argue that usage alone cannot explain the rapidity and uniformity of acquisition (the "poverty of the stimulus" argument). Usage-based theorists respond that rich input, statistical learning and social cognition suffice.

Methods

The approach relies heavily on corpus linguistics, experimental studies of frequency effects, and longitudinal studies of child language, treating variation and gradual change as primary data rather than noise.
