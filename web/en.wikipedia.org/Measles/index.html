Measles

Measles is a highly contagious, vaccine-preventable infectious disease caused by measles virus. Symptoms usually develop 10–12 days after exposure to an infected person and last 7–10 days. Initial symptoms typically include fever, often greater than 40 °C (104 °F), cough, runny nose, and inflamed eyes. Small white spots known as Koplik's spots may form inside the mouth two or three days after the start of symptoms. A red, flat rash which usually starts on the face and then spreads to the rest of the body typically begins three to five days after the start of symptoms.

Signs and symptoms

The classic symptoms include a four-day fever (the "4 D's": fever, and the three C's — cough, coryza, and conjunctivitis — plus rash). Complications occur in about 30% of cases and may include diarrhea, blindness, inflammation of the brain (encephalitis), and pneumonia. Measles also suppresses the immune system for weeks to months, a phenomenon called immune amnesia, leaving survivors vulnerable to other infections.

Cause and transmission

Measles is an airborne disease caused by the measles virus, a single-stranded RNA virus of the genus Morbillivirus in the family Paramyxoviridae. It spreads easily from one person to another through the coughs and sneezes of infected people, and the virus can remain infectious in the air for up to two hours. It is one of the most contagious known diseases, with a basic reproduction number (R0) of 12–18.

Prevention

The measles vaccine — usually given as the combined MMR vaccine (measles, mumps, rubella) — is safe and highly effective. Two doses are about 97% effective at preventing the disease. Widespread vaccination dramatically reduced measles deaths: between 2000 and 2021, vaccination prevented an estimated 57 million deaths. Nevertheless, measles remains a leading cause of vaccine-preventable childhood mortality in regions with low immunization coverage.

History

Measles was first described systematically by the Persian physician Muhammad ibn Zakariya al-Razi in the 10th century. The virus was isolated in 1954 by John Enders and Thomas Peebles, and the first vaccine was licensed in 1963.
