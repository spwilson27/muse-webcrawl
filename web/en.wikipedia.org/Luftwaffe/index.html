Luftwaffe

Luftwaffe (1935–1945)
Branch: aerial warfare branch of the Wehrmacht
Founded: 15 May 1933 (secretly); publicly revealed 1935
Commander-in-chief: Hermann Göring (until April 1945), then Robert Ritter von Greim
Peak strength: ~3.4 million personnel (1944); ~119,871 aircraft produced in total
Notable aircraft: Bf 109, Fw 190, Ju 87 Stuka, He 111, Me 262
Principal campaigns: Battle of Britain, Blitz, Operation Barbarossa, Defence of the Reich

The Luftwaffe (German for "air weapon") was the aerial warfare branch of the Wehrmacht, the armed forces of Nazi Germany, during World War II. Officially unveiled in 1935 in defiance of the Treaty of Versailles, it grew under Hermann Göring into the largest and most powerful air force in Europe by 1939, shaped by combat experience gained with the Condor Legion in the Spanish Civil War. The name Luftwaffe is also used today for the air force of the Federal Republic of Germany, established in 1956 as part of the Bundeswehr.

Bf 109 fighters, the Luftwaffe's principal single-seat fighter

Build-up and doctrine

Germany rebuilt its air arm covertly in the Weimar era through the Lipetsk air base in the Soviet Union, the airline Lufthansa and gliding clubs. The Nazi-era Luftwaffe, led operationally by Walther Wever and after his death in 1936 by Ernst Udet and Hans Jeschonnek, emphasised tactical air power in support of the army — close air support and interdiction within the combined-arms Blitzkrieg — rather than independent strategic bombing. It therefore fielded excellent fighters (the Messerschmitt Bf 109) and dive-bombers (the Junkers Ju 87 Stuka) but lacked a heavy four-engined bomber; the long-range "Ural bomber" programme had been cancelled.

World War II

The Luftwaffe dominated the skies over Poland (1939) and France (1940), but failed in the Battle of Britain (1940) to win the air superiority needed for invasion, and its subsequent night Blitz on British cities did not break morale. From 1941 it supported the invasion of the USSR while also fighting in the Mediterranean and North Africa. Thereafter it was progressively ground down by attrition on multiple fronts, by Allied bombing of aircraft factories and — decisively — of fuel supplies, and by a collapsing pilot-training pipeline. Its Defence of the Reich effort against USAAF and RAF bombers consumed most of its fighter strength by 1944–45, even as it introduced revolutionary aircraft such as the Me 262 jet fighter and Arado Ar 234 jet bomber too late and in too few numbers. Luftwaffe field formations also fought as ground troops, and its Flak arm manned Germany's anti-aircraft defences.

Modern Luftwaffe

The post-war West German Luftwaffe was founded on 9 January 1956, joined NATO, and operated types including the F-104 Starfighter, Panavia Tornado and Eurofighter Typhoon; the East German air force (Luftstreitkräfte der NVA) was absorbed into it at reunification in 1990.
