Calendar reform

Calendar reform is the practice of revising a calendar system in significant ways. Historically, many calendars have been reformed to correct accumulated astronomical errors, to align civil and religious observances, or to make the calendar simpler and more rational. The term most often refers to the replacement of the Julian calendar by the Gregorian calendar, but it also encompasses numerous later proposals for a fixed, perennial civil calendar.

The Gregorian reform

By the sixteenth century the Julian calendar had drifted about ten days from the solar year, displacing the date of Easter from its traditional position relative to the March equinox. Pope Gregory XIII promulgated the Gregorian calendar in the bull Inter gravissimas (1582), removing ten days and refining the leap-year rule so that century years are leap years only when divisible by 400. Catholic countries adopted it promptly; Protestant and Orthodox countries followed over the next three centuries, Britain and its colonies in 1752 and Russia in 1918. Greece was the last European country to adopt it for civil purposes, in 1923.

Proposals for a perennial calendar

The Gregorian calendar's irregular months and shifting weekday alignment have prompted reform proposals, especially in the twentieth century. The International Fixed Calendar, championed by Moses B. Cotsworth and used internally by the Eastman Kodak Company for decades, proposed thirteen months of 28 days. The World Calendar, promoted by Elisabeth Achelis and considered by the League of Nations and the United Nations, proposed twelve months arranged so that each year begins on the same weekday, with one or two intercalary "Worldsday" holidays. The Hanke–Henry Permanent Calendar is a more recent proposal with the same goal. None has been adopted, largely because of religious objections to interrupting the seven-day weekly cycle and the sheer inertia of existing systems.

Other reforms

Revolutionary regimes have sometimes imposed new calendars, notably the French Republican Calendar (1793–1805) and the Soviet revolutionary calendar. Several Eastern Orthodox churches adopted the Revised Julian calendar in the 1920s. Calendar reform remains a subject of study by societies such as the International Calendar Reform associations.
