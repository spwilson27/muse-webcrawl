Ohm

The ohm (symbol: Ω) is the unit of electrical resistance in the International System of Units (SI). It is named after German physicist Georg Ohm. Various empirically derived standard units for electrical resistance were developed in connection with early telegraphy, and the British Association for the Advancement of Science proposed a unit derived from existing units of mass, length and time, convenient for telegraph work, as early as 1861.

Definition

One ohm is the resistance between two points of a conductor when a constant potential difference of one volt, applied to these points, produces a current of one ampere, the conductor not being the seat of any electromotive force. In the 2019 revision of the SI, the ohm is defined in terms of the fixed values of the Planck constant and the elementary charge, and can be realised precisely using the quantum Hall effect.

Relation to Ohm's law

In many materials and conditions, resistance obeys Ohm's law: the voltage across a resistor is proportional to the current through it, V = IR. The reciprocal of resistance is conductance, measured in siemens.

Multiples

Common multiples include the milliohm (mΩ), kilo-ohm (kΩ, 10³ Ω) and mega-ohm (MΩ, 10⁶ Ω). Resistor values are commonly marked with a colour code or printed in notations such as "4k7".

History

Georg Ohm published his law in 1827. The unit was standardised internationally in the late 19th century; a "mercury ohm" based on a column of mercury was used as a practical standard before absolute realisations became available. The Unicode character for the ohm sign is U+2126, distinct from the Greek capital letter omega U+03A9, though the two are typically rendered identically.
