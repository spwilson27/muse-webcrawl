Abilene paradox

The Abilene paradox is a collective decision-making phenomenon in which a group of people collectively decide on a course of action that is contrary to the preferences of most or all of the individuals in the group. It arises when members, fearing separation or conflict, misread one another's wishes and fail to communicate their own true feelings.

The anecdote

The paradox was described by management professor Jerry B. Harvey in a 1974 article. In his anecdote, a family relaxing at home in Coleman, Texas, on a hot afternoon ends up making a long, dusty, uncomfortable drive to Abilene for dinner — a trip nobody actually wanted. The suggestion is made casually, everyone agrees out of assumed politeness, and only afterwards does it emerge that each person had agreed against their own preference, each believing the others wanted to go.

Explanation

Harvey argued that the paradox stems not from conflict but from a failure to manage agreement: individuals experience "action anxiety" and negative fantasies about the consequences of speaking honestly, so they support proposals they privately oppose. It is related to, but distinct from, groupthink, in which members actively suppress dissent in favour of a genuinely shared course of action.
