Image orthicon

The image orthicon is a television camera tube developed at RCA during the Second World War and introduced for broadcast use in the mid-1940s. Far more sensitive than earlier tubes such as the iconoscope, it became the standard studio and outside-broadcast camera tube for roughly two decades, until it was displaced by the Plumbicon and later by solid-state sensors.

Operation

Light strikes a photocathode, releasing electrons that are accelerated and focused onto a thin glass target, where secondary emission creates a positive charge pattern mirroring the scene's brightness. A low-velocity electron beam scans the back of the target; the portion of the beam reflected at each point varies with the stored charge, and this return beam is amplified by an electron multiplier to form the video signal.

Characteristics

The image orthicon could operate in low light, allowing coverage of night sporting events and dim studios, but it produced characteristic artefacts, including dark halos around bright objects and image "sticking" or burn-in. Colour versions used three tubes behind dichroic beam splitters in cameras such as the RCA TK-41.
