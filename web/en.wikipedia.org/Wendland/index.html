Wendland

Round villages (Rundlinge) are a distinctive legacy of Slavic settlement in the Wendland.

The Wendland is a region in the northeastern corner of Lower Saxony, Germany, roughly corresponding to the present-day district of Lüchow-Dannenberg. Its name derives from the Wends, the Slavic peoples who settled the area east of the Elbe during the early Middle Ages and whose Polabian language survived here into the 18th century.

Geography and settlement

The region lies along the Elbe river, bordering Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, Brandenburg, and Saxony-Anhalt. It is characterized by the Elbe floodplain, the Drawehn ridge of pine forest, and the Jeetzel lowland. Its most famous architectural heritage is the Rundling: a circular village in which gabled Low German hall houses are arranged radially around a central green, a layout unique to this area and proposed for UNESCO World Heritage recognition.

History

During the Cold War the Wendland formed a salient of West Germany bulging into East German territory on three sides, which left it sparsely populated and economically peripheral. From the 1970s it became nationally prominent as the focus of Germany's anti-nuclear movement: the salt dome at Gorleben was selected as a site for a nuclear waste repository and interim storage facility, provoking decades of mass protests against the Castor transport convoys. The protests also spawned the self-declared micronation "Free Republic of Wendland" in 1980.

Culture

The Wendland is known for its half-timbered towns such as Lüchow, Dannenberg, Hitzacker, and Salzwedel (just outside the region), its round villages, and an annual cultural festival, the Kulturelle Landpartie, held between Ascension and Pentecost.
