International organization

An international organization, or global organization, is an organization established by a treaty or other instrument governed by international law and possessing its own legal personality, such as the United Nations, the World Health Organization and NATO. International organizations are composed principally of member states, but may also include other entities such as other international organizations, firms, and nongovernmental organizations.

Types

International organizations are commonly divided into two main categories:

  • Intergovernmental organizations (IGOs) — formed by sovereign states through treaties, such as the United Nations, the European Union, the African Union, ASEAN, and the World Bank. IGOs are subjects of international law and are capable of entering into agreements among themselves or with states.
  • International nongovernmental organizations (INGOs) — private organizations operating internationally, such as the Red Cross movement, Médecins Sans Frontières, Amnesty International and the International Olympic Committee.

History

Early international organizations of the modern era include the Central Commission for Navigation on the Rhine (1815) and the International Telegraph Union (1865), founded to administer shared technical standards. The League of Nations, established in 1919 after the First World War, was the first general-purpose political IGO. It was replaced in 1945 by the United Nations, which remains the most prominent international organization, with 193 member states.

Legal personality and privileges

International organizations typically enjoy privileges and immunities in the territory of their member states, including immunity from legal process, inviolability of premises and archives, and tax exemptions. The extent of these privileges is normally established in a headquarters agreement or multilateral convention.

Regional and global organizations

International organizations may be global in membership or regional and sub-regional in scope. The European Union is distinctive in having developed into a supranational union, with institutions able to adopt laws that are directly binding on member states.
