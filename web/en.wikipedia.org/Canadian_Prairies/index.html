Canadian Prairies

The Canadian Prairies (usually referred to as simply the Prairies in Canada) is a region in Western Canada comprising the provinces of Alberta, Saskatchewan, and Manitoba. The region is dominated by grassland plains that form the northern portion of the Great Plains of North America.

Geography

The Prairies cover roughly 1.8 million square kilometres, extending from the foothills of the Rocky Mountains in the west to the Canadian Shield in the east. The land rises in three broad steppes from Manitoba's lowlands to Alberta's high plains. Major rivers include the North and South Saskatchewan, the Red, and the Assiniboine. The southern portion is part of Palliser's Triangle, a semi-arid region once considered unsuitable for farming.

Climate

The region has a continental climate with cold winters, warm summers, and relatively low precipitation. Chinook winds can rapidly raise winter temperatures in Alberta. The Prairies are Canada's most drought-prone region and also experience severe thunderstorms, hail, and tornadoes in summer.

Economy

Agriculture is central to the prairie economy: the region produces much of the world's canola and durum wheat, along with barley, oats, and pulses. Alberta is Canada's energy heartland, with vast oil and natural gas reserves including the Athabasca oil sands. Potash mining in Saskatchewan supplies a large share of global demand.

Settlement and culture

Indigenous peoples, including the Cree, Blackfoot, Assiniboine, and Métis, have inhabited the plains for millennia. Mass European settlement followed the completion of the Canadian Pacific Railway in 1885 and the Dominion Lands Act, drawing immigrants from Britain, Ukraine, Germany, and Scandinavia. Prairie cities include Calgary, Edmonton, Winnipeg, Saskatoon, and Regina.
