Delta-v

Delta-v
SymbolΔv
Unitmetres per second (m/s), km/s
FieldAstrodynamics, astronautics

Delta-v (literally "change in velocity"), symbolised as Δv, is a measure of the impulse per unit of spacecraft mass needed to perform a manoeuvre such as launching from, or landing on, a planet or moon, or an in-space orbital manoeuvre. It is a scalar with the units of speed, and functions as the standard "currency" of mission design: a spacecraft's total delta-v capacity ("delta-v budget") must cover the sum of all manoeuvres in its mission.

Rocket equation

For a rocket, the achievable delta-v is given by the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation,
Δv = ve ln(m0/mf),
where ve is the effective exhaust velocity and m0/mf is the ratio of initial (wet) to final (dry) mass. Because of the logarithm, delta-v grows slowly with mass ratio, which is why reaching orbit (about 9.3–10 km/s including losses from Earth's surface) demands staging.

Use in mission design

Delta-v budgets sum the requirements from delta-v maps of the Solar System; trajectories exploit effects such as the Oberth effect (burning at periapsis, where kinetic energy gain is greatest) and gravity assists, which change velocity relative to a target body without propellant expense. High-thrust chemical rockets deliver delta-v quickly but with low exhaust velocity, while electric propulsion provides very high total delta-v over months of low-thrust operation.

Other contexts

The term is also used for vehicles generally (for example, the delta-v of a car in crash testing, meaning the change in velocity during impact) and, in physics, for any change in a velocity vector.
