Methodism

Methodism, also called the Methodist movement, is a group of historically related denominations of Protestant Christianity whose origins, doctrine and practice derive from the life and teachings of John Wesley. George Whitefield and John's brother Charles Wesley were also significant early leaders in the movement.

Origins

The movement began in 18th-century England. At Oxford, John and Charles Wesley and their companions formed a "Holy Club" whose methodical devotions earned them the nickname "Methodists". After a religious experience in May 1738 — Wesley felt his heart "strangely warmed" at a meeting in Aldersgate Street, London — Wesley began an itinerant ministry, preaching in the open air to working people neglected by the established church, and organising converts into societies and small "classes" for mutual accountability.

Doctrine and practice

Methodist theology is broadly Arminian: salvation is offered to all and can be accepted or rejected, in contrast to Calvinist predestination. Wesley taught justification by faith, the possibility of Christian perfection (entire sanctification) in this life, and the authority of Scripture interpreted through tradition, reason and experience — the combination later called the Wesleyan Quadrilateral. Methodism places a strong emphasis on practical holiness, hymn singing (Charles Wesley wrote thousands of hymns), lay preaching and social action.

Growth and denominations

After Wesley's death the movement separated from the Church of England and spread rapidly in the United States through circuit riders such as Francis Asbury, becoming for a time the largest Protestant tradition there. Today Methodist and Wesleyan churches — including the United Methodist Church and many others — count tens of millions of adherents worldwide, are coordinated loosely by the World Methodist Council, and have played notable roles in education, missions and social reform, from abolitionism to the founding of hospitals and universities.
