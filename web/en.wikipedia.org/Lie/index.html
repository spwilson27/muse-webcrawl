Lie

A lie is an assertion that is believed to be false and is usually used with the purpose of deceiving or misleading someone. The practice of communicating lies is called lying, and a person who communicates a lie may be termed a liar.

Types

Lies take many forms, including outright falsehoods, lies by omission, exaggerations, and "white lies" told to avoid hurting another's feelings. Philosophical and legal contexts distinguish lying from related acts such as perjury (lying under oath), fraud, and mere error: a statement believed true but actually false is a mistake, not a lie, because intent to deceive is absent.

Ethics

Most ethical traditions condemn lying. Immanuel Kant argued that lying is always wrong because it violates a universal duty of truthfulness, while consequentialists judge lies by their outcomes and may permit them to prevent greater harm. The philosopher Augustine classified lies by motive and gravity. Debates continue over lies told to protect others, as in the classic case of misleading a would-be murderer about a victim's whereabouts.

Detection and psychology

Humans are generally poor at detecting lies, performing only slightly better than chance in most studies. Instruments such as the polygraph measure physiological arousal rather than deception itself and remain controversial. Developmental research shows children begin to lie at an early age, and skill at lying correlates with cognitive development and theory of mind.
