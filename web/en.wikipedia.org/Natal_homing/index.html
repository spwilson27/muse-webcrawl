Natal homing

FieldAnimal behaviour, ecology
Best known inPacific salmon, sea turtles

Natal homing is the behaviour by which mature animals return to the place where they were born in order to breed. It is a special case of philopatry and is most famously exhibited by Pacific salmon and by sea turtles, which navigate back across oceans to their natal streams and beaches.

Mechanisms

Research beginning with Arthur Hasler's work on salmon suggested an olfactory imprinting hypothesis: juvenile salmon imprint on the unique chemical signature of their home stream during the smolt stage and, years later, use that remembered odour to home in from the sea. Geomagnetic imprinting has also been proposed: sea turtles and salmon appear to learn the magnetic coordinates of their natal region and use them as a map, with local cues (odour, currents, visual landmarks) guiding the final approach.

Ecological significance

Natal homing structures populations genetically, creating locally adapted stocks — a pattern central to the management of salmon fisheries, where each river system may host a distinct population. Because most homing is precise but imperfect, the small fraction of "straying" individuals allows colonisation of new habitat. Homing migrations also transport marine-derived nutrients inland, fertilising freshwater and riparian ecosystems.
