Benedictine

Benedictine may refer to several related concepts, most prominently the members and institutions of the Order of Saint Benedict (Latin: Ordo Sancti Benedicti, OSB), a monastic order within the Roman Catholic Church founded on the Rule of Saint Benedict, written by Benedict of Nursia in the sixth century. Benedictines live in autonomous monasteries and follow a life balanced between prayer, work, and study, summed up in the motto ora et labora ("pray and work").

The Benedictine Order

The Rule of Saint Benedict, composed around 530 AD at Monte Cassino, governs the daily life of monks and nuns: the eight daily offices of prayer (the Liturgy of the Hours), manual labour, communal meals, silence, stability of place, and obedience to an elected abbot or abbess. Benedictine houses are not centrally governed as a single order but are grouped into congregations, loosely united since 1893 in the Benedictine Confederation, headed by an Abbot Primate resident at Sant'Anselmo in Rome.

History

Benedictine monasticism spread across Europe during the early Middle Ages and became a principal vehicle for the preservation of learning, agriculture, and liturgy. Houses such as Cluny, Monte Cassino, Canterbury, and Melk were major centres of culture. The order declined in places during the Reformation and the secularisations of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, but revived strongly in the nineteenth century.

Other uses

"Benedictine" also denotes Bénédictine, a French herbal liqueur produced at Fécamp in Normandy, developed in the nineteenth century by Alexandre Le Grand using a recipe attributed to Benedictine monks. In cuisine, "Benedictine" can refer to a cucumber-and-cream-cheese spread from Louisville, Kentucky, and to eggs Benedict and its variants.
