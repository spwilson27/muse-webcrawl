Hypertrophic scar

A hypertrophic scar is a cutaneous condition characterised by deposits of excessive amounts of collagen which gives rise to a raised scar, but not to the degree observed with keloids. Like keloids, they form most often at sites of pimples, body piercings, cuts and burns. Hypertrophic scars are elevated, red, and may be itchy or painful, but — unlike keloids — they remain within the borders of the original wound and often regress spontaneously over time.

Causes and risk factors

Hypertrophic scars result from an imbalance in wound healing, with overproduction of collagen and reduced collagenase activity. They are more likely after burns, over joints or areas of high skin tension, in wounds healing by secondary intention, and in younger people and those with darker skin. Tension across a healing wound, infection and foreign material increase the risk.

Treatment

First-line treatments include silicone gel sheeting, pressure therapy (especially after burns), and intralesional corticosteroid injections such as triamcinolone. Other options are laser therapy, cryotherapy, and surgical revision combined with adjunctive treatment to prevent recurrence. Hypertrophic scars generally have a better prognosis than keloids and may flatten over 12–18 months.
