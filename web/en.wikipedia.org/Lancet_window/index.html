Lancet window

A lancet window is a tall, narrow window with a pointed arch at the top. First appearing in the French early Gothic period (c. 1140–1200), it became a defining feature of Gothic architecture, especially the English Early Gothic (Early English) style of the 13th century. The name derives from its resemblance to a lance.

Characteristics

Lancets are typically long and narrow, sometimes grouped in pairs or threes under a single arch or in a shared surround. The earliest examples lack tracery; later grouped lancets helped give rise to plate and bar tracery and eventually to larger traceried windows.

Examples

Notable examples include the tall lancets of Chartres Cathedral's early Gothic work, the Five Sisters windows in the north transept of York Minster — five lancets each over 16 metres high — and the windows of Salisbury Cathedral, built in the Early English style.

Gothic Revival

The lancet returned to favour in the 19th-century Gothic Revival, in churches, chapels and collegiate buildings.
