Black hole thermodynamics

A black hole: its horizon area is proportional to its entropy.

Black hole thermodynamics is the area of study that seeks to reconcile the laws of thermodynamics with the existence of black hole event horizons. Its development began in the 1970s with the work of Jacob Bekenstein and Stephen Hawking and is widely viewed as a major clue toward a quantum theory of gravity.

The four laws

Black hole mechanics comprises four laws analogous to the laws of thermodynamics. The zeroth law states that surface gravity is constant over the horizon of a stationary black hole; the first law relates changes in mass to changes in horizon area, angular momentum, and charge; the second law states that the total horizon area of black holes can never decrease (the area theorem, proved by Hawking); and the third law states that a black hole cannot be reduced to zero surface gravity by any finite process.

Entropy and Hawking radiation

Bekenstein proposed that a black hole carries entropy proportional to its horizon area, given by the Bekenstein–Hawking formula S = kc3A / (4Għ), so that the generalized second law — ordinary entropy plus black hole entropy never decreases — is preserved. In 1974 Hawking applied quantum field theory in curved spacetime to show that black holes emit thermal radiation at a temperature inversely proportional to their mass, implying black holes can slowly evaporate and that the area law has genuine thermodynamic meaning.

Implications

The result that maximum entropy scales with area rather than volume led to the holographic principle, influential in string theory and quantum gravity research. The black hole information paradox, concerning whether information falling into a black hole is destroyed by evaporation, remains an active area of research.
