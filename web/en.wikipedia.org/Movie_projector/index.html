Movie projector

A movie projector is an opto-mechanical device for displaying motion picture film by projecting it onto a screen. Most optical and mechanical elements, except illumination and sound reproduction, are shared with the movie camera: film is pulled intermittently through a gate past a shutter and lens while a bright light source shines through each frame.

Mechanism and history

The intermittent movement, classically a Geneva (Maltese cross) drive, advances the film one frame at a time, holding it still during projection; a rotating shutter masks the pull-down and, by flashing each frame two or three times, raises the flicker rate above the threshold of perception. Standard sound speed is 24 frames per second. Pioneered in the 1890s by inventors including the Lumière brothers (Cinématographe) and Thomas Edison (Vitascope), projectors evolved from hand-cranked 35 mm machines to carbon-arc and xenon-lamp theatre installations, before most cinemas converted to digital projection in the 2000s–2010s.
