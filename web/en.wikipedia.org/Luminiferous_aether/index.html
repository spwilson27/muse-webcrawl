Luminiferous aether

Luminiferous aether (also spelled ether) was the postulated medium through which light was once thought to propagate as waves. By analogy with water waves needing water and sound waves needing air, 19th-century physicists assumed light required a material carrier filling all of space, including the vacuum between the stars. The aether was expected to be extraordinarily rigid to support the high speed of light, yet utterly permeable so that planets could move through it without resistance — a combination of properties that proved impossible to reconcile.

History

Christiaan Huygens proposed a wave theory of light in the 17th century with an aether of fine particles. The idea took firm hold after Thomas Young's interference experiments (1801) and Augustin-Jean Fresnel's wave theory of diffraction established that light behaves as a transverse wave. Fresnel also proposed that moving bodies partially drag the aether, introducing the Fresnel drag coefficient to explain the absence of observable effects in Arago's experiments.

James Clerk Maxwell's electromagnetic theory (1865) identified light as an electromagnetic wave, and the aether became the medium sustaining electric and magnetic fields. Hendrik Lorentz developed an aether theory in which the aether was completely stationary and electrons moved within it, introducing length contraction and local time as devices to explain negative experimental results.

The Michelson–Morley experiment

In 1887 Albert A. Michelson and Edward W. Morley used an interferometer to measure the difference in the speed of light in perpendicular directions, expecting to detect the Earth's motion through the aether — the "aether wind". They found no significant difference. The null result was repeated with increasing precision and became one of the most famous failed experiments in physics.

Abandonment

Albert Einstein's theory of special relativity (1905) dispensed with the aether entirely: the speed of light is the same in all inertial frames, and no privileged medium is required. Although Lorentz and others could reproduce the same predictions by adding contraction hypotheses, the aether became superfluous, and physics adopted Einstein's simpler interpretation. Occasional modern experiments continue to confirm the absence of any detectable aether wind to extraordinary precision. Historians of science use the episode as a classic case study of how a theory-laden null result reshaped physics.
