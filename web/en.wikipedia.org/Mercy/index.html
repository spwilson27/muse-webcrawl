Mercy

Mercy is compassion or forgiveness shown towards someone whom it is within one's power to punish or harm. It is an act of kindness, benevolence or clemency exercised by a person in authority — a judge, ruler, victor or deity — or indeed by anyone who refrains from exacting a penalty they could rightfully impose.

In religion

Mercy is a central attribute of God in the Abrahamic religions. In Judaism, God is described as merciful and gracious (rachum ve-chanun), and the Hebrew chesed denotes steadfast loving-kindness. In Christianity, mercy flows from divine love: the parable of the Good Samaritan and the works of mercy — feeding the hungry, sheltering the homeless, visiting the sick and imprisoned — define practical mercy, and the Beatitudes pronounce "Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy." In Islam, God is Ar-Rahman and Ar-Rahim, the Most Gracious and Most Merciful, phrases opening nearly every chapter of the Qur'an. Concepts akin to mercy, such as karuna (compassion) in Buddhism and Hinduism, extend benevolence to all beings.

In law and government

Legal mercy appears as clemency, pardons, commutation of sentences, royal prerogative of mercy, amnesties and the discretion of judges and juries to temper strict justice. Shakespeare gave the idea its classic literary statement in Portia's speech in The Merchant of Venice: "The quality of mercy is not strained; it droppeth as the gentle rain from heaven."

Philosophy

Philosophers debate the relation of mercy to justice: whether mercy is a virtue that perfects justice by considering individual circumstances, or an unjust departure from what is deserved. Modern restorative-justice movements draw on mercy's emphasis on reconciliation over retribution.
