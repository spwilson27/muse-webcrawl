Benoit B. Mandelbrot (20 November 1924 – 14 October 2010) was a Polish-born French-American mathematician and polymath, widely known as the "father of fractal geometry". He coined the word fractal and developed a "theory of roughness" that described the irregular, self-similar shapes pervasive in nature — coastlines, clouds, mountains, turbulence, and the fluctuations of financial markets — which classical Euclidean geometry could not capture.

Life and career

Born in Warsaw to a Lithuanian Jewish family, Mandelbrot emigrated to France in 1936, fleeing Nazi persecution; his uncle Szolem Mandelbrojt, a prominent mathematician, guided his education. He studied at the École Polytechnique and Caltech, took a doctorate in Paris in 1952, and in 1958 joined IBM's research laboratories in Yorktown Heights, New York, where he spent most of his career. From 1987 he was Sterling Professor of Mathematical Sciences at Yale. His famous 1967 paper "How Long Is the Coast of Britain?" introduced the idea that measured length depends on scale, a hallmark of fractals.

The Mandelbrot set and fractal geometry

Around 1980, using IBM's computers, Mandelbrot produced the first visualisations of the set now named after him: the set of complex numbers c for which the iteration z → z² + c remains bounded. Its infinitely intricate, self-similar boundary became one of the most recognisable images in mathematics. His 1982 book The Fractal Geometry of Nature synthesised his work on scaling in phenomena from river networks to cotton prices and inspired applications across physics, biology, economics and computer graphics.

Legacy

Mandelbrot received numerous honours, including the Wolf Prize in Physics (1993) and the Japan Prize (2003). Though some mathematicians initially considered his approach more visual than rigorous, fractal geometry is now a standard part of mathematics and its applications; his memoir The Fractalist was published posthumously in 2012.
