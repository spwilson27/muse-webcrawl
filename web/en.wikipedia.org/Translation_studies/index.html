Translation studies is an interdiscipline containing elements of social science and the humanities that deals with the systematic study of the theory, description, and application of translation, interpreting, and localization. As an interdiscipline, translation studies borrows much from the various fields of study that support translation. These include comparative literature, computer science, history, linguistics, philosophy, semiotics, and terminology.

History

Reflection on translation is ancient — Cicero and St. Jerome debated sense-for-sense versus word-for-word rendering, and the 17th-century French ideal of the belles infidèles framed translation as adaptation. As an academic discipline, however, translation studies emerged only in the second half of the 20th century. James S. Holmes's 1972 paper "The Name and Nature of Translation Studies" is regarded as its founding statement, proposing the discipline's name and a map of its structure: "pure" research (theoretical and descriptive) and "applied" research (translator training, aids, and criticism). The 1970s–80s saw the descriptive turn of the Manipulation School and Gideon Toury's Descriptive Translation Studies, which studied translations as facts of the target culture, identifying probabilistic "norms" and putative laws (growing standardization and interference).

Major approaches

Linguistic approaches include Eugene Nida's formal versus dynamic equivalence and Katharina Reiss's text typology, developed further in Hans Vermeer's skopos theory, which subordinates translation strategy to the translation's purpose. Polysystem theory (Itamar Even-Zohar) examines the position of translated literature within a culture's literary system. The "cultural turn" of the 1990s (Susan Bassnett and André Lefevere) shifted attention to ideology, patronage, and power; Lawrence Venuti's critique of translator invisibility and his advocacy of "foreignizing" over "domesticating" translation are particularly influential. Later currents include postcolonial, feminist, and sociological (Bourdieusian) approaches, corpus-based studies, cognitive process research using keylogging and eye-tracking, audiovisual translation, localization, and, since the 2010s, sustained engagement with machine translation and post-editing.

Interpreting studies

The study of spoken and signed interpreting — conference, community, court, and medical interpreting — developed partly in parallel, with its own cognitive models (such as Daniel Gile's Effort Models) and professional organizations, and is often treated as a subfield of translation studies.
