LTCM's collapse in 1998 required a Federal Reserve-brokered rescue

Long-Term Capital Management L.P. (LTCM) was a highly leveraged American hedge fund founded in 1994 by John Meriwether, the former vice-chairman and head of bond trading at Salomon Brothers. Its partners included Nobel laureates Myron Scholes and Robert C. Merton, co-developers of the Black–Scholes options pricing model, and former Federal Reserve vice-chairman David Mullins.

Strategy and success

LTCM pursued convergence (relative-value) arbitrage: using mathematical models, it bought underpriced securities and shorted overpriced ones — for example, newly issued ("on-the-run") U.S. Treasury bonds versus older ("off-the-run") issues — expecting prices to converge. Because the spreads were tiny, the fund used enormous leverage, borrowing against its capital many times over and building derivative exposures with a notional value exceeding $1 trillion on an equity base of about $4.7 billion at the start of 1998. Returns were stellar: roughly 21% in 1994, 43% in 1995, 41% in 1996 and 17% in 1997 (after fees).

Collapse and bailout

In August 1998 Russia defaulted on its domestic debt, triggering a global "flight to quality" that moved markets in exactly the correlated way LTCM's models assumed was vanishingly unlikely. Spreads widened instead of converging; the fund lost $1.9 billion in August alone and about $4.6 billion in under five months. Fearing that a disorderly liquidation of LTCM's positions would destabilise the global financial system, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York organised a private rescue: on 23 September 1998 a consortium of 14 banks and firms contributed $3.625 billion in exchange for 90% of the fund. The fund was liquidated in early 2000.

Legacy

LTCM became the canonical example of model risk, leverage risk and the failure of risk models (such as value-at-risk) to account for liquidity crises and tail correlations. The episode, chronicled in Roger Lowenstein's When Genius Failed (2000), foreshadowed debates about "too big to fail" a decade later in the 2008 financial crisis.
