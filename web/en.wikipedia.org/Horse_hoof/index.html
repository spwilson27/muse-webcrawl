Horse hoof

A horse hoof is the lower extremity of each leg of a horse, the part that makes contact with the ground. The hoof is a complex structure of keratin — the same protein as human fingernails and hair — enclosing the coffin bone (pedal bone) and other sensitive internal structures. Wild horses' hooves are worn down by travel over varied terrain, while domestic horses typically require regular trimming and, in many cases, shoeing by a farrier.

Anatomy

The hoof consists of the hoof wall, which bears most of the weight; the sole, the slightly concave underside; the frog, a V-shaped, rubbery structure that acts as a shock absorber and aids circulation; the bars; and the white line, which joins wall and sole. Inside, sensitive laminae interlock with the wall to suspend the coffin bone. The hoof grows continuously from the coronary band at about 6–10 mm per month.

Care

"No hoof, no horse" is a traditional farriers' saying. Domestic horses need their hooves trimmed every four to eight weeks to maintain balance and correct angles. Horseshoes protect the hoof from excessive wear on hard or abrasive surfaces and can be used therapeutically. Common hoof problems include thrush (a bacterial infection of the frog), hoof cracks, abscesses, and laminitis, a painful and potentially devastating inflammation of the laminae.
