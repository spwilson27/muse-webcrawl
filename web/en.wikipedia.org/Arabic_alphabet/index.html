The Arabic alphabet (Arabic: الْأَبْجَدِيَّة الْعَرَبِيَّة, al-abjadīyah al-ʿarabīyah) is the script used for Arabic and, in adapted forms, for many other languages including Persian, Urdu, Pashto, Kurdish (Sorani), Uyghur and historically Ottoman Turkish and Malay. It has 28 basic letters, is written from right to left in a cursive style in which most letters join to their neighbours, and is classified as an abjad because it primarily represents consonants, with short vowels optionally indicated by diacritical marks (ḥarakāt).

History

The Arabic script evolved from the cursive Nabataean form of the Aramaic alphabet used in the kingdom centred on Petra. By the 6th century CE it had developed into a distinct script in Arabia, and its use spread rapidly with the expansion of Islam from the 7th century, when it became the script of the Quran. Systems of dots to distinguish consonants with the same basic shape, and vowel signs attributed to scholars such as Abu al-Aswad al-Du'ali and al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi, were added to reduce ambiguity.

Features

Most Arabic letters have up to four contextual forms — isolated, initial, medial and final — depending on their position in a word. Six letters (alif, dāl, dhāl, rā, zāy, wāw) connect only to the preceding letter. Long vowels are written with the letters alif, wāw and yā; short vowels are normally omitted in everyday text but are marked in the Quran, children's books and dictionaries. Numbers are written with either Western digits or Eastern Arabic numerals depending on region. The script's flowing forms inspired a rich calligraphic tradition, with styles such as naskh, thuluth, diwani and the angular Kufic script used in art and architecture.

Adaptations

Languages beyond Arabic added letters for sounds Arabic lacks: Persian added p, ch, zh and g; Urdu added retroflex letters and aspirates; the Ottoman Turkish alphabet served the Turkish language until Turkey adopted a Latin-based alphabet in 1928. The Arabic script ranks among the most widely used writing systems in the world by number of users and countries.
