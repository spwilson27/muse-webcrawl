Emperor Taishō (31 August 1879 – 25 December 1926), personal name Yoshihito, was the 123rd Emperor of Japan according to the traditional order of succession, reigning from 1912 until his death in 1926. His reign, the Taishō era, saw Japan's brief flowering of liberal politics known as "Taishō democracy", as well as the country's participation in the First World War on the Allied side.

Early life and health

Yoshihito was the third son of Emperor Meiji, born to the imperial concubine Naruko Yanagiwara, and was the only one of Meiji's sons to survive childhood. He contracted cerebral meningitis as an infant, which left lasting effects on his health and, many historians believe, his mental capacity. He was declared crown prince in 1889 and in 1900 married Kujō Sadako, later Empress Teimei, with whom he had four sons, including the future Emperor Hirohito. Unlike his father's commanding presence, Yoshihito was known as a cultured man fond of poetry, travel, and French literature.

Reign

Ascending the throne on his father's death in July 1912, the new emperor presided over a period in which party cabinets gradually displaced the oligarchic genrō. Japan declared war on Germany in 1914 under the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, seized German possessions in Shandong and the Pacific, and emerged from the war with an industrial boom and a seat among the great powers at Versailles. The era also saw the Rice Riots of 1918, universal male suffrage in 1925, and growing party government.

The emperor's health deteriorated markedly from about 1919; his public appearances became erratic — most famously at the 1921 opening of the Diet, where he is said to have rolled the imperial rescript into a tube and peered through it like a telescope. In November 1921 his eldest son Hirohito was appointed regent (sesshō), the first such arrangement of the modern era. Taishō died of a heart attack following pneumonia at the imperial villa in Hayama on 25 December 1926, ending the Taishō era and beginning the Shōwa era.
