The Liber Abaci (also spelled Liber Abbaci, "The Book of Calculation") is a historic 1202 Latin work on arithmetic by Leonardo of Pisa, posthumously known as Fibonacci. It was one of the first Western books to describe the Hindu–Arabic numeral system and to demonstrate its practical superiority over Roman numerals, and it is credited with helping to popularise the system throughout Europe.

Content

Drawing on what Leonardo had learned travelling around the Mediterranean with his father, a Pisan customs official stationed in Bugia (in modern Algeria), the book's fifteen chapters cover reading and writing the nine Indian figures and the sign 0, multiplication and division, fractions, the rule of three, currency conversion, weights and measures, and the solution of linear and quadratic problems by methods including false position. Later chapters treat proportions, geometry and a summary of algebra.

The rabbit problem

The third section contains the famous problem about the growth of a population of rabbits: beginning with one pair, how many pairs will there be in a year if each mature pair produces a new pair each month? The solution generates the sequence 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, … — now known as the Fibonacci sequence, in which each term is the sum of the two preceding ones. The sequence had been described earlier in Indian mathematics, but Liber Abaci introduced it to the West.

Influence

Although adoption was gradual — some cities even banned the new numerals, fearing forgery — the book's clear demonstration of the system's advantages for commerce helped establish Hindu–Arabic numerals in European bookkeeping, banking and science, laying groundwork for the later development of modern arithmetic and algebra.
