Throat singing
Also calledOvertone singing
Cultural originsCentral Asia — Mongolia, Tuva, Altai
StylesKhöömii, sygyt, kargyraa
UNESCOMongolian khöömii inscribed on the Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage

Throat singing is a family of vocal techniques in which a singer manipulates the resonances created as air travels from the lungs, past the vocal folds and out of the lips, to produce more than one pitch at a time — a low drone fundamental together with selected overtones that form a melody.

Central Asian traditions

The best-known traditions are those of the Mongolic and Turkic peoples of southern Siberia and Mongolia. Tuvan throat singing includes styles such as sygyt, with a clear flute-like overtone melody, and the deep, growling kargyraa. Mongolian khöömii is traditionally accompanied by the morin khuur (horsehead fiddle) and evokes sounds of the steppe landscape — wind, water and animals. Traditionally learned by herders, the art is now performed worldwide and has influenced genres from folk to heavy metal (e.g. the Mongolian band The Hu).

Other traditions

Overtone singing also occurs in Inuit katajjaq (a rhythmic vocal game, typically of two women), among the Xhosa of South Africa (umngqokolo), in Tibetan Buddhist chant, and in Sardinian cantu a tenore. In Western music it has been explored by contemporary and experimental vocalists.
