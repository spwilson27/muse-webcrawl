Objective test (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In law, the objective test is a standard by which conduct, intention or belief is judged from the standpoint of a hypothetical reasonable person, rather than by the actor's own subjective state of mind. Courts ask what a reasonable person in the same circumstances would have believed, foreseen or done. The contrast is the subjective test, which asks what the particular person actually believed or intended.

Applications

Objective standards pervade the law of negligence, where the duty of care is measured against the reasonable person; contract law, where the meaning of an agreement and whether an offer was accepted are judged by how a reasonable person would understand the parties' words and conduct; and parts of criminal law, such as gross negligence manslaughter and some defences, where foresight of risk may be assessed objectively. Self-defence, by contrast, often combines a subjective belief in the need for force with an objective assessment of its proportionality.

Rationale and criticism

Proponents argue the objective test gives the law certainty and prevents actors from escaping liability through unreasonable beliefs. Critics note it can be unfair to defendants whose age, experience or capacity falls below that of the reasonable person, prompting hybrid tests that take some individual characteristics into account.
