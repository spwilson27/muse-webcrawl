Garnet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Garnets are a group of silicate minerals that have been used since the Bronze Age as gemstones and abrasives. All species of garnets possess similar physical properties and crystal forms but differ in chemical composition. The name derives from the Latin granatus, referring to the pomegranate, whose red seeds resemble garnet crystals.

Species and properties

The garnet group has the general formula X3Y2(SiO4)3. The principal end-members are pyrope (magnesium-aluminium, deep red), almandine (iron-aluminium, dark red to brown), spessartine (manganese-aluminium, orange), grossular (calcium-aluminium, which includes green tsavorite), andradite (calcium-iron, including the rare green demantoid), and uvarovite (calcium-chromium, bright green). Garnets crystallize in the cubic system, commonly as dodecahedra, have a hardness of about 6.5–7.5 on the Mohs scale, no cleavage, and a vitreous to resinous luster.

Occurrence and uses

Garnets are common in metamorphic rocks such as schist and gneiss and also occur in some igneous rocks and in alluvial deposits. Because of their hardness and angular fracture, crushed garnet is widely used as an abrasive in sandblasting, waterjet cutting, and coated abrasives such as garnet paper. Red garnets — especially pyrope and almandine — remain popular gemstones, and garnet is the birthstone for January.

Geological significance

Because garnet grows over a range of pressures and temperatures and can preserve internal compositional zoning, geologists use it in geothermobarometry to reconstruct the pressure–temperature history of metamorphic rocks, and garnet dating methods help establish the timing of metamorphism and mountain building.
