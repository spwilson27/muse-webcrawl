Proglacial lake

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A proglacial lake at the terminus of a mountain glacier

In geology, a proglacial lake is a lake formed either by the damming action of a moraine during the retreat of a melting glacier, a glacial ice dam, or by meltwater trapped against an ice sheet due to isostatic depression of the crust around the ice. At the end of the last ice age about 10,000 years ago, large proglacial lakes were a widespread feature in the northern hemisphere.

Formation and types

Proglacial lakes may be dammed by moraine ridges deposited at a glacier's terminus, by the ice itself (ice-marginal lakes), or by bedrock. Their water is frequently milky turquoise because of suspended glacial flour — finely ground rock carried by meltwater. Some proglacial lakes are ephemeral on geological timescales: as ice retreats and outlets open, they drain, sometimes catastrophically in glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs), which are a growing hazard in the Himalayas, Andes and Alaska as glaciers thin under climate change.

Famous examples

The Great Lakes of North America originated as proglacial lakes at the margin of the Laurentide Ice Sheet. The largest known proglacial lake was Lake Agassiz, which at its maximum covered more than 440,000 square kilometres of central North America; its final drainage around 8,200 years ago is thought to have disrupted ocean circulation and caused a sharp cooling event. Other examples include Lake Missoula, source of the Channeled Scablands megafloods; Lake Bonneville in the Great Basin; and the Baltic Ice Lake, an early stage of the Baltic Sea.
