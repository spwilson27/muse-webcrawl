Carnivore

Lions are obligate carnivores (placeholder)

A carnivore (from Latin caro, "flesh", and vorare, "to devour") is an animal or plant whose food and energy requirements derive from animal tissues — whether through predation or scavenging. Animals that depend solely on animal flesh are called obligate carnivores (or hypercarnivores), while those that also consume non-animal food are mesocarnivores or hypocarnivores depending on the proportion of meat in the diet. Carnivores sit at the third trophic level or above in food chains, and the term should be distinguished from the taxonomic order Carnivora, most — but not all — of whose members are carnivores.

Characteristics

Carnivorous animals typically possess features suited to capturing and eating prey: sharp teeth, claws, strong jaws, keen senses, speed or stealth, and relatively short digestive tracts. Obligate carnivores such as cats have physiological requirements — for nutrients including taurine, vitamin A, and arachidonic acid — that must come from animal tissue. Facultative carnivores such as dogs can digest some plant matter but thrive best on meat. Beyond mammals, carnivores include many birds (raptors, penguins), reptiles (snakes, crocodilians), fish (sharks), amphibians, invertebrates (spiders, mantises, octopuses), fungi, and even plants: carnivorous plants such as the Venus flytrap supplement poor soils by digesting insects.

Ecology

Apex predators exert top-down control on ecosystems, and their removal can trigger trophic cascades. Scavengers such as vultures and hyenas perform the essential service of consuming carrion. The evolution of carnivory has occurred independently many times, from Cambrian arthropods such as Anomalocaris to modern mammals.

Classification by diet share

  • Hypercarnivore — diet more than 70% meat.
  • Mesocarnivore — diet 30–70% meat.
  • Hypocarnivore — diet less than 30% meat.
  • Piscivore — primarily fish; insectivore — primarily insects.
