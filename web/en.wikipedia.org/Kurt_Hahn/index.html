Kurt Matthias Robert Martin Hahn (5 June 1886 – 14 December 1974) was a German educator whose work in progressive education had a major influence on schools and youth organisations around the world. He founded or co-founded several influential institutions, including Schule Schloss Salem in Germany, Gordonstoun School in Scotland, the Outward Bound movement, the Duke of Edinburgh's Award, and the United World Colleges.

Early life and career

Hahn was born in Berlin into a prosperous Jewish family. He studied at the University of Oxford and at German universities including Berlin, Heidelberg, Freiburg and Göttingen. During the First World War he worked in the German Foreign Office, analysing British press and propaganda. After the war, with Prince Max von Baden, the last Imperial Chancellor of Germany, he founded Schule Schloss Salem in 1920, a boarding school built on principles of character education, physical training, service and academic challenge.

Exile and Gordonstoun

Hahn was arrested by the Nazi regime in 1933 after speaking out against the Sturmabteilung following a political murder, but he was released after intervention by the British Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald and fled to Britain. In 1934 he founded Gordonstoun School in Moray, Scotland, applying the Salem philosophy in a rugged coastal setting. Prince Philip, Duke of Edinburgh, was among its early pupils, and the school was later attended by Charles, Prince of Wales.

Outward Bound and later work

In 1941, with shipping magnate Lawrence Holt, Hahn founded Outward Bound at Aberdyfi in Wales, offering short residential courses of adventure training for young merchant seamen to build resilience and self-reliance. The model spread internationally. Hahn also helped inspire the Duke of Edinburgh's Award scheme (founded 1956) and the United World Colleges movement, whose first college, Atlantic College in Wales, opened in 1962, bringing together students from many countries to promote international understanding.

Educational philosophy

Hahn summarised his creed in his "Seven Laws of Salem", which included giving children opportunities for self-discovery, making them experience both triumph and defeat, and providing opportunities for self-forgetfulness in a common cause. He warned against what he called the "declines of modern youth" — declines in fitness, initiative, care and skill, and compassion — and designed experiential programmes to counteract them. His ideas underpin modern experiential and outdoor education worldwide.
