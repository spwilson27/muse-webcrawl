S-Adenosyl methionine

S-Adenosyl methionine (SAM or SAMe, also AdoMet) is a common cosubstrate involved in methyl-group transfer, transsulfuration, and aminopropylation. It is found in virtually all cells and is the principal biological methyl donor, participating in the methylation of DNA, RNA, proteins, lipids, and small molecules. SAM was first described by Giulio Cantoni in 1952.

Biosynthesis

SAM is synthesised by the enzyme methionine adenosyltransferase from the amino acid methionine and ATP: the adenosyl group of ATP is transferred to the sulfur atom of methionine, forming a sulfonium ion whose high-energy bond makes the methyl group chemically reactive.

Biological roles

Methyltransferase enzymes transfer SAM's methyl group to substrates, producing S-adenosyl homocysteine (SAH), which is hydrolysed to homocysteine and recycled. Through the transsulfuration pathway, homocysteine can be converted to cysteine and glutathione. SAM also donates aminopropyl groups for polyamine synthesis and is the source of the 5'-deoxyadenosyl radical used by radical SAM enzymes in diverse reactions. In gene regulation, methylation of DNA cytosines and histone lysines by SAM-dependent enzymes is a central epigenetic mechanism.

Medical use

SAMe is sold as a dietary supplement, particularly in the United States and Italy, and has been studied for depression, osteoarthritis, and liver disease, with mixed evidence. Because it is chemically unstable, it is supplied as stable salts such as the butanedisulfonate.
