Chisel plough

A chisel plough (American spelling: chisel plow) is a farm implement used for deep tillage while leaving crop residue on the soil surface. Unlike the mouldboard plough, it does not invert the soil. Instead, a series of curved shanks tipped with narrow chisel points penetrate and shatter compacted layers, loosening the soil to depths of roughly 15 to 45 centimetres (6 to 18 inches) while disturbing the surface only minimally.

Design

The implement consists of a sturdy frame mounted on the tractor's three-point hitch or drawn as a trailer, carrying rows of shanks. Spring-loaded shanks trip backward when they strike obstructions such as rocks, while rigid shanks with shear bolts are used in stoneless soils. The chisel points may be straight spikes, twisted shovels that mix soil slightly, or wide sweeps for shallow work. Front-mounted disc coulters are often fitted to cut through heavy residue.

Use in conservation tillage

Because the chisel plough leaves much of the previous crop's residue anchored on the surface, it is a central tool of conservation tillage systems. The residue cover reduces wind and water erosion, conserves soil moisture, and limits the formation of a plough pan. It is commonly used in the autumn after harvest on cereal and maize stubble in North America, Australia, and northern Europe.

Advantages and limitations

Compared with mouldboard ploughing, chisel ploughing requires less energy per hectare at equivalent depths, works faster, and better preserves soil structure and organic matter. However, it buries fewer weed seeds and less residue, so weed control may depend more on herbicides or follow-up cultivations, and it is less effective where a complete seedbed inversion is desired.
