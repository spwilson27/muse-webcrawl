Port Said

Port Said (Arabic: بورسعيد, Būr Saʻīd) is a city in northeastern Egypt, founded in 1859 during the construction of the Suez Canal and named after Sa'id of Egypt, the Khedive who granted the concession to Ferdinand de Lesseps. It stands at the northern, Mediterranean entrance to the canal, opposite its twin city Port Fuad on the eastern bank.

History

The city grew around the canal works and the harbour built at the canal's Mediterranean terminus. Its late-nineteenth and early-twentieth-century architecture reflects a cosmopolitan port population of Egyptians, Greeks, Italians, French, and British. The original lighthouse of Port Said (1869) was a landmark of early concrete construction.

Port Said was a focal point of the Suez Crisis of 1956, when British and French forces landed there following the nationalisation of the canal; the city's resistance is commemorated in Egypt. It suffered again during the wars of 1967 and 1973, when much of the population was evacuated, and was rebuilt after the canal reopened in 1975. In the same year it was declared a duty-free port, spurring a commercial boom.

Economy and landmarks

Today the city is a major port and transshipment centre, with the East Port Said container terminals across the canal. Landmarks include the Suez Canal Authority building with its green dome, the Port Said Military Museum, and Al-Masry football club's stadium; the city was a site of the 2012 Port Said Stadium riot, one of the worst disasters in Egyptian sport.
