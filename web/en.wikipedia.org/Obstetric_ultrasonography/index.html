Obstetric ultrasonography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Obstetric ultrasonography, or prenatal ultrasound, is the use of medical ultrasonography in pregnancy, in which sound waves are used to create images of the embryo or fetus within the uterus. It is a standard part of prenatal care in many countries, used to confirm pregnancy, estimate gestational age, screen for congenital abnormalities, determine fetal sex and number, and monitor growth.

History

Obstetric ultrasound was pioneered in Glasgow in the 1950s by Ian Donald, Tom Brown and John MacVicar, whose 1958 Lancet paper demonstrated the technique. It replaced radiography for fetal imaging because it involves no ionising radiation and has an excellent safety record when used appropriately.

Types and timing

Common examinations include the early or dating scan (around 11–14 weeks, often combined with nuchal translucency screening for Down syndrome), the mid-pregnancy anatomy scan (around 18–22 weeks), and third-trimester growth scans. Scans may be performed transabdominally or transvaginally; Doppler ultrasound assesses blood flow in the umbilical cord and fetal vessels, and 3D/4D imaging renders surface views of the fetus.

Safety and practice

Diagnostic ultrasound has no confirmed adverse effects on the fetus when used at regulated output levels, but professional bodies discourage non-medical "keepsake" scanning and recommend the ALARA principle (as low as reasonably achievable). Ultrasound findings guide management of conditions such as ectopic pregnancy, placenta praevia, multiple pregnancy and fetal growth restriction.
