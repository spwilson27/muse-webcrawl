Mycology

Mycology is the branch of biology concerned with the study of fungi — including their taxonomy, genetics, biochemistry, ecology, and their uses and dangers for humans. Fungi were once classified with plants, but molecular studies show they form their own kingdom, more closely related to animals. A biologist specialising in mycology is called a mycologist. The field encompasses everything from single-celled yeasts and moulds to mushrooms, lichens, and the vast underground mycelial networks that decompose organic matter and nourish most land plants.

History

Humans have used and feared fungi since antiquity, but systematic study began in the seventeenth century with the microscope. Pier Antonio Micheli's Nova plantarum genera (1729) first described fungal spores and showed fungi arise from them rather than by spontaneous generation. In the nineteenth century, Miles Joseph Berkeley founded British mycology and plant pathology, Elias Magnus Fries systematised fungal taxonomy in Sweden, and Anton de Bary — often called the father of mycology — unravelled the life cycles of rusts and smuts and demonstrated that fungi cause plant diseases such as potato late blight. In the twentieth century, Alexander Fleming's discovery of penicillin from a Penicillium mould (1928) transformed medicine, and DNA sequencing later revolutionised fungal classification.

Scope and importance

Medical mycology studies fungal infections (mycoses) such as candidiasis, aspergillosis, and cryptococcosis, which are increasingly significant in immunocompromised patients and have growing antifungal resistance. Plant pathology deals with fungal crop diseases — rusts, blights, and mildews — that destroy a substantial fraction of global harvests. Industrial mycology exploits fungi for bread, beer, cheese, citric acid, enzymes, and antibiotics, while ecological mycology examines decomposition, nutrient cycling, mycorrhizal partnerships, and the fungal role in carbon storage. Estimates suggest there are 2–5 million fungal species, of which only about 150,000 have been formally described.
