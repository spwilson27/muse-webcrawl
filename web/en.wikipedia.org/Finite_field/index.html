In mathematics, a finite field or Galois field (so-named in honour of Évariste Galois) is a field that contains a finite number of elements. As with any field, a finite field is a set on which the operations of multiplication, addition, subtraction and division (by anything other than zero) have been defined and satisfy certain rules.

The number of elements of a finite field is always a prime power pn, where p is a prime number (the characteristic of the field) and n is a positive integer. Conversely, for every prime power there is a finite field of that order, and all finite fields of the same order are isomorphic — so one may speak of "the" finite field with q = pn elements, denoted Fq or GF(q).

Examples

The simplest finite fields have prime order: the integers modulo a prime p, written Fp, with addition and multiplication performed modulo p. For example, F5 consists of {0, 1, 2, 3, 4}, in which 3 + 4 = 2 and 3 × 4 = 2. Fields of order pn with n > 1 cannot be obtained by modular arithmetic alone; they are constructed as quotient rings Fp[x]/(f(x)), where f is an irreducible polynomial of degree n over Fp. The field GF(28), whose elements are bytes, is central to the AES encryption standard.

Structure

The additive group of a finite field is an elementary abelian p-group, and its multiplicative group of non-zero elements is always cyclic. Every finite field is a vector space over its prime subfield, and its subfields are exactly the fields of order pd for d dividing n. The Frobenius map x → xp is a field automorphism, and the full automorphism group of Fpn over Fp is cyclic of order n, generated by Frobenius.

Applications

Finite fields underpin much of modern cryptography and information theory: the AES cipher and Reed–Solomon error-correcting codes (used in CDs, DVDs, and QR codes) operate over GF(28); elliptic-curve cryptography is defined over finite fields; and finite geometry and combinatorial design theory are built on them. Galois's original investigations grew out of the theory of polynomial equations, and finite fields now also appear in number theory and algebraic geometry.
