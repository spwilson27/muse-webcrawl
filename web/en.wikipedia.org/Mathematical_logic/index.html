Mathematical logic

Mathematical logic is the study of formal logic within mathematics. Major subareas include model theory, set theory, proof theory and computability theory (also known as recursion theory). Research in mathematical logic commonly addresses the mathematical properties of formal systems of logic such as their expressive or deductive power.

Subfields

Set theory studies collections of objects and is the usual foundation of mathematics, codified in Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory with the axiom of choice (ZFC). Model theory studies the relationship between formal languages and the structures that interpret them. Proof theory treats proofs as mathematical objects and seeks results about consistency, following Hilbert's program. Computability theory studies computable functions and Turing machines, originating with the work of Gödel, Turing and Church in the 1930s.

Landmark results

Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) showed that any consistent formal system strong enough to express basic arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove, and cannot prove its own consistency. Gödel's completeness theorem established that first-order logic is complete: every valid formula is provable. The independence of the continuum hypothesis from ZFC was shown by Gödel and Paul Cohen. Mathematical logic also underpins theoretical computer science, automated reasoning and parts of philosophy and linguistics.
