Carbon footprint

A carbon footprint is the total amount of greenhouse gases — including carbon dioxide and methane — emitted directly and indirectly by an individual, organisation, product, or activity, expressed as carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e). The concept derives from the ecological footprint idea and became widespread in the 2000s.

Measurement

Footprints are calculated using emission factors for activities such as energy use, transport, food, and purchased goods. Different gases are converted to CO2e using their global warming potential over a chosen time horizon, commonly 100 years. Standards such as the GHG Protocol divide corporate emissions into Scope 1 (direct), Scope 2 (purchased energy), and Scope 3 (other indirect, including supply chains) emissions.

Typical values

Per-capita footprints vary widely: the global average is around 4–5 tonnes of CO2 per year, while the average for the United States is roughly 14–16 tonnes and for many low-income countries under 1 tonne. Energy supply, transport, agriculture, and industry are the largest contributing sectors globally.

Reduction and criticism

Footprints can be reduced by energy efficiency, low-carbon electricity, modal shifts in transport, dietary change, and product redesign. Carbon offsetting is sometimes used to compensate for residual emissions. Critics note that the popularisation of the personal carbon footprint — promoted in advertising by fossil-fuel companies in the 2000s — can shift attention from systemic and corporate emissions to individual behaviour.
