Contact geometry

A contact structure is a field of planes that twists too much to be tangent to any surface.

Contact geometry is the branch of differential geometry concerned with contact structures: maximally non-integrable hyperplane fields on odd-dimensional manifolds. It is the odd-dimensional counterpart of symplectic geometry, and arose from the study of differential equations, geometric optics, and thermodynamics in the work of Sophus Lie and others in the nineteenth century.

Definitions

On a manifold of dimension 2n+1, a contact structure can be given locally by a differential 1-form alpha such that alpha wedged with the n-fold wedge power of its derivative d-alpha never vanishes. The kernel of alpha is a field of hyperplanes which, by the Frobenius theorem, cannot be tangent to any hypersurface; instead the planes twist as one moves through the manifold. The standard example in three dimensions has planes rotating steadily about one axis. Darboux's theorem states that all contact structures are locally equivalent, so contact geometry is concerned with global phenomena.

Legendrian submanifolds and invariants

Submanifolds everywhere tangent to the contact planes, of the largest possible dimension n, are called Legendrian; their study, and the knot theory of Legendrian knots in three dimensions, is a major theme. The field was transformed after 1985 by the work of Mikhael Gromov and Yakov Eliashberg, and by the discovery of "overtwisted" and "tight" contact structures, which obey an h-principle or exhibit rigidity respectively. Modern research uses tools such as symplectic field theory and embedded contact homology, with applications to dynamics, low-dimensional topology, and mathematical physics.
