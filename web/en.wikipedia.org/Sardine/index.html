Sardines, or pilchards, are small, oily forage fish in the herring family Clupeidae. The term "sardine" was first used in English in the early 15th century, probably from the Mediterranean island of Sardinia, around which sardines were once abundant. Depending on the region and regulating body, fish from more than twenty species across several genera may be canned and sold as sardines, including the European pilchard (Sardina pilchardus), often considered the "true sardine".

Biology and ecology

Sardines are silvery fish typically 15–20 cm (6–8 in) long that travel in immense schools and feed on plankton. They inhabit the surface waters of temperate and subtropical seas and are a crucial forage species, eaten by larger fish, seabirds and marine mammals. Famous concentrations include the California sardine fishery, the Japanese sardine, and the "sardine run" off South Africa, one of the largest marine migrations on Earth.

Fisheries and food

Sardine fisheries have boomed and collapsed dramatically: the California fishery immortalized in John Steinbeck's Cannery Row peaked at some 700,000 tons in the 1930s before crashing in the 1940s–50s. Canned sardines, packed in oil, tomato sauce or mustard, are a staple food and an excellent source of omega-3 fatty acids, vitamin D, calcium and protein. Sardines are also grilled fresh around the Mediterranean, reduced to fish meal, and used as bait.
