Grotesque

The grotesque is a literary and artistic form characterised by the distorted, incongruous and fantastic — typically the blending of human, animal and plant forms, or the exaggeration of features to produce effects that are at once comic, strange and disturbing. Since at least the 18th century in English (and in French and German before that), grotesque has also been used as a general adjective for the strange, fantastic, ugly or monstrous.

Origins

The word comes from the Italian grottesca, from grotta (grotto, cave), because in the late 15th century the buried ruins of Nero's Domus Aurea in Rome were rediscovered and explored like caves, revealing walls painted with fanciful hybrid ornaments — part human, part animal, part vegetal. Renaissance artists including Raphael and his workshop imitated the style, most famously in the Vatican Loggie, and grotesque ornament spread through European decorative art and architecture.

In literature and art theory

In literature the grotesque encompasses figures such as the hunchback Quasimodo, Gogol's "The Nose", Hoffmann's tales, and much of Kafka. Theorists have treated it in opposing ways: John Ruskin saw the grotesque as the expression of profound truths through play and imperfection; Wolfgang Kayser described it as "the estranged world"; and Mikhail Bakhtin, in Rabelais and His World, celebrated the "grotesque body" of carnival — open, exaggerated, eating and laughing — as a liberating popular tradition opposed to the closed classical canon. In art, the grotesque runs from medieval drolleries and Bosch's hybrid demons to Goya's Caprichos and modern caricature.

Typography

In typography, "grotesque" (in German, Grotesk) names an early family of sans-serif typefaces of the 19th and early 20th centuries, such as Akzidenz-Grotesk — sturdy, irregular designs that preceded the more geometric "neo-grotesque" faces like Helvetica.
