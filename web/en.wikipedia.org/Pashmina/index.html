Pashmina

Pashmina is a fine variety of spun cashmere wool taken from the soft undercoat of the changthangi (pashmina) goat of the high-altitude Himalayan regions of Ladakh, Nepal, and Tibet. The word derives from the Persian pashm, meaning wool. Shawls woven from pashmina in Kashmir have been prized for centuries for their exceptional warmth, softness, and lightness.

Production

The goats grow a fine downy undercoat (fibres roughly 12–16 microns thick) to survive winters that can reach −30 °C; the fibres are shed or combed out in spring. The raw pashm is cleaned, spun — traditionally by hand, largely by women — and woven on wooden looms, famously in Srinagar. Weaving a single shawl can take weeks; elaborate patterned kani shawls, woven with small wooden sticks instead of shuttles, historically took months or years.

History

Kashmiri shawls became luxury goods in Mughal India and, from the late 18th century, in Europe, where "cashmere shawls" were fashionable status symbols; Paisley in Scotland built an industry imitating them, giving the boteh motif its Western name. The trade declined with changing fashion and the Franco-Prussian War, but pashmina revived as a global fashion item in the 1990s.

Authenticity

Because genuine pashmina is costly, the word is widely misused for blends and synthetics. In 2019 Ladakh pashmina received a Geographical Indication (GI) tag in India to protect authentic production, and fibre testing is used to certify purity.
