Hedonic treadmill

Also known asHedonic adaptation
FieldPsychology, happiness research
Term coinedBrickman & Campbell, 1971
Key studyBrickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman (1978), lottery winners and accident victims

The hedonic treadmill, also known as hedonic adaptation, is the observed tendency of humans to return to a relatively stable level of happiness despite major positive or negative events or life changes. The metaphor suggests that, like a person running on a treadmill, one must keep working simply to maintain the same level of happiness.

Research

The concept was named by Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell in 1971. A widely cited 1978 study by Brickman, Coates and Janoff-Bulman compared lottery winners with victims of accidents who had become paraplegic, and found that the two groups differed in happiness far less than expected — evidence that people adapt to both good and bad fortune. Later research has refined the idea: adaptation is often partial rather than complete, people have differing set points that can themselves shift, and adaptation is slower or absent for some conditions such as chronic pain, unemployment and bereavement.

Implications

Hedonic adaptation helps explain why income gains above a certain point yield diminishing returns in reported life satisfaction (related to the Easterlin paradox), and it informs positive psychology interventions that aim to counteract adaptation, such as gratitude exercises and variety in pleasurable activities.
