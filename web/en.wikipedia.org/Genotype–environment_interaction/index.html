Genotype–environment interaction

Genotype–environment interaction (often abbreviated G×E) is said to occur when different genotypes respond to variations in environmental conditions in different ways. In other words, the effect of an organism's genes on a trait depends on the environment it experiences, and conversely the effect of the environment depends on the genotype. G×E is a central concept in quantitative genetics, evolutionary biology, medicine, and psychology.

A useful visual tool for describing G×E is the norm of reaction: a graph of the phenotype produced by a single genotype across a range of environments. When the reaction norms of two genotypes are parallel, there is no interaction — one genotype is consistently higher than the other. When the lines diverge, converge, or cross, the relative performance of the genotypes changes with the environment, indicating interaction.

Examples

A classic medical example is phenylketonuria (PKU). Individuals homozygous for a defective phenylalanine hydroxylase gene develop severe intellectual disability if they eat a normal diet, because they cannot metabolize the amino acid phenylalanine. On a low-phenylalanine diet, however, the same genotype develops essentially normally. The effect of the gene thus depends entirely on the dietary environment.

In plants, genotypes bred for high yield under fertilized, irrigated conditions may perform worse than traditional varieties under drought or poor soil, because selection in one environment does not guarantee performance in another. Such interactions are a major concern in crop breeding and in predicting how populations will respond to climate change.

Statistical and conceptual issues

In the statistical analysis of variance, an interaction appears when the combined effect of genotype and environment on a trait differs from the sum of their separate effects. Detecting G×E depends on the scale of measurement: an interaction present on one scale may disappear after transformation. The presence of interaction also complicates estimates of heritability, because the proportion of variation attributable to genes can differ between environments.

G×E is related to, but distinct from, genotype–environment correlation, in which genotypes are non-randomly distributed among environments — for example, when individuals actively seek out environments suited to their predispositions.

Role in evolution

Genotype–environment interaction maintains genetic variation in populations, because an allele favored in one environment may be neutral or harmful in another. It also underlies phenotypic plasticity, the capacity of a single genotype to produce multiple phenotypes, which can itself evolve by natural selection.
