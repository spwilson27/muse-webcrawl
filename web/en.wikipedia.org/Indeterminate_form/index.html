Indeterminate form

In calculus, an indeterminate form is an algebraic expression obtained when evaluating a limit by substitution, whose form alone does not determine the limit's value. The term is attributed to Cauchy's student Moigno in the mid-19th century. The seven classic indeterminate forms are:

0/0,   ∞/∞,   0·∞,   ∞ − ∞,   00,   1∞,   ∞0

Explanation

For example, if f(x) and g(x) both tend to 0, the limit of f(x)/g(x) can be anything: x/x → 1, x2/x → 0, and sin(x)/x → 1, while other choices diverge. By contrast, forms such as 0/∞ (= 0) or ∞ + ∞ (= ∞) are determinate. Division by zero in itself is undefined, but indeterminate forms arise specifically as limits.

Evaluation

The principal tool for resolving indeterminate forms of the type 0/0 or ∞/∞ is L'Hôpital's rule, which replaces the limit of a quotient with the limit of the quotient of derivatives, subject to conditions. Other forms are first manipulated into quotients: 0·∞ by rewriting one factor as a denominator, ∞ − ∞ by combining fractions or rationalizing, and the power forms 00, 1∞, ∞0 by taking logarithms. Series expansions and algebraic simplification are also commonly used.
