The United States Geological Survey (USGS) is a scientific agency of the United States government, part of the Department of the Interior. Its scientists study the landscape of the United States, its natural resources, and the natural hazards that threaten it — including earthquakes, volcanoes, floods, and landslides. The USGS is notable in having no regulatory responsibility; it provides impartial scientific information.

History

The USGS was established on 3 March 1879 by an act of Congress that consolidated four competing surveys of the western territories, following the recommendation of a National Academy of Sciences report. Clarence King served as its first director, followed in 1881 by John Wesley Powell, the explorer of the Grand Canyon, who shaped the agency's scientific mission and began its topographic mapping programme.

The agency produced the famous 7.5-minute quadrangle topographic map series covering the entire country and, from 1972, co-managed the Landsat programme of Earth-observing satellites with NASA, creating the longest continuous space-based record of Earth's land surface.

Mission areas

USGS work is organized into mission areas including:

  • Earthquake hazards — monitoring through the Advanced National Seismic System and issuing rapid impact assessments (PAGER) and ShakeMaps
  • Volcano hazards — five volcano observatories, including those covering Alaska, Hawaii, the Cascades, Yellowstone, and California
  • Water resources — operating a national network of streamgages and groundwater monitoring
  • Ecosystems and Land Resources — biological research and the National Land Cover Database
  • Energy and minerals — assessments of geologic energy and mineral resources
  • National Geospatial Program — maintaining The National Map

Organization

The agency employs roughly 8,500 people at more than 400 locations. Its headquarters is the John Wesley Powell Building in Reston, Virginia, with major science centres including the National Earthquake Information Center in Golden, Colorado, and the EROS Center in South Dakota, which archives Landsat data.
