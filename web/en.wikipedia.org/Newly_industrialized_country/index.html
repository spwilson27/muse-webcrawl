A newly industrialized country (NIC) is a socioeconomic classification applied to countries whose economies have not yet reached the status of a developed country but have, in a macroeconomic sense, outpaced their developing counterparts. Such countries are typically characterised by rapid economic growth (usually export-oriented), an ongoing movement of workers from agriculture into manufacturing, and rising standards of living and levels of urbanisation.

The term came into use in the 1970s to describe the "Asian Tigers" — South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, and Hong Kong — which have since generally graduated to developed-economy status in most classifications. Analysts' lists of present-day NICs vary, but commonly include Brazil, China, India, Indonesia, Malaysia, Mexico, the Philippines, South Africa, Thailand, and Turkey.

Characteristics

NICs typically show a sharply increasing share of national output from manufacturing, strong export growth, large inflows of foreign direct investment, and an expanding middle class. They differ from least developed countries in infrastructure and industrial base, and from developed countries in income per capita, financial-market depth, and institutional measures — though the boundaries are debated and some listed countries (notably China and India) are classified differently by different institutions such as the IMF, the World Bank, and index providers like MSCI and FTSE.

Related concepts

The category overlaps with "emerging markets", a broader investment term, and with groupings such as BRICS and the Next Eleven. Economists discuss whether some NICs risk falling into the "middle-income trap", in which growth stalls before reaching high-income status.
