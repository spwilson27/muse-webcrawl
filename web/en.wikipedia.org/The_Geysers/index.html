The Geysers


Steam rising from a plant at The Geysers geothermal field.

The Geysers is a geothermal field in the Mayacamas Mountains of northern California, about 72 miles (116 km) north of San Francisco, straddling the Sonoma–Lake county border. With a complex of geothermal power plants drawing steam from more than 350 wells, it is the largest geothermal field in the world; despite its name, the field contains no true geysers — the name derives from the fumaroles and hot springs (called "The Geysers" by early settler William Bell Elliott in 1847) in the canyon of Big Sulphur Creek.

Geology

The reservoir is a dry-steam system: fractured greywacke sandstone capped by low-permeability rock traps superheated steam at temperatures around 240 °C (465 °F). The heat source is believed to be a shallow silicic magma body associated with the young Clear Lake volcanic field. The Geysers is unusual among major geothermal fields in producing dry steam directly usable in turbines without separation.

Power generation

Commercial generation began in 1960 with an 11-megawatt unit operated by Pacific Gas and Electric. Development accelerated in the 1970s and 1980s, peaking at roughly 2,000 megawatts of installed capacity in the late 1980s, when The Geysers supplied a significant share of California's renewable electricity. Steam pressure then declined from overproduction; output stabilized after large wastewater recharge projects began piping treated effluent from Lake County and Santa Rosa to be injected into the reservoir. Most plants are now operated by Calpine, with others run by the Northern California Power Agency and other owners; current output is on the order of 850 megawatts — enough for roughly a million homes in good years.

Seismicity and environment

Steam extraction and injection induce frequent small earthquakes, generally below magnitude 3, monitored closely by the U.S. Geological Survey; the largest recorded induced events have reached about magnitude 4.5. The field lies in rugged, sparsely populated terrain, and reinjection has largely addressed earlier concerns about subsidence and steam depletion.
