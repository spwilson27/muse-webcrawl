Cellular differentiation

Cellular differentiation is the process in which a stem cell changes from one type to a differentiated one. Usually, the cell changes to a more specialized type. Differentiation happens multiple times during the development of a multicellular organism as it changes from a simple zygote to a complex system of tissues and cell types. Differentiation continues in adulthood as adult stem cells divide and create fully differentiated daughter cells during tissue repair and during normal cell turnover.

Some differentiation occurs in response to antigen exposure. Differentiation dramatically changes a cell's size, shape, and metabolic activity. These changes are largely due to highly controlled modifications in gene expression and are the study of epigenetics. With a few exceptions, cellular differentiation almost never involves a change in the DNA sequence itself.

Cell types

Cells capable of giving rise to many types are called pluripotent (in animals, induced pluripotent stem cells and embryonic stem cells); cells that can form all cell types of the body, including the germline and placental cells, are totipotent. Multipotent cells, such as hematopoietic stem cells, produce a limited family of types.

Mechanisms

Differentiation is controlled by cascades of transcription factors, signalling pathways such as Wnt, Notch and Hedgehog, and epigenetic marks including DNA methylation and histone modification. Morphogen gradients specify positional identity during development, while asymmetric cell division can produce daughters with distinct fates.

Dedifferentiation and transdifferentiation

Some cells can revert to a less differentiated state (dedifferentiation), a process important in regeneration in animals such as salamanders. Direct conversion between differentiated types, transdifferentiation, occurs naturally in a few cases and can be induced experimentally by forced expression of transcription factors, as in the production of induced pluripotent stem cells by the Yamanaka factors.
