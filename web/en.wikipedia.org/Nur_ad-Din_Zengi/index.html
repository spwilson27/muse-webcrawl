Nur ad-Din Mahmud ibn Zengi (1118–1174), commonly known as Nur ad-Din (Arabic for "Light of the Faith"), was a member of the Turkic Zengid dynasty who ruled Syria in the twelfth century. He unified Muslim Syria and led the counter-crusade against the Crusader states, laying the groundwork for Saladin's later reconquest of Jerusalem.

Rise and unification of Syria

Nur ad-Din was the second son of Imad ad-Din Zengi, the atabeg who had captured Edessa from the Crusaders in 1144. On his father's assassination in 1146, Nur ad-Din inherited Aleppo while his brother took Mosul. He defeated the Second Crusade's attack on Damascus in 1148 and in 1154 absorbed Damascus itself, uniting Syria from Edessa to Damascus under one ruler for the first time in the crusading era.

Wars and policy

Nur ad-Din waged a patient, decades-long jihad against the Crusader states while also contending with Byzantium and rival Muslim powers. He intervened repeatedly in Fatimid Egypt, sending his general Shirkuh — accompanied by Shirkuh's nephew Saladin — whose campaigns ended with Saladin becoming vizier of Egypt in 1169 and abolishing the Fatimid caliphate in 1171 under Nur ad-Din's nominal authority. At home he was famed for justice, piety, and patronage: he built madrasas, hospitals (including the Bimaristan Nur al-Din in Damascus), mosques, and caravanserais, and promoted Sunni orthodoxy.

Death and legacy

Nur ad-Din died of illness in Damascus in 1174 while preparing to bring the increasingly independent Saladin to heel. His young son as-Salih Ismail succeeded briefly, but Saladin took control of his empire. Remembered as an exemplar of the just Muslim ruler, Nur ad-Din created the political unity and ideological momentum that made the recovery of Jerusalem in 1187 possible.
