In Greek mythology, Prometheus (/prəˈmiːθiəs/; Greek: Προμηθεύς, "forethought") is a Titan, a culture hero, and a trickster figure who is credited with the creation of humanity from clay and with defying the gods by stealing fire and giving it to mankind — an act that enabled progress and civilisation.

The theft of fire

According to Hesiod's Theogony and Works and Days, Prometheus tricked Zeus at Mecone over the division of sacrificial meat, provoking Zeus to withhold fire from mortals. Prometheus stole the fire back, hiding it in a hollow fennel stalk. In punishment, Zeus had him chained to a rock in the Caucasus, where an eagle (or vulture) ate his liver each day, the organ regenerating each night. He was eventually freed by Heracles. Zeus's anger also produced Pandora, whose jar released evils into the world.

In literature

Prometheus is the subject of Aeschylus's tragedy Prometheus Bound, in which he appears as a defiant benefactor of humanity against a tyrannical Zeus. The myth was reinterpreted by the Romantics — Percy Bysshe Shelley wrote the lyrical drama Prometheus Unbound (1820), and Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (1818) was subtitled "The Modern Prometheus." Prometheus has since become an enduring symbol of human striving, scientific daring, and rebellion against authority.
