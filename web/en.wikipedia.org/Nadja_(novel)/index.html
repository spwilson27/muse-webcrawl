Nadja (novel)

Nadja
Author: André Breton
Language: French
Genre: Surrealist novel / récit
Published: 1928 (revised 1963)
Publisher: Éditions Gallimard

Nadja is a 1928 novel-like narrative (récit) by French writer André Breton, the founder of Surrealism. Part autobiography, part philosophical meditation, it is considered the quintessential surrealist prose work, blending text with 44 photographs of Paris and its people.

Summary

The book opens with the question "Who am I?" and proceeds through Breton's encounters with the marvels of everyday Paris — flea markets, deserted squares, strange signs — before recounting his ten-day relationship with a young woman who calls herself Nadja (from the Russian word for hope, she explains, "and because it is only the beginning"). Their wandering conversations and her drawings fill the central section. Nadja's eventual confinement in a psychiatric asylum prompts Breton's fierce critique of psychiatry and of the logic that separates the sane from the mad.

Style and influence

Breton deliberately rejected conventional novelistic description, using photographs (by Man Ray, Jacques-André Boiffard and others) in place of description and an associative, collage-like structure. The book closes with the famous declaration that "beauty will be CONVULSIVE or will not be at all." Nadja strongly influenced later French literature and the Situationists' practice of the dérive. An English translation by Richard Howard appeared in 1960. See also Surrealism.
