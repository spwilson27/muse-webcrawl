Extra time (association football)

Extra time is an additional period of play used in association football knockout matches when the score is level at the end of normal time (90 minutes plus stoppage time). Under Law 7 of the Laws of the Game and competition rules, extra time consists of two periods of 15 minutes each, played in full in most modern competitions, followed if necessary by a penalty shoot-out to determine the winner.

Usage

Extra time applies only where a match must produce a winner — typically single-leg knockout ties and finals. In two-legged ties it is used when scores are level on aggregate (and, where applicable, after the away goals rule). League matches that end level simply finish as draws. Some competitions, such as earlier rounds of domestic cup ties, historically used replays instead, and a few now go straight to penalties to reduce fixture congestion.

Golden goal and silver goal

From the mid-1990s, FIFA and UEFA experimented with the golden goal: the first goal in extra time ended the match immediately. Introduced to encourage attacking play, it was used at Euro 1996 and Euro 2000 and at the 1998 and 2002 World Cups, but it was widely criticized for making teams more cautious, and was abolished in 2004. A variant, the silver goal — where a team leading at half-time of extra time wins — was briefly used in European club competition before also being scrapped.

Notable matches

Famous extra-time matches include the 1966 World Cup Final, in which England defeated West Germany 4–2 after extra time, and the 1970 World Cup semi-final between Italy and West Germany, the "Game of the Century," which produced five goals in extra time and finished 4–3. Since 2006, several World Cup finals — 2010, 2014, and 2022 among them — have been decided in extra time or on penalties thereafter.
