The East India Company (EIC) was an English (later British) joint-stock company founded in 1600 and dissolved in 1874. It was formed to trade in the Indian Ocean region, initially with the East Indies (South and Southeast Asia), and later with China. The company eventually came to rule large areas of India, exercising military power and assuming administrative functions, effectively governing much of the subcontinent on behalf of the British Crown.

Origins and trade

Chartered by Elizabeth I as "The Company of Merchants of London trading into the East-Indies," the company established factories (trading posts) at Surat, Madras, Bombay, and Calcutta. Its principal commodities were cotton textiles, silk, saltpetre, tea, and — notoriously — opium, which it exported to China, precipitating the Opium Wars.

Rule in India

After Robert Clive's victory at the Battle of Plassey in 1757, the company began its transformation into a territorial power, collecting revenue in Bengal and fielding private armies that at their peak numbered some 260,000 men — twice the size of the British Army. A series of parliamentary acts progressively subordinated the company to the Crown.

Decline and dissolution

The Indian Rebellion of 1857 led the British government to assume direct rule through the Government of India Act 1858, establishing the British Raj. The company lingered as a managing agent until it was formally dissolved on 1 June 1874.

Legacy

The company is often cited as the world's first multinational corporation and as a pioneering example of the joint-stock company; at its height it accounted for roughly half of the world's trade in several basic commodities. Its rule left a contested legacy of both infrastructure development and exploitation in India.
