Primary color

Additive primariesRed, green, blue (RGB)
Subtractive primariesCyan, magenta, yellow (CMY/CMYK)
Traditional (painting)Red, yellow, blue (RYB)

A primary color is one of a set of colors that can be combined in varying proportions to produce a wide range (gamut) of other colors. Primary colors are not a fundamental property of light but a practical choice determined by the physiology of human vision and the available colorants or light sources.

Additive mixing

In additive mixing, used in displays and stage lighting, colored lights are combined. The standard primaries are red, green, and blue (RGB), corresponding roughly to the three types of cone cells in the human eye. Red and green light make yellow; green and blue make cyan; blue and red make magenta; all three together make white.

Subtractive mixing

In subtractive mixing, used in printing, pigments remove (subtract) wavelengths from white light. The modern primaries are cyan, magenta, and yellow (CMYK, where K is black, added for practical density and economy). The traditional painter's primaries — red, yellow, and blue (RYB) — are historically important in art education but produce a smaller gamut than CMY.

Perception

Because any three primaries can generate only a limited gamut, no real set of primaries can reproduce every perceivable color. The concept traces to early color theories and was placed on a scientific footing in the nineteenth century by Thomas Young, Hermann von Helmholtz, and James Clerk Maxwell.
