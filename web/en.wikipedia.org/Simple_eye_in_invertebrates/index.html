A simple eye (sometimes called a pigment pit) is an eye form found in many invertebrates which, in most cases, functions only to detect whether the surroundings are light or dark — the sensation of photoperiod. In more developed forms, a simple eye with a single lens is called an ocellus (plural ocelli). Unlike the compound eyes of most arthropods, a simple eye has one lens rather than many.

Types

Simple eyes range from mere clusters of photoreceptor cells shaded by pigment — capable only of registering brightness — to pit eyes that give directional information, cup eyes, and lensed eyes such as those of some spiders, which can form moderately sharp images. The main eyes of jumping spiders, technically simple eyes, rival compound eyes in acuity and support colour vision.

Ocelli in arthropods

Many insects bear three dorsal ocelli arranged in a triangle on top of the head, in addition to their compound eyes. These react rapidly to changes in light intensity and are thought to aid flight stability and horizon detection. Larvae of holometabolous insects have simple eyes called stemmata.

Other groups

Simple eyes occur in jellyfish (as part of rhopalia, sometimes with lenses), starfish (at the tips of the arms), flatworms, molluscs, and many other phyla. Their independent evolution in so many lineages makes them a classic example of convergent evolution.
