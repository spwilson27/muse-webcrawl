Whiskey (or whisky) is a type of liquor distilled from fermented grain mash. Various grains — including barley, corn, rye, and wheat — are used for different varieties. Whiskey is typically aged in wooden casks, generally made of charred white oak.

Etymology and spelling

The word derives from the Classical Gaelic uisce beatha, meaning "water of life". The spelling whisky is generally used in Scotland, Canada, and Japan, while whiskey is used in Ireland and the United States.

Production

Grain is milled, mashed with hot water, fermented with yeast, and distilled — traditionally in copper pot stills for malt whisky or in column stills for grain whiskey. The spirit is then matured in oak barrels, during which it gains colour and flavour from the wood. Scotch whisky must be aged in Scotland for at least three years; American bourbon must be made from at least 51% corn and aged in new charred oak containers.

Styles

Major styles include Scotch whisky (single malt and blended), Irish whiskey, American bourbon and rye, Canadian whisky, and the rapidly growing Japanese whisky. In recent decades, distilleries have also proliferated in countries such as India, Taiwan, and Australia.

History

The art of distillation spread to Ireland and Scotland by the 15th century. The first written record of whisky in Scotland dates to 1494, when the Exchequer Rolls note malt given to Friar John Cor "to make aqua vitae". Whiskey production was central to the Whiskey Rebellion of the 1790s in the young United States.
