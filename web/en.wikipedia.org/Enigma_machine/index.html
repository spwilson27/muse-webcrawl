The Enigma machine is a cipher device developed and used in the early- to mid-20th century to protect commercial, diplomatic, and military communication. It was employed extensively by Nazi Germany during World War II, in all branches of the German military. The Enigma machine used an electromechanical rotor mechanism to scramble the 26 letters of the alphabet; each key press lit a substituted letter on a lampboard, and the rotors advanced so that the substitution changed with every letter, producing a polyalphabetic cipher with an astronomically large number of possible settings.

Design

A military Enigma had a keyboard, a plugboard (Steckerbrett) swapping ten pairs of letters, an entry wheel, three (or, in the naval M4, four) removable rotors chosen from a set and settable to 26 ring positions each, and a reflector that sent the signal back through the rotors by a different path. The reflector made encryption reciprocal — the same settings encrypted and decrypted — but also meant a letter could never be encrypted as itself, a weakness cryptanalysts later exploited. Daily key books specified rotor order, ring settings and plugboard connections, and operators chose a message key for each transmission.

Codebreaking

Enigma was first broken by the Polish Cipher Bureau in December 1932, when Marian Rejewski reconstructed the machine's wiring using mathematics and intelligence material purchased by French intelligence. Poland shared its methods — including electromechanical "bombas" and perforated sheets — with Britain and France in July 1939. At Bletchley Park, Alan Turing, Gordon Welchman and others industrialised the attack with the Bombe machines, exploiting operating errors, predictable phrases ("cribs") and captured key material; naval Enigma required the capture of codebooks from U-boats such as U-110 and U-559. The resulting intelligence, codenamed Ultra, is often credited with shortening the war.

Legacy

Enigma has become the most famous cipher machine in history, the subject of museums, films and a large surviving population of machines in collections. Modern analysis confirms that with correct procedures the system would have been extremely strong for its era; its defeat combined mathematical insight, engineering, espionage and German procedural mistakes.
