Preference falsification

Preference falsification is the act of misrepresenting one's genuine wants under perceived social pressures. It involves concealing or distorting a preference from others, so the publicly expressed preference differs from the privately held one. The concept was developed by the economist and social scientist Timur Kuran in his book Private Truths, Public Lies (1995).

Mechanism and consequences

People falsify preferences to gain approval, avoid punishment or maintain standing within a group. Because individuals judge what is popular from what others publicly say, falsification can sustain a status quo that few privately support — a state related to pluralistic ignorance — and can distort public knowledge about the distribution of opinion. Polls and elections may then mislead, and regimes that appear stable can collapse suddenly when private preferences are revealed in a cascade, as Kuran argued happened during the revolutions of 1989 in Eastern Europe.

Examples

Applications of the theory include the durability of unpopular norms such as foot-binding, the sudden shifts in public opinion on issues such as same-sex marriage, under-reporting of prejudice in surveys, and "shy voter" effects in polling. Related concepts include the spiral of silence, preference cascades and the Abilene paradox.
