Seljuk Turks

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Seljuk caravanserai portal in Anatolia, reflecting the dynasty's Persianate culture.

The Seljuk Turks were a Sunni Muslim Turkic people and dynasty originating from the Oghuz Turks of the Central Asian steppe. Named after their founder Seljuk ibn Duqaq, they migrated from the region north of the Aral Sea into Persia in the 11th century, where under Tughril Beg they defeated the Ghaznavids at the Battle of Dandanaqan (1040) and entered Baghdad in 1055, becoming the effective rulers of the Abbasid Caliphate as sultans.

The Great Seljuk Empire

Under Alp Arslan and Malik-Shah I, aided by the great vizier Nizam al-Mulk, the Seljuk Empire stretched from Anatolia to Central Asia and became the dominant power of the Islamic world. Alp Arslan's victory over the Byzantine Emperor Romanos IV at the Battle of Manzikert in 1071 opened Anatolia to Turkmen settlement, a turning point that eventually prompted the Byzantine appeal for Western aid and thus the Crusades. The empire fostered a Persianate cultural renaissance, with madrasas (the Nizamiyya network), astronomy under Omar Khayyam, and the administrative manual Siyasatnama.

Fragmentation and successor states

After Malik-Shah's death in 1092, succession struggles fragmented the empire into rival sultanates and atabegates: the Sultanate of Rum in Anatolia, the Seljuks of Kerman, Syria, and Iraq, among others. The Anatolian Seljuks of Rum, with their capital at Konya, survived longest as a major power, flourishing in trade and architecture until Mongol victory at Köse Dağ in 1243 reduced them to vassals; the last Rum sultanate dissolved in the early 14th century, leaving the Anatolian beyliks from which the Ottoman Empire emerged.

Legacy

The Seljuks Turkified Anatolia and revitalized Sunni Islam against Fatimid Shi'a influence, building a durable model of Turco-Persian statecraft. Their art and architecture — brick minarets, glazed tilework, and grand caravanserais — shaped the Islamic world from Persia to Turkey.
