A germ layer is one of the primary layers of cells that form during embryonic development. In most animals, three germ layers are established during gastrulation: the outer ectoderm, the middle mesoderm, and the inner endoderm. Each layer gives rise to specific tissues and organs of the body, and the fate of each layer is remarkably consistent across species.

The three layers

The ectoderm produces the epidermis, hair and nails, the entire nervous system, and parts of the sensory organs. The mesoderm forms muscle, bone, cartilage, blood and blood vessels, the heart, kidneys, and reproductive organs. The endoderm lines the digestive and respiratory tracts and gives rise to the liver, pancreas, thyroid, and bladder lining. Some simple animals, such as jellyfish and sea anemones, are diploblastic, with only two layers; bilaterians are triploblastic.

Development

Germ layers arise as the hollow blastula reorganises itself in gastrulation, through movements such as invagination and ingression. Cells acquire their identities through signalling between layers — the mesoderm-inducing signals and the organiser region discovered by Spemann and Mangold are classic examples — and the germ-layer concept, developed by nineteenth-century embryologists such as Pander, von Baer, and Remak, remains a foundation of developmental biology.
