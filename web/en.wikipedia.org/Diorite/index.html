Diorite is an intrusive igneous rock formed by the slow cooling underground of magma (molten rock) that has a moderate content of silica and a relatively low content of alkali metals. It is an intermediate intrusive rock, composed principally of the silicate minerals plagioclase feldspar (typically andesine), biotite, hornblende, and sometimes pyroxene. Its speckled black-and-white appearance is sometimes called "salt and pepper" texture.

Formation and classification

Diorite forms in volcanic arcs above subduction zones and in areas of continental crust thickening, typically as stocks, dikes, and sills within batholith complexes. It differs from gabbro in having sodic rather than calcic plagioclase and less mafic mineral content, and from granodiorite and granite in lacking significant quartz. In the QAPF classification, diorite contains less than 5% quartz.

Historical use

Because diorite is extremely hard and durable, it was prized by ancient civilisations for sculpture and inscriptions. The Code of Hammurabi was inscribed on a tall diorite stele (c. 1754 BC), and Egyptian sculptors used diorite for statues such as that of Khafre. Inca and pre-Columbian cultures also worked the stone. Polished diorite is still used decoratively as dimension stone, sometimes marketed as "black granite".
