Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics are summary statistics that quantitatively describe or summarize features of a collection of information — the data set actually observed. They are distinguished from inferential statistics, which use a sample to draw conclusions about the population from which it was drawn. Descriptive statistics aim to summarize the data at hand, whether that data constitute the whole population or a sample of it.

Measures of central tendency

The mean (arithmetic average), median (middle value of an ordered set) and mode (most frequent value) summarize the centre of a distribution. Each has strengths: the mean uses all the data but is sensitive to outliers, while the median is robust and preferred for skewed distributions such as income.

Measures of dispersion

Spread around the centre is described by the range, interquartile range, variance and its square root, the standard deviation. Quantities such as percentiles and quartiles divide the data into ordered parts. The shape of a distribution is further characterized by skewness (asymmetry) and kurtosis (tailedness). Categorical data are summarized by counts and proportions, often displayed in contingency tables.

Graphical descriptions

Histograms, bar charts, box plots, stem-and-leaf displays and scatter plots form the visual core of descriptive statistics and of exploratory data analysis as championed by John Tukey. Bivariate descriptions add the correlation coefficient and cross-tabulations.

Use

Univariate descriptive statistics underpin virtually every empirical discipline, from census reports and sports analytics to quality control and clinical trial summaries. They provide the foundation on which inferential methods are built and the first check of whether data behave as expected.
