Topoisomerase

Topoisomerase
FunctionAlters DNA topology: relieves supercoiling, untangles chromosomes
Type ICuts one strand; changes linking number in steps of 1
Type IICuts both strands; ATP-dependent; steps of 2 (e.g. DNA gyrase)
Drug targetsFluoroquinolone antibiotics; etoposide, doxorubicin (anticancer)

Topoisomerases are enzymes that participate in the overwinding and underwinding of DNA. Because DNA is a double helix, the processes of replication and transcription generate torsional stress: DNA ahead of a replication fork becomes positively supercoiled, separate strands can become tangled (catenated), and knots can form. Topoisomerases resolve these topological problems by transiently cutting the DNA backbone, passing strands through the break, and resealing it — changing the linking number of the molecule without altering its sequence.

Classes

Type I topoisomerases cut a single strand of DNA. Subtype IA enzymes pass the intact strand through the nick and are required to relax negative supercoils and decatenate; subtype IB enzymes (including human topoisomerase I) swivel the cut strand around the intact one and relax both positive and negative supercoils. Type II topoisomerases cut both strands simultaneously, pass an entire second duplex through the gap in an ATP-dependent reaction, and reseal it. Bacterial DNA gyrase, a type II enzyme, uniquely introduces negative supercoils into DNA; its sibling, topoisomerase IV, specialises in decatenating daughter chromosomes after replication.

Clinical significance

Because they are essential and their cleavage intermediates are potentially lethal, topoisomerases are major drug targets. Fluoroquinolone antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin poison bacterial gyrase and topoisomerase IV. In cancer chemotherapy, topoisomerase I inhibitors (irinotecan, topotecan, derived from camptothecin) and topoisomerase II inhibitors (etoposide, doxorubicin) stabilise the DNA–enzyme cleavage complex, converting the enzyme into a source of DNA double-strand breaks that kill rapidly dividing cells — at the cost of toxicities including, for some agents, secondary leukaemias.
