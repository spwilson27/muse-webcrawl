Prediction of volcanic activity

Prediction of volcanic activity, or volcanic eruption forecasting, is an interdisciplinary monitoring and research effort to predict the time and nature of a volcano's eruption, and to assess hazards. Volcano monitoring combines observations of seismicity, ground deformation, gas emissions, and thermal changes to detect unrest that may precede an eruption.

Methods

Volcanologists use seismometers to detect volcanic earthquakes and tremor caused by magma movement; GPS stations, tiltmeters, and satellite radar (InSAR) to measure swelling of the edifice as magma accumulates; and gas sensors to track changes in sulphur dioxide and carbon dioxide emissions. Changes in crater lakes, fumarole temperatures, and gravity can also signal intrusions.

Case studies

The 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines is considered a forecasting success: escalating monitoring data led to evacuations that saved thousands of lives. The 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens was preceded by months of deformation and earthquakes, though its lateral blast was unexpected. At the other extreme, phreatic eruptions such as Mount Ontake in 2014 occurred with little detectable warning.

Limitations

Forecasts remain probabilistic: many episodes of unrest do not culminate in eruption, and alert-level systems (such as those of the US Geological Survey) are used to communicate escalating risk to authorities and the public.
