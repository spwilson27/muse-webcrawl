Infix

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An infix is an affix inserted inside an existing word or word stem — as opposed to a prefix, attached at the beginning, or a suffix, attached at the end. True infixes are common in some languages but rare in English, where apparent cases are usually analysed differently.

In world languages

Infixation is a productive process in many Austronesian languages: in Tagalog, the actor-focus infix -um- turns sulat ("write") into sumulat ("wrote [actor focus]"). Khmer, Malay, and numerous Indigenous languages of the Americas also employ infixes grammatically. In the history of Indo-European languages, the celebrated nasal infix inserted -n- into verb roots to form present stems — compare Latin vincō ("I conquer") beside perfect vīcī.

In English

Standard English has no productive grammatical infixation. The best-known quasi-infix is expletive infixation, in which a profanity is inserted inside a word at a point determined by stress: abso-bloody-lutely, fan-flaming-tastic, un-freaking-believable. Linguists use the phenomenon as evidence for the psychological reality of syllable and stress structure, since speakers agree strongly on where the expletive may and may not go. Playful formations such as "edu-ma-cation" parody the pattern.

Tmesis

Inserting a whole separate word inside another (or inside a phrase) is called tmesis; it overlaps with, but is distinct from, true infixation, where the inserted element is a bound morpheme.
