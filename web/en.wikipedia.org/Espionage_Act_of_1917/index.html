Espionage Act of 1917

The Espionage Act of 1917 is a United States federal law enacted on 15 June 1917, shortly after the United States entered the First World War. It prohibits interference with military operations, support of the nation's enemies in wartime, and — in the provisions now codified at 18 U.S.C. §§ 792–799 — the gathering, transmission, or loss of information relating to the national defense. Penalties can reach life imprisonment, and in wartime, death.

History and prosecutions

Passed amid fears of German sabotage and sedition, the Act was supplemented in 1918 by the Sedition Act, which criminalised disloyal speech and was repealed in 1920. Under the Espionage Act, Socialist leader Eugene V. Debs was imprisoned for an anti-war speech (upheld in Debs v. United States, following the "clear and present danger" test of Schenck v. United States), and over two thousand prosecutions were brought. Later famous prosecutions include Julius and Ethel Rosenberg, convicted of passing atomic secrets to the Soviet Union and executed in 1953; Daniel Ellsberg, charged over the Pentagon Papers in 1971 (charges dismissed for government misconduct); and, in the twenty-first century, whistleblowers and leakers including Chelsea Manning, Reality Winner, and Edward Snowden, who was charged under the Act in 2013. Julian Assange was indicted under it in 2019 for publishing classified material, a use of the Act against a publisher contested by press-freedom advocates before his 2024 plea agreement.

Free speech debate

The Act has been continuously debated for stifling dissent and press activity. Defenders argue it is essential to protect national defense information; critics, including the ACLU and successive newspaper editorial boards, argue its broad language — especially §793(e), used against those who disclose information to the press — lacks a public-interest defence and chills reporting. Reforms have repeatedly been proposed but none has substantially narrowed the statute.
