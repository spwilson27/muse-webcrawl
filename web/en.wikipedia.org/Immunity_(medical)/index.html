Immunity (medical)

Immunity is the body's state of protection against infectious disease.

Medically, immunity is the capability of multicellular organisms to resist harmful microorganisms. It involves both specific and nonspecific components. The nonspecific components act as barriers or eliminate a wide range of pathogens irrespective of their specific antigenic makeup; the specific component adapts to each new disease encountered and can generate a pathogen-specific response, including long-lasting immunological memory. The science of immunity is immunology, and the system responsible is the immune system.

Types of immunity

Immunity is conventionally divided into innate (inborn) defenses — physical and chemical barriers, phagocytic cells, complement, and inflammation — and adaptive immunity mediated by lymphocytes and antibodies. Adaptive immunity may be acquired actively, when the individual's own immune system responds to infection or to a vaccine and retains memory, or passively, through the transfer of ready-made antibodies, as from mother to infant across the placenta and in breast milk, or through antibody preparations such as immune globulin.

History

The concept is ancient: Thucydides noted that survivors of the plague of Athens could nurse the sick without falling ill again. Deliberate variolation against smallpox was practised in China and the Ottoman world before Edward Jenner's 1796 cowpox vaccination, and Louis Pasteur later generalised the principle of attenuation to produce vaccines against anthrax and rabies. Failures of immunity include immunodeficiency disorders and autoimmune diseases, in which the immune system attacks the body's own tissues.
