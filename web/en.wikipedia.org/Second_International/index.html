Second International

The Second International (1889–1916), also called the Socialist International, was an organisation of socialist and labour parties formed in Paris in 1889 by delegates from twenty countries. It coordinated the growing socialist movement, promoted the eight-hour day and International Workers' Day on May 1, and collapsed at the outbreak of the First World War, when most member parties supported their own governments.

History

The founding congress met in Paris on the centenary of Bastille Day, 14 July 1889. Excluding anarchists after 1896, the International united Marxist parties such as the German SPD — then the largest socialist party in the world — with trade union movements. Its congresses debated colonialism, militarism, and the general strike.

Collapse

Although the Stuttgart (1907) and Basel (1912) resolutions pledged opposition to imperialist war, in August 1914 the French and German parties voted war credits to their governments. The International effectively ceased to function, and anti-war minorities met at Zimmerwald in 1915. After the war, the movement split three ways: a revived (Berne) International, the centrist "Two-and-a-half" International, and the Communist International (Comintern) founded in Moscow in 1919. The Labour and Socialist International of 1923 carried on the Second International's tradition.
