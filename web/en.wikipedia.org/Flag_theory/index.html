Flag theory

Flag theory is a strategy, associated with the libertarian investment and expatriation movements, of diversifying the legal jurisdictions in which a person places different aspects of their life — so that no single government has complete authority over them. The metaphor is that of "planting flags" in different countries: each flag represents a function such as citizenship, tax residence, banking, business incorporation, asset storage, or lifestyle, and each is located in the jurisdiction judged most favourable for that function.

Origins

The idea is usually traced to investment commentator Harry D. Schultz, who proposed a "three flags" approach in the 1960s: hold a second passport, keep assets in a safe-haven country different from one's residence, and live in a third country, particularly a tax haven. The concept was later expanded by W. G. Hill in works such as PT: The Perpetual Traveller (1980s), where "PT" was variously glossed as "Permanent Tourist", "Prior Taxpayer", or "Perpetual Traveller". Hill's formulation is often given as the five flags:

  1. Citizenship — a passport from a country that does not tax foreign income or control travel.
  2. Residence — legal residence in a (preferably low-tax or territorial-tax) country different from one's physical home.
  3. Business — incorporation where income is earned, in a business-friendly jurisdiction.
  4. Assets — banking and brokerage in a stable financial centre.
  5. Playgrounds — the places where one actually spends time, chosen for lifestyle.

Later writers have proposed a sixth flag (digital life: hosting, domains, and communications in privacy-respecting jurisdictions) and a seventh (charity or philanthropy).

Criticism and legality

Practitioners emphasise that flag theory in itself is a framework for lawful jurisdictional diversification — second citizenships, residence-by-investment programmes, and offshore companies are legal in most countries — but critics note that the same structures can shade into tax evasion, money laundering, or sanctions evasion if reporting obligations (such as the U.S. FATCA regime or the OECD Common Reporting Standard) are ignored. Tax authorities generally tax residents and, in the case of the United States and Eritrea, citizens, regardless of where flags are planted, and controlled-foreign-corporation and exit-tax rules limit the strategy's effectiveness for many people.
