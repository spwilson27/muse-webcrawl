Henry the Lion (German: Heinrich der Löwe; c. 1129/1131 – 6 August 1195) was a member of the Welf dynasty and Duke of Saxony (as Henry III) from 1142 and Duke of Bavaria (as Henry XII) from 1156 until his deposition in 1180. He was one of the most powerful princes of the Holy Roman Empire during the reign of his cousin Emperor Frederick Barbarossa.

Rise to power

Henry was the son of Henry the Proud, Duke of Saxony and Bavaria, and Gertrude, daughter of Emperor Lothair III. After his father's death in 1139 the young Henry's inheritance was contested, but he recovered Saxony in 1142 and was granted Bavaria in 1156 after its transfer from the Babenberg margraves. He supported Frederick Barbarossa's Italian campaigns and in return received extensive rights east of the Elbe.

Rule and eastward expansion

Henry consolidated power in the north, subjugating the Slavic Obotrites and other Wendish peoples in the Wendish Crusade and subsequent campaigns, founding or refounding cities including Lübeck (1159) and Munich (1158), and promoting settlement and trade. He made Brunswick his residence, building the castle of Dankwarderode and Brunswick Cathedral, and erected the Brunswick Lion — the first large freestanding bronze sculpture north of the Alps — as a symbol of his authority. In 1168 he married Matilda, daughter of Henry II of England.

Fall and exile

Relations with Barbarossa broke down after Henry refused military support for the emperor's Italian campaign in 1176. At the imperial diets of 1179–1180 Henry was placed under the imperial ban and stripped of both duchies; Saxony was divided, with Westphalia going to the Archbishop of Cologne, and Bavaria granted to Otto of Wittelsbach. Henry went into exile at the court of his father-in-law in Normandy and England, returning in 1185 and again being exiled from 1189 to 1190. He was eventually reconciled with Barbarossa's successor, Henry VI.

Death and legacy

Henry died in Brunswick in 1195 and was buried in Brunswick Cathedral beside his wife Matilda. His sons included Otto IV, Holy Roman Emperor. He is remembered as a founder of cities and a central figure of German eastward expansion in the twelfth century.
