Rachel Louise Carson (May 27, 1907 – April 14, 1964) was an American marine biologist, writer, and conservationist. Her sea trilogy — Under the Sea Wind (1941), The Sea Around Us (1951), and The Edge of the Sea (1955) — brought ocean life to a broad readership. Her final book, Silent Spring (1962), documented the environmental harm caused by indiscriminate pesticide use, especially DDT, and is credited with launching the modern environmental movement.

Early life and education

Carson was born on a farm near Springdale, Pennsylvania. A precocious writer, she published stories in children's magazines as a schoolgirl. She studied English and then zoology at the Pennsylvania College for Women (now Chatham University), graduating in 1929, and earned a master's degree in zoology from Johns Hopkins University in 1932.

Career at the Bureau of Fisheries

In 1936 Carson joined the U.S. Bureau of Fisheries (later the Fish and Wildlife Service), one of the first women hired there in a scientific role. She wrote radio scripts and informational brochures, rose to editor-in-chief of the service's publications, and published widely in magazines. The Sea Around Us won the National Book Award and stayed on bestseller lists for many months, allowing her to leave government service in 1952 and write full time.

Silent Spring

Published in September 1962 after serialization in The New Yorker, Silent Spring argued that synthetic pesticides — above all DDT — accumulated in food chains, thinned the eggshells of birds of prey, and posed risks to human health. The chemical industry attacked the book and Carson personally, but a presidential science advisory committee largely vindicated her findings. The controversy helped spur the creation of the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (1970) and the eventual ban of DDT for agricultural use in the United States in 1972.

Death and legacy

Carson died of a heart attack in 1964, following breast cancer. She was posthumously awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1980. Her work is widely regarded as foundational to environmental science writing and to the modern environmental movement.
