Sakamoto Ryōma

Sakamoto Ryōma (Japanese: 坂本龍馬; born Sakamoto Naonari; 3 January 1836 – 10 December 1867) was a Japanese samurai and influential figure in the Bakumatsu period — the final years of the Tokugawa shogunate. A low-ranking samurai from Tosa Domain, he played a key role in bringing about the alliance between the rival Satsuma and Chōshū domains and in negotiating the shogunate's peaceful surrender of power, paving the way for the Meiji Restoration. He is revered in Japan as a national hero.

Early life

Ryōma was born in Kōchi, castle town of Tosa Domain on Shikoku, into a wealthy family of gōshi — rural, lower-ranked samurai who combined farming and trade. As a youth he was unpromising and bullied, but he excelled at swordsmanship, training in Edo at the famed Hokushin Ittō-ryū school. The arrival of Commodore Perry's Black Ships in 1853–54 awakened his interest in national defence and foreign affairs.

Flight from Tosa and Katsu Kaishū

Drawn into the loyalist movement to expel foreigners and restore imperial rule (sonnō jōi), Ryōma deserted Tosa in 1862 without permission — a crime punishable by death. In Edo he sought out Katsu Kaishū, the shogunate's naval commissioner, intending (by some accounts) to kill him as a collaborator with foreigners; instead, Katsu convinced him that Japan needed a modern navy and openness to survive. Ryōma became Katsu's disciple and worked at the Kobe Naval Training Center until the shogunate closed it in 1864.

The Satchō Alliance

Ryōma's greatest diplomatic achievement came in 1866, when he helped broker the Satchō Alliance between Satsuma and Chōshū — two powerful domains that had been bitter enemies. Acting through the Nagasaki trading firm Kameyama Shachū, he arranged for Satsuma to supply Chōshū with modern weapons at a time when the shogunate was preparing a second punitive expedition against Chōshū. The alliance united the two domains against the Tokugawa.

Kaientai and the eight-point plan

In 1867 Ryōma refounded his company as the Kaientai, a maritime trading and shipping concern that also served as a private navy. That year he drafted the celebrated "Eight-Point Plan" (Senchū Hassaku) while sailing with Tosa official Gotō Shōjirō: it called for the return of political power to the Imperial Court, a bicameral legislature, merit-based appointment of officials, new treaties based on international law, and modern military and legal codes. The plan influenced Tosa's memorial to the shogun, leading Tokugawa Yoshinobu to resign his powers in November 1867 (the Taisei Hōkan).

Assassination

On the night of 10 December 1867 (15th day of the 11th month by the lunar calendar), Ryōma and his associate Nakaoka Shintarō were attacked at the Ōmiya inn in Kyoto by a group of swordsmen, usually identified with the Kyoto Mimawarigumi, a pro-shogunate police force. Ryōma died that night, aged 31; Nakaoka died of his wounds two days later. Neither lived to see the Meiji Restoration proclaimed weeks afterward. The two are buried together at Ryōzen Gokoku Shrine in Kyoto.

Legacy

Ryōma became one of the most beloved figures of modern Japanese history, popularized by Shiba Ryōtarō's 1960s novel Ryōma Goes His Way. He is celebrated as a visionary who imagined a modern, democratic Japan; the 2010 NHK Taiga drama Ryōmaden renewed nationwide interest, and Kōchi Sakamoto Ryōma Memorial Museum stands on his home ground.
