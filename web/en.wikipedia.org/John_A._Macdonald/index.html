Sir John Alexander Macdonald (11 January 1815 – 6 June 1891) was the first prime minister of Canada, serving from 1867 to 1873 and again from 1878 until his death in 1891. The dominant figure of Canadian Confederation, he led the new Dominion through its first quarter-century, binding it together with a transcontinental railway and a policy of tariffs and western settlement.

Early life and Confederation

Born in Glasgow, Macdonald emigrated with his family to Kingston, Upper Canada, in 1820, became a successful lawyer, and entered politics in the Province of Canada. A pragmatic Conservative, he formed governing coalitions with George-Étienne Cartier and others, and at the Charlottetown and Quebec conferences of 1864 and the London Conference of 1866 he was a principal architect of the union of the British North American colonies, achieved on 1 July 1867.

Prime minister

Macdonald's first government created the North-West Mounted Police, negotiated the entry of Manitoba, British Columbia, and Prince Edward Island, and began the Canadian Pacific Railway. The Pacific Scandal of 1873—campaign donations from railway interests—drove him from office, but he returned in 1878 on the "National Policy" of protective tariffs, immigration, and western development; the railway was completed in 1885. His second tenure also encompassed the suppression of the North-West Rebellion and the execution of Louis Riel, a decision with lasting repercussions in Quebec.

Assessment

Macdonald is remembered as the indispensable founder of the Canadian state, but his record toward Indigenous peoples—including the Indian Act regime, residential schools, and the use of starvation policy on the Plains—has drawn severe modern criticism, and his statues have been removed from several public spaces.
