Neurofibroma

Cutaneous neurofibromas are benign tumours of the peripheral nerve sheath.

A neurofibroma is a benign nerve-sheath tumour in the peripheral nervous system. In 90% of cases, neurofibromas occur as stand-alone tumours, while the remainder are found in persons with neurofibromatosis type I (NF1), an autosomal dominant genetically inherited disease. Neurofibromas are composed of a mixture of Schwann cells, perineurial-like cells, fibroblasts, mast cells, and residual axons embedded in extracellular matrix.

Types

Clinicians distinguish cutaneous (dermal) neurofibromas, which arise in the skin and typically appear around puberty; subcutaneous neurofibromas, which lie deeper and can be tender; nodular neurofibromas arising on peripheral nerves; and plexiform neurofibromas, which involve multiple nerve fascicles, grow along the length of a nerve, and occur almost exclusively in NF1. Plexiform tumours may cause marked disfigurement and carry a lifetime risk of roughly 8–13% of transformation into a malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually clinical, supported by biopsy and imaging when lesions are deep or atypical. Solitary lesions are managed by surgical excision when symptomatic. In 2020 the MEK inhibitor selumetinib was approved in the United States for children with symptomatic, inoperable plexiform neurofibromas associated with NF1 — the first approved medical therapy for this indication.
