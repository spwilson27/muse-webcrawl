The goldfish (Carassius auratus) is a freshwater fish in the family Cyprinidae of the order Cypriniformes. It is commonly kept as a pet in indoor aquariums, and is one of the most popular aquarium fish. Goldfish were selectively bred for colour in imperial China, where they were first domesticated over a thousand years ago from the Prussian carp.

[ Image placeholder: An orange common goldfish in an aquarium ]

Native to East Asia, the goldfish was first introduced to Europe in the late 17th century. Contrary to a popular myth, goldfish have memories of at least several months and can learn to recognise people and respond to feeding cues. They typically grow to 15–30 cm and can live for decades with proper care; the oldest recorded goldfish lived to 43 years.

History

By the Tang dynasty (618–907), ornamental carp were kept in ponds in China, and gold-coloured mutant offspring were selectively maintained under the Song dynasty. Breeding spread during the Ming dynasty, when goldfish began to be kept in indoor bowls, and distinct varieties — with double tails, egg-shaped bodies, and telescope eyes — were developed in China and Japan.

Varieties

Over 200 varieties are recognised, including the common goldfish, comet, shubunkin, fantail, oranda, ranchu, ryukin, pearlscale, and the bubble eye. "Fancy" varieties with extreme body shapes often have compromised swimming and vision and require specialised care.

Biology and care

Goldfish are social, omnivorous fish that forage on plants, detritus, and small invertebrates. They produce substantial waste and require large, filtered volumes of water; the tradition of keeping them in small bowls is now widely regarded as harmful. Released goldfish can become invasive, growing large and disturbing ecosystems in North America, Australia, and elsewhere.
