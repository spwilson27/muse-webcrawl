Scottish Gaelic (endonym: Gàidhlig [ˈkaːlɪkʲ]) is a Goidelic language native to the Gaels of Scotland, developed out of Old Irish and brought to Scotland by settlers from Ireland from around the 4th–5th centuries AD. Once spoken across most of Scotland, it retreated to the Highlands and Islands under pressure from Scots and English, and is now classed as an endangered language, though revitalisation efforts have grown since the late 20th century.

History

Gaelic was the language of the kingdom of Alba and of the medieval Scottish court; its decline followed the reforms of David I, the Statutes of Iona (1609), and the Education (Scotland) Act 1872, which ignored it. Clearances and economic migration devastated its island heartlands. The 2022 census recorded modest growth in speakers, helped by Gaelic-medium education.

Features

Gaelic is a VSO language with initial consonant mutations (lenition), broad/slender consonant contrasts, prepositional pronouns, and a traditional alphabet of 18 letters in which letters are named after trees. It is closely related to Irish and Manx, the three descending from Middle Irish; Gaelic and Irish remain partly mutually intelligible, especially Ulster Irish.

Revival

The Gaelic Language (Scotland) Act 2005 gave Gaelic official status "commanding equal respect" with English and created Bòrd na Gàidhlig. Media include BBC Alba television and BBC Radio nan Gàidheal; cultural flagships include the Royal National Mòd festival and the fèisean movement.
