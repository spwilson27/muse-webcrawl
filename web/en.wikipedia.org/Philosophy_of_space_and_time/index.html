Philosophy of space and time

Philosophy of space and time
FieldsMetaphysics, philosophy of physics
Key debatesSubstantivalism vs relationalism; A-theory vs B-theory; conventionalism
Key figuresNewton, Leibniz, Kant, Mach, Einstein, Minkowski

The philosophy of space and time is the branch of philosophy concerned with the nature of space and time: whether they exist independently of the objects and events within them, whether time flows, and how the answers relate to physics. It stands at the intersection of metaphysics and the interpretation of physical theory.

Substantivalism and relationalism

Isaac Newton defended absolute space and time as a kind of container existing independently of matter, illustrating the reality of absolute rotation with his rotating-bucket argument. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, in his correspondence with Samuel Clarke (1715–16), countered that space is nothing but the order of coexisting objects and time the order of successive events—a relational view. Ernst Mach criticized Newton's absolute space in the nineteenth century, and Einstein's general theory of relativity, in which spacetime is a dynamic field interacting with matter, revived relationalist intuitions while also being read substantivally as making spacetime itself a substance-like entity.

The nature of time

Philosophers debate whether past, present, and future are equally real (eternalism), whether only the present exists (presentism), and whether temporal becoming is objective or perspectival. The A-theory treats tense as a real feature of the world; the B-theory treats time as a static four-dimensional order. McTaggart's 1908 argument for the unreality of time remains a landmark. Relativity's denial of absolute simultaneity is often taken to support eternalism and to complicate free will and persistence through time.

Other topics

The field also addresses the arrow of time and entropy, time travel and its paradoxes, the discreteness or continuity of space and time (Zeno's paradoxes, quantum gravity), and Kant's claim that space and time are forms of human intuition rather than things in themselves.
