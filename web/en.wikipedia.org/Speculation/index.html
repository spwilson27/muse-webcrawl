Speculation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In finance, speculation is the purchase of an asset (a commodity, goods, or real estate) with the hope that it will become more valuable in a brief amount of time. In finance, speculation involves taking market risk, particularly speculating that the price of an asset will rise, in a transaction not supported by the asset's intrinsic value. Many speculators pay little attention to the fundamental value of a security and instead focus purely on price movements. In principle, speculation can involve any tradable good or financial instrument.

Speculation versus investment

Speculators are often distinguished from investors by their shorter time horizons, greater use of leverage, and willingness to assume risk in exchange for potentially high returns. The boundary is contested: Benjamin Graham defined an investment operation as one which, upon thorough analysis, promises safety of principal and an adequate return, and called operations not meeting those requirements speculative. Hedgers, by contrast, use the same markets to reduce risk rather than to seek profit from price changes.

Economic role

Defenders argue that speculators provide liquidity, bear risk that others wish to avoid, and help prices reflect information, aiding price discovery. Critics argue that speculation can destabilize markets, inflate asset price bubbles — from tulip mania to housing bubbles — and divert resources from productive activity. Economist John Maynard Keynes famously compared professional investment to a beauty contest in which participants guess others' guesses.

Regulation

Speculative activity is regulated in various ways, including position limits in commodity futures, margin requirements, short-selling rules, and taxes proposed on financial transactions. Historical episodes such as the Hunt brothers' attempt to corner the silver market in 1980 illustrate the risks of concentrated speculation.
