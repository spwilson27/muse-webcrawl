Billboard Hot 100

The Billboard Hot 100 is the music industry standard record chart in the United States for songs, published weekly by Billboard magazine. Chart rankings are based on sales (physical and digital), radio play, and online streaming in the United States. The first number-one song of the Hot 100 was "Poor Little Fool" by Ricky Nelson, on the chart dated August 4, 1958.

History

Before 1958, Billboard published several separate popularity charts, including Best Sellers in Stores, Most Played by Jockeys, and Most Played in Juke Boxes. The Hot 100 combined these measures into a single all-genre singles chart. Its methodology has evolved repeatedly: SoundScan point-of-sale data replaced retailer reports in 1991, ending an era in which chart positions could be swayed by label promotion, and digital downloads were incorporated from 2005. Streaming data from services such as Spotify and YouTube was added in the following decade, fundamentally changing which songs reach the top.

Methodology

The chart is compiled from US sales, radio audience impressions, and streams, weighted according to Billboard's formulas and tracked by Luminate (formerly Nielsen Music/MRC Data). The tracking week for sales and streaming runs Friday through Thursday, while radio airplay is measured Monday through Sunday. Charts are typically announced on Monday or Tuesday following the tracking period, dated to the Saturday of the following week.

Records

The Beatles hold the record for the most number-one singles by an act, with 20, and also once occupied the entire top five simultaneously in 1964. Mariah Carey has the most cumulative weeks at number one for a solo artist. Lil Nas X's "Old Town Road" holds the record for the most weeks at number one for a single song, spending 19 weeks atop the chart in 2019. Reaching number one remains a widely reported milestone of commercial success in American popular music.

Criticism

The chart's formulas — particularly the weight given to radio, the treatment of song bundles and remixes, and the influence of streaming "farming" by devoted fan bases — are regularly debated. Rule changes in 2020 ended the practice of counting merchandise-and-ticket bundles toward chart sales.
