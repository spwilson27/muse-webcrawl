The Y chromosome is one of two sex chromosomes in therian mammals and some other organisms. In humans, cells of typical males carry one X and one Y chromosome (XY), while typical females carry two X chromosomes (XX). The Y chromosome carries the SRY gene, which triggers male embryonic development, and is passed almost unchanged from father to son.

Structure

The human Y is one of the smallest chromosomes, spanning about 57–62 million base pairs. Most of its length — the male-specific region (MSY) — does not recombine with the X chromosome; only the small pseudoautosomal regions PAR1 and PAR2 pair and recombine during meiosis. The MSY is rich in repetitive sequences and palindromes that allow gene conversion between its own arms.

Evolution and degeneration

The X and Y evolved from an ordinary pair of autosomes roughly 200–300 million years ago. Once recombination was suppressed around the sex-determining locus, the Y lost most of its genes through Muller's ratchet and genetic drift; the human Y retains only a few dozen of the thousand-plus genes on the X. Predictions of its eventual complete loss remain contested, and some rodents have indeed lost the Y entirely.

Genetic genealogy

Because the non-recombining region passes patrilineally with only mutational change, Y-chromosome haplogroups are used to trace paternal ancestry; all living men's Y chromosomes coalesce to a common ancestor dubbed "Y-chromosomal Adam", estimated to have lived roughly 200,000–300,000 years ago.

Clinical relevance

Variations include XXY (Klinefelter syndrome), XYY, Y-chromosome microdeletions causing infertility, and mosaic loss of Y in ageing men's blood cells, which is associated with several diseases.
