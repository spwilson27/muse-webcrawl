Armenian genocide recognition

Armenian genocide recognition is the formal acknowledgement by governments, parliaments and international bodies that the massacres and deportations of Armenians in the Ottoman Empire during and after the First World War constituted genocide. The overwhelming consensus of historians and genocide scholars is that the events were a genocide; the International Association of Genocide Scholars affirmed this in resolutions from 1997 onward. The government of Turkey, by contrast, maintains the official position that the deaths resulted from wartime conditions, civil strife and famine, and rejects the term genocide.

History

Contemporary Allied governments condemned the massacres in 1915 as "crimes against humanity and civilization". Ottoman courts-martial in 1919–1920 convicted several perpetrators. Systematic international recognition began decades later: Uruguay was the first country to recognize the genocide, in 1965, followed over the years by Cyprus, Russia, Canada, France (by law in 2001), Germany (Bundestag resolution, 2016) and many others. As of the mid-2020s, more than thirty countries have officially recognized the genocide.

United States

After decades in which administrations avoided the term, the United States Congress recognized the genocide in 2019 (House and Senate resolutions), and on 24 April 2021 President Joe Biden formally recognized the Armenian genocide in his annual remembrance statement — the first US president to do so since Ronald Reagan's passing reference in 1981.

Other recognition

Pope Francis described the events as "the first genocide of the 20th century" in 2015. The European Parliament has repeatedly called for recognition, and many sub-national legislatures — including most US states — have adopted recognition measures. Recognition remains a major factor in Turkey's relations with Armenia and with several Western states, and Turkey has typically responded to foreign recognition votes with diplomatic protests.
