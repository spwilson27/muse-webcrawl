Perkin reaction

Type: Condensation reaction
Named after: William Henry Perkin
Discovered: 1868
Product class: α,β-Unsaturated aromatic acids

The Perkin reaction is an organic reaction developed by the English chemist William Henry Perkin in 1868 for making cinnamic acids. It gives an α,β-unsaturated aromatic acid by the aldol-type condensation of an aromatic aldehyde and an acid anhydride, in the presence of an alkali salt of the acid, which acts as a base catalyst. The classic example is the synthesis of cinnamic acid from benzaldehyde and acetic anhydride with sodium acetate.

Mechanism

Under basic conditions the anhydride forms an enolate at its α-carbon. The enolate attacks the carbonyl carbon of the aromatic aldehyde, giving an alkoxide intermediate that is acetylated and then undergoes elimination and hydrolysis on work-up to yield the unsaturated acid, predominantly as the E (trans) isomer. Aromatic aldehydes without α-hydrogens are used so that the aldehyde cannot undergo self-condensation instead.

Applications and variants

The reaction was historically important for the synthesis of cinnamic acid, coumarins, and perfume and flavour compounds, and the related Perkin condensation with succinic anhydride underlies variants such as the Stobbe condensation. Yields can be modest and the reaction requires vigorous conditions, so it has largely been superseded in laboratories by milder methods, though it remains a textbook named reaction and is still used industrially in some processes.
