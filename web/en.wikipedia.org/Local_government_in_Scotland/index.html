Local government in Scotland

Local government in Scotland is organised through 32 unitary council areas, each administered by a local council elected by proportional representation. The councils are responsible for a wide range of local services, while the Scottish Parliament and Scottish Government — established by devolution in 1999 — hold legislative and policy responsibility for local government itself.

Structure

The present single-tier structure was created by the Local Government etc. (Scotland) Act 1994 and took effect on 1 April 1996, replacing the two-tier system of regions and districts introduced in 1975. The 32 council areas vary enormously in size and population, from Glasgow City, the largest, to the island councils of Orkney, Shetland and the Western Isles (Comhairle nan Eilean Siar).

Councillors are elected every five years using the single transferable vote in multi-member wards, a system first used in 2007. Each council elects a leader and, in most cases, governs through a cabinet-style executive or committee system. Below council level, community councils provide a statutory tier of representation in many areas, though their powers are chiefly consultative.

Functions and finance

Councils provide services including education, social care, housing, planning, roads and transport, waste collection, libraries, leisure services and environmental health. Police and fire services, once locally run, were merged into national services (Police Scotland and the Scottish Fire and Rescue Service) in 2013.

Funding comes mainly from the Scottish Government block grant and from council tax on domestic properties; councils also levy non-domestic (business) rates, though the rate is set nationally. The balance between central funding and local taxation has been a recurring political issue, including a long council tax freeze in the 2000s and 2010s.
