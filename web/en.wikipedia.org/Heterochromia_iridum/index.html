Heterochromia iridum

SpecialtyOphthalmology
TypesComplete, sectoral, central
CausesGenetic, chimerism, injury, disease, medication
Complete heterochromia: one brown and one blue iris

Heterochromia iridum is a difference in coloration of the iris. It may be complete, in which one iris is a different colour from the other; sectoral, in which part of one iris differs from the rest; or central, in which a ring around the pupil is a different colour from the outer iris.

Causes

Eye colour is determined by the concentration and distribution of melanin in the iris. Congenital heterochromia is usually inherited as an autosomal dominant trait and is harmless, though it can occasionally be a feature of syndromes such as Waardenburg syndrome, Sturge–Weber syndrome or Horner's syndrome. Acquired heterochromia may result from injury, inflammation, tumours, or medications such as prostaglandin-analogue eye drops used for glaucoma. A mosaic genetic condition called chimerism can also produce the trait.

In animals and culture

Heterochromia is common in certain animal breeds, including Siberian Huskies, Australian Shepherds, Turkish Van cats and Japanese Bobtails; "odd-eyed" white cats are particularly noted. Famous people reported with the condition include the actor Kate Bosworth (sectoral) and, reputedly, Alexander the Great. The condition — with differing eye colours in one individual — is generally benign, but new-onset heterochromia in adults warrants medical examination to exclude underlying disease.
