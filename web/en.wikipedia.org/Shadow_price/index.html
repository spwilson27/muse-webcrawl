Shadow price

Shadow price of a binding constraint

A shadow price is the monetary value assigned to an abstract good, commodity, or constraint for which no actual market price exists or can easily be assigned. In mathematical optimisation, the shadow price of a constraint is the rate at which the optimal value of the objective function changes when the constraint is relaxed by one unit — formally, the corresponding Lagrange multiplier, or equivalently the optimal value of the associated variable in the dual problem.

In linear programming

In a linear programming problem, the shadow price of a resource constraint tells the decision-maker how much profit would increase if one additional unit of that resource were available. It is valid only within the range over which the optimal basis does not change (the "allowable increase" and "allowable decrease" of sensitivity analysis). A constraint that is not binding at the optimum has a shadow price of zero.

In economics

Economists use shadow prices in cost–benefit analysis and welfare economics to value goods not traded in markets — clean air, time savings, statistical lives, or carbon emissions. The "shadow price of carbon," for example, represents the marginal social damage of an additional tonne of CO₂. In the theory of planned economies, shadow prices (such as those in Kantorovich's work and in Lange–Lerner models) served as accounting prices intended to mimic the allocative function of market prices. The concept is closely related to opportunity cost: the shadow price of a resource is its value in its best alternative use at the optimum.
