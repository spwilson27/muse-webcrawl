Stirling Castle

Stirling Castle, located in Stirling, is one of the largest and most historically and architecturally important castles in Scotland. The castle sits atop Castle Hill, an intrusive crag of volcanic rock, and is surrounded on three sides by steep cliffs, giving it a strong defensive position. Its strategic location, guarding the crossing of the River Forth, made it a key royal residence and a focal point of the Wars of Scottish Independence.

History

A castle has stood on the site since at least the early 12th century. Stirling changed hands repeatedly during the Wars of Scottish Independence (1296–1328): Edward I of England besieged it in 1304, and its relief was the objective of the Scottish army that won the Battle of Bannockburn in 1314. In the 15th and 16th centuries the Stewart kings transformed it into a Renaissance palace. James IV built the Great Hall and the King's Old Building; James V constructed the lavish Palace block; and James VI's Chapel Royal was built in 1594 for the baptism of his son Prince Henry. Mary, Queen of Scots, was crowned in the castle in 1543 and spent much of her childhood there.

Buildings and features

The castle's principal survivals include the Forework defences, the Great Hall — the largest banqueting hall ever built in Scotland — the Palace of James V with its richly carved Stirling Heads ceiling roundels, and the Chapel Royal. Extensive restoration completed in 2011 recreated the Palace interiors as they may have appeared in the 1540s. The Argyll and Sutherland Highlanders regimental museum is also housed within the walls.

Today

Stirling Castle is cared for by Historic Environment Scotland and is one of Scotland's most popular visitor attractions. The surrounding views take in the Wallace Monument and the sites of the battles of Stirling Bridge (1297) and Bannockburn (1314).
