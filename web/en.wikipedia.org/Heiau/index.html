Heiau

TypeHawaiian temple / place of worship
RegionHawaiian Islands
Major typesLuakini (state/sacrificial), healing, agricultural, fishing shrines
Notable examplePuʻukoholā Heiau, built by Kamehameha I (1790–91)

A heiau is a Hawaiian temple or place of worship. Heiau ranged from small family shrines to enormous stone platforms built by kings, and they were dedicated to purposes including religion, healing, agriculture, fishing, surfing and war.

Structure

Heiau were typically constructed as terraces, platforms or enclosures of dry-laid stone, sometimes with wooden structures, carved images (kiʻi) and an oracle tower (ʻanuʻu) covered in kapa cloth. Offerings of food, animals and — at the largest state temples — human sacrifices were made at the direction of priests (kāhuna) and chiefs (aliʻi).

Types and sites

The most imposing class was the luakini heiau, built by a ruling chief in times of war or major political undertaking and dedicated to the god Kū. Puʻukoholā Heiau on Hawaiʻi Island, built by Kamehameha I in 1790–91 to fulfil a prophecy that he would unite the islands, is among the largest restored heiau and is now a National Historic Site. Piʻilanihale Heiau on Maui is the largest in Polynesia. Following the abolition of the kapu system in 1819, many heiau were abandoned or destroyed, though numerous sites survive and some are again used for Native Hawaiian cultural practice.
