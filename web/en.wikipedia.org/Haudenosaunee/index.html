The Haudenosaunee ("People of the Long House"), historically known as the Iroquois Confederacy or the Five Nations (later Six Nations), are an Iroquoian-speaking confederacy of Native Americans and First Nations peoples in northeast North America. The member nations are the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca, joined by the Tuscarora in 1722.

The Great Law of Peace

According to oral tradition, the confederacy was founded by the Great Peacemaker (Deganawida), aided by the Onondaga leader Hiawatha and the Seneca prophetess Jigonhsasee, who persuaded warring nations to bury their weapons beneath a great white pine and unite under the Great Law of Peace. Its council of fifty chiefs (sachems), nominated by clan mothers of matrilineal clans, met at Onondaga, where the central council fire is kept. Condolence ceremonies, wampum records, and consensus decision-making bound the league together; its founding date is debated, with proposals ranging from 1142 (linked by some to a solar eclipse) to the late 16th century.

History

By the 17th century the confederacy, armed through the Dutch and English fur trade, dominated the interior Northeast, dispersing the Huron in the Beaver Wars and balancing French and English rivals through the Covenant Chain diplomacy. The league split during the American Revolution—most nations sided with Britain—and the Sullivan Expedition of 1779 devastated its towns; many Haudenosaunee relocated to Canada (Six Nations of the Grand River) while others remained on reservations in New York. In the 20th and 21st centuries Haudenosaunee governments have asserted sovereignty in land claims, cross-border rights under the Jay Treaty, and lacrosse—the confederacy fields its own national team in the game its peoples created.

Influence debate

Some scholars argue the confederacy's federal structure influenced the United States Constitution—an idea acknowledged in a 1988 Senate resolution—and others regard the influence as overstated; the debate itself illustrates the league's enduring political significance.
