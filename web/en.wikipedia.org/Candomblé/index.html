Candomblé

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Candomblé (Portuguese pronunciation: [kɐ̃dõˈblɛ]) is an Afro-Brazilian religion that developed in Brazil during the 19th century among enslaved West Africans transported to the colony. It centres on the veneration of deities known as orixás (in the Ketu tradition), voduns (Jeje tradition), or inkices (Bantu/Angola tradition), who embody forces of nature and serve as intermediaries with the supreme creator, called Olorum, Mawu, or Zambi depending on the nation.

History

Candomblé coalesced in Salvador, Bahia, where large numbers of Yoruba, Fon, and Bantu people were enslaved. Because Portuguese colonial authorities required enslaved Africans to be baptized as Roman Catholics, practitioners often associated their deities with Catholic saints, a syncretism that helped the religion survive periods of persecution. Famous temples (terreiros) in Salvador include the Casa Branca do Engenho Velho (Ilê Axé Iyá Nassô Oká), regarded as one of the oldest, and the Gantois temple founded by Mãe Menininha.

For much of Brazil's history Candomblé was repressed, and terreiros required police permits to hold ceremonies well into the 20th century. Legal restrictions were lifted in Bahia in 1976, and the religion is now a recognized and culturally prominent part of Brazilian life, though practitioners still face episodes of religious intolerance.

Beliefs and practice

Worship takes place in terreiros led by a priestess (mãe de santo, or ialorixá) or priest (pai de santo, or babalorixá). Ceremonies involve drumming, song in liturgical Yoruba or other African languages, dance, offerings of food, and ritual states in which initiates are believed to be mounted by their orixá. Initiation involves seclusion, instruction, and obligations observed over years. Axé refers to the sacred life force accumulated and transmitted through ritual.

The best-known orixás include Exu, the messenger; Ogum, of iron and war; Oxóssi, of the hunt; Xangô, of thunder; Iansã (Oiá), of winds and storms; Oxum, of fresh waters and love; Iemanjá, of the sea; and Oxalá, the creator deity associated with peace and whiteness.
