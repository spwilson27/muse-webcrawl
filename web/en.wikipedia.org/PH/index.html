In chemistry, pH is a scale used to specify the acidity or basicity of an aqueous solution. It is defined as the negative base-10 logarithm of the activity of the hydrogen ion (H+, more precisely the hydronium ion H3O+):

pH = −log10 [H+]

Acidic solutions have lower pH values and basic (alkaline) solutions higher ones. At 25 °C, pure water has a pH of 7 and is neutral. Each whole pH value below 7 is ten times more acidic than the next higher value, so a solution of pH 3 is one hundred times more acidic than one of pH 5.

History

The concept was introduced in 1909 by the Danish chemist Søren Peder Lauritz Sørensen at the Carlsberg Laboratory, and revised to the modern activity-based definition in 1924. The letters pH are commonly explained as standing for "power of hydrogen", though Sørensen's original notation used p and q for the two electrodes in his measurements.

Measurement

pH can be estimated with indicator papers and dyes that change colour — such as litmus, phenolphthalein and universal indicator — or measured precisely with a pH meter, which uses a glass electrode sensitive to hydrogen-ion activity, calibrated against standard buffer solutions. In the human body, blood pH is tightly regulated around 7.35–7.45; deviations cause acidosis or alkalosis.

Examples

  • Gastric acid: about 1.5–2
  • Lemon juice: about 2
  • Black coffee: about 5
  • Pure water: 7
  • Seawater: about 8.1
  • Household ammonia: about 11
  • Bleach: about 12.5
