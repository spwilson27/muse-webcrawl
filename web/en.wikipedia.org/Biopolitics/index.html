Biopolitics

Biopolitics is a concept in political philosophy and social theory concerning the ways in which political power governs human life itself — populations, bodies, health, reproduction, and mortality. The term was given its influential modern sense by the French philosopher Michel Foucault in the 1970s, who used it to describe a form of power, biopower, that manages populations through statistics, public health, and regulation rather than through the threat of death alone.

Foucault's account

In The History of Sexuality (1976) and his lecture courses Society Must Be Defended and Security, Territory, Population, Foucault contrasted older sovereign power — the right "to take life or let live" — with modern biopower, which aims "to make live and let die". He distinguished two poles: an anatomo-politics disciplining individual bodies (in schools, barracks, hospitals), and a biopolitics of the population, which treats births, deaths, illness, and longevity as political and economic variables to be measured and optimised. On this account, biopolitics emerges in the 18th century alongside statistics, demography, and public hygiene.

Later developments

Giorgio Agamben extended the concept in Homo Sacer (1995), arguing that the production of "bare life" — life stripped of political rights, exemplified by the camp — is the hidden foundation of Western politics. Antonio Negri and Michael Hardt used biopolitics to describe how contemporary capitalism produces social life itself. Roberto Esposito developed the theme through the notion of immunity. The concept has been applied to debates over eugenics, vaccination mandates, reproductive rights, pandemic governance, genetic screening, and border and refugee regimes.

Earlier uses

The word appeared earlier in the 20th century in organicist political theories — for example in the Swedish political scientist Rudolf Kjellén — where it described the state as a living organism; some of these early uses were associated with racial and nationalist doctrines, a history distinct from, though noted by, later theorists.

Criticism

Critics charge that biopolitics is an overly elastic concept that can describe almost any modern governance, diluting its explanatory force, and that Foucault understated the persistence of violent sovereign power, colonialism, and racism — debates taken up by postcolonial theorists such as Achille Mbembe, whose concept of "necropolitics" inverts the emphasis onto the power to expose populations to death.
