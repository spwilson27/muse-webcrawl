Suppressed evidence

Suppressed evidence in law has two principal meanings. In argumentation, it refers to evidence that is deliberately withheld or concealed because it would weaken a position. In criminal procedure in the United States, it refers to evidence that the prosecution fails to disclose to the defence, or evidence that a court rules inadmissible at trial.

Prosecutorial disclosure

Under Brady v. Maryland (1963), the US Supreme Court held that suppression by the prosecution of evidence favourable to the accused violates due process where the evidence is material to guilt or punishment. The related Giglio rule covers evidence affecting witness credibility. Undisclosed exculpatory evidence discovered after conviction can lead to reversal or a new trial.

Exclusion at trial

Evidence may also be "suppressed" when a court grants a motion to suppress, excluding it from trial — most commonly under the exclusionary rule of the Fourth Amendment for illegally obtained evidence, or the Fifth Amendment's protections against self-incrimination, following cases such as Mapp v. Ohio and Miranda v. Arizona.
