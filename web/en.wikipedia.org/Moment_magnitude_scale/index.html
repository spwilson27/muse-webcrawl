Moment magnitude scale

The moment magnitude scale (denoted Mw) is a measure of an earthquake's magnitude (the size of the earthquake) based on its seismic moment. It was defined in a 1979 paper by Thomas C. Hanks and Hiroo Kanamori. Similar to the local magnitude/Richter scale (ML) defined by Charles Francis Richter in 1935, it uses a logarithmic scale; small earthquakes have approximately the same magnitudes on both scales.

Moment magnitude is the most common scale on which earthquakes larger than approximately magnitude 5 are reported by organizations such as the United States Geological Survey. The moment magnitude scale is considered the authoritative magnitude scale for ranking earthquakes by size, because it is based on the physical properties of the fault rupture and does not saturate for the largest events.

Definition

The seismic moment M0 is proportional to the area of ground that slips and the average amount of slip, together with the rigidity of the rock. The moment magnitude is defined as:

Mw = (2/3) log10(M0) − 10.7

where M0 is the seismic moment in dyne-centimetres. An increase of one unit of magnitude corresponds to an increase of about 31.6 times in the energy released.

History and advantages

The Richter scale and other local magnitude scales saturate for large earthquakes: their values stop increasing proportionally once the rupture becomes larger than the wavelength of the seismic waves used to measure them. Kanamori proposed in 1977, and Hanks and Kanamori in 1979, a magnitude based instead on seismic moment, which is measured from the long-period portion of the seismogram or from the total deformation caused by the earthquake. Because seismic moment directly reflects the fault area, slip, and rock rigidity, the scale remains consistent from the smallest recorded events to the greatest earthquake ever measured, the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile at Mw 9.5.

Energy

The energy release of an earthquake approximates to an increase by a factor of about 31.6 for each whole-number increase in magnitude. The 2011 Tōhoku earthquake in Japan, at Mw 9.1, released roughly twice the energy of the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake at Mw 9.1–9.3 estimates aside, and about a thousand times the energy of a magnitude 7 event.
