Reciprocity (social psychology)

The norm of reciprocity creates an obligation to return favours, gifts and concessions.

In social psychology and sociology, reciprocity is a social norm of responding to a positive action with another positive action, rewarding kind actions and punishing unkind ones. The norm of reciprocity, identified as a near-universal of human societies by the sociologist Alvin Gouldner in 1960, holds that people ought to help, and not injure, those who have helped them. It underlies gift exchange, trade, diplomacy and much everyday cooperation.

Forms of reciprocity

The anthropologist Marshall Sahlins distinguished three forms along a spectrum of social distance. Generalised reciprocity, typical within families, involves giving without expectation of an immediate or specified return. Balanced reciprocity involves direct exchange of equivalent value within a set time frame, as in gift-giving between friends. Negative reciprocity is the attempt to get something for nothing — haggling, barter between strangers, or outright exploitation — and shades into conduct a society regards as theft.

Experimental findings

Laboratory research confirms that unsolicited favours reliably increase compliance. In a classic 1971 study by Dennis Regan, participants who received a soft drink from a confederate later bought roughly twice as many raffle tickets from him as those who had received nothing, even though the favour was small and unrequested. Reciprocity also operates in negotiation through mutual concessions: the "door-in-the-face" technique works because a retreat from a large request to a smaller one is perceived as a concession that obliges the other party to concede in turn.

Reciprocal altruism

In evolutionary biology, Robert Trivers's theory of reciprocal altruism (1971) proposed that helping non-relatives can evolve when the favour is likely to be returned, explaining grooming exchange in primates and food sharing in vampire bats. Psychologists link the norm to emotions of gratitude and indebtedness, and to anger at those who take without giving — the "free rider" problem studied in public goods games.

Applications

Marketers exploit reciprocity through free samples and gifts; fundraisers enclose small gifts such as address labels in appeals, measurably increasing donations. Because the sense of obligation can be triggered by trivial or unwanted gifts, ethics codes for lobbyists and physicians restrict the favours they may accept.
