In phonology and linguistics, a phoneme is a unit of sound that can distinguish one word from another in a particular language. If swapping one sound for another changes a word's meaning, the two sounds belong to different phonemes in that language. Phonemes are abstract mental categories rather than the physical sounds themselves; the actual sounds produced are called phones, and the systematic variants of a single phoneme are its allophones.

Notation and minimal pairs

Phonemes are conventionally written between slashes using the International Phonetic Alphabet, as in /p/, while phones are written in square brackets, as in [pʰ]. The standard test for phoneme status is the minimal pair: two words differing by only one sound in the same position. English pat and bat show that /p/ and /b/ are separate phonemes, whereas the aspirated [pʰ] in pin and the unaspirated [p] in spin never contrast, so both are allophones of /p/.

Phoneme inventories

Languages differ greatly in the size of their phoneme inventories. Rotokas of New Guinea has as few as 11 phonemes, while the Taa language (!Xóõ) of Botswana has over 100, counting its click consonants. English has roughly 40–46 phonemes depending on dialect and analysis. The set of phonemes in a language, and the rules governing how they combine, form part of its phonology.

History

The concept developed in the late 19th century in the work of Jan Baudouin de Courtenay and Mikołaj Kruszewski, and was central to the Prague School of Nikolai Trubetzkoy and to Leonard Bloomfield's American structuralism. Later theories — including generative phonology, which treats a phoneme-like level of "underlying representation", and exemplar models, which question discrete categories — have reinterpreted the concept while retaining its practical utility in transcription, language teaching, and literacy design.

Relation to graphemes

Alphabetic writing systems approximate phonemes with letters (graphemes), but the correspondence is imperfect: English uses the digraph sh for the single phoneme /ʃ/, and the letter c serves for both /k/ and /s/. Writing systems with shallow orthographies, such as Finnish or Spanish, map letters to phonemes much more consistently.
