Binational state

A binational state is a political state that comprises two distinct nations, ethnic groups, or national communities within a single sovereign framework. Unlike a multinational state with many constituent groups, a binational state is structured around a partnership — formal or informal — between exactly two communities, which may share power through federalism, consociational arrangements, or official bilingualism and dual symbolism.

Characteristics

Binational states typically recognise two official languages, two founding peoples, or two constituent units. Power-sharing may be guaranteed constitutionally, as in Belgium's arrangements between Dutch-speaking Flanders and French-speaking Wallonia, or embodied in peace agreements. The concept overlaps with, but is narrower than, the multinational state: Canada is often described as binational in its "two founding peoples" (English and French) tradition, although Indigenous nations complicate that framing.

Examples and proposals

Historical and present examples discussed in the literature include Belgium, Czechoslovakia (a binational federation of Czechs and Slovaks until its peaceful dissolution in 1993), and the former union of Serbia and Montenegro. The term is most frequently encountered in debate over the Israeli–Palestinian conflict, where a "binational state" or one-state solution would unite Israel, the West Bank, and the Gaza Strip into a single state shared by Jews and Palestinians. A similar solution was advocated before 1948 by groups such as Brit Shalom and figures including Judah Magnes and Martin Buber. Binational arrangements have also been proposed for Cyprus and discussed in relation to Bosnia and Herzegovina.

Arguments

Supporters argue that binational states can end partition conflicts, guarantee equal citizenship, and reflect demographic intermixture that makes separation impractical. Critics respond that deep national divisions make shared institutions unstable — pointing to the breakup of Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia — and that each community may fear permanent minority status. Political scientists note that successful cases tend to combine territorial federalism with strong minority protections and external guarantees.
