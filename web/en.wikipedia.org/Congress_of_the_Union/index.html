The Congress of the Union (Spanish: Congreso de la Unión), formally the General Congress of the United Mexican States, is the legislature of the federal government of Mexico. It consists of two chambers: the Senate of the Republic and the Chamber of Deputies. It meets in Mexico City.


The Legislative Palace of San Lázaro, seat of the Chamber of Deputies.

Composition

Senate

The Senate has 128 members serving six-year terms. Each of Mexico's 31 states and Mexico City elects three senators — two for the winning ticket and one for the first minority — and 32 are allocated by nationwide proportional representation from party lists.

Chamber of Deputies

The Chamber of Deputies has 500 members serving three-year terms: 300 are elected by plurality in single-member districts, and 200 by proportional representation in five regional constituencies. Constitutional reforms in 2014 permitted consecutive re-election of legislators (deputies up to four terms, senators up to two), ending a no-re-election rule that dated to the Mexican Revolution; the first re-elections under the new system occurred in 2021.

Powers

Under the Constitution of 1917, Congress enacts federal laws, approves the federal budget (a power in which the Chamber of Deputies has the leading role), declares war, approves treaties through the Senate, ratifies certain presidential appointments, and can amend the Constitution jointly with a majority of state legislatures. Each chamber has exclusive powers: the Senate over foreign policy and senior appointments, the Chamber of Deputies over revenue and budget bills and presidential impeachment accusations.

Sessions

Ordinary sessions run from 1 September to 15 December and from 1 February to 30 April. During recesses a Permanent Commission of 37 members (19 deputies and 18 senators) handles urgent business and may call extraordinary sessions.

History

Mexican congresses date to the independence era and the Constitution of 1824. For much of the 20th century Congress was dominated by the Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI) and largely subordinate to the presidency; opposition gains from 1988 and the loss of the PRI's lower-house majority in 1997 made it a genuinely independent power.
