Free trade area

A free trade area is a region encompassing a trade bloc whose member countries have signed a free trade agreement (FTA). Such agreements involve cooperation between at least two countries to reduce trade barriers — import quotas and tariffs — and to increase trade of goods and services with each other. Unlike a customs union, members of a free trade area retain their own external tariffs against non-members, and therefore use rules of origin to prevent trade deflection through the member with the lowest external tariff.

Stages of integration

Economists such as Béla Balassa described stages of economic integration: a preferential trade area, a free trade area, a customs union, a common market, and an economic and monetary union. A free trade area is the second, relatively shallow stage: internal tariffs are abolished on substantially all trade, but fiscal and monetary policies remain national.

Examples

Well-known free trade areas include the United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA, successor to NAFTA), the European Free Trade Association (EFTA), the ASEAN Free Trade Area (AFTA), the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA, launched in 2018), and the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP). Bilateral FTAs, such as those between the European Union and individual partners, create similar arrangements between two parties.

Effects

Jacob Viner's classic analysis distinguishes trade creation (shifting purchases to more efficient producers within the bloc, raising welfare) from trade diversion (shifting purchases from more efficient outside producers to less efficient members because of preferential tariffs, lowering welfare). Modern agreements often also cover services, investment, intellectual property, and regulatory cooperation.
