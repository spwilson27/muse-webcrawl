Reflecting telescope

A reflecting telescope (also called a reflector) is a telescope that uses a single or a combination of curved mirrors that reflect light and form an image. It was invented in the 17th century by Isaac Newton as an alternative to the refracting telescope, which suffered from severe chromatic aberration.

Designs

In the Newtonian design, a paraboloidal primary mirror at the back of the tube reflects light to a flat secondary mirror that diverts it to an eyepiece at the side. The Cassegrain design uses a convex secondary to reflect light back through a hole in the primary. Large research telescopes — including the Hubble Space Telescope and the Keck telescopes — are reflectors, since mirrors can be made far larger and lighter than lenses and introduce no chromatic aberration.

History

Newton built his first reflector in 1668 with a 1.3-inch (33 mm) mirror. William Herschel later built reflectors up to 49.5 inches, discovering Uranus with a smaller one in 1781.
