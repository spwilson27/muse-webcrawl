Twin paradox

In physics, the twin paradox is a thought experiment in special relativity involving twins, one of whom takes a journey into space in a high-speed rocket and returns home to find that the twin who remained on Earth has aged more. This result appears puzzling because each twin sees the other twin as moving, and so, by a naive application of time dilation, each should paradoxically find the other to have aged less.

Resolution

The apparent paradox is resolved by noting that the situation is not symmetric: the traveling twin undergoes acceleration when the rocket turns around to return, changing inertial frames, while the stay-at-home twin remains in a single inertial frame. The traveling twin's world line through spacetime is shorter in proper time than the Earth twin's, and proper time along a world line is an invariant: both twins agree on the final ages when they reunite. During the turnaround, the traveling twin's planes of simultaneity sweep rapidly across the Earth twin's world line, accounting for the "missing" aging.

History

The scenario was discussed by Albert Einstein in 1905 in terms of clocks, and Paul Langevin gave the twin version in 1911. The effect is real and has been confirmed experimentally: atomic clocks flown around the world in the Hafele–Keating experiment of 1971 aged differently from clocks left on the ground, and the effect — combined with gravitational time dilation — is routinely accounted for in the Global Positioning System. A real-life approximation occurred with astronaut Scott Kelly, who spent nearly a year aboard the International Space Station while his identical twin Mark Kelly remained on Earth; Scott aged a few milliseconds less than he otherwise would have.
