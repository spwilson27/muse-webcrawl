Sandbox game

A sandbox game is a video game, or a mode within a game, characterised by open-ended play: the player is given tools and a simulated environment and is free to explore, experiment and create with few prescribed goals or restrictions. The name derives from the children's sandbox, in which play is unstructured and imaginative rather than rule-bound. Sandbox elements overlap with, but are distinct from, the broader category of open-world games.

Characteristics

Sandbox games emphasise player agency and emergent behaviour. Typical features include an interactive, often physics-simulated world; building and crafting systems; nonlinear or entirely absent objectives; and game systems whose simple rules combine to produce unscripted outcomes. Rather than progressing through a fixed sequence of challenges, players set their own goals — constructing elaborate structures, engineering machines, or simply experimenting with the simulation.

History and examples

Early antecedents include the space trading game Elite (1984) and city-building simulations such as SimCity (1989). The term "sandbox" became widespread in the 2000s, applied both to open-world action games like the Grand Theft Auto series and to pure creation games. The genre's best-known exemplar is Minecraft (2011), which couples survival systems with near-unlimited block-based construction and is among the best-selling video games of all time. Other prominent sandbox titles include Garry's Mod, a physics playground built on the Source engine; Terraria; The Sims life-simulation series; and building-focused engineering games such as Kerbal Space Program and Factorio. Many role-playing and action games include a dedicated "sandbox mode" or "creative mode" that removes survival pressure and unlocks all tools.

Design considerations

Designers of sandbox games balance freedom against direction: too little structure can leave players aimless, so many titles layer optional goals, achievements and progression over the open systems. Sandbox play is also closely tied to modding communities and user-generated content, which extend games' lifespans far beyond their shipped material.
