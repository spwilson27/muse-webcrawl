Freedom of association is the right of individuals to join or leave groups of their choosing, and for groups to take collective action to pursue the interests of their members. It is recognized as a fundamental human right and civil liberty, closely linked to freedom of assembly and freedom of speech, and is a cornerstone of labour rights and democratic civil society.

Legal recognition

Article 20 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) proclaims that everyone has the right to freedom of peaceful assembly and association and that no one may be compelled to belong to an association. Article 22 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and Article 11 of the European Convention on Human Rights protect the right, including forming and joining trade unions. ILO Conventions No. 87 (1948) and No. 98 (1949) elaborate the right in the workplace. In the United States, freedom of association is not named in the Bill of Rights but has been read into the First Amendment, notably in NAACP v. Alabama (1958).

Scope and limits

The right covers political parties, trade unions, religious bodies, clubs, and NGOs, and generally includes a negative right not to associate. States may impose only restrictions prescribed by law and necessary in a democratic society — for example for national security or public order — and courts in many jurisdictions scrutinize bans on organizations, compelled membership, and restrictions on union activity under this proportionality standard.

Significance

Freedom of association enables collective bargaining, political pluralism, and the functioning of independent civil society; its suppression is a common marker of authoritarian rule.
