Herb

In general use, herbs are plants — or parts of plants — valued for their flavour, scent, medicinal properties or other qualities, and used in cooking, medicine, perfumery and ritual. In botany, the term has a narrower technical meaning: a herbaceous plant is one whose stem is not woody and which typically dies back at the end of the growing season.

Culinary herbs

Culinary herbs are usually the leafy green parts of plants, used fresh or dried to flavour food, in contrast to spices, which are typically the other parts — seed, bark, root, fruit or flower — and are more often used dried. Common culinary herbs include basil, parsley, coriander (cilantro), mint, rosemary, thyme, oregano, dill, chives, sage and tarragon. Herbs have been cultivated since antiquity: Egyptian, Chinese, Greek and Roman sources all describe their uses, and medieval monasteries maintained physic gardens. Fresh herbs are generally added near the end of cooking, while dried herbs release flavour slowly and are used in smaller or larger quantities depending on potency.

Medicinal and other uses

Herbal medicine — the use of herbs to treat illness — is among the oldest forms of healthcare and remains the primary medicine for much of the world's population; it is also the historical source of many modern pharmaceuticals, from aspirin (derived from willow and meadowsweet compounds) to digitalis from foxglove. Herbs also serve as sources of essential oils and fragrances, as ceremonial and ritual plants in many religions, and as strewing and pest-repellent plants in the household.

Botanical meaning

To botanists, "herb" (or "herbaceous plant") denotes a vascular plant with little or no persistent woody tissue above ground. Annual herbs complete their life cycle in one season, biennials in two, and perennial herbs regrow from underground parts each year. Many familiar garden plants, grasses and wildflowers are herbs in this sense regardless of any culinary or medicinal use.
