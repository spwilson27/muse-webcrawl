Sandstone

Sandstone is a clastic sedimentary rock composed mainly of sand-sized (0.0625 to 2 mm) mineral particles or rock fragments. Most sandstone is composed of quartz or feldspar, the minerals most resistant to weathering at the Earth's surface. Like uncemented sand, sandstone may be any color but is most commonly tan, brown, yellow, red, grey, pink, white, or black, depending on the mineral content and cement.

Formation

Sandstone forms in two main stages. First, a layer or layers of sand accumulate as sediment in environments such as river channels, floodplains, beaches, deltas, shallow seas, or desert dunes. Second, after burial, the sand is compacted and cemented by minerals — commonly calcite, quartz (silica), or iron oxides — precipitated from groundwater, a process called lithification or diagenesis.

Types

Sandstones are classified by their mineral composition and texture. Quartz arenites are more than 90% quartz. Arkoses contain significant feldspar, usually derived from weathered granite. Greywackes contain substantial clay matrix and rock fragments and are often deposited by turbidity currents. The red color of many sandstones comes from hematite coatings on grains; such rocks are sometimes called red beds.

Uses

Sandstone has been used since prehistory for construction, paving, grindstones, and sculpture. Famous buildings made of sandstone include many cathedrals, the Red Fort in Delhi, and numerous civic buildings in Britain and Germany. Porous sandstone aquifers are important groundwater reservoirs, and sandstone formations commonly host petroleum and natural gas deposits. Spectacular sandstone landforms include the cliffs of Zion National Park, Uluru in Australia, and the carved city of Petra in Jordan.
