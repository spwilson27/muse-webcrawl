American Gothic is a 1930 painting by Grant Wood in the collection of the Art Institute of Chicago. Wood was inspired to paint what is now known as the American Gothic House in Eldon, Iowa, along with "the kind of people [he] fancied should live in that house". It depicts a farmer standing beside his daughter — or, as often assumed, his wife — and is one of the most familiar images in 20th-century American art, widely parodied in popular culture.

The figures were modelled by Wood's sister Nan Wood Graham and their dentist, Dr. Byron McKeeby of Cedar Rapids. The woman wears a colonial print apron evoking 20th-century rural America, and the man holds a pitchfork. The house behind them, with its distinctive Gothic Revival window, gave the painting its name.

Creation and reception

Wood entered the painting in a competition at the Art Institute of Chicago in 1930, where it won a bronze medal and a $300 prize; the museum purchased it the same year. Initially, many Iowans resented the portrayal, but during the Great Depression the painting came to be read as a celebration of the steadfast American pioneer spirit. Art historians later debated whether it was sincere homage or gentle satire — Wood himself was ambiguous.

Legacy

American Gothic is among the most recognisable and imitated paintings in the world; its composition has been reproduced and parodied in advertising, cartoons, film, and photography countless times. The American Gothic House in Eldon is now a state historic site and visitor attraction.
