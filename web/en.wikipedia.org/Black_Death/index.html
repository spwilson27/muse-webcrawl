Black Death

The Black Death was a bubonic plague pandemic that swept through Afro-Eurasia between 1346 and 1353, killing an estimated 75 to 200 million people — perhaps 30 to 60 per cent of Europe's population — and ranking as the deadliest pandemic recorded in human history. It was caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis, spread by fleas carried on rodents, and manifested in bubonic, pneumonic and septicaemic forms. Recurrences of plague continued for centuries: the pandemic of which the Black Death was the first outbreak, the second plague pandemic, lasted into the 19th century.

Course of the pandemic

The plague appears to have originated in Central Asia or East Asia and travelled west along the Silk Road and by ship. In 1347 it reached Constantinople and the Mediterranean; the siege of the Genoese port of Caffa in the Crimea is a famous (and possibly embroidered) episode in its arrival. Genoese ships carried it to Sicily, Marseille and Italy in late 1347. It spread through France and Spain in 1348, reached England in the summer of 1348, and moved through Germany, Scandinavia and into Russia by 1351–53, while also devastating the Middle East and North Africa. Contemporary accounts, from Boccaccio's Florence in the Decameron to the chronicles of the Middle East, describe societies overwhelmed: mass graves, abandoned villages, and the breakdown of burial customs and public order.

Symptoms and transmission

The characteristic sign of bubonic plague is the bubo — a painful swelling of the lymph nodes in the groin, armpit or neck — accompanied by fever, chills and prostration; untreated bubonic plague is often fatal within days. Pneumonic plague, an infection of the lungs, could spread directly between people and was almost invariably lethal. Ancient DNA recovered from Black Death cemeteries, notably London's East Smithfield burial ground, has confirmed Y. pestis as the agent and allowed the reconstruction of the bacterium's phylogeny.

Consequences

The mortality transformed European society. Labour scarcity raised wages and weakened serfdom in the West, prompting attempts such as England's Statute of Labourers (1351) to freeze wages; whole villages were deserted. Religious responses ranged from penitential flagellant processions to violent scapegoating: Jewish communities were accused of well-poisoning and massacred across the Rhineland and beyond, despite papal condemnations. In art and literature the plague fed a lasting preoccupation with death — the danse macabre and the figure of the plague doctor in later centuries. Historians continue to debate the pandemic's long-run effects on wages, institutions, and the divergence of Western from Eastern Europe.

Later outbreaks

Plague returned to Europe in waves for over 300 years — the Great Plague of London (1665–66) and the Marseille plague of 1720 among the last major Western outbreaks — before the third pandemic emerged from China in the 19th century, during which Alexandre Yersin identified the bacillus in Hong Kong in 1894. Y. pestis remains endemic in rodent populations on several continents, and isolated human cases still occur.
