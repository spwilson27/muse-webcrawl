Black Death

Black Death
Disease: Bubonic plague
Cause: Yersinia pestis
Dates: 1346–1353
Deaths: 75–200 million (estimates)
Citizens of Tournai bury plague victims, from a 14th-century chronicle manuscript.

The Black Death was a bubonic plague pandemic that occurred in Europe from 1346 to 1353. It was one of the most fatal pandemics in human history, killing an estimated 75 to 200 million people across Eurasia and North Africa, and peaking in Europe between 1347 and 1351. The pandemic is thought to have originated in Central Asia or East Asia, from where it travelled along trade routes, including the Silk Road, to Crimea and then to the Mediterranean basin and the rest of Europe aboard merchant ships.

The plague was caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis, spread primarily through the bite of infected fleas carried by rodents such as black rats, though pneumonic and septicaemic forms also spread between people. The disease's name comes from the dark swellings (buboes), internal bleeding that blackened the skin, and the acral necrosis that afflicted victims.

Spread

The plague reached the trading ports of the Black Sea by 1346–1347. A famous account describes the siege of Kaffa (now Feodosia) in Crimea, where the besieging Mongol army reportedly catapulted plague corpses into the city. Genoese ships carried the disease to Constantinople, Messina in Sicily (October 1347), and onward to Genoa, Venice and Marseille. From the Mediterranean ports it spread overland throughout Europe, reaching England in 1348, Scandinavia by 1349, and the Baltic and Russia by the early 1350s.

Mortality

Europe's population may have fallen by 30 to 60 per cent. Cities were hit especially hard; Florence lost roughly half its population, and chroniclers such as Boccaccio described the catastrophe in the Decameron. Some communities were almost entirely destroyed, while a few areas — for instance parts of Poland and certain towns that imposed early quarantines, such as Milan — fared comparatively better. The demographic collapse took well over a century to reverse, and plague recurred in Europe for centuries, most notably in the Great Plague of London of 1665–1666.

Consequences

The Black Death had profound social, economic and cultural consequences. Labour shortages raised wages and weakened the manorial system and serfdom in Western Europe. Persecutions of minorities, including pogroms against Jewish communities accused of well-poisoning, erupted in many towns. The Catholic Church lost clergy and prestige, flagellant movements spread, and art and literature became preoccupied with death, as in the danse macabre. Long-term effects debated by historians include changes in land use, the acceleration of technological substitution for labour, and shifts in religious practice.

Modern research

Ancient DNA studies, including a 2022 analysis of burials in Kyrgyzstan, have identified the strain responsible in cemeteries near Lake Issyk-Kul in 1338–1339, supporting a Central Asian origin shortly before the pandemic's westward spread. Genome sequencing of Y. pestis from Black Death burial sites in London and elsewhere has confirmed the bacterium's identity and shown the pandemic strain sits near the base of a major diversification of the plague bacterium.
