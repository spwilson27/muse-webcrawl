Quarantine

A quarantine is a restriction on the movement of people, animals, and goods which is intended to prevent the spread of disease or pests. It is often used in connection with disease and illness, preventing the movement of those who may have been exposed to a communicable disease, yet do not have a confirmed medical diagnosis. It is distinct from medical isolation, in which those confirmed to be infected are separated from the healthy population.

History

The practice originates with plague control in the Adriatic port of Ragusa (modern Dubrovnik). In 1377 the city council ordered a 30-day isolation period for arrivals from plague areas; this was later extended to 40 days, quaranta giorni in Italian, giving rise to the term. During the Black Death and later plague outbreaks, cities across Europe built pesthouses and lazarettos and enforced maritime quarantine at ports.

Modern practice

Quarantine measures are applied to diseases such as cholera, Ebola, and COVID-19. Legal authority varies by country; in the United States, the federal government derives its authority from the Commerce Clause of the Constitution. Ethical debates concern the balance between collective safety and individual liberty.
