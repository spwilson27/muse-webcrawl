Public health

Public health is "the science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life and promoting health through the organized efforts and informed choices of society, organizations, public and private, communities and individuals" (Winslow, 1920). Analyzing the determinants of health of a population and the threats it faces is the basis for public health.

The modern concept of public health developed in response to epidemic diseases. The Black Death prompted European cities in the 14th and 15th centuries to establish the first organized systems of quarantine, isolation hospitals (lazarettos), and boards of health — institutions that laid the groundwork for state public health administration.

History

Early public health measures included the Ragusan (Dubrovnik) ordinance of 1377 imposing a thirty-day isolation (trentino) on travellers from plague-affected areas, later extended to forty days — quaranta giorni — the origin of the word quarantine. John Snow's investigation of the 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak in London is often regarded as the founding event of modern epidemiology.

Programs

Common public health initiatives include vaccination programs, sanitation and clean water provision, disease surveillance, health education, and regulation of food safety and occupational hazards.
