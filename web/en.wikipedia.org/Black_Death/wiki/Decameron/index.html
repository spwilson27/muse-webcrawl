The Decameron is a collection of short stories by the 14th-century Italian author Giovanni Boccaccio. The book is structured as a frame story containing 100 tales told by a group of seven young women and three young men — the brigata — sheltering in a secluded villa outside Florence to escape the Black Death that was afflicting the city.

Structure and themes

Over ten days, each member of the company tells one story each day, giving one hundred tales in total ("Decameron" derives from the Greek for "ten days"). The tales range from the bawdy and comic to the tragic, satirizing the clergy, celebrating cleverness and love, and criticizing greed. Each day is ruled by a king or queen who sets the theme, with the mischievous Dioneo granted freedom to speak last on any topic.

Influence

The work is a masterpiece of classical Italian prose and a landmark of early Renaissance humanism. It influenced Chaucer's Canterbury Tales and the later development of the European short story and novella.
