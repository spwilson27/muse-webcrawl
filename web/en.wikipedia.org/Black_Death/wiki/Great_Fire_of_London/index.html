Great Fire of London

The Great Fire of London was a major conflagration that swept through the central parts of London from Sunday, 2 September to Thursday, 6 September 1666. It began in Thomas Farriner's bakery on Pudding Lane shortly after midnight and spread rapidly westward, driven by a strong east wind through the city's closely packed timber buildings.

Destruction

The fire destroyed some 13,200 houses, 87 parish churches, St Paul's Cathedral, and much of the City of London, leaving tens of thousands homeless. Remarkably, very few deaths were officially recorded, though historians debate the true toll among the poor. Fire-fighting with water squirts and bucket chains proved useless; the blaze was finally halted by demolishing houses to create firebreaks and by the wind dropping.

Aftermath

London was rebuilt largely on the same street plan but in brick and stone under new building regulations. Sir Christopher Wren designed a new St Paul's Cathedral and many city churches. The Monument was erected to commemorate the fire. The fire also ended the Great Plague epidemic then afflicting the city, though the connection is debated.
