Yersinia pestis is a gram-negative, non-motile, coccobacillus bacterium that causes plague, the disease behind the Black Death and the second plague pandemic. It is a facultative anaerobe that can infect humans via the Oriental rat flea (Xenopsylla cheopis) and other flea vectors.

Discovery

The bacterium was isolated in Hong Kong in 1894 by Alexandre Yersin of the Pasteur Institute, working independently of, and in rivalry with, Shibasaburo Kitasato. It was initially named Pasteurella pestis and later renamed in Yersin's honor.

Biology and virulence

Y. pestis evolved relatively recently from Yersinia pseudotuberculosis. Its virulence depends on plasmids encoding a type III secretion system (the Yop effectors) that disables immune cells, a capsule-like F1 antigen, and the murine toxin that lets the bacterium survive in the flea gut, causing the flea to bite repeatedly and regurgitate bacteria into new hosts. The bacterium circulates in wild rodent reservoirs on every inhabited continent except Australia and still causes sporadic human cases and small outbreaks, treatable with antibiotics if caught early.
