Great Plague of London

The Great Plague of London, lasting from 1665 to 1666, was the last major epidemic of bubonic plague in England. Caused by Yersinia pestis, it killed an estimated 100,000 people — about a quarter of London's population — in eighteen months.

Course of the epidemic

Plague cases rose in the spring of 1665. King Charles II and the court fled the city, parish searchers recorded deaths, and marked houses were shut up with their inhabitants under guard, watched by watchmen. Bills of Mortality charted the weekly toll, which peaked in September 1665 at over 7,000 deaths a week in London. Samuel Pepys's diary is the most famous eyewitness account.

End and aftermath

The epidemic waned during the winter of 1665–66. The Great Fire of London in September 1666 destroyed much of the city but is not generally credited with ending the plague, which had already subsided. The outbreak was the last time plague ravaged England on this scale, possibly owing to quarantine of shipping and changes in housing and rodent ecology.
