Yersinia pestis

Yersinia pestis is a gram-negative, non-motile, coccobacillus bacterium and the causative agent of plague — in its bubonic, septicaemic, and pneumonic forms. It is responsible for the three great plague pandemics of history.

Discovery

The bacterium was discovered in 1894 during a plague outbreak in Hong Kong by Alexandre Yersin, a Swiss-French bacteriologist of the Pasteur Institute, and the genus Yersinia was later named for him. The flea-borne transmission cycle was elucidated in the following years.

Biology and transmission

Y. pestis evolved from the gut pathogen Yersinia pseudotuberculosis relatively recently in evolutionary terms. It maintains a life cycle between rodents — marmots, ground squirrels, rats — and their fleas. In the flea gut it forms a biofilm that blocks the foregut, causing the starving flea to bite repeatedly and regurgitate bacteria into new hosts. Key virulence factors are encoded on plasmids and include a type III secretion system that injects Yop effector proteins into immune cells.

Modern status

Plague remains enzootic in parts of Asia, Africa, and the Americas. Human cases are now rare and treatable with antibiotics, though the bacterium's pandemic history has also placed it on lists of potential bioterrorism agents.
