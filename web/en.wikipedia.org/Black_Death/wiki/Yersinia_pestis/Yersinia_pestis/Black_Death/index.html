Black Death

BacteriumYersinia pestis
Dates1346–1353
Death toll75–200 million (est.)

The Black Death (1346–1353) was the deadliest pandemic recorded in human history, caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis. The disease struck Eurasia and North Africa, peaking in Europe from 1347 to 1351, and may have reduced the world population from around 475 million to 350–375 million.

Origins

Research on ancient DNA points to an origin in Central Asia, near Lake Issyk-Kul in present-day Kyrgyzstan, where plague gravestones from 1338–1339 record a pestilence matching the later pandemic. Mongol armies and trade routes carried the infection westward along the Silk Road.

Spread

The plague reached Constantinople and Sicily in 1347 aboard Genoese trading ships fleeing the besieged port of Caffa in Crimea. Within four years it had spread through virtually all of Europe, following trade routes and carried by rat fleas.

Scientific confirmation

The identity of the pathogen was long debated, with some historians proposing anthrax or a viral haemorrhagic fever. Genetic analysis of skeletons from Black Death burial grounds, published from 2010 onward, has confirmed Y. pestis, ending the controversy.
