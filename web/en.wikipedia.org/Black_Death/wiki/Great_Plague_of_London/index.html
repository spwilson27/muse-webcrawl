Great Plague of London

The Great Plague of London, lasting from 1665 to 1666, was the most recent major epidemic of bubonic plague in England, and the last of the second plague pandemic there. It killed an estimated 100,000 people — almost a quarter of London's population — in eighteen months, with weekly deaths peaking above 7,000 in September 1665 according to the Bills of Mortality.

Course of the epidemic

The outbreak began in the parish of St Giles-in-the-Fields in late 1664 or early 1665 and spread through the crowded, unsanitary city during the hot summer. King Charles II and his court fled to Oxford; theatres and public gatherings were closed, infected houses were marked with a red cross and the words "Lord have mercy upon us", and watchmen guarded them. Samuel Pepys's diary and Daniel Defoe's later A Journal of the Plague Year are famous accounts of the epidemic.

Aftermath

The plague declined during 1666; its end was popularly attributed to the Great Fire of London in September 1666, which destroyed much of the city within the walls, though the epidemic was already waning and the fire mainly burned districts that the plague had touched less. London never again suffered plague on this scale.
