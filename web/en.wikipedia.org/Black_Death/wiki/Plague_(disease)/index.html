Plague (disease)

Plague is an infectious disease caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis, which circulates among wild rodents and their fleas and can be transmitted to humans. Depending on the route of infection it takes three main forms — bubonic, septicaemic and pneumonic — and untreated it is frequently fatal. Plague has caused three great pandemics, including the Black Death of the 14th century.

Forms and symptoms

Bubonic plague, the most common form, follows the bite of an infected flea. After an incubation of 2–8 days there is sudden fever, chills and weakness, with the development of one or more swollen, exquisitely painful lymph nodes — buboes — usually in the groin, armpit or neck. Septicaemic plague occurs when the bacteria multiply in the bloodstream, causing shock and bleeding into the skin and organs, which can blacken and turn gangrenous. Pneumonic plague infects the lungs, producing rapidly progressing pneumonia; it is the only form that spreads directly from person to person through respiratory droplets.

Transmission and ecology

Yersinia pestis is maintained in natural reservoirs among ground squirrels, marmots, prairie dogs and other rodents in parts of Asia, Africa and the Americas. Human cases usually follow flea bites, handling of infected animal tissues, or inhalation of infectious droplets. Cats are notably susceptible and can transmit the disease to their owners.

History

The First Pandemic began with the Plague of Justinian (541–549) in the Byzantine Empire. The Second Pandemic opened with the Black Death (1346–1353), which killed an estimated 30–60% of Europe's population, and recurred in waves for centuries, including the Great Plague of London (1665–66). The Third Pandemic began in China in the 1850s and spread worldwide by steamship; it was during this pandemic, in Hong Kong in 1894, that Alexandre Yersin identified the causative bacillus.

Diagnosis, treatment and status today

Diagnosis is confirmed by identifying Y. pestis in blood, sputum or bubo aspirates. Prompt antibiotic treatment — with agents such as gentamicin, doxycycline or fluoroquinolones — greatly reduces mortality. Plague persists endemically in several countries, notably Madagascar, the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Peru, with a few cases most years in the western United States; the World Health Organization reports roughly a few hundred to a few thousand cases globally per year.
