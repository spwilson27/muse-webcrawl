Black Death

DiseaseBubonic plague
BacteriumYersinia pestis
Dates1346–1353
Deaths75–200 million (est.)
RegionsEurasia and North Africa

The Black Death was a bubonic plague pandemic occurring in Europe from 1346 to 1353. It was one of the most fatal pandemics in human history; as many as 50 million people perished, perhaps 50% of Europe's 14th-century population. The disease is caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis, spread by fleas on rodents.

Probably originating in Central Asia or East Asia, the plague travelled along the Silk Road and by ship reached Crimea in 1347, from where it spread throughout the Mediterranean Basin and Europe.

Consequences

The Black Death had profound effects on European society: labour shortages raised wages and weakened feudalism; persecutions of minorities, including Jewish communities blamed for the disease, intensified; and art and religion were marked by an obsession with mortality for generations. Recurrences of plague continued in Europe until the early 19th century.

Name

Contemporaries called the catastrophe the "Great Mortality" or the "Great Pestilence"; the name "Black Death" became common only centuries later, possibly referring to the blackening of extremities in septicemic cases.
