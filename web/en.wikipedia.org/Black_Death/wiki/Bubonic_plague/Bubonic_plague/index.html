Bubonic plague

Bubonic plague is one of the three main forms of plague, caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis. It is transmitted to humans mainly through the bite of infected fleas that live on rodents, and is named for the painful swollen lymph nodes — buboes — that characterise it.

Signs and symptoms

After an incubation period of two to six days, symptoms appear suddenly: fever, chills, headache, and weakness, together with one or more tender, enlarged lymph nodes, usually in the groin, armpit, or neck. Without treatment the infection may spread to the blood (septicaemic plague) or lungs (pneumonic plague), and historical fatality rates were very high.

History

Bubonic plague caused the great historical pandemics: the Plague of Justinian (541–549), the Black Death (1346–1353), and the third pandemic that spread from China in the nineteenth century. The bacillus was identified in 1894 by Alexandre Yersin in Hong Kong. Plague persists today in animal reservoirs on several continents, with a small number of human cases each year, treatable with antibiotics if caught early.
