Bubonic plague
CauseYersinia pestis

Bubonic plague is one of three types of plague caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis. It is transmitted to humans through the bite of infected fleas that live on rodents. The disease is named for its characteristic buboes — painful, swollen lymph nodes, typically in the groin, armpit, or neck.

Symptoms and course

Two to eight days after exposure, patients develop sudden fever, chills, headache, and exhaustion, followed by the appearance of one or more buboes. Without treatment, the infection may spread to the blood (septicemic plague) or lungs (pneumonic plague), and historical fatality rates ranged from 40% to 70%. With prompt modern antibiotics, mortality falls to roughly 10% or less.

History

Bubonic plague was the form responsible for the Black Death, the devastating pandemic that swept Eurasia in the 14th century and killed an estimated 25–50 million people in Europe alone. Sporadic cases still occur today in rural areas of Africa, Asia, and the Americas, including a handful of cases most years in the western United States.
