Bubonic plague

CauseYersinia pestis
VectorOriental rat flea
Key signBuboes
Onset1–7 days after exposure

Bubonic plague is one of three types of plague caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis. One to seven days after exposure, flu-like symptoms develop, including fever, headaches, and vomiting, along with swollen and painful lymph nodes — buboes — occurring in the area closest to where the bacteria entered the skin.

Bubonic plague was the form responsible for the Black Death, the pandemic that struck Afro-Eurasia in the 14th century and killed an estimated 75–200 million people. The bacteria are transmitted through the bites of infected fleas that live on small mammals, especially rats.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is made by finding the bacteria in blood, sputum, or fluid from a lymph node. Without treatment, bubonic plague has a mortality rate of roughly 30–90 percent; with prompt antibiotics, most patients recover.

Present day

Plague remains endemic in parts of Africa, Asia, and the Americas, with the majority of reported cases in recent decades occurring in Madagascar, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Peru.
