Black Death

The Black Death was a pandemic of bubonic plague that swept Afro-Eurasia from 1346 to 1353, the deadliest pandemic in recorded history. Caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis and spread largely by fleas on rodents, it killed an estimated 75–200 million people, including between 30% and 60% of Europe's population.

Course of the pandemic

The plague spread from Central Asia along trade routes to the Black Sea, reaching Constantinople and Sicily in 1347 aboard Genoese ships. It then moved through Italy, France, Spain, England, and the rest of Europe, and also devastated the Middle East and North Africa. Towns responded with flight, quarantine, and — in some places — violent scapegoating of minorities.

Consequences

The demographic collapse reshaped European society: labour became scarce and wages rose, serfdom weakened, land use changed, and the disaster left deep marks on art, religion, and literature, from Boccaccio's Decameron to the danse macabre. Plague returned in waves for centuries, including the Great Plague of London in 1665–66.
