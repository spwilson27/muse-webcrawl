Yersinia pestis

Yersinia pestis is a gram-negative, non-motile, coccobacillus bacterium of the family Yersiniaceae. It is the causative agent of plague, the disease responsible for the Black Death of the fourteenth century and for plague pandemics throughout history. The bacterium was discovered in 1894 by Alexandre Yersin during an outbreak in Hong Kong.

Transmission and disease

Y. pestis circulates among wild rodents and their fleas; humans are infected mainly through the bite of an infected flea (such as the oriental rat flea, Xenopsylla cheopis), producing bubonic plague. Infection can also become septicemic or pneumonic, the latter able to spread directly between people via respiratory droplets. Untreated bubonic plague has a high mortality rate, but it responds well to antibiotics such as streptomycin, gentamicin, and doxycycline.

Genetics and evolution

The genome of Y. pestis is about 4.65 million base pairs with three plasmids carrying key virulence factors, including the F1 capsular antigen and the plasminogen activator Pla. Ancient DNA studies have identified Y. pestis in Neolithic and Bronze Age burials, showing the bacterium evolved from the relatively mild gut pathogen Yersinia pseudotuberculosis within the last several thousand years.

Modern occurrence

Plague remains endemic in parts of Africa, Asia, and the Americas, with a few thousand cases reported worldwide each year. Because of its history and aerosol infectivity, Y. pestis is classified as a high-priority pathogen and a potential bioterrorism agent.
