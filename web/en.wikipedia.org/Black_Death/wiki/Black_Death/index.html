The Black Death was a bubonic plague pandemic that swept through Europe, Asia, and North Africa from 1346 to 1353. It was the most fatal pandemic recorded in human history, killing a very large fraction of the population of Afro-Eurasia — plausibly a third to a half of Europe's inhabitants. The disease is caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis, transmitted principally by fleas living on rodents, and confirmed by DNA recovered from plague burials.

Course of the pandemic

The pandemic appears to have originated in Central or East Asia and travelled westward along trade routes. It reached the Black Sea port of Kaffa in the Crimea by 1347 and from there spread through the Mediterranean on merchant ships, arriving in Sicily, Italy, France, and Spain in 1347–1348, England in 1348, and Scandinavia and Russia by 1349–1351. Cities suffered catastrophic mortality; some communities lost half or more of their inhabitants, and villages were abandoned wholesale.

Forms of disease

The plague manifested chiefly as bubonic plague, marked by painful swollen lymph nodes (buboes), fever, and a high fatality rate; pneumonic plague spread from person to person through respiratory droplets and was almost invariably fatal; a rarer septicemic form infected the bloodstream. The name "Black Death" (from Latin atra mors) became attached to the catastrophe in later centuries, referring in part to the dark discoloration of infected tissue.

Consequences

The demographic collapse produced labor shortages, rising wages, the breakdown of serfdom in much of Western Europe, and upheavals in religious and social life, including persecution of minorities blamed for the disease. Recurrences of plague continued for centuries, shaping public-health responses such as quarantine, which was first institutionalized in Ragusa (Dubrovnik) in 1377. Modern outbreaks are rare and treatable with antibiotics, but Y. pestis remains endemic in rodent populations on several continents.
