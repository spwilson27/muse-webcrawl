Petrarch

Francesco Petrarca (20 July 1304 – 18/19 July 1374), commonly anglicised as Petrarch, was an Italian scholar, poet, and early humanist whose rediscovery and imitation of classical antiquity helped inaugurate the Italian Renaissance. He is often called the founder of humanism.

Life and works

Born in Arezzo and raised in Avignon, Petrarch travelled widely in search of lost classical manuscripts, recovering works of Cicero and others. In 1341 he was crowned poet laureate in Rome. His vernacular Canzoniere, 366 poems mostly addressed to his idealised love Laura, perfected the sonnet form that bears his name — the Petrarchan sonnet — and influenced European lyric poetry for centuries.

The plague years

Petrarch lived through the Black Death, which reached Avignon in 1348 and killed Laura and many of his friends; his letters record grief and horror at the pandemic, and his writings helped shape how later generations remembered it. He died at Arquà in 1374, reportedly found slumped over a book.

Legacy

His Latin works, including the epic Africa and the dialogue Secretum, his letters, and his ascent of Mont Ventoux — often read as a symbol of a new outlook on the world — made him the archetype of the Renaissance man of letters.
