Giovanni Boccaccio

Giovanni Boccaccio (16 June 1313 – 21 December 1375) was an Italian writer, poet, correspondent of Petrarch, and an important Renaissance humanist. He is best known for The Decameron, a collection of one hundred tales told by ten young people sheltering from the Black Death in Florence, which became a landmark of Italian prose and a source for Chaucer and many later writers.

Life

Born in Tuscany, the illegitimate son of a merchant, Boccaccio was sent to Naples to learn banking and law, neither of which suited him. At the Neapolitan court he fell in love with a woman he called Fiammetta, who inspired much of his early work. Returning to Florence around 1340, he witnessed the plague of 1348, which killed his father and stepmother and shaped the frame story of The Decameron, written shortly afterwards.

Works

His works include the prose romance Il Filocolo, the epic Teseida, the biographical collection De mulieribus claris (On Famous Women), and extensive Latin writings. Together with Dante and Petrarch, Boccaccio is regarded as one of the "three crowns" of early Italian literature; he championed Dante's work, lectured publicly on the Divine Comedy, and wrote a biography of him.
