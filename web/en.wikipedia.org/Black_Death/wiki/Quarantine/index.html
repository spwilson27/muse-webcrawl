Quarantine

A quarantine is a restriction on the movement of people, animals, and goods intended to prevent the spread of disease or pests. It is distinguished from medical isolation, which separates people already known to be ill. The word derives from quaranta giorni, "forty days" in Italian — the period ships arriving in Venice from plague-affected ports were required to wait at anchor before landing, a practice introduced by Ragusa (Dubrovnik) in 1377 with an initial thirty-day period.

History

Maritime quarantine stations (lazarettos) spread across Mediterranean and later world ports during the centuries of plague. Quarantine was applied against cholera, yellow fever, and other epidemic diseases, and codified in international sanitary conventions beginning in the nineteenth century. During the 1918 influenza pandemic, some Pacific islands used strict maritime quarantine to great effect.

Modern practice

Quarantine is still used for diseases such as Ebola and was applied on an unprecedented scale during the COVID-19 pandemic, when governments restricted travel and required arriving travellers and close contacts of cases to remain separated from the public for defined periods. Quarantine raises legal and ethical questions about liberty, compensation, and enforcement, which are addressed in public-health law such as the International Health Regulations.
