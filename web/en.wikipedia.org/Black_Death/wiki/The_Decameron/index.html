The Decameron

The Decameron is a collection of short stories by the Italian author Giovanni Boccaccio, written in the years after the Black Death reached Florence in 1348. Its frame story is famous: ten young Florentines — seven women and three men — flee the plague-stricken city for a villa in the countryside, where over ten days each tells one story a day, making one hundred tales in all.

Content

The tales range across comedy, tragedy, romance, and bawdy farce: clever merchants, foolish husbands, resourceful lovers, and corrupt clergy. Boccaccio drew on fables, folk tales, and earlier story collections, and his vivid prose in the Tuscan vernacular helped establish Italian prose literature. The introduction contains one of the most harrowing contemporary descriptions of the plague itself — the symptoms, the breakdown of burial customs, and the collapse of social bonds.

Influence

The work influenced Chaucer's Canterbury Tales and storytellers across Europe. Though sometimes censored for its anticlerical satire, it is now regarded as a masterpiece of early Renaissance literature.
