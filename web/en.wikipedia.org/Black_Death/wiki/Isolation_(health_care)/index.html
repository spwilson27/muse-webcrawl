Isolation (health care)

In health care, isolation refers to measures designed to prevent the spread of infectious disease from a patient to other patients, health care workers, and visitors. It is distinct from quarantine, which restricts the movement of people who were exposed but are not yet ill.

Types of precautions

Modern infection control uses standard precautions for all patients, supplemented by transmission-based precautions: contact precautions (gloves and gowns) for organisms spread by touch, droplet precautions (masks) for respiratory droplets, and airborne precautions (negative-pressure rooms and respirators) for agents such as tuberculosis and measles. Protective isolation works in reverse, shielding severely immunocompromised patients from outside infection.

History

The idea is ancient: leper colonies and plague lazarettos separated the sick from the healthy, and maritime quarantine was formalised in fourteenth-century Venice and Ragusa. Systematic isolation nursing developed in the late nineteenth century as germ theory displaced miasma theory, and isolation hospitals became standard features of cities.

Effects on patients

Isolation can cause loneliness, anxiety, and depression, and isolated patients may receive fewer visits from staff; health systems balance these harms against infection risk.
