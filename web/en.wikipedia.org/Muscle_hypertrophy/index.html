Muscle hypertrophy

Muscle hypertrophy is an increase in the size of skeletal muscle through a growth in the size of its component cells (myofibers). Two factors contribute to hypertrophy: sarcoplasmic hypertrophy, which focuses more on increased muscle glycogen and fluid storage, and myofibrillar hypertrophy, which focuses more on increased myofibril size and contractile protein content.

Mechanism

Resistance exercise creates mechanical tension and microscopic damage in muscle fibers, stimulating muscle protein synthesis. When protein synthesis exceeds protein breakdown over time, fibers grow in cross-sectional area. Key signalling pathways include mTOR; hormones such as testosterone, growth hormone and insulin-like growth factor 1 also influence growth, as do satellite cells, which donate nuclei to growing fibers.

Training and nutrition

Progressive overload through resistance training is the principal stimulus for hypertrophy. Adequate dietary protein — commonly cited around 1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight per day — and sufficient total energy intake support growth. Genetics, age, sex and training history all affect the rate and ceiling of hypertrophy. The opposite process, loss of muscle mass, is called atrophy.
