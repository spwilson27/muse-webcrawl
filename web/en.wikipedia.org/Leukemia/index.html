Leukemia (also spelled leukaemia) is a group of blood cancers that usually begin in the bone marrow and produce large numbers of abnormal white blood cells. These malignant cells — often immature "blasts" in acute forms — crowd out normal blood-cell production, causing anaemia, susceptibility to infection, and bleeding. The name derives from the Greek leukos ("white") and haima ("blood"), referring to the whitish appearance of blood heavy with white cells.

Classification

Leukemias are classified first by tempo — acute (rapidly progressing, with immature cells) or chronic (slower, with more mature cells) — and second by the cell line affected, lymphoid or myeloid. The four main types are acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), the most common cancer of childhood; acute myeloid leukemia (AML); chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), mainly of older adults; and chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), defined by the BCR–ABL fusion gene (the Philadelphia chromosome).

Signs and diagnosis

Symptoms arise from bone-marrow failure and organ infiltration: tiredness and pallor, recurrent infections, bruising and gum bleeding, bone pain, and painless enlargement of lymph nodes, spleen, or liver. Diagnosis rests on a full blood count and blood film, confirmed by bone-marrow aspiration and biopsy with immunophenotyping and genetic studies that now drive prognosis and treatment choice.

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment varies by type: intensive chemotherapy and stem-cell transplantation for acute leukemias; watchful waiting, targeted drugs, and immunotherapy for CLL; and tyrosine-kinase inhibitors such as imatinib for CML, which transformed that disease after 2001 from a fatal illness into a chronic condition with near-normal life expectancy. Childhood ALL cure rates now exceed 85–90 percent in high-income countries, while outcomes in AML and in older patients remain more guarded.
