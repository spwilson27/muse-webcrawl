Minsky moment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Minsky moment is a sudden, major collapse of asset values that marks the end of the growth phase of a credit cycle or business cycle. The term is named after economist Hyman Minsky and was coined by Paul McCulley of PIMCO in 1998 to describe the 1997 Asian financial crisis and the ensuing Russian debt default; it came into wide use during the financial crisis of 2007–2008.

Minsky's financial instability hypothesis

Minsky argued that capitalist financial systems are inherently unstable: during prosperous times, firms and investors progress through three financing postures — hedge finance (cash flows cover principal and interest), speculative finance (cash flows cover only interest, requiring refinancing), and Ponzi finance (cash flows depend on rising asset prices to cover even interest). As stability breeds risk-taking, the system drifts toward Ponzi finance until any interruption to credit triggers cascading asset sales and collapse. "Stability is destabilizing" is the hypothesis's common summary.

Usage

The term has been applied to the 2007–08 crisis, to the Chinese stock market turbulence of 2015, and prospectively to episodes of rapid asset-price decline following credit booms. It is sometimes used loosely for any crash preceded by excessive leverage.
