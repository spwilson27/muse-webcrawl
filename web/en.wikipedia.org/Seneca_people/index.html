Seneca people

Seneca (Onöndowa'ga:' — "Great Hill People")
RegionsWestern New York; Oklahoma; Ontario
LanguagesSeneca (an Iroquoian language), English
GovernmentSeneca Nation of Indians; Tonawanda Band; Seneca-Cayuga Nation

The Seneca (Seneca: Onöndowa'ga:', "Great Hill People") are a group of Indigenous Iroquoian-speaking people who historically lived south of Lake Ontario, one of the five original nations of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy). As the westernmost of the Five Nations, they were known as the "Keepers of the Western Door". At their height in the 17th century, Seneca villages stretched across what is now the Finger Lakes region and Genesee valley of New York.

History

Seneca society was matrilineal, organized in clans, with longhouse settlements supported by maize agriculture and hunting. Through the mid-1600s Beaver Wars, the Seneca expanded their influence across the Great Lakes and Ohio country. During the American Revolutionary War the Confederacy split; most Seneca sided with the British, and the punitive Sullivan Expedition of 1779 destroyed dozens of Seneca towns. Subsequent treaties, notably at Big Tree (1797), reduced Seneca lands to reservations in western New York.

Modern nations

Today Seneca citizens belong to several governments: the Seneca Nation of Indians (Allegany, Cattaraugus, and Oil Springs territories in New York), the Tonawanda Band of Seneca, the Seneca-Cayuga Nation in Oklahoma (descended from those removed west in the 19th century), and the Seneca at Six Nations of the Grand River in Ontario. The flooding of some 10,000 acres of Allegany land by the Kinzua Dam in the 1960s, despite treaty guarantees, remains a defining grievance. Seneca governments operate gaming enterprises under the name Seneca Gaming Corporation, and language-revitalization programmes work to sustain the severely endangered Seneca language.

Culture

Traditional culture includes lacrosse (the "medicine game"), the Longhouse religion codified by the prophet Handsome Lake around 1800, and ceremonies of the yearly cycle such as Midwinter and Green Corn.
