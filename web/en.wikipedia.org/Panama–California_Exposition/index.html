Panama–California Exposition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Panama–California Exposition was a world's fair held in San Diego, California, in Balboa Park from 1915 to 1916, celebrating the opening of the Panama Canal and promoting San Diego as the first American port of call for ships passing through it. A smaller rival event to San Francisco's Panama–Pacific International Exposition, it outlasted expectations and was extended into a second year as the Panama–California International Exposition.

Architecture and grounds

Under chief architect Bertram Goodhue, the fair was designed in an elaborate Spanish Colonial Revival style — an influential departure from the neoclassicism of earlier American fairs. Goodhue's California Quadrangle, with the richly ornamented California Building and its 200-foot tower (now the Museum of Us), remains Balboa Park's emblem. Grounds along the main avenue, El Prado, were planted by horticulturist Kate Sessions and others with lush gardens, including the Botanical Building and lily pond.

Exhibits

Exhibits promoted the agriculture, resources and tourism of the American Southwest and Latin America. Notable features included a large working model farm, an Indian Village with residents from Southwest pueblos (organised with the Santa Fe Railway's Painted Desert exhibit), and displays by the US Navy and Marine Corps — the military presence growing as the United States approached entry into the First World War.

Legacy

Unlike most fair buildings, many exposition structures were built to be permanent or were later rebuilt in permanent form, and they still house Balboa Park's museums. The fair popularised Spanish Colonial Revival architecture across California and the Southwest, gave Balboa Park its name (replacing "City Park"), and laid out much of the park's present landscape.
