Common garter snake

Scientific nameThamnophis sirtalis
FamilyNatricidae
RangeNorth America

The common garter snake (Thamnophis sirtalis) is one of the most widespread snakes in North America, occurring from Canada to Mexico and from coast to coast. Named for its resemblance to a striped garter, it is a slender, harmless-looking species frequently found near water. It is the state reptile of Massachusetts.

Description

Adults typically measure 46–66 cm in total length, though large specimens approach 130 cm. The colour pattern is highly variable, but most individuals show three pale longitudinal stripes — yellow, greenish, or brown — running the length of a dark body. Several subspecies are recognised, including the eastern garter snake (T. s. sirtalis), the red-sided garter snake (T. s. parietalis), and the San Francisco garter snake (T. s. tetrataenia), which is endangered.

Behaviour and diet

Common garter snakes are active mainly by day and feed on amphibians, earthworms, fish, slugs, and small rodents. They are well known for their tolerance of cold, being among the first snakes to emerge from brumation in spring; in Manitoba, tens of thousands gather at communal dens such as the Narcisse Snake Dens. Although long considered non-venomous, they produce a mild venom in a Duvernoy's gland that helps subdue amphibians but is not dangerous to humans. Females are ovoviviparous, giving birth to litters of live young, sometimes numbering several dozen.
