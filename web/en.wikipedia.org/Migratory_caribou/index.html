Migratory caribou

SpeciesRangifer tarandus
RangeArctic and subarctic North America
MigrationUp to several thousand km per year
Major herdsPorcupine, Western Arctic, George River, Bathurst

Migratory caribou are herds of caribou (Rangifer tarandus) in northern North America that undertake long seasonal migrations between wintering grounds in the boreal forest or southern tundra and calving grounds near the Arctic coast. Caribou migrations are among the longest terrestrial migrations of any mammal, with some herds travelling several thousand kilometres annually.

Migration cycle

In spring, pregnant cows lead the movement north to traditional calving grounds, where calves are born in early June. Summer aggregation follows, driven partly by relief from biting insects. In autumn the herds move south again to winter ranges where lichens — their principal winter forage — are accessible beneath shallow snow.

Major herds

Notable migratory herds include the Porcupine herd of Alaska and Yukon (named for the Porcupine River), the Western Arctic herd, and in Canada the George River, Leaf River, Bathurst, and Beverly herds. Herd sizes fluctuate dramatically over decades; the George River herd fell from an estimated 800,000 animals in the early 1990s to only a few thousand by the late 2010s.

Threats and conservation

Climate change alters forage phenology and icing conditions, while roads, mines, and hydroelectric development fragment migration routes. Migratory caribou are central to the subsistence and culture of Indigenous peoples across the North, and co-management boards involving Indigenous governments oversee harvest and monitoring in many regions.
