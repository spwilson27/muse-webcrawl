Bevacizumab

Trade nameAvastin
TypeMonoclonal antibody
TargetVEGF-A
Approved2004 (U.S. FDA)

Bevacizumab, sold under the brand name Avastin among others, is a monoclonal antibody medication used to treat a number of types of cancer and a specific eye disease. It works by inhibiting vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A), thereby blocking angiogenesis — the growth of new blood vessels that tumors need to grow.

Medical uses

Given by intravenous infusion, typically in combination with chemotherapy, bevacizumab is used for metastatic colorectal cancer, non-small-cell lung cancer, glioblastoma, renal cell carcinoma, cervical cancer, and epithelial ovarian cancer. Off-label intravitreal injections are widely used for neovascular age-related macular degeneration and diabetic macular edema because the drug is far cheaper per dose than licensed alternatives such as ranibizumab.

History

The drug was developed by Genentech from a mouse antibody against human VEGF, based on the angiogenesis research pioneered by Judah Folkman and the VEGF discovery by Napoleone Ferrara. It was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2004 for colorectal cancer — the first angiogenesis inhibitor approved in the United States. Its accelerated approval for breast cancer (2008) was revoked in 2011 after confirmatory trials failed to show a survival benefit, a notable regulatory controversy. Biosimilars have been marketed since 2017.

Adverse effects

Serious risks include hypertension, bleeding, gastrointestinal perforation, impaired wound healing, proteinuria, and thromboembolism. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines for certain indications.
