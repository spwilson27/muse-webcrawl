Taboo

A taboo (also spelled tabu) is a social group's prohibition of certain actions, words, or things, based on the judgement that such behaviour is prohibited by supernatural or moral authority. Breaking a taboo is usually considered objectionable or abhorrent by society in general. The term comes from the Tongan word tapu, spread into English through the voyages of Captain James Cook in the 1770s.

In Polynesian cultures, tapu marked places and objects as sacred and forbidden.

Origins and meaning

In Polynesian societies, tapu denoted both the sacred and the forbidden: chiefs, temples, and objects charged with mana were tapu and could not be touched by commoners. European observers generalised the word to any strongly sanctioned prohibition. Anthropologists distinguish taboo from mere custom or etiquette by the belief that violations bring automatic or supernatural punishment.

Common examples

Nearly universal taboos include incest and cannibalism. Dietary taboos — such as prohibitions on pork in Judaism and Islam, or beef in Hinduism — mark group identity and ritual purity. Death taboos govern the handling of corpses and mourning; speech taboos restrict profanity, blasphemy, and certain names. Taboos also govern menstruation, childbirth, and contact with rulers or sacred objects in many cultures.

Analysis

Sigmund Freud's Totem and Taboo (1913) linked taboo to ambivalent desire and the Oedipus complex. Later anthropologists such as Mary Douglas interpreted taboos as boundary-maintaining devices protecting a culture's classifications — "matter out of place". Functionalist accounts emphasise their role in social cohesion and ecological restraint, for example protecting scarce resources.
