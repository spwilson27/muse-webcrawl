Escapement

An escapement is a mechanical linkage in a mechanical watch or clock that gives impulses to the timekeeping element and periodically releases the gear train to move forward, advancing the clock's hands. The impulse action transfers energy to the clock's pendulum or balance wheel, replacing the energy lost to friction during its cycle and keeping it oscillating. The periodic release lets the gears advance — "escape" — a fixed amount with each oscillation, converting the gear train's continuous rotation into the ticking motion that gives clocks their sound.

History

The earliest known escapement is the water-powered mechanism of the Chinese polymath Su Song's astronomical clock tower of 1088, and in Europe the verge escapement appeared around the late thirteenth century, enabling the first true mechanical clocks. The verge, used for some 350 years, was inaccurate (errors of 15 minutes a day or more). Accuracy was transformed by the pendulum clock of Christiaan Huygens (1656) and by the anchor escapement (c. 1657–1670, attributed to Robert Hooke or William Clement), whose longer pendulum swing and smaller arc reduced errors to seconds per day. Later refinements included the deadbeat escapement of George Graham (1715), which eliminated recoil, and John Harrison's grasshopper escapement in his marine chronometers.

Watch escapements

In watches the dominant design is the lever escapement, developed by Thomas Mudge around 1755, in which a pivoted lever with two pallets alternately locks and unlocks the escape wheel while receiving impulses from the balance wheel through a roller jewel. Modern refinements include the co-axial escapement patented by George Daniels (1974) and commercialised by Omega, which reduces sliding friction and lengthens service intervals. In clocks, temperature-compensated pendulums with deadbeat escapements remained the precision standard until quartz clocks displaced them in the twentieth century.
