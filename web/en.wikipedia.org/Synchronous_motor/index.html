Synchronous motor

A synchronous electric motor is an AC motor in which, at steady state, the rotation of the shaft is synchronised with the frequency of the supply current; the rotation period is exactly equal to an integral number of AC cycles. Synchronous motors contain multiphase AC electromagnets on the stator that create a rotating magnetic field, and a rotor magnetised by permanent magnets or by DC excitation through slip rings, which locks in step with that field.

Principle of operation

The synchronous speed of the motor depends only on the supply frequency and the number of magnetic pole pairs: ns = 60f / p revolutions per minute, where f is the frequency in hertz and p the number of pole pairs. Unlike an induction motor, which always runs slightly below synchronous speed by an amount called slip, the synchronous motor runs exactly at synchronous speed from no load up to its maximum (pull-out) torque. Exceeding that torque causes the rotor to fall out of synchronism and stall.

Starting

Large synchronous motors are not inherently self-starting: the rotating stator field sweeps past the stationary rotor too quickly for it to lock on. Common solutions include starting as an induction motor using damper (amortisseur) windings in the rotor until near-synchronous speed is reached, using a pony motor, or ramping the supply frequency with a variable-frequency drive.

Applications and power factor

Synchronous motors are used where constant, precise speed is required and in very large sizes (for compressors, mills, and pumps) where their high efficiency is valuable. Because their power factor can be adjusted by varying the field excitation, over-excited synchronous machines can supply reactive power and are used as synchronous condensers for power-factor correction. Small synchronous motors drive electric clocks and timers, where the accuracy of the mains frequency keeps time.
