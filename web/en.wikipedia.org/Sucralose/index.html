Sucralose

Sucralose is an artificial sweetener and sugar substitute. The majority of ingested sucralose is not broken down by the body, so it is noncaloric. In the European Union, it is also known under the E number E955. It is produced by chlorination of sucrose, selectively replacing three of the hydroxy groups in the sucrose molecule with chlorine atoms.

Sucralose is about 320 to 1,000 times sweeter than sucrose, twice as sweet as saccharin, and three times as sweet as aspartame. It is a stable sweetener under a wide range of pH conditions and storage conditions, and can be used in baking, unlike aspartame which breaks down when heated.

History

Sucralose was discovered in 1976 by scientists from Tate & Lyle working with researchers Leslie Hough and Shashikant Phadnis at Queen Elizabeth College (now part of King's College London). While researching novel uses of sucrose, Phadnis was instructed to "test" a chlorinated sugar compound; due to a language misunderstanding he reportedly tasted it and found it exceptionally sweet. It was first approved for use in Canada in 1991, in the United States by the FDA in 1998, and in the European Union in 2004. It is sold under the brand name Splenda.

Safety

Regulatory bodies including the US FDA, EFSA, and JECFA have concluded that sucralose is safe at accepted daily intake levels, based on extensive toxicological studies. Research on possible effects on the gut microbiome and on compounds formed when sucralose is heated continues to be evaluated.
