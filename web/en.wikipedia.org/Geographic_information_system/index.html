A geographic information system (GIS) is a type of database containing geographic data (that is, descriptions of phenomena for which location is relevant), combined with software tools for managing, analyzing, and visualizing those data. In a broader sense, one may consider such a system also to include human users and manual methods, as well as software and data. GIS technology integrates common database operations, such as query and statistical analysis, with maps.

History

One of the earliest applications of spatial analysis was John Snow's 1854 map of cholera cases in Soho, London. The field of GIS began in the 1960s with the Canada Geographic Information System, developed by Roger Tomlinson — often called the "father of GIS" — for the Canada Land Inventory. Commercial software such as Esri's ARC/INFO (1982) and open-source systems such as GRASS GIS followed. The widespread availability of satellite imagery, GPS data and the web transformed GIS in the 1990s and 2000s.

Data models and techniques

GIS data represents real objects (such as roads, land use, elevation) with digital data. The two main data models are raster (grid cells, used for imagery and surfaces) and vector (points, lines and polygons, used for discrete features). Techniques include data capture and digitising, georeferencing, spatial analysis (buffering, overlay, interpolation, network analysis), cartographic visualisation, and web mapping. Modern formats include shapefiles, GeoJSON, GeoPackage and spatial databases such as PostGIS.

Applications

GIS is used in urban planning, environmental management, disaster response, transportation, agriculture, epidemiology, archaeology, marketing and navigation. Web and mobile services such as Google Maps and OpenStreetMap are built on GIS principles, while volunteered geographic information and remote sensing continue to expand available data.
