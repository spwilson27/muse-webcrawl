Reindeer herding

Reindeer herding is the practice of herding semi-domesticated reindeer (called caribou in North America) by Indigenous peoples across the circumpolar Arctic and sub-Arctic regions of Eurasia—from Norway through Siberia to the Bering Strait. Peoples engaged in reindeer herding include the Sámi of Norway, Sweden, Finland, and Russia's Kola Peninsula, and the Nenets, Khanty, Evenki, Chukchi, and other peoples of Siberia.

History

Reindeer were among the last animals domesticated, probably within the last 2,000–3,000 years in Siberia and Fennoscandia. Herding likely grew from hunting cultures that began managing wild herds, using tame deer as decoys, draught animals, and milk and meat sources. Over time, intensive large-scale pastoralism developed, with families following seasonal migrations between winter forest pastures and summer coastal or tundra pastures.

Practices

Traditional herding combines free-ranging grazing with periodic roundups for calf marking, castration, slaughter, and veterinary care. Reindeer provide meat, hides, antlers (sold for traditional medicine), milk, and historically transport—sledge-pulling reindeer remain iconic, including in Santa Claus folklore. Modern herders use snowmobiles, all-terrain vehicles, helicopters, and GPS collars alongside traditional skills and herding dogs.

Challenges

Herders face pressures from industrial development (mines, oil and gas, wind farms), infrastructure that fragments migration routes, predators such as wolves and wolverines, and climate change, which alters snow and ice conditions and forage availability. Rights to grazing land remain politically contentious, particularly for the Sámi, whose herding is protected by law in Norway, Sweden, and Finland but conflicts with forestry, mining, and tourism interests. In Russia, the Yamal Peninsula hosts some of the world's largest herds, managed by nomadic Nenets brigades.
