Dál Riata or Dál Riada (also Dalriada) was a Gaelic kingdom that encompassed the western seaboard of Scotland and north-eastern Ireland, on each side of the North Channel, from roughly the 5th to the 9th centuries. In Scotland it covered Argyll ("coast of the Gaels") and part of the Inner Hebrides, with its royal centre at the hillfort of Dunadd near present-day Kilmartin. In Ireland it occupied territory in what is now County Antrim.

Origins

Later Gaelic tradition, recorded in works such as the Senchus fer n-Alban ("History of the Men of Scotland"), told that Fergus Mór mac Eirc and his brothers migrated from Irish Dál Riata to Argyll around the year 500. Modern scholars debate whether this was a sudden invasion or the formalisation of much older Gaelic settlement and sea-borne contact across the narrow straits between Antrim and Kintyre; the Irish Sea province shared language and culture long before the historical kingdom appears.

Society and kingship

The kingdom was organised into kindreds (cenéla), principally the Cenél nGabráin (in Kintyre and surrounding areas), Cenél Loairn (Lorne), and Cenél nÓengusa (Islay), each furnishing kings at different times. The Senchus preserves a remarkable assessment of the kingdom's households and its military obligations, including what is often described as the earliest record of a naval levy in Britain. Kings of Dál Riata, such as Áedán mac Gabráin (r. c. 574–608), campaigned widely in northern Britain, fighting Picts, Britons, and Northumbrians; Áedán was defeated at the Battle of Degsastan (603).

Iona and Columba

In 563 St Columba (Colum Cille) founded the monastery of Iona, traditionally on land granted by Dál Riata. Iona became the spiritual heart of Gaelic Christianity, evangelising Pictland and Northumbria, and its abbot Adomnán wrote the Life of Columba—a key source for the period—and promulgated the Law of the Innocents (697), protecting non-combatants in war. The Book of Kells is associated with Iona's scriptorium.

Decline and legacy

Dál Riata suffered devastating defeats by the Picts under Óengus I in the 730s–740s, and from the 790s Viking raids struck its island heartlands, including Iona. The Scottish part of the kingdom was absorbed into the rising Pictish-Gaelic realm; tradition credits Kenneth MacAlpin (Cináed mac Ailpín), of Dál Riatan royal descent, with uniting Scots and Picts in the 840s to form the Kingdom of Alba, the ancestor of the Kingdom of Scotland. The Irish territory persisted longer as a lordship before being absorbed by neighbouring powers. Dál Riata's enduring legacy is the Gaelic language and identity of Scotland—"Scot" originally meant a Gael.
