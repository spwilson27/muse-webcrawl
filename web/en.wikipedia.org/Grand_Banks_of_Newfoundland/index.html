Grand Banks of Newfoundland

A trawler on the Grand Banks, historically among the richest fishing grounds in the world

The Grand Banks of Newfoundland are a series of underwater plateaus south-east of the island of Newfoundland on the North American continental shelf. The Grand Banks were one of the world's richest fishing grounds, supporting vast numbers of Atlantic cod, haddock, capelin, and other species for centuries.

Geography and oceanography

The banks cover roughly 280,000 square kilometres, with depths ranging from about 15 to 275 metres. The cold Labrador Current and the warm Gulf Stream meet over the banks; their mixing churns up nutrients that support dense plankton growth and, in turn, enormous fish populations. The same convergence produces frequent fog. The shallow waters lie partly within Canada's exclusive economic zone and partly in international waters regulated by the Northwest Atlantic Fisheries Organization.

History of the fishery

Indigenous peoples fished the region long before European contact. After John Cabot's 1497 voyage reported seas teeming with cod, Basque, Portuguese, French, Spanish, and English fleets exploited the banks for centuries. The fishery shaped the settlement and economy of Newfoundland. In the 20th century, factory trawlers dramatically increased catches; northern cod stocks collapsed, and in 1992 the Canadian government declared a moratorium on cod fishing, throwing tens of thousands out of work in one of the largest industrial shutdowns in Canadian history. The moratorium was lifted for a limited commercial fishery in 2024.

Oil and gas

The Grand Banks also host offshore oil fields, including Hibernia, Terra Nova, and White Rose, developed from the 1990s onward. The Hibernia platform, completed in 1997, is one of the largest offshore structures in the world.
