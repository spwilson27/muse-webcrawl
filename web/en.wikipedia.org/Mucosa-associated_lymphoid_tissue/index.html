Mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue

The mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT), also called mucosa-associated lymphatic tissue, is a diffuse system of small concentrations of lymphoid tissue found in various submucosal membrane sites of the body, such as the gastrointestinal tract, nasopharynx, thyroid, breast, lung, salivary glands, eye, and skin.

A Peyer's patch, part of gut-associated lymphoid tissue

Components

MALT is populated by lymphocytes such as T cells and B cells, as well as plasma cells and macrophages. In the gut, the gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) includes Peyer's patches in the ileum and isolated lymphoid follicles. Other components include bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (BALT) and nasal-associated lymphoid tissue (NALT). M cells in the epithelium sample antigens from the lumen and deliver them to the underlying lymphoid tissue.

Role in disease

MALT is the origin of MALT lymphomas, low-grade B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphomas associated with chronic inflammation or autoimmune disease, most famously gastric MALT lymphoma associated with Helicobacter pylori infection.
