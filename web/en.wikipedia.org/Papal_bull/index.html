Papal bull

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A papal bull is a type of public decree, letters patent, or charter issued by a pope of the Catholic Church. It is named after the leaden seal (bulla in Latin) that was traditionally appended to the end of the document to authenticate it. Bulls have been used since the early Middle Ages for the most important formal pronouncements of the papacy, though the term came to be applied more broadly over time.

Format

Traditionally, a bull was written in Latin on parchment, beginning with the pope's name and the formula episcopus, servus servorum Dei ("bishop, servant of the servants of God"), and was cited by its incipit — its opening words. The lead bulla, stamped on one side with the heads of Saints Peter and Paul and on the other with the pope's name, was attached by hemp or silk cords, depending on the document's character. Since the late nineteenth century the lead seal has generally been replaced by a red ink stamp, except for particularly solemn documents.

Notable bulls

Famous examples include Unam sanctam (1302), Boniface VIII's assertion of papal supremacy; Inter caetera (1493), by which Alexander VI divided the newly encountered lands outside Europe between Spain and Portugal; Exsurge Domine (1520), condemning the teachings of Martin Luther; Regnans in Excelsis (1570), excommunicating Elizabeth I of England; Ineffabilis Deus (1854), defining the dogma of the Immaculate Conception; and Munificentissimus Deus (1950), defining the Assumption of Mary. Bulls have served many purposes, from canonising saints and founding universities to proclaiming holy years (jubilees) and excommunicating rulers.

Modern usage

Since the reforms of the twentieth century, apostolic constitutions and other document types have taken over many of the bull's former functions, but bulls are still issued, for example to proclaim jubilees or to erect dioceses. The study and authentication of such documents is part of the discipline of diplomatics.
