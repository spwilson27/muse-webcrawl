The Alps are the highest and most extensive mountain range in Europe, stretching approximately 1,200 kilometres in an arc from the Mediterranean coast of France through Switzerland, Italy, Austria and Slovenia to the Pannonian plain. Their highest summit is Mont Blanc (4,809 m), on the Franco-Italian border.

Geography and geology

The Alps formed over tens of millions of years as the African tectonic plate collided with the Eurasian plate, folding and thrusting sedimentary and crystalline rocks upward — a process that continues today at a few millimetres per year. The range is conventionally divided into Western, Central and Eastern Alps. Great river systems rise in the Alps, including the Rhine, Rhône, Po and tributaries of the Danube, and the range is a major European watershed. Glaciers, though rapidly retreating under climate change, still cover significant high-altitude areas.

Climate and ecology

Alpine climate varies sharply with altitude, from warm valleys with vineyards and chestnut groves to arctic conditions on the highest peaks. Characteristic phenomena include the warm downslope Föhn wind. The Alps support distinctive flora and fauna — edelweiss, alpine ibex, chamois, marmots and golden eagles — and altitudinal zonation from deciduous forest through coniferous forest to alpine meadow and bare rock.

Human history and economy

Humans have lived in and crossed the Alps since prehistory; Ötzi the Iceman, found in a glacier in 1991, dates to about 3300 BC. Hannibal's crossing in 218 BC and Napoleon's in 1800 are among history's most famous military feats. Mountain passes such as the Brenner, Gotthard and Mont Cenis long carried European trade, and modern road and rail tunnels now pierce the range. Today about 14 million people live in the Alps, whose economy rests on tourism (skiing, mountaineering and hiking), hydroelectric power, dairy farming and forestry.
