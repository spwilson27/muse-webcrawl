An endangered language or moribund language is a language that is at risk of disappearing as its speakers shift to other languages or die out. Of the roughly 7,000 languages spoken today, a large proportion — estimates commonly cite around 40 per cent — are endangered, and linguists project that a language dies roughly every few weeks. Language loss is driven by globalization, urbanization, compulsory education in dominant languages, economic pressure, and, historically, deliberate suppression of minority languages.

Classification

UNESCO's Atlas of the World's Languages in Danger grades vitality by the rate of intergenerational transmission: a language is vulnerable when most children speak it but it is restricted to certain domains; definitely endangered when children no longer learn it as a mother tongue at home; severely endangered when it is spoken mainly by the grandparental generation; and critically endangered when the youngest speakers are elderly and use it only partially. The Expanded Graded Intergenerational Disruption Scale (EGIDS), used by Ethnologue, provides a finer 13-level classification.

Consequences and documentation

When a language falls out of use, its community loses oral literature, traditional ecological and medicinal knowledge encoded in its vocabulary, and a component of cultural identity; linguists lose evidence about the range of possible human grammars. The field of language documentation races to record grammars, dictionaries, and texts with the last fluent speakers — famous cases include Marie Smith Jones, the last native speaker of Eyak in Alaska, who died in 2008, and Bo Sr., the last speaker of Bo in the Andaman Islands, who died in 2010.

Revitalization

Language revitalization efforts range from documentation to immersion schooling: Māori kōhanga reo language nests (from 1982), Hawaiian Pūnana Leo preschools, Welsh-medium education and broadcasting, and the reclamation of "sleeping" languages such as Wampanoag and Kaurna from written records. Hebrew remains the classic case of a language restored to full vernacular use. Technology — apps, online courses, and community archives — increasingly supports these movements, though success depends on passing the language to children in the home.
