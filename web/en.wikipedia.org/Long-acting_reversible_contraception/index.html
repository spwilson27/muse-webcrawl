Long-acting reversible contraception (LARC) refers to methods of birth control that provide effective contraception for an extended period without requiring user action and whose effect ends when the method is removed. The main methods are intrauterine devices (IUDs) — both copper and hormonal (levonorgestrel-releasing) — and subdermal contraceptive implants.

Effectiveness

LARCs are the most effective reversible methods of contraception, with typical-use failure rates below one pregnancy per 100 users per year, because their effectiveness does not depend on correct daily or per-act use. Major medical bodies, including the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, recommend them as a first-line option for most people, including adolescents.

Use

IUDs are small devices placed in the uterus by a clinician and can remain effective for three to twelve years depending on type. Implants are small rods placed under the skin of the upper arm that release progestin for about three years. Fertility generally returns quickly after removal of either method.
