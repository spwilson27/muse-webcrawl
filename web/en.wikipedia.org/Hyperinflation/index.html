Hyperinflation

In economics, hyperinflation is a very high and typically accelerating inflation. It quickly erodes the real value of the local currency, as the prices of all goods increase. This causes people to minimise their holdings in that currency as they usually switch to more stable foreign currencies. Economist Phillip Cagan defined hyperinflation as beginning in a month in which the inflation rate exceeds 50% and ending when it falls below that rate for at least a year.

Causes

Hyperinflation is generally associated with governments financing large budget deficits by printing money, often under conditions of war, economic collapse, supply shocks or heavy foreign-currency debt. As prices rise, tax revenues collected later fall in real terms (the Tanzi effect), worsening the deficit and forcing still faster money creation — a self-reinforcing spiral.

Historical examples

The most famous case occurred in the Weimar Republic in 1923, when prices doubled every few days and workers were paid twice daily so they could spend wages before they lost value. Hungary in 1946 experienced the highest monthly inflation ever recorded, with prices doubling roughly every 15 hours. Later episodes include Yugoslavia in 1994, Zimbabwe in 2008, and Venezuela beginning in the late 2010s. Escapes usually involve a credible new currency or currency board, fiscal reform, and an end to monetary financing.
