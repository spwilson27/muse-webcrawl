Akrotiri (prehistoric city)

Akrotiri (Greek: Ακρωτήρι) is a Bronze Age settlement on the volcanic island of Thera (Santorini) in the Cyclades, Greece. It was a prosperous Minoan-influenced port town that was buried under volcanic ash in the Thera eruption of around 1627 BC, which preserved its buildings and famous frescoes in remarkable detail, in a manner often compared to Pompeii.

History

Akrotiri was settled by the Neolithic period and grew into an important harbour by the Middle and Late Bronze Age, trading across the Aegean with Crete, mainland Greece, Cyprus and beyond. Before the eruption, an earthquake damaged the town; rebuilding was under way when the catastrophic eruption buried it. Notably, no human remains or valuables in precious metal have been found, suggesting the population evacuated in time.

Excavations

Systematic excavation began in 1967 under Spyridon Marinatos of the Archaeological Society of Athens, and continues under the Greek Archaeological Service. Multi-storey houses with drainage systems, storerooms with pithoi jars, and staircases have been uncovered along paved streets.

Frescoes

The town is celebrated for its well-preserved wall paintings, including the Flotilla Fresco, the Spring Fresco with swallows, the Boxing Boys, and the Fisherman fresco, now displayed in the Museum of Prehistoric Thera and the National Archaeological Museum of Athens.
