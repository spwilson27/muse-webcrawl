Heinrich Schliemann (1822–1890) was a German businessman and pioneering archaeologist who became famous for excavating the site of ancient Troy at Hisarlik in modern Turkey and the Bronze Age citadel of Mycenae in Greece. A passionate believer in the historicity of Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, he used his considerable self-made fortune to fund excavations that he believed would locate the world of the Homeric heroes.

Early life and fortune

Born in Mecklenburg to a poor pastor's family, Schliemann worked as a grocer's apprentice and ship's boy before joining a trading firm in Amsterdam, where he learned numerous languages — reportedly by a method of intensive reading and memorisation. As an agent in St. Petersburg and later as an independent merchant (including a profitable spell in California during the Gold Rush and trading in indigo and war materiel during the Crimean War), he amassed a large fortune and retired in 1863 to devote himself to archaeology.

Troy

Following the suggestions of Frank Calvert, who owned part of the site, Schliemann excavated at Hisarlik from 1871 to 1873 and in later campaigns. In 1873 he announced the discovery of "Priam's Treasure", a hoard of gold and bronze objects which he smuggled out of the Ottoman Empire — sparking a legal dispute and a long controversy over the finds, which eventually went to Berlin and were taken to Moscow after the Second World War. Schliemann's deep trench through the mound damaged later layers; subsequent work, including by his collaborator Wilhelm Dörpfeld, showed the city had many phases and that Schliemann's "Troy of Priam" belonged to a much earlier Bronze Age level.

Mycenae and later work

In 1876 Schliemann excavated the shaft graves of Grave Circle A at Mycenae, finding rich gold burials including a gold funerary mask he dubbed the "Mask of Agamemnon" — in fact centuries older than any plausible historical Agamemnon. He also excavated at Tiryns and Orchomenos and sought Odysseus's palace on Ithaca. He died in Naples in 1890.

Legacy

Schliemann is credited with demonstrating that Homeric epic preserved memories of a real Bronze Age world and with founding Aegean prehistory as a field. He is also criticised for destructive methods, self-promotion, and embellishing or falsifying parts of his diaries and accounts — a debate that continues among historians of archaeology.
