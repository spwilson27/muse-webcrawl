Radiation therapy (or radiotherapy) is a medical treatment that uses ionising radiation to control or kill malignant cells, most commonly as part of cancer treatment. It works by damaging the DNA of cancerous tissue; cells whose DNA is damaged beyond repair stop dividing or die. Roughly half of all cancer patients receive radiation therapy at some point in their treatment.

Mechanism

Ionising radiation deposits energy in tissue, directly breaking DNA strands and indirectly generating free radicals from water that attack DNA. Cancer cells are generally more vulnerable than normal cells because they divide rapidly and have impaired DNA-repair mechanisms. Treatment is divided into fractions — small daily doses over several weeks — to let healthy tissue recover while cumulative damage kills tumour cells.

Types

External beam radiation therapy

A linear accelerator generates high-energy X-rays or electrons directed at the tumour from outside the body. Modern techniques include three-dimensional conformal therapy, intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), stereotactic radiosurgery, and image-guided therapy, all designed to maximise tumour dose while sparing surrounding organs. Proton therapy exploits the Bragg peak to deposit dose at a precise depth, and is favoured for some paediatric and skull-base tumours.

Brachytherapy

Sealed radioactive sources (such as iridium-192 or iodine-125 seeds) are placed inside or next to the tumour, delivering a high local dose. It is widely used for prostate, cervical and breast cancers.

Systemic therapy

Unsealed radionuclides circulate in the body, e.g. iodine-131 for thyroid cancer or lutetium-177 labelled agents for neuroendocrine tumours and prostate cancer.

Side effects

Side effects depend on the site treated. Common effects include fatigue and skin irritation; site-specific effects may include mucositis, nausea, or bowel and bladder changes. Late effects, appearing months to years later, can include fibrosis, organ dysfunction and a small risk of secondary malignancy.

History

Radiotherapy began soon after Röntgen's discovery of X-rays in 1895 and the Curies' isolation of radium; Emil Grubbe is credited with an early X-ray treatment in 1896. The development of cobalt-60 units in the 1950s and linear accelerators thereafter made megavoltage therapy routine.
