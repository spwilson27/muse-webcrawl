Ground proximity warning system

AcronymGPWS; enhanced version: EGPWS / TAWS
PurposePrevent controlled flight into terrain (CFIT)
InventorC. Donald Bateman (Honeywell)
MandatedUS airliners from 1974; enhanced systems from 2000s
Key alerts"Pull up", "Terrain", "Sink rate", "Don't sink"

A ground proximity warning system (GPWS) is an aircraft safety system that warns pilots if their aircraft is in immediate danger of flying into the ground or an obstacle — the accident type known as controlled flight into terrain (CFIT). Its aural warnings, delivered in a synthetic voice, include "sink rate", "pull up", "terrain, terrain" and "don't sink".

How it works

Classic GPWS, developed by Canadian engineer C. Donald Bateman at Honeywell in the late 1960s and early 1970s, uses the radio altimeter and other inputs to detect hazardous situations in five basic modes: excessive descent rate, excessive terrain closure rate, altitude loss after take-off, unsafe terrain clearance with gear or flaps not in landing configuration, and excessive deviation below the ILS glideslope.

The Enhanced GPWS (EGPWS), marketed as part of the broader Terrain Awareness and Warning System (TAWS) category, adds a GPS position and a worldwide terrain and obstacle database, allowing it to look ahead and warn 30–60 seconds before a potential impact, with terrain displayed on cockpit screens. This overcame classic GPWS's blindness to steeply rising terrain directly ahead.

History and effectiveness

After a series of CFIT crashes, including TWA Flight 514 in 1974, the US Federal Aviation Administration required GPWS on large turbine aircraft, making the US the first to do so. The mandate is credited with a dramatic fall in CFIT accidents, once the leading killer in commercial aviation; the enhanced system was required on most US airliners by 2005 under rules phased in from 2000. Bateman received the US National Medal of Technology and Innovation for the invention.

Limitations

Classic GPWS could give false alarms — leading crews to mistrust or delay response — and could not warn in time when flying directly at cliffs or steep slopes, the principal motivation for EGPWS. Even so, GPWS alerts demand an immediate, trained escape manoeuvre, and accident reports continue to examine crew responses to them.
