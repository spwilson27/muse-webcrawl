Tartaric acid

Tartaric acid is a white, crystalline organic acid that occurs naturally in many fruits, most notably grapes, bananas, and tamarinds. Chemically it is a dicarboxylic dihydroxy acid, C4H6O6, first isolated from potassium tartrate ("tartar", the sediment in wine casks) by Carl Wilhelm Scheele in 1769. It is widely used as an acidulant in food and drink (E number E334) and in pharmaceuticals.

Stereochemistry

Tartaric acid has two chiral carbon atoms and played a foundational role in the discovery of molecular chirality. Natural tartaric acid is the (2R,3R) enantiomer, dextrorotatory. Its mirror image, (2S,3S), and the achiral meso form also exist. In 1848 Louis Pasteur manually separated the left- and right-handed crystals of sodium ammonium tartrate, founding stereochemistry. Racemic tartaric acid ("racemic acid") is a 1:1 mixture of the enantiomers.

Salts and uses

Important salts include cream of tartar (potassium bitartrate), a by-product of winemaking used with baking soda as a leavening component; Rochelle salt (potassium sodium tartrate), historically important in piezoelectricity and in Fehling's solution; and tartar emetic (antimony potassium tartrate), once used in medicine. In the kitchen and winery, tartaric acid stabilizes colour and adjusts acidity; "wine diamonds" are harmless crystals of potassium bitartrate that precipitate in bottles.

Production

Industrial tartaric acid is recovered largely from wine-industry residues (lees and argol) or produced synthetically from maleic anhydride. Global demand centres on beverages, confectionery, and baking.
