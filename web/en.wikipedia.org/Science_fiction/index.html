Science fiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Science fiction (sometimes shortened to sci-fi or SF) is a genre of speculative fiction that deals with imaginative and futuristic concepts such as advanced science and technology, space exploration, time travel, parallel universes, and extraterrestrial life. It often explores the potential consequences of scientific, social, and technological innovations, and has been called the "literature of ideas." Science fiction can be found in books, film, television, theatre, games, and other media, and is closely related to—but distinct from—fantasy and horror, though it frequently blends with them.

Definition

Definitions vary widely. Editor Hugo Gernsback, who coined the term "science fiction" in the 1920s, emphasized scientific accuracy and wonder; critic Darko Suvin characterized the genre by an "estranging" novum—a rationally plausible novelty that transforms the fictional world. A widely quoted informal definition is that of author Rod Serling: "Fantasy is the impossible made probable. Science fiction is the possible made probable."

History

Precursors include ancient tales of voyages to the Moon, but recognizable science fiction emerged with the Scientific Revolution and the Industrial Age: Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (1818) is often called the first science fiction novel; the works of Jules Verne and H. G. Wells defined the genre's early possibilities. In the 1920s–30s, American pulp magazines such as Amazing Stories created a dedicated readership. Under editor John W. Campbell, the late 1930s and 1940s became the "Golden Age," launching writers such as Isaac Asimov, Robert A. Heinlein, and Arthur C. Clarke. The more literary New Wave of the 1960s–70s (J. G. Ballard, Ursula K. Le Guin, Samuel R. Delany), and the cyberpunk movement of the 1980s (William Gibson, Bruce Sterling), successively reshaped the field. In film and television, franchises such as Star Trek and Star Wars carried the genre to a mass audience.

Subgenres and themes

Major subgenres include space opera, hard and soft science fiction, cyberpunk and its derivatives, alternate history, military science fiction, dystopian and post-apocalyptic fiction, and climate fiction. Recurring themes include first contact with aliens, artificial intelligence and robots, the future of humanity, and the ethical consequences of new technologies. The genre's highest-profile awards are the Hugo Award and the Nebula Award.
