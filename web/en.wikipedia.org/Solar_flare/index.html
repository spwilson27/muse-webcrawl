A solar flare is a relatively intense, localized emission of electromagnetic radiation in the Sun's atmosphere. Flares occur in active regions around sunspots and are usually accompanied by coronal mass ejections and solar particle events. The radiation spans the spectrum from radio waves to gamma rays and reaches Earth in about eight minutes, where it can disrupt radio communications and satellite operations.

Mechanism

Flares are powered by the sudden reconnection of magnetic field lines in the corona, converting stored magnetic energy into light, heat, and accelerated particles within minutes. Plasma in the flaring loops is heated to tens of millions of kelvin, far hotter than the surrounding corona.

Classification

Flares are classified A, B, C, M, or X according to their peak soft X-ray flux measured by satellites, each class being ten times the previous, with a linear sub-scale from 1 to 9. X-class flares are major events capable of planet-wide radio blackouts and geomagnetic storms when associated ejections strike Earth.

History and notable events

The first observed flare, the Carrington Event of 1859, produced auroras visible in the tropics and disrupted telegraph systems. Modern notable events include the March 1989 storm that blacked out Quebec's power grid and the "Halloween storms" of 2003. The largest flares of the space age have been estimated at well above X28.

Effects

Flare X-rays and ultraviolet radiation enhance ionospheric ionization, causing shortwave radio fadeouts; energetic particles endanger astronauts and satellites; and associated geomagnetic storms can induce damaging currents in power grids and pipelines.
