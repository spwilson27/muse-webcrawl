H-alpha

Wavelength656.28 nm (air)
ColourDeep red
SeriesBalmer series
Transitionn = 3 → n = 2

H-alpha (Hα) is a specific deep-red visible spectral line in the Balmer series with a wavelength of 656.28 nm in air; it occurs when a hydrogen electron falls from its third to second lowest energy level. H-alpha is the strongest hydrogen spectral line in the visible part of the spectrum and is emitted by hydrogen throughout the universe wherever the gas is ionised and recombining.

Astronomical use

Because hydrogen is the most abundant element in the cosmos, H-alpha emission traces star-forming regions: the glowing pinkish-red clouds of H II regions such as the Orion Nebula shine largely in H-alpha light. Astronomers use narrowband H-alpha filters to isolate this emission, revealing the structure of nebulae and the spiral arms of galaxies while suppressing light pollution.

Solar observation

H-alpha filters are widely used by amateur and professional solar observers: viewing the Sun at 656.28 nm shows the chromosphere, with prominences, filaments, flares and the mottled network of spicules invisible in ordinary white light. Dedicated solar telescopes and etalon filters make such observation safe and routine.

Physics

The line's wavelength is accurately predicted by the Rydberg formula for the Balmer series. It was through measurements of the Balmer lines including H-alpha that early quantum theory, in the form of the Bohr model of the atom, first triumphed; fine structure and the Lamb shift in the line later tested quantum electrodynamics.
