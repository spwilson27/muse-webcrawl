Salt Lake Temple

The Salt Lake Temple is a temple of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints on Temple Square in Salt Lake City, Utah. Dedicated in 1893 after forty years of construction, it is the largest and most iconic Latter-day Saint temple, and a symbol of the church worldwide.

Construction

Church president Brigham Young selected the site four days after the Mormon pioneers entered the Salt Lake Valley in July 1847 and broke ground in 1853. Designed by architect Truman O. Angell in a Gothic Revival style with six spires — the eastern three, representing the First Presidency, taller than the western three — the temple was built of quartz monzonite from Little Cottonwood Canyon, hauled first by ox team and later by railroad. The capstone was laid in 1892 before a crowd of some 50,000, and President Wilford Woodruff dedicated the completed temple on 6 April 1893.

Features and symbolism

The building covers 253,000 square feet and is rich in symbolism: the east centre tower was long topped by a gilded Angel Moroni statue (removed for restoration in 2020), and exterior carvings depict sun, moon and star stones, the Big Dipper, clasped hands and the all-seeing eye. Like other Latter-day Saint temples, it is not a Sunday meetinghouse but is used for marriages, endowment ceremonies and sealings, and is open only to church members in good standing; the grounds, however, welcome the public.

Renovation

The temple closed in December 2019 for a major multi-year renovation including seismic retrofitting with base isolators, restoration of its historic interiors and redesigned surrounding plazas on Temple Square.
