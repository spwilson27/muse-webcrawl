Inorganic chemistry

Inorganic chemistry is the branch of chemistry concerned with the synthesis, structure, bonding and reactions of inorganic and organometallic compounds — broadly, compounds of all the elements, encompassing metals, minerals, salts, acids and bases, and coordination compounds. Traditionally defined by contrast with organic chemistry (the chemistry of hydrocarbons and their derivatives), it covers most of the periodic table: the chemistry of over a hundred elements as against the handful (chiefly C, H, N, O) that dominate organic chemistry. The boundary is deliberately porous: organometallic chemistry, bioinorganic chemistry and materials chemistry all straddle it.

Subfields

  • Coordination chemistry — complexes of metal ions with surrounding ligands; founded by Alfred Werner (Nobel Prize 1913), explained today by crystal-field and ligand-field theory.
  • Organometallic chemistry — compounds with metal–carbon bonds, from Grignard reagents to the catalysts of industrial processes (Ziegler–Natta polymerisation, cross-coupling, olefin metathesis).
  • Main-group chemistry — the chemistry of s- and p-block elements, including boranes, silicones and phosphorus compounds.
  • Solid-state and materials chemistry — oxides, semiconductors, superconductors, zeolites and metal–organic frameworks.
  • Bioinorganic chemistry — metal ions in biology: iron in haemoglobin, magnesium in chlorophyll, molybdenum in nitrogenase, zinc fingers.
  • Industrial inorganic chemistry — bulk processes such as the Haber–Bosch synthesis of ammonia, the Ostwald process for nitric acid, the contact process for sulfuric acid, and the chloralkali industry.

Key concepts

Inorganic chemists describe bonding with models ranging from ionic and covalent pictures to molecular-orbital theory; they classify acids and bases by the Lewis definition (electron-pair acceptors and donors) as well as the Brønsted–Lowry definition; and they rationalise reactivity with concepts such as oxidation state, the 18-electron rule, hard–soft acid–base theory, and the spectrochemical series. Characteristic techniques include X-ray crystallography, NMR and EPR spectroscopy, magnetochemistry and electrochemistry. The field underpins catalysis, mining and metallurgy, fertilisers, pigments, pharmaceuticals (e.g. the platinum drug cisplatin) and much of modern energy technology, from batteries to solar cells.

History

The subject grew from alchemy and early metallurgy through the nineteenth-century systematisation of the elements — Dalton's atomic theory, Mendeleev's periodic table (1869) — and landmark syntheses and discoveries such as the isolation of aluminium, the noble gases (Ramsay) and the first noble-gas compound (Bartlett, 1962). Werner's coordination theory resolved the structure of metal ammines that classical valence theory could not. In the twentieth century the discovery of ferrocene (1951) launched modern organometallic chemistry, and the field's importance is reflected in numerous Nobel Prizes, including those for the Haber process, Ziegler–Natta catalysis, and olefin metathesis.
