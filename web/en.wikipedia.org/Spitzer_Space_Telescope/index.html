The Spitzer Space Telescope, launched in 2003, was an infrared space telescope and the fourth and final of NASA's Great Observatories programme, alongside Hubble, the Compton Gamma Ray Observatory, and the Chandra X-ray Observatory. It was named in honour of astrophysicist Lyman Spitzer, who had proposed space telescopes in 1946.

Mission design

Spitzer's 85 cm primary mirror and instruments were cooled to a few kelvin above absolute zero by liquid helium. Instead of orbiting Earth, Spitzer followed an Earth-trailing heliocentric orbit, drifting away from Earth by about 15 million kilometres per year — keeping it away from Earth's heat and allowing efficient radiative cooling. The cryogenic ("cold") mission lasted 5.5 years, far beyond the 2.5-year requirement, until the helium ran out in May 2009. Two of its IRAC channels (3.6 and 4.5 μm) continued working in the "warm mission" for another decade.

Discoveries

Spitzer peered through dust to study star-forming regions, mapped the structure of the Milky Way (the GLIMPSE survey), detected some of the most distant galaxies known, directly imaged exoplanets and measured their atmospheres, and characterised the seven Earth-sized planets of the TRAPPIST-1 system (2017). It observed comet Tempel 1's ejecta after the Deep Impact collision and discovered a huge, previously unseen ring around Saturn in 2009.

End of mission

As Spitzer drifted ever further from Earth, communications became increasingly difficult; the mission was ended on 30 January 2020, after more than 16 years of operation. Its archive remains a major resource, and it paved the way for the James Webb Space Telescope.
