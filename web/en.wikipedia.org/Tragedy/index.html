Tragedy

An ancient Greek tragic mask

Tragedy (from the Greek: τραγῳδία, tragōidia) is a genre of drama based on human suffering and, principally, the terrible or sorrowful events that befall a main character. Traditionally, the intention of tragedy is to invoke an accompanying catharsis, or a pain that awakens pleasure. While many cultures have developed forms that provoke this paradoxical response, the term tragedy often refers to a specific tradition of drama that has played a unique and important role historically in the self-definition of Western civilisation.

Greek tragedy

Athenian tragedy of the 5th century BCE, developed from the dithyrambic choruses sung in honour of Dionysus, is the earliest surviving form. Of the works of the great tragedians Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides, only a small fraction survive, including Aeschylus's Oresteia, Sophocles' Oedipus Rex and Antigone, and Euripides' Medea and The Bacchae. Aristotle's Poetics (c. 335 BCE) offers the first surviving theory of the genre: tragedy is an imitation of a serious, complete action of a certain magnitude, whose plot turns on reversal (peripeteia) and recognition (anagnorisis), and whose hero falls through some error (hamartia), arousing pity and fear that effect catharsis in the audience.

Later developments

Roman tragedy, through Seneca, transmitted the form to Renaissance Europe, where it flourished in Elizabethan and Jacobean England — Shakespeare's Hamlet, Othello, King Lear and Macbeth are its most famous products — and in the classical theatre of Corneille and Racine in France. From the 18th century onward, "bourgeois" and domestic tragedy brought ordinary protagonists to the stage, a development completed by modern dramatists such as Ibsen, Strindberg, Chekhov and Arthur Miller, whose 1949 essay "Tragedy and the Common Man" argued that the common person is as apt a subject for tragedy as kings. The term is also used more loosely for any narrative of downfall and suffering, and, in everyday language, for disastrous events.
