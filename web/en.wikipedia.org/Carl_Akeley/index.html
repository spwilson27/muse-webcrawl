Carl Akeley

Carl Ethan Akeley (19 May 1864 – 17 November 1926) was an American naturalist, taxidermist, artist, and inventor, regarded as the founder of modern taxidermy. He created some of the most celebrated habitat dioramas in museum history and, late in life, became an advocate for African wildlife conservation.

Career

Born in Clarendon, New York, Akeley apprenticed in commercial taxidermy before working at the Milwaukee Public Museum, where he mounted a muskrat group — often cited as the first habitat diorama. At Chicago's Field Museum he developed the "Akeley method": building a sculpted mannequin of the animal over an armature and stretching the skin over it, capturing lifelike musculature and motion. His elephant group "The Fighting African Elephants" remains a Field Museum centrepiece.

African Hall and conservation

From 1909 Akeley worked for the American Museum of Natural History in New York, leading expeditions to Africa to collect for the planned African Hall. His encounters with mountain gorillas in the Virunga Mountains transformed him: he campaigned for their protection, and in 1925 persuaded King Albert I of Belgium to create Albert National Park (now Virunga National Park), Africa's first national park. He died of fever in the Virungas in 1926 during his fifth expedition and was buried at Mount Mikeno, near the gorillas he studied.

Inventions

Akeley also invented the Akeley camera, a rugged motion-picture camera with a novel pan-tilt head widely used by early filmmakers and in the First World War, and a cement gun used in construction and in his taxidermy work.
