Time-division multiplexing

Time-division multiplexing interleaves samples from several signals into successive time slots of a single channel.

Time-division multiplexing (TDM) is a method of transmitting and receiving independent signals over a common signal path by means of synchronised switches at each end of the transmission line so that each signal appears on the line only a fraction of time according to agreed rules, e.g. with each signal's time slot set in a repeating frame structure. TDM is used for long-distance and local loop applications.

How it works

In synchronous TDM, each input signal is assigned a fixed time slot in every frame, whether or not it has data to send. The multiplexer samples or buffers each input in turn and transmits the combined stream at the aggregate rate; the demultiplexer at the far end separates the slots back into individual channels. Statistical TDM (asynchronous TDM) improves efficiency by allocating slots only to active inputs, at the cost of needing to identify which channel each slot belongs to.

Applications

TDM formed the basis of the digital telephone network: the North American T-carrier system (T1 carrying 24 voice channels at 1.544 Mbit/s) and the European E-carrier system (E1 carrying 30 channels at 2.048 Mbit/s), built on pulse-code modulation sampling of voice at 8 kHz. Higher-level hierarchies such as SONET/SDH multiplexed many such streams onto optical fibre. TDM concepts also appear in time-division duplex radio systems and in time-division multiple access (TDMA) cellular networks.
