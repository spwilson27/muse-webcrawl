Animal testing, also known as animal experimentation, animal research, and in vivo testing, is the use of non-human animals in experiments that seek to control the variables that affect the behaviour or biological system under study. It is used in fundamental biological research, applied biomedical research, and the safety testing of drugs, chemicals, and consumer products.

Uses

Animals are used in basic research into genetics, developmental biology, and physiology; in applied research toward the diagnosis and treatment of disease; in xenotransplantation; and in toxicity testing, including the testing of pharmaceuticals, food additives, pesticides, and industrial chemicals. Mice and rats account for the large majority of animals used, followed by fish, birds, rabbits, and farm animals; non-human primates make up a small fraction.

Regulation and ethics

Animal research is governed by legislation in most countries, such as the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986 in the United Kingdom and the Animal Welfare Act in the United States. Ethical review commonly applies the "three Rs" framework proposed by Russell and Burch in 1959: replacement (of animals with non-animal methods where possible), reduction (of the number of animals used), and refinement (of procedures to minimise suffering). The practice remains controversial, with animal rights organisations opposing it and scientific bodies arguing that it has been essential to many medical advances, from insulin and antibiotics to vaccines and organ transplantation.

Alternatives

Alternatives to animal testing include in vitro cell and tissue culture, computer (in silico) modelling, studies with human volunteers, and imaging techniques. Regulatory acceptance of such methods has expanded, and the European Union banned the testing of cosmetics on animals in 2013.
