Battle of the Milvian Bridge
Date28 October AD 312
LocationMilvian Bridge, Rome, over the Tiber
ResultVictory for Constantine; death of Maxentius

The Battle of the Milvian Bridge took place on 28 October AD 312 between the Roman emperors Constantine I and Maxentius, rivals for control of the western empire. Constantine's victory left him master of the West and, by tradition, marks his conversion to Christianity and the turning point in the religion's rise within the Roman world.

Background

After the collapse of Diocletian's Tetrarchy, Maxentius seized Italy and Africa from his base in Rome, while Constantine ruled Gaul and Britain. In 312 Constantine invaded Italy, defeating Maxentius's forces at Turin and Verona. Rather than await a siege behind Rome's walls, Maxentius — reportedly encouraged by omens and the Sibylline Books — marched out to fight north of the city, with the Tiber at his back and the Milvian Bridge, partly replaced by a pontoon bridge, as his line of retreat.

The vision and the battle

Christian tradition, recorded by Lactantius and by Eusebius, holds that before the battle Constantine saw a vision — a cross of light in the sky with the words "In hoc signo vinces" ("In this sign, conquer") — and ordered his soldiers to mark their shields with the Christian Chi-Rho symbol. In the battle, Constantine's cavalry and infantry drove Maxentius's army back onto the river; the pontoon bridge collapsed under the rout, and Maxentius drowned in the Tiber along with many of his troops. His body was recovered and his head paraded through Rome.

Aftermath

Constantine entered Rome in triumph and credited the Christian God with his victory. The following year he and Licinius issued the Edict of Milan, legalising Christianity throughout the empire. The Arch of Constantine, dedicated in 315, commemorates the battle, which is also depicted in Raphael's famous fresco in the Vatican.
