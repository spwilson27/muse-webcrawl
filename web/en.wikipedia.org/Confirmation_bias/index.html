Confirmation bias

Confirmation bias is the tendency to search for, interpret, favour, and recall information in a way that confirms or supports one's prior beliefs or values. People display this bias when they select information that supports their views, ignoring contrary information, or when they interpret ambiguous evidence as supporting their existing attitudes. The effect is strongest for desired outcomes, emotionally charged issues, and deeply entrenched beliefs.

Selective attention to confirming evidence

Effects

  • Biased search for information — asking questions likely to yield answers that confirm a hypothesis, as in Peter Wason's 2-4-6 task.
  • Biased interpretation — judging the same evidence differently depending on prior beliefs; in a classic study by Lord, Ross and Lepper (1979), supporters and opponents of capital punishment each found mixed studies to support their own position.
  • Biased memory — remembering confirming instances more readily than disconfirming ones.
  • Attitude polarisation — exposure to mixed evidence can make opposing groups more extreme.

History of the concept

Francis Bacon observed in the Novum Organum (1620) that the human understanding, once it has adopted an opinion, "draws all things else to support and agree with it". The term "confirmation bias" was coined by the English psychologist Peter Wason in the 1960s.

Consequences and countermeasures

Confirmation bias contributes to overconfidence, poor medical and legal decisions, scientific disputes, and the persistence of misinformation and filter bubbles online. Proposed remedies include seeking disconfirming evidence, "consider the opposite" strategies, structured analytic techniques, and pre-registration of scientific hypotheses.
