The Indus script, also known as the Harappan script and the Indus Valley script, is a corpus of symbols produced by the Indus Valley Civilisation. Most inscriptions containing these symbols are extremely short, making it difficult to judge whether they constitute a writing system used to record the as-yet unidentified language of the Indus Valley Civilisation. Despite many attempts, the script has not been deciphered, and no known bilingual texts have been found to aid decipherment.

The script generally wrote from right to left. Some 400 to 600 distinct signs have been identified, appearing chiefly on small steatite stamp seals, but also on pottery, bronze tools, and larger public inscriptions such as the wooden signboard from Dholavira. The average inscription is only about five signs long; the longest contains 34.

Decipherment attempts

More than 100 decipherment claims have been published, most proposing that the underlying language was Dravidian (the school associated with Asko Parpola and Iravatham Mahadevan, who read the common "fish" sign as a Dravidian homophone), and others an early Indo-Aryan, Munda, or lost language. In 2004, Steve Farmer, Richard Sproat, and Michael Witzel argued controversially that the signs are non-linguistic symbols rather than writing at all, citing the brevity of the texts and the absence of long inscriptions; statistical studies of sign sequences since then have produced evidence on both sides of that debate.

Corpus

Mahadevan's 1977 concordance catalogued nearly 3,000 texts; the ongoing Corpus of Indus Seals and Inscriptions continues the work. Because inscriptions are short and the language unknown, the Indus script remains, along with Linear A and rongorongo, one of the most famous undeciphered writing systems in the world.
