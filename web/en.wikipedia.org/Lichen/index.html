Lichen

A lichen is a composite organism arising from a symbiosis between a fungus (the mycobiont) and a photosynthetic partner (the photobiont), usually a green alga or a cyanobacterium, living within a structured fungal body called a thallus. Roughly 20,000 species are known. Lichens are pioneer colonisers of bare rock, bark, and soil, occur from polar deserts to rainforests, and some individuals are among the oldest living organisms on Earth.

Symbiosis

The fungus provides structure, shelter, and absorbed water and minerals, while the photobiont supplies sugars through photosynthesis; cyanobacterial partners can also fix atmospheric nitrogen. Modern research has revealed additional partners, including basidiomycete yeasts embedded in the cortex of many lichens. The symbiosis lets lichens survive extremes of desiccation, cold, and radiation: specimens have survived exposure to outer space.

Forms and reproduction

Lichens are grouped by growth form: crustose (crust-like, inseparable from the substrate), foliose (leafy lobes), fruticose (shrubby or beard-like), and others such as squamulose and gelatinous forms. They reproduce sexually through fungal fruiting bodies (apothecia) and asexually via propagules such as soredia and isidia containing both partners.

Ecology and uses

Lichens fix carbon and nitrogen in ecosystems, feed reindeer and other animals, shelter invertebrates, and serve as sensitive bioindicators of air pollution because they absorb substances directly from the atmosphere. Historically they yielded dyes (litmus, orchil) and medicines, and they remain models for studying symbiosis and extremophile biology.
