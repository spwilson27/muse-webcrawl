Mariana Trench

Mariana Trench
Location: East of the Mariana Islands, Pacific Ocean
Length: about 2,550 km (1,580 mi)
Max depth: about 10,935 m at Challenger Deep
Formed by: Subduction of the Pacific Plate

The Mariana Trench is an oceanic trench located in the western Pacific Ocean, about 200 kilometres east of the Mariana Islands. It is the deepest oceanic trench on Earth, reaching a maximum known depth of roughly 10,935 metres (35,876 ft) at its southern end, in the depression known as the Challenger Deep — deep enough that Mount Everest, placed at the bottom, would still be covered by more than two kilometres of water.

Formation

The trench marks the boundary where the Pacific Plate subducts beneath the smaller Mariana Plate. This subduction also built the volcanic island arc of the Marianas and the back-arc Mariana Trough. Water pressure at the bottom exceeds 1,000 standard atmospheres, and temperatures hover between 1 and 4 °C.

Exploration

The trench was first sounded by HMS Challenger in 1875. In 1960 Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh descended to the bottom in the bathyscaphe Trieste; filmmaker James Cameron repeated the feat solo in 2012 in Deepsea Challenger, and from 2019 Victor Vescovo's Limiting Factor made repeated crewed dives. Despite the pressure, the trench supports life including xenophyophores, amphipods and snailfish, and studies have detected both natural and human-made pollutants in its sediments.
