Acid mine drainage

Acid mine drainage (AMD), also called acid rock drainage (ARD), is the outflow of acidic water from metal or coal mines. It forms when sulfide minerals, principally pyrite (FeS₂), are exposed to air and water by mining, producing sulfuric acid that leaches toxic metals such as iron, aluminum, manganese, copper and cadmium into streams. Affected waterways often turn orange with precipitated iron hydroxide colloquially called "yellow boy".

Chemistry

The overall reaction can be summarized as pyrite reacting with oxygen and water to yield ferrous iron, sulfate and hydrogen ions. Once the water becomes acidic, iron- and sulfur-oxidizing bacteria such as Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans accelerate the process enormously, regenerating ferric iron that attacks more pyrite. Drainage can continue for centuries after a mine closes, making AMD one of the most persistent legacies of mining.

Impacts

Acid drainage devastates aquatic ecosystems: low pH and dissolved metals kill fish and invertebrates, and metal precipitates smother streambeds. In the United States alone, tens of thousands of kilometers of streams are affected, notably in Appalachian coal country and at western hard-rock mines. The Rio Tinto in Spain, mined since antiquity, is a classic natural example of the same geochemistry.

Prevention and treatment

Prevention includes excluding water and oxygen from waste rock, capping sulfidic material, and blending with alkaline rock. Active treatment neutralizes the water with lime or caustic soda and precipitates metals as sludge, but must continue indefinitely. Passive systems — anoxic limestone drains, constructed wetlands and successive alkalinity-producing systems — treat smaller flows at lower cost and are widely used at abandoned mine sites.
