Hyperemesis gravidarum

Hyperemesis gravidarum (HG) is a pregnancy complication characterised by severe nausea and vomiting, weight loss, and possibly dehydration. It is considered more severe than morning sickness, and signs can include vomiting several times a day, losing more than 5% of pre-pregnancy body weight, ketonuria, and electrolyte imbalance. Hyperemesis gravidarum typically occurs in the first trimester and affects an estimated 0.3–3% of pregnancies.

Causes

The exact cause is unknown, but it is believed to be related to rapidly rising levels of hormones such as human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) and estrogen. Recent research has implicated the hormone GDF15, produced by the fetus, and the mother's sensitivity to it. Other risk factors include a previous history of HG, a family history, multiple pregnancy, and molar pregnancy.

Consequences

Untreated, severe vomiting can lead to dehydration, malnutrition, Wernicke encephalopathy from thiamine deficiency, and harm to the fetus including low birth weight and preterm birth. The novelist Charlotte Brontë is often cited as a famous historical case, dying in 1855 in early pregnancy.

Treatment

Management begins with dietary modification and antiemetics such as pyridoxine and doxylamine; severe cases require hospital admission, intravenous rehydration, thiamine supplementation, and other medications such as metoclopramide or ondansetron. In refractory cases, enteral or parenteral nutrition may be needed.
