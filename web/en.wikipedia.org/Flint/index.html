Flint

Flint, occasionally flintstone, is a sedimentary cryptocrystalline form of the mineral quartz, categorized as the variety of chert that occurs in chalk or marly limestone. Flint was widely used historically to make stone tools and start fires.

Occurrence

Flint occurs as nodules and masses within sedimentary rocks such as chalks and limestones. Inside the nodule, flint is usually dark grey, black, green, white, or brown in colour, and often has a glassy or waxy appearance. The exact mode of formation is not yet clear, but it involves the redistribution of silica from sponges and other organisms within the sediment.

Uses

Stone tools

Because flint fractures with a conchoidal fracture, producing sharp edges, it was one of the principal materials of the Stone Age. Knapped flint was used for arrowheads, hand axes, blades, and scrapers; mining sites such as Grime's Graves in England show the scale of prehistoric extraction.

Fire starting

When struck against steel, flint yields sparks hot enough to ignite tinder. This property gave rise to flintlock firearms, in which a piece of flint striking a steel frizzen ignited the priming charge, and to the ferrocerium "flints" used in modern lighters.

Building material

Flint nodules have been used for centuries as a building material, particularly in flint churches and walls in southern and eastern England, where knapped flint flushwork is a characteristic decorative technique.
