Patrice Émery Lumumba (2 July 1925 – 17 January 1961) was a Congolese independence leader and politician who served as the first prime minister of the independent Republic of the Congo (now the Democratic Republic of the Congo) from June to September 1960. A founder of the Mouvement National Congolais (MNC), he played a central role in the Congo's transition from Belgian colonial rule to independence, and his murder in January 1961 — with Belgian and American involvement later documented — made him a martyr of Pan-Africanism and anti-colonial struggle.

Early life and rise

Born in Onalua in the Kasai region, Lumumba was educated at mission schools and worked as a postal clerk in Stanleyville (Kisangani) and as a beer salesman, becoming a prominent évolué and writer in Congolese civic associations. In 1958 he co-founded the MNC, the colony's first nationwide political party, and attended the All-African Peoples' Conference in Accra, where he met Kwame Nkrumah. After riots in Léopoldville in January 1959, Belgium rapidly conceded independence; the MNC won the May 1960 elections, and Lumumba formed a government with Joseph Kasa-Vubu as president. His unscheduled speech at the independence ceremony on 30 June 1960, answering King Baudouin's praise of colonisation with a denunciation of its humiliations, made him famous across Africa.

Congo Crisis and death

Within days the army mutinied and the mineral-rich province of Katanga seceded under Moise Tshombe with Belgian support. Lumumba's appeals to the United States and the United Nations failing to restore order, he turned to the Soviet Union for help, alarming the West at the height of the Cold War. In September Kasa-Vubu dismissed him; days later Colonel Joseph Mobutu seized power in a coup. Lumumba escaped house arrest but was captured on 1 December 1960, imprisoned, and flown to Katanga, where he was tortured and executed by firing squad on 17 January 1961 under the supervision of Katangan and Belgian officers; his body was dissolved in acid. Only the return of one of his teeth to his family by Belgium in 2022 closed the long search for his remains.

Legacy and responsibility

Investigations have established converging responsibilities: the Church Committee of the US Senate (1975) documented CIA plotting to assassinate Lumumba, while a Belgian parliamentary inquiry (2001) found that Belgian officials bore moral responsibility, prompting an official Belgian apology in 2002. Lumumba remains a national hero of the Congo and an icon of African liberation; universities, streets and monuments across Africa and beyond bear his name, and his political testament, written in prison, is a classic of anti-colonial literature.
