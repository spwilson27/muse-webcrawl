Cross-laminated timber

Cross-laminated timber (CLT) is a subcategory of engineered wood consisting of layers of solid-sawn lumber (lamellae) stacked crosswise — typically at 90 degrees — and glued together under pressure. Panels are usually composed of three, five, seven, or nine layers and can be manufactured in large dimensions for use as walls, floors, and roofs, including in multi-storey "mass timber" buildings.

Cross-section of a CLT panel.

History

CLT was developed in Europe in the early 1990s, building on research in Switzerland and Austria; the first CLT-like products emerged from work at the Technical University of Graz and in German-speaking alpine countries, where regulations and forestry traditions favoured solid wood construction. From the 2000s it spread internationally, enabling timber high-rises such as the Stadthaus in London (2009), Brock Commons in Vancouver (2017), and Mjøstårnet in Norway (2019), at 85.4 m one of the tallest timber buildings in the world.

Manufacturing

Boards of softwood (commonly spruce, pine, fir, or larch) are kiln-dried, graded, finger-jointed, and planed, then stacked with alternating grain direction and bonded with structural adhesives (such as polyurethane or melamine resins) in a hydraulic or vacuum press. Finished panels can be up to roughly 3.5 m wide and 20 m long and are cut to shape with CNC machinery before delivery to site.

Properties and use

The crosswise layup gives CLT dimensional stability and load-bearing capacity in two directions, allowing it to act as a structural panel comparable in role to precast concrete but at about one-fifth the weight. Proponents cite rapid prefabricated assembly, carbon storage in the wood, and reduced embodied emissions relative to concrete and steel; considerations include fire performance (CLT chars predictably), moisture management, acoustics, and cost.
