Jus sanguinis (Latin for "right of blood") is a principle of nationality law by which citizenship is determined or acquired by the nationality or citizenship of one or both parents. Children at birth may be citizens of a particular state if either or both of their parents have citizenship in that state, regardless of the place of birth.

Overview

Jus sanguinis is one of the two main principles for transmitting citizenship at birth, the other being jus soli ("right of soil"), under which citizenship follows the place of birth. Many states combine the two: a child born abroad to a citizen may acquire citizenship by descent, while children born in the territory to foreign parents may acquire it by birthplace, sometimes conditionally.

Historically, jus sanguinis reflected the idea of the nation as a community of descent — an ethnic or familial bond — whereas jus soli reflected a territorial, civic conception. In practice most modern legal systems are mixed, and pure forms of either principle are rare.

Application

Under jus sanguinis regimes, citizenship is commonly transmitted automatically at birth if at least one parent is a citizen. Some states limit transmission across generations born abroad: for example, a citizen born abroad may be required to register a child's birth at a consulate, or transmission may stop after one or two generations unless the parent has resided in the home country.

Countries with strong jus sanguinis traditions include Germany, Italy, Spain, Japan, and China. Italy is well known for permitting citizenship by descent potentially without generational limit, subject to conditions such as an unbroken chain of citizenship and rules on female-line transmission before 1948. Ireland allows registration of foreign-born grandchildren of Irish citizens in the Foreign Births Register.

History

The principle has roots in Roman law and in the feudal and tribal systems of early Europe, where membership in a community was inherited. The French Civil Code of 1804 emphasized paternal descent, and the model spread widely across Europe and its legal sphere during the nineteenth century. Reforms in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, such as Germany's nationality reform of 2000, introduced elements of jus soli into traditionally descent-based systems.

Advantages and disadvantages

Supporters argue jus sanguinis preserves the connection between emigrants and their homeland and avoids statelessness for children born abroad. Critics note it can create hereditary classes of non-citizens among long-settled immigrant populations and complicate integration. Combined systems attempt to balance these concerns.
