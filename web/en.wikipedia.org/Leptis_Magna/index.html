Leptis Magna

Leptis Magna (also Lepcis Magna) was a prominent city of the Carthaginian Empire and later of Roman Libya, located near present-day Khoms, about 130 km east of Tripoli. Its ruins are among the most spectacular and best-preserved Roman sites in the Mediterranean, and were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1982.

History

Founded by Phoenicians around the 7th century BC, Leptis grew wealthy on the trans-Saharan trade and the export of olive oil. Under Rome it flourished, and its fortunes peaked when a native son, Septimius Severus, became emperor in 193 AD. Severus lavished building projects on his birthplace: a grand new forum and basilica, a triumphal arch, colonnaded streets, an enlarged harbour and the Severan nymphaeum. At its height the city may have held 100,000 inhabitants. Leptis declined after Vandal and Byzantine rule, raids and the silting of its harbour, and was largely abandoned following the Arab conquest in the 7th century; sand buried and preserved its ruins.

Principal remains

Surviving monuments include the Theatre (built 1–2 AD), the Hadrianic Baths, the Hunting Baths with their painted vaults, the market (macellum), the Chalcidicum, the Arch of Septimius Severus and the Severan Forum and Basilica — one of the largest basilicas of the Roman world.
