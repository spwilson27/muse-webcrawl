Grand Tour

Young aristocrats touring the ruins of Italy, 18th century

The Grand Tour was the traditional educational journey of Europe undertaken by upper-class young European men of sufficient means and rank, primarily from the mid-17th century to the early 19th century. The custom flourished from about 1660 until the advent of large-scale rail transport in the 1840s and was associated with a standard itinerary centred on France and above all Italy.

Itinerary

Accompanied by a tutor or "bear-leader", the tourist crossed the Channel to Calais, visited Paris, then travelled through Switzerland to Turin, Milan, Florence, and Rome — the essential destination, with its classical ruins and Renaissance art — often continuing to Naples, Pompeii and Herculaneum, and returning via Venice, Germany, and the Low Countries. The tour could last months or several years, and travellers collected antiquities, commissioned portraits and vedute paintings, and acquired the polish expected of future statesmen.

Cultural significance

The Grand Tour shaped British and northern European taste: it spread Palladian and later Neoclassical architecture, fed the antiquities market, and produced classic travel accounts by writers from John Evelyn to Goethe, whose Italian Journey (1816–17) recorded his transformative trip of 1786–88. Its popularity declined as railways and steamships democratised travel and as the Napoleonic Wars and shifting fashions changed the meaning of the journey.
