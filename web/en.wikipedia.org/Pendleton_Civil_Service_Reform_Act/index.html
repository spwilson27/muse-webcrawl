Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act

EnactedJanuary 16, 1883
Signed byPresident Chester A. Arthur
SponsorSenator George H. Pendleton (D-OH)
PurposeMerit-based federal employment

The Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act is a United States federal law passed in 1883 that established that positions within the federal government should be awarded on the basis of merit instead of political affiliation (the spoils system). The act provided selection of government employees by competitive exams, and it made it illegal to fire or demote covered employees for political reasons.

Background

Since the presidency of Andrew Jackson, federal jobs had been distributed as patronage. The assassination of President James A. Garfield in 1881 by Charles J. Guiteau, a disgruntled office-seeker, galvanised public demand for reform. President Chester A. Arthur, himself a product of the patronage system, pressed Congress to act and signed the bill.

Provisions

The act created a bipartisan Civil Service Commission to administer examinations and enforce the rules. Initially it covered only about 10% of federal employees—some 13,900 positions—but it allowed presidents to extend coverage by executive order, and successive administrations "blanketed in" their own appointees, so the merit system steadily expanded. It also banned compulsory political contributions ("assessments") from federal employees.

Legacy

The Pendleton Act began the transformation of the American federal bureaucracy into a professional civil service, shifting party finance away from office-holders toward business donors. The Civil Service Commission's functions later passed to the Office of Personnel Management and the Merit Systems Protection Board under the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978.
