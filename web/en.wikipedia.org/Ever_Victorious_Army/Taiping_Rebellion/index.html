Taiping Rebellion

DateDecember 1850 – August 1864
LocationChina
ResultQing victory; fall of the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom
BelligerentsQing dynasty (with British and French support) vs. Taiping Heavenly Kingdom

The Taiping Rebellion, also known as the Taiping Civil War, was a massive rebellion and civil war in China between the Manchu-led Qing dynasty and the Hakka-led Taiping Heavenly Kingdom under Hong Xiuquan. Lasting from 1850 to 1864, it was one of the deadliest conflicts in history, with total deaths commonly estimated at 20–30 million, including civilians.

Origins

Hong Xiuquan, a Hakka schoolteacher who had repeatedly failed the imperial examinations, came to believe — influenced by Christian pamphlets and his own visions — that he was the younger brother of Jesus Christ, sent to rid China of the "demons" he identified with the Manchu Qing. His movement, the God Worshipping Society, grew amid poverty, ethnic tension, and Qing weakness after the First Opium War.

Course of the war

The rebellion began in Guangxi in 1850. Taiping forces swept north, capturing Nanjing in 1853, which became their capital, Tianjing ("Heavenly Capital"). The Taiping proclaimed radical reforms: communal property, equality of men and women in principle, a new calendar, and bans on opium, foot-binding, and prostitution. A northern expedition failed to take Beijing, and internal purges — notably the Tianjing Incident of 1856 — fatally weakened the leadership.

The Qing response was led largely by regional armies, above all Zeng Guofan's Xiang Army, which slowly ground down Taiping territory in the Yangtze valley. Foreign-officered units, including the Ever Victorious Army commanded first by Frederick Townsend Ward and then by Charles George Gordon, defended Shanghai and aided the Qing advance. Nanjing fell in July 1864; Hong Xiuquan had died weeks earlier, and organized resistance soon collapsed.

Legacy

The rebellion devastated some of China's richest provinces, weakened the Qing, accelerated the rise of regional militarism, and remains a landmark in Chinese historiography — later celebrated by the Chinese Communist Party as a peasant revolutionary movement. The last emperor, Puyi, would face a very different China half a century later.
