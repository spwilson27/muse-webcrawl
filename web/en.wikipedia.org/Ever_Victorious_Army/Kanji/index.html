Kanji

Kanji (漢字, pronounced [kaɲdʑi]) are logographic characters adapted from Chinese characters (hanzi) and used in the modern Japanese writing system, alongside the syllabic scripts hiragana and katakana. Kanji represent words or morphemes, and a single character typically has multiple readings: on'yomi, derived from historical Chinese pronunciations, and kun'yomi, native Japanese readings.

History

Chinese characters reached Japan by the 5th century AD via Korea, initially as the writing of Buddhism, government, and scholarship. Over centuries the Japanese adapted the characters to their own language, developing the kana syllabaries from simplified characters. After the Second World War, the Japanese government standardised a list of characters for general use; today's jōyō kanji list contains 2,136 characters taught through secondary school.

Readings and use

Most kanji have at least two readings. For example, 山 ("mountain") is read san in Sino-Japanese compounds but yama as a standalone word. Kanji are used for most nouns, verb and adjective stems, and proper names, while hiragana supplies inflections and function words and katakana handles foreign loanwords and emphasis. Japanese children learn the 1,026 kyōiku kanji during primary school.

Stroke order and radicals

Characters are written following conventional stroke orders and are classified in dictionaries by their components, especially the 214 historical radicals. Simplified forms (shinjitai) adopted after the war sometimes differ from traditional Chinese and older Japanese forms.
