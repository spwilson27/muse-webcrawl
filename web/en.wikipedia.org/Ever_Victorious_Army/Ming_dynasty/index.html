The Ming dynasty (1368–1644), officially the Great Ming, was an imperial dynasty of China founded by the peasant rebel leader Zhu Yuanzhang, who became the Hongwu Emperor after expelling the Mongol-led Yuan dynasty. The Ming ruled China for 276 years and was the last imperial dynasty in China ruled by ethnic Han Chinese.

History

The Hongwu Emperor rebuilt China's economy and administration after Mongol rule, while ruling with notorious severity. His son, the Yongle Emperor, moved the capital to Beijing, built the Forbidden City, commissioned the great Yongle Encyclopedia, and sponsored the treasure voyages of Admiral Zheng He, whose fleets reached East Africa between 1405 and 1433. The Ming also rebuilt and extended the Great Wall — most of the wall visible today is Ming work — to defend against renewed Mongol incursions, notably after the Tumu Crisis of 1449, when the Zhengtong Emperor was captured.

The later Ming saw flourishing commerce, a silver-based economy fed by trade with Europe and Japan, brilliant porcelain and painting, and the great vernacular novels. But fiscal strain, corruption, eunuch factionalism, peasant rebellions, and the rising Manchu threat weakened the dynasty. In 1644 rebel leader Li Zicheng captured Beijing and the Chongzhen Emperor hanged himself; Manchu forces then entered China and established the Qing dynasty. Ming loyalist regimes (the Southern Ming) resisted until 1662, and Zheng Chenggong (Koxinga) held Taiwan until 1683.

Culture and achievements

Ming China produced blue-and-white porcelain that became a global luxury, advances in printing and literature (novels such as Journey to the West and Jin Ping Mei), Neo-Confucian philosophy under Wang Yangming, and maritime technology. Population roughly doubled, reaching perhaps 160–200 million by 1600, and the capital, Beijing, was among the largest cities in the world.
