Chinese characters are logograms used in the writing of Chinese and other languages, including Japanese (where they are known as kanji) and historically Korean (hanja) and Vietnamese (chữ Hán). Each character generally corresponds to a single syllable and, usually, a morpheme. Tens of thousands of characters have been recorded, though a literate person typically knows three to four thousand.

History

The earliest confirmed Chinese writing is the oracle bone script of the late Shang dynasty (c. 1200 BCE), used for divination. Scripts evolved through bronze inscriptions and seal script; the script was standardised under the Qin dynasty, then developed into the clerical and regular (kaishu) scripts still used today. In the 20th century, the People's Republic of China promoted simplified characters, while traditional characters remain standard in Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macau.

Principles of formation

Characters are traditionally classified by the liùshū (six categories): pictographs, simple ideographs, compound ideographs, phono-semantic compounds (the large majority, combining a semantic radical with a phonetic component), transfer characters, and loan characters. Most characters are built from a limited set of strokes and components (radicals), which are also used to organise dictionaries.

Use in other languages

Chinese characters spread throughout the Sinosphere: Japanese kanji, Korean hanja and Vietnamese chữ Hán adapted them, often alongside indigenous phonetic scripts (kana, hangul, chữ Nôm).
