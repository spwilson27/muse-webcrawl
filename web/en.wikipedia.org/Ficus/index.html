Ficus

Ficus L.
Family: Moraceae
Tribe: Ficeae
Species: about 850

Ficus is a genus of about 850 species of woody trees, shrubs, vines, epiphytes and hemiepiphytes in the family Moraceae. Collectively known as fig trees or figs, they are native throughout the tropics with a few species extending into the semi-warm temperate zone.

The common fig (Ficus carica) is cultivated for its fruit throughout the temperate world. Many other species are grown as ornamentals, including the weeping fig (F. benjamina), the rubber plant (F. elastica) and the fiddle-leaf fig (F. lyrata), while species such as the banyan (F. benghalensis) and the sacred fig (F. religiosa) have deep religious significance in South Asia.

Description

Figs are characterised by their unique inflorescence, the syconium — a fleshy, hollow receptacle lined on the inside with tiny flowers — and by a distinctive pollination syndrome involving species-specific fig wasps of the family Agaonidae. The "fruit" is a multiple fruit formed from the mature syconium. Many species begin life as epiphytes; "strangler figs" send roots to the ground and can eventually envelop and kill their host tree.

Ecology

Figs are keystone species in many tropical forests: because different species fruit at different times of year, they provide food for fruit-eating birds, bats and primates even when other fruit is scarce.
