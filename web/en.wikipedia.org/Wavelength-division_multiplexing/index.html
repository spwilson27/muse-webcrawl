Wavelength-division multiplexing

Wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) is a technology in fiber-optic communications that multiplexes a number of optical carrier signals onto a single optical fiber by using different wavelengths (colors) of laser light. This allows a multiplier of the data-carrying capacity of a fiber and enables bidirectional communications over a single strand.

Types

Coarse WDM (CWDM) uses widely spaced channels (20 nm apart) in the 1270–1610 nm range, typically up to 18 channels, and is used for shorter distances with uncooled lasers. Dense WDM (DWDM) uses closely spaced channels (0.8 nm or less, on the ITU grid in the C-band around 1550 nm), permitting 40, 80, or more channels per fiber, each carrying data rates of 100 Gbit/s or higher in modern systems with coherent detection.

Systems

A WDM system uses a multiplexer at the transmitter to combine signals and a demultiplexer at the receiver to separate them, using components such as arrayed waveguide gratings and thin-film filters. Optical amplifiers (notably erbium-doped fiber amplifiers) allow DWDM signals to travel thousands of kilometres, making WDM the foundation of long-haul and submarine telecommunications networks and of much of the Internet backbone.
