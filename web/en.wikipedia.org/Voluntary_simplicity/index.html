Voluntary simplicity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Voluntary simplicity (also called simple living or downshifting) is a lifestyle characterised by the deliberate reduction of consumption, possessions, and professional striving in favour of time, relationships, self-sufficiency, and non-material sources of satisfaction. Unlike poverty, it is a choice made by people who could afford to consume more.

History

Ideas of simplicity run through many religious and philosophical traditions, from Stoicism and the teachings of Jesus to Buddhist monasticism. In the modern West, Henry David Thoreau's Walden (1854) became a foundational text, recounting his two years living in a cabin at Walden Pond. The term "voluntary simplicity" was popularised by Richard Gregg in 1936 and revived by Duane Elgin's 1981 book of that title, which linked simple living to ecological sustainability and personal growth.

Motivations

Adherents cite varied motives: environmental concern about resource use and waste; financial independence, including the FIRE movement; reduced stress and more leisure time; spiritual fulfilment; and protest against consumer culture. Practices include decluttering, growing food, frugality, repairing rather than replacing goods, living in smaller homes, and working part-time.

Related movements

Voluntary simplicity overlaps with minimalism, the tiny house movement, slow living, degrowth economics, and the anti-consumerist critique found in works such as Affluenza. Critics note that choosing simplicity presupposes a cushion of wealth and security unavailable to the genuinely poor, and that its environmental impact depends on what the saved money is spent on instead.
