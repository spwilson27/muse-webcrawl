Ivan Mazepa

Born: 20 March 1639, Mazepyntsi
Died: 2 October 1709, Bender
Title: Hetman of Zaporizhian Host, 1687–1708

Ivan Stepanovych Mazepa (1639–1709) was Hetman of the Zaporizhian Host — the Cossack Hetmanate of Left-bank Ukraine — from 1687 to 1708. One of the longest-serving hetmans, he was a great patron of the Orthodox Church and the arts, and he remains one of the most contested figures in Ukrainian and Russian history: a traitor in the Russian imperial tradition, a hero of statehood in the Ukrainian one.

Early life

Mazepa was born into a Ukrainian noble family near Bila Tserkva and educated at the Kyiv-Mohyla Academy and abroad; he served as a page at the court of King John II Casimir of Poland. A court scandal — immortalised, with much embellishment, by Voltaire and by Byron's poem Mazeppa — ended his Polish service: he was supposedly bound naked to a wild horse and set loose, surviving to join the Cossacks. From the 1660s he rose in Cossack service under hetmans Petro Doroshenko and Ivan Samoilovych, becoming a trusted diplomat.

Hetmanate

Elected hetman in 1687 after Samoilovych's fall, Mazepa consolidated the Hetmanate under Moscow's suzerainty while governing with considerable autonomy. He amassed great wealth, endowed churches and monasteries in the style now called Ukrainian or "Mazepa" Baroque, supported the Kyiv Academy, and made Kyiv and Chernihiv centres of learning and print. For two decades he served Peter the Great loyally in campaigns against the Crimean Tatars, the Ottomans and Sweden.

Defection and Poltava

Fearing Peter's plans to abolish Cossack autonomy — and with Swedish armies advancing in the Great Northern War — Mazepa secretly negotiated with Charles XII and Stanisław Leszczyński. In October 1708 he defected to the Swedes with a few thousand Cossacks. Peter responded with fury: Mazepa was anathematised by the Orthodox Church, his capital Baturyn was stormed and its inhabitants massacred, and Prince Menshikov supervised the election of a replacement hetman. After the Russian victory at Poltava (1709), Mazepa fled with Charles to Bender in Ottoman Moldavia, where he died in October 1709.

Legacy

Mazepa's name became a byword for treachery in Russian culture — Pushkin's poem Poltava is the classic statement — while Ukrainian national historiography treats his defection as a bid for independence. His image appears on the Ukrainian 10-hryvnia banknote, and streets and monuments across Ukraine bear his name.
