The Western Roman Empire was the western portion of the Roman Empire following its administrative division — first under Diocletian's reforms and definitively after the death of Theodosius I in 395 AD, when the empire was split between his sons Honorius (west) and Arcadius (east). It comprised Italy, Gaul, Hispania, Britain for a time, and north-west Africa, administered from Milan and later Ravenna.

Decline

Through the 5th century the West suffered barbarian invasions and migrations — the crossing of the Rhine in 406, the Visigothic sack of Rome in 410, and the Vandal sack in 455 — alongside civil wars, shrinking tax revenues and growing dependence on Germanic foederati troops. Strongmen such as Stilicho and Aëtius (victor over Attila at the Catalaunian Plains in 451) propped up a succession of weak emperors.

Fall

On 4 September 476 the Germanic leader Odoacer deposed the last western emperor, Romulus Augustulus, and sent the imperial insignia to Constantinople — the conventional date for the fall of the Western Empire. The Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire, wealthier and more defensible, survived for nearly another thousand years. The West fragmented into Germanic successor kingdoms, including those of the Visigoths, Ostrogoths and Franks.

Legacy

The Western Empire's institutions, law, language and church shaped medieval Europe: Latin evolved into the Romance languages, Roman law was preserved in codes such as that of the Visigoths, and the Roman Catholic Church maintained much of the empire's administrative geography.
