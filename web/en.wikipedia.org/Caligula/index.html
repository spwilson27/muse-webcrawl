Gaius Caesar Augustus Germanicus (31 August 12 – 24 January 41), better known by his nickname Caligula ("little boot"), was Roman emperor from 37 to 41 CE, the third of the Julio-Claudian dynasty. Remembered in the ancient sources as a tyrant of legendary cruelty and extravagance, he is among the most notorious rulers of antiquity, though modern historians question how much of the tradition is hostile senatorial propaganda.

Early life and accession

Son of the popular general Germanicus and great-grandson of Augustus, the young Gaius earned his nickname as a toddler mascot in his father's Rhine camp, wearing miniature soldier's boots (caligae). After Tiberius's death in 37, the Senate and people welcomed him with enormous goodwill; he freed prisoners, abolished treason trials, and staged lavish games.

Reign

Following a serious illness in late 37, relations with the Senate deteriorated into executions, humiliations, and demands for divine honours. Ancient authors (Suetonius, Cassius Dio, Josephus, Philo) recount episodes such as his (probably mythical) plan to make his horse Incitatus a consul, a bridge of boats across the Bay of Baiae, and an aborted invasion of Britain. His annexation of Mauretania and public works—including aqueducts and harbour improvements—were substantial, but his spending emptied the treasury and his planned statue in the Temple in Jerusalem nearly provoked revolt. On 24 January 41, officers of the Praetorian Guard led by Cassius Chaerea assassinated him, along with his wife Caesonia and infant daughter; his uncle Claudius succeeded him.

Historiography

All surviving accounts are hostile and written after his death; scholars debate whether Caligula was genuinely deranged—possibly by illness—or a shrewd, if ruthless, autocrat whose style offended senatorial sensibilities.
