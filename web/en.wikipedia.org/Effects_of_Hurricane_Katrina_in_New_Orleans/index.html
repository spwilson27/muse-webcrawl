Effects of Hurricane Katrina in New Orleans

The effects of Hurricane Katrina in New Orleans were among the most devastating of any natural disaster in United States history. As the hurricane made landfall on the morning of Monday, 29 August 2005, storm surge caused failures of the levee system protecting the city, flooding roughly 80 percent of New Orleans. Tens of thousands of residents who had not evacuated were stranded, and more than 1,000 people in the city lost their lives in the storm and its aftermath.

Preparations and evacuation

On 28 August, Mayor Ray Nagin ordered the first mandatory evacuation in the city's history, and an estimated 80 percent of the population left. The Louisiana Superdome was opened as a shelter of last resort for those who could not evacuate, eventually housing some 30,000 people in deteriorating conditions. The contraflow lane reversal on highways sped the evacuation but poorer residents without cars were largely left behind.

Levee failures and flooding

Although the city was spared the worst of Katrina's winds, the storm surge overwhelmed the drainage canals. Breaches occurred on the 17th Street Canal, the London Avenue Canal, and the Industrial Canal, among others. Water poured into neighbourhoods including the Lower Ninth Ward, Gentilly, Lakeview, and New Orleans East. Subsequent investigations by the American Society of Civil Engineers and others found that the levee failures were principally the result of design and construction flaws in the system built by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, rather than the storm exceeding design specifications in many locations.

Humanitarian crisis

In the days following the storm, conditions in the city deteriorated sharply. Thousands gathered at the Ernest N. Morial Convention Center without food, water, or sanitation. Rescue operations by the Coast Guard and others saved tens of thousands from rooftops. Lawlessness was widely reported, though later accounts found some reports exaggerated. Federal, state, and local responses were heavily criticised; FEMA director Michael D. Brown resigned, and the disaster prompted major reforms in emergency management.

Recovery

The city was drained by early October 2005, but rebuilding took years. New Orleans' population fell from about 484,000 before the storm to roughly 230,000 in mid-2006, recovering gradually thereafter. The federal government funded a rebuilt hurricane protection system costing some $14.5 billion, completed in 2011. Debates over rebuilding low-lying neighbourhoods, the fate of public housing, the school system — converted to a largely charter-based model — and the racial and economic disparities exposed by the disaster continued for decades.
