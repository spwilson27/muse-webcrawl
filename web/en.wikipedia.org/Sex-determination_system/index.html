A sex-determination system is a biological system that determines the development of sexual characteristics in an organism. Most organisms that create their offspring using sexual reproduction have two common sexes, male and female, although hermaphroditic species produce individuals with both types of reproductive organs. Sex determination may be genetic — governed by sex chromosomes or other genes — or environmental, in which cues such as temperature control development.

Chromosomal systems

XY system

In the XY sex-determination system, found in most mammals, many insects, and some plants, females have two X chromosomes (XX) and males have one X and one Y (XY). In mammals the SRY gene on the Y chromosome triggers testis development; in the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster, by contrast, sex is set by the ratio of X chromosomes to sets of autosomes, and the Y is required only for male fertility.

ZW system

In the ZW system, found in birds, many reptiles, some fish, butterflies and moths, the female is the heterogametic sex (ZW) and the male is homogametic (ZZ). In birds, dosage of the DMRT1 gene on the Z chromosome is a major determinant of testis development.

XO and other systems

In the XO system, found in grasshoppers, crickets, and the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, females are XX and males have a single X and no second sex chromosome. Some species show multiple sex chromosomes, complex hybrid systems, or have secondarily lost sex chromosomes altogether.

Haplodiploidy

In haplodiploidy, males develop from unfertilised eggs and are haploid, while females develop from fertilised eggs and are diploid. The system characterises the insect order Hymenoptera (ants, bees, and wasps) and occurs independently in some beetles, mites, and other groups. It is thought to facilitate the evolution of eusociality, because sisters in a colony can be more closely related to one another than to their own offspring.

Environmental sex determination

In environmental sex determination, sex is fixed after conception by external cues. The best-studied case is temperature-dependent sex determination in reptiles: in many turtles, warm eggs produce females and cool eggs males, while in crocodilians and some lizards the pattern is reversed or more complex. In some fish, social status determines sex — in clownfish the dominant individual of a group is female, and in many wrasses large dominant males arise from females that change sex. Other species use photoperiod, nutrition, or the site of settlement, as in the marine worm Bonellia viridis, whose larvae become females if they settle on the sea floor and tiny males if they settle on a female.

Evolution

Sex-determination mechanisms evolve rapidly: closely related species may use different master switches, and sex chromosomes are thought to arise repeatedly from ordinary autosomes when a new sex-determining gene appears and recombination around it is suppressed, after which the heterogametic chromosome (Y or W) tends to degenerate. Transitions between genetic and environmental systems have occurred many times, and climate change is a conservation concern for species with temperature-dependent sex determination because skewed sex ratios can result.
