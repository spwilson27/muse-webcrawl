Multiple unit

A multiple unit (MU) is a self-propelled train unit capable of coupling with other units of the same or similar type and still being controlled from one cab. The term generally applies to passenger trains in which each car, or most cars in the train, have their own means of propulsion. When a single driver can control multiple units together, they are said to be working in multiple, with a single crew operating the whole train.

Multiple units come in two main varieties: electric multiple units (EMUs), which draw power from an external electrical supply such as an overhead line or a third rail, and diesel multiple units (DMUs), which carry diesel engines. In an EMU the traction motors may be distributed on several cars; in a DMU each powered car has its own engine and transmission.

History

The multiple unit control system was developed by Frank J. Sprague and first applied in Chicago in 1897. It allowed several motor cars to be driven from one controller via electrical connections, replacing the need for a driver in each powered car. The concept was quickly adopted for elevated railways, subways and suburban rail services worldwide.

Advantages

Multiple units accelerate faster than locomotive-hauled trains because power is distributed along the train, reducing axle loads and rail wear. Trains can be lengthened or shortened by coupling or uncoupling units, and a failed unit does not necessarily disable the whole train. Because most axles can be motored and braked, adhesion is used efficiently, making MUs especially suited to frequent-stop services.
