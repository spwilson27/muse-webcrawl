Hyperparasite

Hyperparasite
Definition: A parasite whose host is itself a parasite
Examples: Hyperparasitoid wasps, hyperparasitic fungi, bacteria infecting parasitic worms
Higher levels: Third- and fourth-level parasitism known

A hyperparasite, also known as a metaparasite, is a parasite the host of which is itself a parasite, typically a parasitoid. Hyperparasites are common in nature, especially among insects: many parasitoid wasps that attack plant-eating insects are themselves attacked by other wasps. The term was used as early as the 19th century and popularised by Jonathan Swift's verse about ever-smaller fleas.

Types

Facultative hyperparasites can develop on either the primary host or its parasite, while obligate hyperparasites require the parasitised host. In insects, hyperparasitoids often locate hosts by detecting chemical cues from the primary host's plant or frass. Fungi that parasitise other fungi, viruses infecting parasitic organisms, and bacteria such as Wolbachia in parasitic nematodes also qualify.

Ecology

Hyperparasites sit at the fourth trophic level or higher and can stabilise or destabilise food webs. By attacking parasitoids they sometimes release herbivore populations from control, an important consideration in biological pest control: introducing a parasitoid may fail if local hyperparasites suppress it. Chestnut blight offers a beneficial case — a hypovirus that infects the blight fungus acts as a hyperparasite and has been used as a biocontrol agent in Europe.

Examples

Well-known examples include the wasp Lysibia wasp parasitising Cotesia parasitoids of cabbage white caterpillars; hyperparasitic fungi in the genus Ampelomyces that attack powdery mildews; and bacteriophages infecting bacteria that themselves infect animals.
