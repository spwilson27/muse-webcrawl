"The Market for 'Lemons': Quality Uncertainty and the Market Mechanism" is a 1970 paper by economist George Akerlof. It is a foundational work in the theory of information asymmetry, demonstrating how markets can break down — or even collapse entirely — when sellers know more about product quality than buyers. Akerlof shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, in part for this work, with Michael Spence and Joseph Stiglitz.

The used car example

Akerlof's central example is the market for used cars. A "lemon" is American slang for a defective used car. Because buyers cannot distinguish good cars from lemons, they will only pay a price reflecting average expected quality. Owners of good cars, whose vehicles are worth more than that price, withdraw them from the market. This lowers average quality further, lowering the price buyers will pay, and the adverse-selection spiral can drive good products out entirely — a market failure arising purely from asymmetric information.

Applications and responses

Akerlof extended the argument to insurance markets, employment of minorities, and credit markets in developing countries. Markets develop countermeasures against information asymmetry, including warranties and guarantees, brand reputations, licensing, and third-party certification. Spence's signalling theory and Stiglitz's screening theory address how the less-informed or better-informed parties can restore information flow.

Reception

The paper was reportedly rejected by several journals for being "trivial" before the Quarterly Journal of Economics published it. It has since become one of the most cited papers in economics, and "lemon laws" protecting used-car buyers in many jurisdictions echo its terminology.
