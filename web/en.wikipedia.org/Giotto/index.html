Giotto

Giotto di Bondone (c. 1267 – 8 January 1337), known mononymously as Giotto, was an Italian painter and architect from Florence during the Late Middle Ages. He is generally considered the first in a line of great artists who contributed to the Italian Renaissance. Working during the Gothic and Proto-Renaissance period, Giotto's art broke with the stylised Byzantine tradition, introducing a new naturalism, spatial depth, and emotional expression that transformed Western painting.

Life

According to Vasari's legend, the young Giotto was discovered by the painter Cimabue while drawing sheep on a rock; Cimabue took the boy into his workshop. However apocryphal, the story reflects Giotto's contemporary fame. Dante praised him in the Divine Comedy, noting that Giotto had eclipsed Cimabue's fame. In 1334 Giotto was appointed master of works of Florence Cathedral, for which he designed the campanile (bell tower) that still bears his name.

Major works

Giotto's masterpiece is the fresco cycle of the Scrovegni Chapel (Arena Chapel) in Padua (c. 1305), narrating the lives of the Virgin and Christ with unprecedented dramatic and emotional power. Other major works include the frescoes of the Peruzzi and Bardi chapels in Santa Croce, Florence; the Ognissanti Madonna (c. 1310); and, at the end of his life, the Stefaneschi Triptych. The frescoes of the Upper Church of San Francesco at Assisi, long attributed to him, remain a matter of scholarly dispute.

Legacy

Giotto's innovations — solid, three-dimensional figures, observed gestures, coherent space — laid the groundwork for Masaccio and the Renaissance masters of the following century. The ESA space probe Giotto, which flew by Halley's Comet in 1986, was named in his honour, after his depiction of the comet as the Star of Bethlehem in the Scrovegni Chapel.
