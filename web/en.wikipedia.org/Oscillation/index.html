Oscillation

Oscillation is the repetitive or periodic variation, typically in time, of some measure about a central value (often a point of equilibrium) or between two or more different states. Familiar examples of oscillation include a swinging pendulum and alternating current.

Oscillations occur in many areas of science and everyday life: the vibration of a string, the beating of the heart, waves on the sea, the cycles of day and night, seasons, business cycles, and the alternating voltage of an electrical outlet. The term vibration is sometimes used more narrowly for mechanical oscillations.

Simple harmonic motion

The simplest form of oscillation is simple harmonic motion, in which the restoring force is directly proportional to the displacement from equilibrium and acts in the opposite direction (Hooke's law for a spring). Its motion is described by a sinusoidal function of time, characterized by amplitude, period (or frequency), and phase. A mass on a spring and a pendulum swinging through small angles are classic approximations.

Damped, driven and coupled oscillations

Real oscillators lose energy through friction or resistance, so their amplitude decays — damped oscillation. A driven (forced) oscillator receives energy from an external periodic force; when the driving frequency matches the natural frequency, the amplitude grows dramatically, an effect called resonance. Two or more oscillators connected together are coupled oscillators, which exhibit normal modes and energy exchange, such as the in-phase and out-of-phase modes of two pendulums joined by a spring.
