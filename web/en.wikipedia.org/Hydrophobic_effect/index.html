Hydrophobic effect

Oil and water do not mix; nonpolar droplets coalesce to minimise their contact with water.

The hydrophobic effect is the observed tendency of nonpolar substances to aggregate in an aqueous solution and to be excluded by water. The word hydrophobic literally means "water-fearing", and it describes the segregation of water and nonpolar substances, which maximises hydrogen bonding between molecules of water and minimises the area of contact between water and nonpolar molecules. In terms of thermodynamics, the hydrophobic effect is the free-energy change of water surrounding a solute. A positive free-energy change of the surrounding solvent indicates hydrophobicity, whereas a negative free-energy change implies hydrophilicity.

The hydrophobic effect is responsible for the separation of a mixture of oil and water into its two components. It is also responsible for effects related to biology, including cell membrane and vesicle formation, protein folding, insertion of membrane proteins into the nonpolar lipid environment and protein–small molecule associations. Hence the hydrophobic effect is essential to life.

Amphiphiles

Amphiphiles are molecules that have both hydrophobic and hydrophilic domains. Detergents are composed of amphiphiles that allow hydrophobic particles to be solubilised in water by the formation of micelles and bilayers. Amphiphiles are also important in the formation of cell membranes.

Cause

The hydrophobic effect is commonly explained in terms of entropy. Water molecules around a nonpolar solute form a more ordered, cage-like structure (sometimes described as "iceberg-like") because they must arrange their hydrogen bonds tangentially around the solute. This ordering lowers the entropy of the system. When nonpolar molecules aggregate, the total surface area exposed to water decreases, some of the ordered water molecules are released into the bulk solvent, and entropy increases. This entropy gain is the principal driving force for association at room temperature, although enthalpic contributions from hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions also play a role.

Biological importance

The hydrophobic effect is fundamental to the folding of globular proteins: hydrophobic amino-acid side chains tend to be buried in the protein interior, away from water, while hydrophilic residues remain on the surface. It also drives the self-assembly of lipid bilayers, in which the hydrophobic fatty-acid tails of phospholipids point inward, shielding them from the aqueous environment on both sides of the membrane.
