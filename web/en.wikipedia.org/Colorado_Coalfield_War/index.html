Colorado Coalfield War

Date: September 1913 – December 1914

Location: Southern Colorado, United States

Parties: United Mine Workers vs. coal companies incl. Colorado Fuel & Iron

The Colorado Coalfield War was a major labour conflict in the United States, fought between striking coal miners organised by the United Mine Workers of America (UMWA) and the coal companies of southern Colorado — principally the Rockefeller-owned Colorado Fuel and Iron Company (CF&I) — from September 1913 to December 1914. It was one of the deadliest episodes of labour violence in American history.

Background and strike

Miners in the southern fields, most of them immigrants, worked under hazardous conditions and lived in company towns subject to scrip payment, company stores, and private mine guards. When the UMWA called a strike in September 1913 for union recognition, enforcement of mining laws, and an eight-hour day, thousands of families were evicted from company housing and moved into union tent colonies, the largest at Ludlow.

Ludlow Massacre and aftermath

On 20 April 1914, Colorado National Guard troops and company guards attacked the Ludlow colony, which burned; around twenty people died, including women and children who suffocated in a pit beneath a tent. The massacre provoked national outrage and an armed miners' uprising known as the Ten Days' War, in which strikers attacked mines across the region until federal troops were deployed. The strike was called off in December 1914 without union recognition, though subsequent investigations and Rockefeller's public-relations response made the conflict a landmark in American labour and corporate history.
