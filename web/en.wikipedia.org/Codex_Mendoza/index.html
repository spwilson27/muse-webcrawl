Codex Mendoza

The Codex Mendoza is an Aztec codex, believed to have been created around 1541, shortly after the Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire. It was painted by Aztec scribes (tlacuilos) on European paper and contains a history of Aztec rulers and their conquests, a list of the tribute paid by conquered provinces, and a description of daily Aztec life, all accompanied by Spanish commentaries. It is named after Antonio de Mendoza, the first viceroy of New Spain, who may have commissioned it for Charles V, the Holy Roman Emperor and King of Spain.

Contents

The codex comprises 71 folios in three sections. The first section recounts the founding of Tenochtitlan and the reigns of its rulers from 1325 to 1521, with the towns each ruler conquered. The second, modeled on the earlier Matrícula de Tributos, records the tribute in goods — such as warrior costumes, cacao, feathers, and jade — owed by 38 provinces. The third describes Aztec life from birth through childhood, education, marriage, occupations, justice, and warfare.

History

The manuscript never reached Spain: the ship carrying it was attacked by French corsairs, and the codex came into the possession of the French royal cosmographer André Thévet, whose name appears on its pages. It later passed to the English geographer Richard Hakluyt, and after several further owners was purchased in 1659 for the Bodleian Library at the University of Oxford, where it remains. Long neglected, it was rediscovered in the 19th century and is now regarded as one of the most important sources for Aztec history and culture.
