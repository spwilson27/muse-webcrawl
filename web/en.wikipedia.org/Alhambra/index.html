The Alhambra (Arabic: الحمراء, al-Ḥamrāʾ, "The Red One") is a palace and fortress complex on a hill above Granada, Spain. Built principally during the 13th and 14th centuries by the Nasrid emirs, the last Muslim rulers in Spain, it is the most famous surviving monument of Islamic architecture in the Iberian Peninsula and Spain's most visited monument.

History

A small fortress existed on the Sabika hill by 889. After the fall of Córdoba, the Nasrid ruler Muhammad I (Ibn al-Ahmar) established the Emirate of Granada in 1238 and began the palace-city; his successors, above all Yusuf I (1333–54) and Muhammad V (1354–91), created the palaces seen today. In 1492 Ferdinand and Isabella received the surrender of the last emir, Boabdil, in the Alhambra, ending Muslim rule in Spain — the same monarchs who issued the Alhambra Decree expelling the Jews.

Architecture

The complex comprises the military Alcazaba, the Nasrid Palaces (Mexuar, Comares Palace with its Hall of the Ambassadors, and the Palace of the Lions), the Renaissance palace of Charles V, and the Generalife, the sultans' summer estate and gardens. Its interiors are celebrated for muqarnas ("stalactite") vaulting, carved stucco, zellij tilework, Arabic calligraphy repeating the Nasrid motto "There is no conqueror but God", and courtyards engineered around water — above all the Court of the Lions, ringed by 124 slender columns.

Legacy

Neglected for centuries, the Alhambra was romanticised by Washington Irving's Tales of the Alhambra (1832) and became an icon of Orientalist imagination and of modern conservation. Together with the Generalife and the Albaicín quarter it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
