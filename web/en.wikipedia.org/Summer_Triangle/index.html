The Summer Triangle is an astronomical asterism in the northern celestial hemisphere formed by three bright stars—Vega, Altair, and Deneb—that mark the vertices of a large triangle spanning the constellations Lyra, Aquila, and Cygnus. It is one of the most recognizable patterns of the northern summer night sky and serves as a useful guidepost for locating other stars and constellations.

Constituent stars

  • Vega (Alpha Lyrae), apparent magnitude 0.03, is the brightest star of the triangle and the fifth-brightest star in the night sky. It lies about 25 light-years away and was the northern pole star around 12,000 BCE and will be again around 13,700 CE due to axial precession.
  • Altair (Alpha Aquilae), magnitude 0.76, is about 17 light-years away and one of the nearest bright stars; it rotates so rapidly that its equator bulges noticeably.
  • Deneb (Alpha Cygni), magnitude 1.25, is a distant blue-white supergiant roughly 2,600 light-years away and among the most luminous stars known.

Visibility

From mid-northern latitudes, the triangle rises in the east during spring evenings, passes nearly overhead around midnight in midsummer, and remains visible into late autumn. The band of the Milky Way runs through the triangle, passing through Cygnus, which makes the region rich in star clusters and nebulae. The term "Summer Triangle" was popularized in the mid-20th century; British astronomer Patrick Moore is often credited with promoting the name in the 1950s, though the grouping had been noted by earlier writers such as Joseph Ashbrook and by Austrian astronomer Oswald Thomas, who called it the "Great Triangle".

Related features

Within and near the triangle lie several notable objects, including the Ring Nebula (M57) in Lyra, the Coathanger cluster (Brocchi's Cluster) in Vulpecula, the double star Albireo in Cygnus, and the small constellations Lyra, Sagitta, and Vulpecula. The Northern Cross is a related asterism formed by the brightest stars of Cygnus, with Deneb at its top.
