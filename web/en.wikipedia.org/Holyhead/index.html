Holyhead

Holyhead (Welsh: Caergybi, "Cybi's fort") is a town and port on Holy Island, off the northwest coast of Anglesey in Wales. With a population of about 12,000, it is the largest town in the Isle of Anglesey County, though not its county town (that is Llangefni). Holyhead is best known for its ferry port, one of Britain's principal links with Ireland, with services to Dublin; the port handles millions of passengers and a large share of freight between Britain and Ireland.

History

The town grew around the late Roman fort of Caer Gybi, whose walls — unusually, three sides survive — enclosed a small fortress by the shore, probably a naval base guarding against Irish raiders; within them stands St Cybi's Church, founded by the 6th-century saint Cybi. Holyhead's importance grew with the coaching and mail trade to Ireland: Thomas Telford built the London–Holyhead road (now the A5) and the Menai Suspension Bridge (1826) to speed the Irish mail, and the Chester and Holyhead Railway arrived in 1848. The Admiralty constructed a great harbour of refuge, whose breakwater — at about 2.7 km the longest in the United Kingdom — was built between 1848 and 1873 using stone quarried from Holyhead Mountain by a workforce of some 1,300. The town was also a centre of shipbuilding and, in the 20th century, aluminium production.

The port and transport

The Port of Holyhead, owned by Stena Line Ports, is one of the busiest ferry ports in Britain, with Ro-Ro services to Dublin operated by Stena Line and Irish Ferries, including fast craft crossings of about two hours. The railway station, adjacent to the ferry terminal, is the terminus of the North Wales Coast Line with direct trains to London Euston and Cardiff. The A55 expressway links Holyhead across Anglesey and the Britannia Bridge to the mainland motorway network, making the town the western end of Euroroute E22. Changes in trade patterns following Brexit reduced port traffic in the early 2020s, a significant economic blow to the town.

Geography and sights

Holyhead stands on Holy Island, connected to Anglesey by the Four Mile Bridge and the Stanley Embankment causeway. Holyhead Mountain (220 m), the highest point in the county, rises west of the town, topped by the Iron Age hillfort of Caer y Twr and surrounded by the Breakwater Country Park and the RSPB reserve at South Stack, famous for its lighthouse (1809), towering sea cliffs, and breeding seabirds including puffins. The town has a maritime museum, the Roman fort and church at its centre, and a strong Welsh-speaking heritage — in recent censuses a majority of residents reported speaking Welsh.
