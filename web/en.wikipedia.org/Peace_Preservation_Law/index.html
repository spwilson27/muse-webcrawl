Peace Preservation Law

The Peace Preservation Law (治安維持法, Chian Iji Hō) was a Japanese law enacted on 22 April 1925, with the aim of allowing the Special Higher Police to more effectively suppress socialists and communists. In addition to criminalizing forming an association aimed at altering the kokutai (the imperial system) or the system of private property, the law also empowered the police to suppress a wide range of other movements.

Enforcement

The law's vague wording enabled mass arrests, beginning with the March 15 incident of 1928 against the Japanese Communist Party. Amendments in 1928 introduced the death penalty for the most serious offences. By 1933, thought control had spread into academia and the arts, famously in the persecution of Kyoto University professor Takigawa Yukitoki. More than 70,000 people are recorded as having been arrested under the law between 1928 and 1945, though a smaller proportion were prosecuted.

Abolition

The law was repealed after World War II on the orders of the Allied occupation in October 1945, and political prisoners were released. It remains a symbol in Japanese debates over civil liberties and the balance between security and freedom of thought.
