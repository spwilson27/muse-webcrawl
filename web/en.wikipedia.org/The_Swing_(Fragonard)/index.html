The Swing (Fragonard)

ArtistJean-Honoré Fragonard
Yearc. 1767
MediumOil on canvas
Dimensions81 cm × 64.2 cm
LocationWallace Collection, London

The Swing (French: L'Escarpolette), originally titled The Happy Accidents of the Swing, is an 18th-century oil painting by Jean-Honoré Fragonard, made around 1767. It is considered a masterpiece of the Rococo era and the most famous work of the artist.

Composition

A young woman in a billowing pink dress rides a swing suspended in a lush, overgrown garden, pushed by an older man nearly hidden in shadow — by some readings her husband. At the height of her arc she kicks off her shoe, her skirt flying up, toward a statue of Cupid; in the lower left, concealed in the bushes, her young lover gazes up beneath her with rapture, hat in hand. Cherubs, a barking dog (symbol of fidelity) and a broken dolphin statue complete the coded scene of erotic play and deception.

Commission

According to the memoir of the playwright Charles Collé, a courtier — identified as the tax farmer François-David Bollioud de Saint-Julien — commissioned the picture with a remarkably specific brief: his mistress on a swing pushed by a bishop, with himself placed to see up her skirts. The painter Gabriel-François Doyen refused the risqué commission, which passed to Fragonard, who substituted a layman for the bishop. The anecdote has never been fully verified but perfectly captures the painting's spirit.

Style and legacy

The Swing epitomises the frivolous, artificial world of the late French aristocracy on the eve of the Revolution: frothy foliage, porcelain colour and feigned innocence masking calculated eroticism. It entered the Wallace Collection in London in 1900, where it remains one of the museum's signature works. The painting has been endlessly referenced in popular culture, from Disney's Frozen to fashion photography.
