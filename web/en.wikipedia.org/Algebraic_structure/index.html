In mathematics, an algebraic structure consists of a nonempty set A (called the carrier set or domain), a collection of operations on A (usually binary operations, functions taking elements of A to elements of A), and a finite set of axioms — equations called identities — that these operations must satisfy. Algebraic structures are the central object of study of abstract algebra.

Examples

A group is a set with one binary operation that is associative, has an identity element, and in which every element has an inverse — for instance the integers under addition. A ring adds a second operation (multiplication) that distributes over the first; a field is a ring in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse, such as the rational or real numbers. Other structures include monoids, lattices, Boolean algebras, and vector spaces, which combine a field of scalars with an abelian group of vectors.

Properties and comparison

Structures are classified by the axioms they obey: commutativity, associativity, distributivity, idempotence, absorption, and the existence of identity and inverse elements. Adding axioms produces stronger, more specific structures, giving the familiar hierarchy magma → semigroup → monoid → group. When the operations and constants of one structure preserve those of another under a map, the map is a homomorphism; bijective homomorphisms are isomorphisms, identifying structures that differ only in the nature of their elements.

Universal algebra

Universal algebra studies algebraic structures themselves as mathematical objects, in a framework also known as general algebra. It provides common definitions of substructures, quotient structures, products and free structures that apply across all varieties of algebras, and connects algebra to model theory and category theory.
