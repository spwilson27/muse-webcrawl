Thallium

Thallium is a chemical element; it has symbol Tl and atomic number 81. It is a gray post-transition metal that is not found free in nature. When isolated, thallium resembles tin, but discolors when exposed to air. Chemists William Crookes and Claude-Auguste Lamy discovered thallium independently in 1861, in residues of sulfuric acid plants, using flame spectroscopy; its name comes from the Greek thallos, "a green shoot or twig," after the bright green spectral emission lines of the element.

Characteristics

Thallium is a soft, malleable metal that can be cut with a knife. It tarnishes slowly in air, forming a protective oxide layer, and reacts with water and acids. Most thallium compounds have the Tl(I) oxidation state, whose ionic size resembles that of potassium — the basis of its extreme toxicity, since thallium ions are taken up by potassium channels and accumulate in cells, disrupting enzymes and the nervous system. The Tl(III) state is a powerful oxidizing agent.

Occurrence and production

Thallium is moderately abundant in the Earth's crust (about 0.7 ppm), occurring in potassium minerals and sulfide ores of copper, lead, and zinc. Commercial production is small — on the order of tens of tonnes per year — recovered as a by-product of smelting sulfide ores.

Applications

Historical uses in rat poisons and depilatories were abandoned because of thallium's toxicity and its notoriety as a poisoner's poison. Modern niche uses include thallium compounds in electronics (photoresistors, infrared detectors), thallium-201 in nuclear medicine cardiac imaging, thallium-doped crystals in radiation detectors and high-temperature superconductors, and mercury–thallium amalgams in low-temperature thermometers. Prussian blue is an antidote for thallium poisoning.
