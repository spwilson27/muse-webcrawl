Dew point

The dew point of a given body of air is the temperature to which it must be cooled, at constant pressure, for water vapour to condense into liquid water. When the temperature falls below the dew point, the air is saturated and the moisture capacity of the air is reduced; the excess vapour condenses as dew, fog or frost, or on surfaces such as windows and cold drinks containers.

Relationship to humidity

Unlike relative humidity, which depends on the current temperature, the dew point directly expresses the absolute amount of water vapour in the air. The closer the dew point is to the air temperature, the higher the relative humidity; when they are equal, humidity is 100 per cent. A useful approximation, the Magnus formula, relates dew point to temperature and relative humidity, and is accurate within about a degree for typical conditions.

Human comfort

Because it tracks absolute moisture, the dew point is a good indicator of how humid it feels. Dew points below 10 °C feel dry; around 13–16 °C is comfortable to slightly humid; 18 °C feels muggy; and dew points above 21 °C are widely perceived as oppressive, since sweat evaporates poorly. Extreme dew points approach 30 °C in the most humid coastal climates of the Persian Gulf and Red Sea.

Measurement and significance

Dew point is measured with chilled-mirror hygrometers, which detect the temperature at which condensation forms on a polished surface, and is reported in weather observations worldwide. In aviation, the spread between temperature and dew point warns of fog formation; in building science it predicts condensation and mould risk; and at night, grass and cars radiating heat to a clear sky cool to the dew point and collect dew.
