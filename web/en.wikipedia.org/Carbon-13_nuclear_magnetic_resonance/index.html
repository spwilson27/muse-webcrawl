Carbon-13 nuclear magnetic resonance (most commonly known as 13C NMR spectroscopy or sometimes simply as carbon NMR) is the application of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy to carbon. It is analogous to proton NMR and allows the identification of carbon atoms in an organic molecule, just as proton NMR identifies hydrogen atoms.

Principles

Only the carbon-13 isotope, with a natural abundance of about 1.1%, possesses a nuclear spin (I = ½) observable by NMR; the dominant carbon-12 isotope has spin zero and is NMR-silent. Because of the low abundance and a smaller gyromagnetic ratio, 13C NMR is much less sensitive than 1H NMR, requiring longer acquisition times and signal averaging.

Spectra

Chemical shifts span a wide range, roughly 0–220 ppm relative to tetramethylsilane (TMS), so signals rarely overlap. In the routine proton-decoupled experiment, broadband irradiation of the protons removes C–H coupling, and each chemically non-equivalent carbon appears as a singlet, giving a direct count of distinct carbon environments. Techniques such as DEPT distinguish CH3, CH2, and CH groups, while two-dimensional experiments (e.g. HSQC, HMBC) correlate carbons with attached and nearby protons. Quantitative integration is generally unreliable in standard 13C spectra because of differing relaxation times and nuclear Overhauser effects.
