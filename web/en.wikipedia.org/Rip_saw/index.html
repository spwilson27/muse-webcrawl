Rip saw

A rip saw is a wood saw that is specially designed for making a rip cut, a cut made parallel to the wood grain. The cutting edge of each tooth is flat and at a slight angle, so that each tooth acts like a small chisel, paring away a chip of wood as the saw is pushed. Rip saw teeth are larger, fewer per inch (often about 4–7 teeth per inch on a hand saw), and have little or no "set" compared with a crosscut saw, whose teeth are bevelled like knife points to sever fibres across the grain.

Hand rip saws

Traditional hand rip saws have long blades (26 inches or more) and relatively coarse teeth, allowing fast cutting along the length of a board when breaking down stock to width. Because the teeth slice with the grain rather than across it, they remove material quickly but leave a rougher surface than a crosscut.

Power rip saws

On a table saw, ripping is done with the workpiece guided by a rip fence parallel to the blade; dedicated rip blades have flat-top grind teeth and deep gullets to clear chips. Bandsaws fitted with wide blades and a fence are also used for ripping, especially for resawing thick stock into thinner boards. Safe ripping practice involves using a riving knife or splitter, push sticks, and keeping the blade guard in place to guard against kickback.
