The European Potato Failure was a food crisis caused by potato late blight that affected Northern and Western Europe in the mid-1840s. Often referred to as the Hungry Forties, the crisis was at its worst in Ireland, where it became the Great Famine, but it also struck the Scottish Highlands, Belgium, the Netherlands, Prussian territories and parts of France, causing widespread starvation, disease and emigration.

Cause

The blight is caused by the oomycete Phytophthora infestans, which arrived in Europe from North America around 1845, probably in shipments of seed potatoes or guano. Cool, wet weather in 1845–1846 allowed the pathogen to spread rapidly through potato crops that were genetically uniform and therefore highly vulnerable, most notoriously the Irish 'Lumper' variety.

Impact by region

In Ireland, where the rural poor depended almost entirely on the potato, the failure of successive harvests caused roughly one million deaths and the emigration of another million. In the Highland Potato Famine in Scotland, destitution was severe though mass mortality was largely avoided through relief efforts. In Belgium (especially Flanders), the Netherlands and Prussia, potato losses combined with poor grain harvests in 1846–47 to produce sharp price rises, food riots and excess mortality, contributing to the unrest preceding the Revolutions of 1848.

Legacy

The crisis spurred research into plant pathology — notably by Miles Joseph Berkeley and Anton de Bary, who established the causal role of the pathogen — and influenced land reform, poor relief policy and mass migration from Europe to North America.
