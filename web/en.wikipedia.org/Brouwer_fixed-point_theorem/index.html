Brouwer fixed-point theorem

Every continuous map from a closed disc to itself has a fixed point. (Image placeholder)

The Brouwer fixed-point theorem is a fixed-point theorem in topology, named after the Dutch mathematician L. E. J. Brouwer. It states that for any continuous function f mapping a compact convex set to itself, there is a point x0 such that f(x0) = x0. In its most famous form, any continuous function from a closed ball (or disc) to itself has at least one fixed point.

Statement and examples

In one dimension the theorem reduces to the intermediate value theorem: a continuous function from a closed interval to itself must cross the diagonal. A common everyday illustration is that if you stir a cup of coffee, at least one point of the liquid surface remains in its original position. Another is that a map placed on the floor of the room it depicts contains a point that lies exactly above the point it represents ("you are here").

History

Special cases were known earlier, but Brouwer proved the general result around 1910–1912, using the new tools of algebraic topology, in particular the notion of the degree of a continuous mapping. The theorem does not hold for non-compact or non-convex domains: a rotation of an annulus, for example, has no fixed point.

Consequences and applications

The theorem underpins John von Neumann's and John Nash's existence proofs of equilibria in game theory (the Nash equilibrium), results in mathematical economics such as the existence of general equilibrium, and the Schauder and Leray–Schauder fixed-point theorems in functional analysis. A famous consequence is the "hairy ball" style result that there is always a point on Earth where the horizontal wind velocity is zero.
