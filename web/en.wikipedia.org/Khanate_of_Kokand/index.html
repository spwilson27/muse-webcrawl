Khanate of Kokand

The Khanate of Kokand (Uzbek: Qo'qon Xonligi) was a Central Asian state in the Fergana Valley of present-day Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Kyrgyzstan, and Kazakhstan that existed from 1709 to 1876. From its capital at Kokand, the khanate became one of the three Uzbek khanates of Central Asia, alongside Bukhara and Khiva.

History

The khanate was founded by Shahrukh Biy of the Ming tribe of Uzbeks, who built a citadel at Kokand in 1709. Under rulers such as Alim Khan and Umar Khan in the early 19th century, Kokand expanded northward along the Syr Darya, founding fortresses including Ak-Mechet and taking Tashkent. At its height it controlled much of the Fergana Valley, Tashkent, and the steppe towns of southern Kazakhstan, and its merchants traded widely along the Silk Road routes.

The state was weakened by civil wars between nomadic Kipchak and settled Sart factions, and by the turbulent reign of Khudoyar Khan, who was repeatedly enthroned and deposed.

Russian conquest

Russian forces took Tashkent in 1865 and progressively reduced Kokand to a vassal state by treaty in 1868. After a rebellion in 1875–76 against Khudoyar and Russian influence, General Mikhail Skobelev stormed Kokand, and the khanate was abolished in 1876, its territory becoming the Fergana Oblast of Russian Turkestan.

Legacy

The palace of Khudoyar Khan in Kokand, built in 1863–1873 with ornate tilework, survives as a museum and symbol of the khanate.
