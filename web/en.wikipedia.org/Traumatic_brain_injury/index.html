Traumatic brain injury
Other namesIntracranial injury
CausesFalls, vehicle collisions, violence, sports

A traumatic brain injury (TBI), also known as an intracranial injury, is an injury to the brain caused by an external force. TBIs can be classified by severity (mild, moderate, or severe, commonly using the Glasgow Coma Scale), by mechanism (blunt or penetrating), and by pathological features such as contusion, haemorrhage, and diffuse axonal injury.

Signs and symptoms

Mild TBI (concussion) may cause headache, confusion, dizziness, and brief loss of consciousness, usually without structural damage on imaging. Moderate and severe TBIs can cause prolonged unconsciousness, seizures, focal deficits, and death; survivors may face cognitive, behavioural, and physical impairments. Secondary injury — from swelling, raised intracranial pressure, and reduced blood flow — evolves over hours to days and is a major target of treatment.

Causes and prevention

Falls (especially in the elderly and young children), road traffic collisions, assaults, and sports are the major causes. Prevention includes seat belts, helmets, fall prevention, and rules changes in contact sports. Repeated mild TBIs have been linked to chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) in athletes and military personnel.

Treatment

Care focuses on preventing secondary injury: maintaining oxygenation and blood pressure, monitoring and treating intracranial pressure, surgery for haematomas, and multidisciplinary rehabilitation.
