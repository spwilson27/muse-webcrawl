Dead zone (ecology)

Dead zones are hypoxic (low-oxygen) areas in the world's oceans and large lakes, caused by excessive nutrient pollution from human activities coupled with other factors that deplete the oxygen required to support most marine life in bottom and near-bottom water. In the mid-20th century, few dead zones were scientifically recognised; by the early 21st century, several hundred had been identified, and the phenomenon is considered a major environmental problem. The term is also applied to areas of soil or atmosphere depleted of life, but its principal use is aquatic.

The Gulf of Mexico dead zone forms each summer at the mouth of the Mississippi River.

Mechanism

Dead zones form through eutrophication. Runoff carries nitrogen and phosphorus — from fertilised farmland, livestock waste, sewage, and atmospheric deposition — into coastal waters. The nutrient surge fuels blooms of phytoplankton; when the algae die and sink, bacteria decomposing them consume dissolved oxygen in deeper water. Stratification of the water column, with fresher or warmer water floating above denser bottom water, prevents re-oxygenation from the surface. When oxygen falls below about 2 milligrams per litre, mobile animals flee and sessile organisms die or are stressed; microbial communities shift to anaerobic processes that can release hydrogen sulfide, which is toxic.

Major dead zones

The best known examples include the northern Gulf of Mexico, where the Mississippi River basin drains much of the agricultural heartland of the United States; its hypoxic area has in some years exceeded 20,000 square kilometres, threatening shrimp and other fisheries. The Baltic Sea contains some of the world's largest dead zones, driven by nutrient loads from surrounding countries and poor water exchange with the North Sea. Other significant zones occur in Chesapeake Bay, the Black Sea, the East China Sea, Lake Erie, and the northern Adriatic. A 2008 study counted more than 400 hypoxic coastal systems worldwide; the total ocean area affected by oxygen depletion has grown as warming waters hold less dissolved gas and stratify more strongly.

Naturally occurring oxygen-minimum zones exist in the open ocean — in the eastern tropical Pacific and the Arabian Sea — and these have been expanding, a trend linked to climate change and distinct from, but worsening alongside, coastal nutrient-driven zones.

Effects and reversibility

Dead zones reduce biodiversity, kill benthic invertebrates, displace fish, and can cause mass mortality events when hypoxic water is pushed toward shore. Economically they damage fisheries and aquaculture. Some systems have partially recovered when nutrient inputs were cut: the Black Sea's northwestern shelf improved after fertiliser use collapsed with the Soviet economy in the 1990s, and reductions in phosphorus helped Lake Erie recover in the 1980s — though it has since suffered renewed algal blooms and hypoxia. Recovery measures include fertiliser management, cover crops, wetland restoration, wastewater treatment upgrades, and basin-wide nutrient targets, as in the Mississippi River/Gulf of Mexico Hypoxia Task Force and the EU Water Framework Directive.
