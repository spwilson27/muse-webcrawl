Sci-Hub is a shadow library website that provides free access to millions of research papers and books, bypassing publishers' paywalls in most cases by illegally accessing them through educational institution proxies or donated credentials. Founded by Alexandra Elbakyan, a Kazakhstani computer scientist, in 2011, it is one of the best-known examples of large-scale copyright infringement of academic literature, and a prominent flashpoint in debates over open access and the economics of scholarly publishing.

History

Elbakyan created Sci-Hub in 2011 while a researcher frustrated by the cost of accessing paywalled articles; the site automated the retrieval of papers requested via a DOI. Its collection grew from tens of thousands of papers to tens of millions. In 2015 the academic publisher Elsevier sued Sci-Hub and the related Library Genesis (LibGen) in a United States federal court, winning a default judgment of $15 million in 2017; the American Chemical Society won a separate $4.8 million judgment the same year. The site has repeatedly lost domains and been blocked by ISPs in various countries but has remained accessible through mirrors and new domains.

Usage and coverage

Studies have found that Sci-Hub hosted a large majority of all scholarly articles with DOIs—one 2018 analysis estimated coverage of about 85% of paywalled articles—and that its download logs show use from every country, including wealthy institutions whose members nonetheless find it convenient. Publishers and some researchers criticize the site for copyright infringement and for security risks associated with the credentials it uses; supporters argue it exposes the dysfunction of paywalled publishing and advances the principle that publicly funded research should be freely available.

Related legal and policy debates

Sci-Hub is frequently discussed alongside the open-access movement, the Aaron Swartz case, and Elbakyan's own framing of the site as civil disobedience. Some in the media have called Elbakyan a "Robin Hood of science"; others describe the project as piracy that undermines scholarly societies dependent on subscription revenue.
