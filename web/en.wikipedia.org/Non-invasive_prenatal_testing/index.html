Non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), also called non-invasive prenatal screening (NIPS), is a method of prenatal screening that analyzes cell-free DNA (cfDNA) fragments originating from the placenta and circulating in the mother's blood. From about ten weeks of pregnancy, a maternal blood sample can be used to screen the fetus for chromosomal abnormalities, most notably Down syndrome (trisomy 21), Edwards syndrome (trisomy 18), and Patau syndrome (trisomy 13), with much higher accuracy than traditional serum-and-ultrasound screening and without the miscarriage risk of invasive procedures such as amniocentesis.

Technology

The test rests on the 1997 discovery by Dennis Lo and colleagues that fetal DNA circulates in maternal plasma. Most commercial tests use massively parallel shotgun sequencing: millions of cfDNA fragments are sequenced and mapped to chromosomes, and an excess of fragments from a particular chromosome indicates a possible trisomy. Targeted and single-nucleotide polymorphism-based methods are also used. The "fetal fraction" — the proportion of cfDNA from the placenta — must be adequate (usually above ~4%) for a reliable result; low fetal fraction is a common cause of test failure.

Performance and limitations

For trisomy 21, NIPT has a detection rate above 99% with a false-positive rate around 0.1%, far better than first-trimester combined screening. Accuracy is lower for trisomies 18 and 13, sex chromosome abnormalities, and especially for expanded panels covering microdeletions and rare autosomal trisomies, where positive predictive values can be modest. NIPT is a screening test, not diagnostic: positive results should be confirmed by amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling. Confined placental mosaicism, vanishing twins, maternal copy-number variants, and occult maternal malignancy can all distort results.

Ethics and practice

Introduced commercially in 2011, NIPT has rapidly become standard care in many countries. Its ease raises ethical questions about informed consent, routinization of testing, screening expansion to conditions of variable severity, and potential effects on the disability community, which professional guidelines address by recommending pre-test counseling.
