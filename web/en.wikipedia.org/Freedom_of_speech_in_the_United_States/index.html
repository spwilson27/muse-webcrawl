Freedom of speech in the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the United States, freedom of speech and expression is strongly protected from government restrictions by the First Amendment to the U.S. Constitution, many state constitutions, and state and federal laws. The First Amendment provides that "Congress shall make no law ... abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press," a prohibition the Supreme Court has applied to state and local governments through the Fourteenth Amendment.

History

Colonial precedents such as the 1735 trial of John Peter Zenger influenced American conceptions of press freedom. Despite the First Amendment's adoption in 1791, the Sedition Act of 1798 criminalized criticism of the federal government. Modern doctrine developed largely in the 20th century, beginning with cases arising from prosecutions under the Espionage Act during the First World War, including Schenck v. United States (1919), and Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.'s influential dissent in Abrams v. United States.

Doctrine

The Supreme Court generally treats content-based restrictions on speech as presumptively unconstitutional and subject to strict scrutiny. In Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969), the Court held that advocacy of illegal action is protected unless it is directed to inciting imminent lawless action and is likely to produce such action. Certain narrow categories — including obscenity, defamation, fraud, incitement, true threats, and speech integral to criminal conduct — receive lesser or no protection.

The First Amendment also protects freedom of the press, the right to associate, and, as developed in later cases, symbolic and expressive conduct. It constrains only government action: private employers and private platforms are generally not bound by it.
