Theobroma cacao

Kingdom: Plantae
Family: Malvaceae
Genus: Theobroma
Species: T. cacao
Binomial name: Theobroma cacao L.

Theobroma cacao is a small (6–12 m tall) evergreen tree in the mallow family Malvaceae. Its seeds, cocoa beans, are used to make chocolate liquor, cocoa solids, cocoa butter and chocolate. Native to the Amazon basin and the tropical regions of Mesoamerica, the tree is now cultivated throughout the humid tropics, with West Africa — especially Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana — producing the largest share of the world crop.

Description and cultivation

The cacao tree bears flowers and fruit directly on its trunk and older branches (cauliflory), pollinated by tiny midges. The fruit, a cacao pod, is an ovoid berry 15–30 cm long containing 20 to 60 seeds embedded in a sweet white pulp. Three broad varieties are recognised: Criollo, Forastero and Trinitario. Harvested beans are fermented and dried, processes essential to developing chocolate flavour. Major threats include fungal diseases such as witches' broom and frosty pod rot, and world production is dominated by smallholder farms.

History of use

Cacao was domesticated at least 5,000 years ago in the upper Amazon and was central to Mesoamerican cultures: the Olmec, Maya and Aztec prized a bitter drink of ground cacao, used beans as currency, and associated the plant with ritual and sacrifice. The genus name Theobroma, given by Linnaeus, means "food of the gods". Carried to Europe by Spanish colonisers in the sixteenth century and sweetened there, cacao became the basis of the modern chocolate industry.
