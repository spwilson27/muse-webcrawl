Roth's theorem

Roth's theorem is a result in additive combinatorics concerning the absence of three-term arithmetic progressions in large subsets of the integers. It is named after Klaus Roth, who proved it in 1953 using the Hardy–Littlewood circle method, in work that contributed to his being awarded the Fields Medal in 1958.

Statement

Roth's theorem states that any subset of the integers {1, 2, …, N} containing no three-term arithmetic progression (no three distinct elements x, y, z with x + z = 2y) has size o(N) as N grows; more precisely, its upper density must tend to zero. Roth gave an explicit bound of order N / (log log N) for the largest progression-free set, often denoted r3(N).

Context and improvements

The theorem answered a conjecture arising from work of van der Waerden and Erdős–Turán, and it is the three-term case of the much deeper Szemerédi's theorem (1975), which guarantees arithmetic progressions of every length in any set of positive upper density. Improving the quantitative bound in Roth's theorem has been a central problem in analytic number theory and additive combinatorics. Major advances include bounds by Heath-Brown and Szemerédi in the 1980s, Bourgain's use of Fourier-analytic and incidence ideas in 1999 and 2008, Sanders's quasi-polynomial improvements, and the work of Bloom and Sisask in 2020, which broke the "logarithmic barrier," showing that a progression-free set has size at most N / (log N)1+c for some small positive constant c.

Subsequent work by Kelley and Meka in 2023 produced a dramatic improvement—an upper bound of roughly N · exp(−(log N)1/12)-type—by importing methods related to the structure of sumsets and density increments, narrowing the gap toward the best known constructions, which (following Behrend's 1946 example) exhibit progression-free sets of size about N · exp(−C√(log N)).

Related results

The finite-field analogue of Roth's theorem—the cap set problem, asking for the largest subset of F3n without a three-term progression—saw a breakthrough in 2016 when Ellenberg and Gijswijt, building on the polynomial method of Croot, Lev and Pach, proved an exponentially small upper bound that reshaped the field.
