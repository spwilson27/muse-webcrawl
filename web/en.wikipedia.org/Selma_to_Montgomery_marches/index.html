Selma to Montgomery marches

The Selma to Montgomery marches were three protest marches held in March 1965 along the 54-mile highway from Selma, Alabama, to the state capital of Montgomery. Organised by civil rights activists including the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC), the marches sought to secure voting rights for African Americans in the face of systematic disenfranchisement.

Bloody Sunday

On 7 March 1965, some 600 marchers led by John Lewis and Hosea Williams set out from Selma. At the Edmund Pettus Bridge, state troopers and local possemen attacked the peaceful demonstrators with clubs and tear gas. The day became known as Bloody Sunday; televised images of the violence shocked the nation and galvanised support for federal voting-rights legislation. Activist Amelia Boynton was beaten unconscious, and the photographs of her and of Lewis became iconic.

Turnaround Tuesday and the third march

Two days later, Martin Luther King Jr. led a second march to the bridge, where the demonstrators prayed and turned back to comply with a federal injunction — "Turnaround Tuesday." That night, white supremacists murdered Unitarian minister James Reeb. After a federal court order cleared the way, the full march proceeded under federal protection from 21 to 25 March, culminating in a rally of some 25,000 people at the Alabama State Capitol. Viola Liuzzo, a volunteer driver, was murdered by Ku Klux Klan members on the final night.

Legacy

The marches were a pivotal event of the civil rights movement and contributed directly to the passage of the Voting Rights Act of 1965, signed by President Lyndon B. Johnson that August. The route is now commemorated as the Selma to Montgomery National Historic Trail.
