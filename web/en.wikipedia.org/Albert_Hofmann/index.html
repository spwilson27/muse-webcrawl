Albert Hofmann

Albert Hofmann (11 January 1906 – 29 April 2008) was a Swiss chemist best known for discovering lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD). Working at Sandoz Laboratories in Basel, he first synthesized LSD in 1938 while researching ergot derivatives, and discovered its psychoactive effects in April 1943 — famously experiencing the first intentional LSD trip on a bicycle ride home on 19 April, a date celebrated as "Bicycle Day." He also isolated and synthesized psilocybin, the active compound of psychedelic mushrooms.

Work and views

Hofmann's research programme sought medicines from the ergot fungus; it produced compounds including the postpartum drug methylergometrine as well as LSD-25. After Sandoz marketed LSD as a psychiatric tool (Delysid), the substance escaped the laboratory into 1960s counterculture. Hofmann called LSD his "problem child" in his 1979 memoir LSD: My Problem Child, deploring its recreational misuse while defending its value in therapy and research and its spiritual significance — a position he maintained into his hundredth year, when he asked Steve Jobs-era Silicon Valley attention aside, he supported the 2006 Basel symposium marking his centenary. He died at home aged 102.

Legacy

Hofmann ranked among the leading intellectual figures of the twentieth century in public polls, and the modern renaissance of psychedelic research has revived interest in his work; the Albert Hofmann Foundation preserves his legacy in psychedelic science.
