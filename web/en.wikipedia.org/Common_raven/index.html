Common raven

Scientific name: Corvus corax
Family: Corvidae
Wingspan: 115–150 cm

The common raven (Corvus corax) is a large all-black passerine bird and the most widely distributed of all corvids, found across the Northern Hemisphere from the Arctic to North Africa and Central America. Together with the thick-billed raven it is one of the two largest corvids, with a wingspan rivalling small raptors.

Description and behaviour

Ravens have glossy black plumage, a massive bill, shaggy throat feathers, and a wedge-shaped tail visible in their acrobatic flight, which includes rolls and somersaults seemingly performed for play. They are omnivorous scavengers and predators, eating carrion, insects, small animals, eggs, grain, and human refuse.

Intelligence

Ravens rank among the most intelligent birds: they solve multi-step puzzles, plan for future needs, use calls to recruit others to carcasses, and appear to recognise individual humans. Pairs mate for life and defend territories, while juveniles gather in roaming flocks.

In culture

Ravens loom large in mythology — as Odin's messengers Huginn and Muninn, tricksters in Indigenous traditions of the Pacific Northwest, and omens in literature, most famously Edgar Allan Poe's poem "The Raven". The ravens kept at the Tower of London are wrapped in a legend that the Crown will fall if they leave.
