Divine command theory

Divine command theory

Field: Meta-ethics, philosophy of religion

Claim: Morality is grounded in God's commands

Key objection: Euthyphro dilemma

Notable defenders: William of Ockham, Robert Merrihew Adams

Divine command theory is a meta-ethical theory which proposes that an action's status as morally good is equivalent to whether it is commanded by God. The theory asserts that morality is ultimately based on the commands or character of God, and that the morally right action is the one that God commands or requires. Versions of the view appear in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, whose scriptures present extensive divine legislation, and it was defended by medieval thinkers such as William of Ockham.

The Euthyphro dilemma

The most famous challenge comes from Plato's dialogue Euthyphro: is an action good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good? If the first, morality seems arbitrary — God could make cruelty right by commanding it. If the second, morality is independent of God, and divine commands merely report a standard that exists without him. Either horn threatens the theory as a foundation for ethics.

Responses

Defenders answer in several ways. Robert Merrihew Adams proposed a modified divine command theory identifying the good with the commands of a loving God, arguing that God's necessarily loving nature constrains what he could command. Others ground morality in God's unchanging character rather than his bare commands, so that commands express what goodness necessarily is. Some theologians accept the first horn but deny it makes morality arbitrary, since God's nature is fixed and necessarily benevolent.

Criticisms

Critics raise further problems: how humans can know God's commands (the epistemic problem), disagreements among religions about those commands, and the apparent moral knowledge of non-believers. Divine command theory remains a live position in philosophy of religion and a standard point of comparison for natural law theory, virtue ethics, and secular moral realism.
