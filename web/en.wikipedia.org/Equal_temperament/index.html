Equal temperament

An equal temperament is a musical temperament or tuning system that approximates just intervals by dividing an octave (or other interval) into steps of equal size. The dominant system in Western music, twelve-tone equal temperament (12-TET), divides the octave into 12 parts, each a semitone with a frequency ratio of the twelfth root of two (21/12 ≈ 1.059463).

Properties

In 12-TET every interval is slightly mistuned relative to the pure ratios of just intonation — fifths are narrowed by about two cents and major thirds widened by about fourteen cents — but every key is equally usable, so music can modulate freely between all major and minor keys. This uniformity underlies the design of modern keyboards, fretted instruments and most fixed-pitch ensembles.

History

The exact calculation of equal temperament was first published by the Chinese prince Zhu Zaiyu in 1584 and independently developed in Europe by Simon Stevin and others. Earlier systems, such as Pythagorean tuning and meantone temperament, favoured pure intervals in a few keys at the cost of unusable ("wolf") intervals elsewhere. Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier celebrated a circulating temperament of his day — not necessarily equal temperament itself — but by the nineteenth century 12-TET had become the standard for pianos and, eventually, for nearly all Western instruments.

Other equal temperaments

Systems with 19, 31 or 53 divisions of the octave approximate just intervals more closely and are used in some experimental and non-Western music; equal temperaments also arise naturally in traditions such as Thai classical music (7 equal steps) and Indonesian sléndro.
