Rationalization (psychology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In psychology, rationalization is a defence mechanism in which a person explains controversial behaviours, feelings or failures with seemingly logical, plausible reasons, while concealing the true (often uncomfortable or unacceptable) motivations — even from themselves. The term was introduced into psychoanalysis by Ernest Jones in 1908 and developed by Sigmund Freud and Anna Freud as one of the ego's defences against anxiety and threats to self-esteem. Unlike deliberate lying, rationalization is largely unconscious: the explanations are genuinely believed by the person offering them.

Examples

Two classic patterns take their names from fables and folk sayings. In sour grapes, after failing to obtain something desired, a person devalues it ("I didn't want it anyway") — as in Aesop's fable of the fox and the grapes. In the converse sweet lemon pattern, a person forced to accept an unwanted outcome exaggerates its benefits ("it was for the best"). Everyday examples include justifying a poor purchase by inventing virtues for it, explaining a rejection as proof the opportunity was unworthy, attributing one's own aggressive behaviour entirely to provocation, or constructing elaborate reasons for procrastination. Rationalization also operates collectively, for instance in the post-hoc justifications of unjust policies or cult members' explanations when prophecies fail.

Relation to other concepts

Rationalization overlaps with cognitive dissonance theory: Leon Festinger's experiments showed that people generate justifying reasons to reduce the discomfort between their actions and beliefs. It also resembles intellectualization, though that defence uses abstract reasoning to distance oneself from emotion rather than to fabricate motives. Modern research on motivated reasoning, confabulation in split-brain patients, and choice blindness demonstrates how readily the brain produces confident explanations for behaviour it did not actually choose. While occasional rationalization is normal and can be adaptive, chronic use can prevent honest self-assessment, sustain addictions and prejudice, and block therapeutic change; cognitive behavioural therapy and motivational interviewing work partly by gently exposing it.
