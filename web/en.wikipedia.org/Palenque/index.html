Palenque

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Palenque (Spanish pronunciation: [paˈleŋke]; Yucatec Maya: Bàak'), also anciently known in the Itza language as Lakamha ("big water"), was a Maya city-state in southern Mexico that perished in the 8th century. Its ruins, in the modern state of Chiapas near the Usumacinta River, are celebrated for their elegant architecture and fine carved reliefs, and the site was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987.

History

Palenque was occupied from around 226 BC and reached its peak under the king K'inich Janaab' Pakal ("Pakal the Great", reigned 615–683) and his sons. Pakal's reign saw the construction of the city's finest buildings, including the Palace and the Temple of the Inscriptions. In 1952 the archaeologist Alberto Ruz Lhuillier discovered Pakal's tomb deep inside the Temple of the Inscriptions, with its famous carved sarcophagus lid — one of the great finds of Maya archaeology.

Other major structures include the Temple of the Cross group, built by Pakal's son K'inich Kan Bahlam II, the Temple of the Sun, and an aqueduct that channelled the Otulum stream beneath the city's main plaza. Palenque declined and was abandoned around 800, after which the jungle reclaimed it.

Rediscovery

The ruins were reported from the late eighteenth century and investigated by explorers including John Lloyd Stephens and Frederick Catherwood in the 1840s, whose drawings introduced Palenque to the wider world. Systematic excavation and restoration by Mexican and international projects continue; only a fraction of the city's estimated extent has been cleared.
