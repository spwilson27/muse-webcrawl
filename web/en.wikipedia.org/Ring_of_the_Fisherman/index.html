Ring of the Fisherman

The Ring of the Fisherman (Latin: Anulus Piscatoris; Italian: Anello Piscatorio), also known as the Piscatory Ring, is an official part of the regalia worn by the pope, who by Catholic tradition is the successor of Saint Peter, a fisherman by trade. It traditionally features a bas-relief of Peter fishing from a boat, and was used as a seal to sign papal briefs.

History and use

The ring is first mentioned in a letter of Clement IV in 1265. For centuries each pope had a unique ring made for him, bearing his name around the image, and it was presented at his coronation or inauguration. Upon the pope's death, the ring was ceremonially defaced or destroyed by the camerlengo to prevent forged documents.

Modern practice

In modern times the ring is primarily ceremonial; official documents are no longer sealed with it. Pope Benedict XVI wore his ring daily, while Pope Francis chose to wear a simpler silver-gilt ring originally cast for Paul VI. After Benedict's resignation in 2013, his ring was defaced with cuts rather than destroyed.
