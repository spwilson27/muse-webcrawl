Smoke explosion


Dense, unburned smoke accumulating in a compartment can ignite explosively

A smoke explosion is a phenomenon in fire dynamics in which a layer of smoke, containing unburned fuel gases produced by pyrolysis and incomplete combustion, accumulates in a compartment or adjoining space and subsequently ignites, producing an explosion. It is considered one of the dangerous phenomena firefighters may encounter, along with flashover and backdraft.

Mechanism

When a fire burns in a ventilation-limited compartment, the smoke it produces is rich in flammable products of incomplete combustion, including carbon monoxide and vaporized pyrolysis products. If this smoke migrates into an adjacent compartment or void where it mixes with air to within its flammable range, the fuel–air mixture needs only an ignition source—such as flames, embers, or the original fire—to ignite. The resulting deflagration can produce a powerful pressure wave capable of blowing out windows and walls and injuring firefighters.

Difference from backdraft

A smoke explosion differs from a backdraft in that the smoke–air mixture is already within its flammable limits and requires only an ignition source, whereas a backdraft occurs when oxygen is suddenly introduced to a hot, oxygen-starved compartment and the mixture is above its upper flammable limit until air enters. Unlike a backdraft, a smoke explosion can occur in a space remote from the fire itself, wherever the smoke has collected and mixed.

Warning signs and prevention

Warning signs include large volumes of dense smoke in compartments away from the fire, smoke under pressure, and discoloured or "cold" smoke that is cool enough to touch yet fuel-rich. Firefighters mitigate the risk by venting smoke from high points before entry, monitoring with thermal imaging cameras, and treating accumulated smoke in adjoining spaces as a potential explosive atmosphere.
