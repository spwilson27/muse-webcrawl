Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya (1292–1350), born Shams al-Dīn Muḥammad ibn Abī Bakr ibn Ayyūb, was a medieval Islamic jurisconsult, theologian, and spiritual writer of the Hanbali school in Mamluk Damascus. His name means "son of the principal of [the madrasa] al-Jawziyya," where his father was superintendent. He is best known as the foremost disciple and populariser of the controversial reformer Ibn Taymiyya, whose ideas he systematised and transmitted in dozens of works that remain widely read in the Muslim world.

Life

Ibn Qayyim studied in Damascus under many scholars before attaching himself to Ibn Taymiyya in 1313, remaining his close student until the master's death in 1328. He shared his teacher's imprisonment in the Citadel of Damascus (1326–1328) for opinions such as condemning the practice of travelling to visit the Prophet's tomb. After his release he taught at the al-Sadriyya and other schools, wrote prodigiously, and was noted for personal piety and extensive private worship. He died in Damascus in 1350 and was buried beside his teacher in the Sufi cemetery.

Works and thought

Among his many books are Zād al-Maʿād ("Provision for the Hereafter"), a vast compendium of the Prophet Muhammad's guidance; Madārij al-Sālikīn, a commentary on a Sufi manual of spiritual stations; Iʿlām al-Muwaqqiʿīn, a foundational treatise on the principles of Islamic law and legal reasoning; al-Ṣawāʿiq al-Mursala, a refutation of speculative theology; and works on prayer, patience, and Prophetic medicine (al-Ṭibb al-Nabawī). Like Ibn Taymiyya, he insisted on returning to the Qur'an and Sunna over later scholarly convention, criticised blind imitation (taqlīd), and condemned practices such as tomb veneration, while also writing with deep sympathy on the purification of the heart. His works strongly influenced later reform movements, including 18th-century Arabian revivalism and modern Salafism, and are staples of Islamic publishing today.
