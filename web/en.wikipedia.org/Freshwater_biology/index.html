Freshwater biology

Freshwater biology is the scientific biological study of freshwater ecosystems and the organisms that inhabit them. It is a branch of limnology concerned with lakes, ponds, rivers, streams, springs, bogs, and wetlands, and with the physical and chemical conditions—such as low salinity, flow, temperature, and nutrient levels—that shape life in inland waters.

Habitats

Freshwater habitats are divided into lentic systems (still waters such as lakes and ponds), lotic systems (flowing waters such as rivers and streams), and wetlands. Fresh water makes up only a small fraction of Earth's water, yet freshwater environments support a disproportionately rich diversity, including roughly a tenth of all known species.

Organisms

Freshwater communities include phytoplankton and zooplankton, aquatic plants, invertebrates such as insect larvae, molluscs, and crustaceans, and vertebrates including fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals. Many groups, such as mayflies and dragonflies, spend their immature stages in water, and their presence is widely used as an indicator of water quality.

Threats

Freshwater biodiversity is among the most threatened on Earth, pressured by pollution, eutrophication, damming and water abstraction, invasive species, and climate change. Freshwater biology informs conservation, fisheries management, and the biomonitoring of water quality.
