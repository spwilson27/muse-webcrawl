Baiae

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Baiae (Italian: Baia) was an ancient Roman town situated on the northwest shore of the Gulf of Naples, in the modern comune of Bacoli in Campania. For several centuries in the late Republic and early Empire it was the fashionable seaside resort of Rome's elite, famed for its hot sulphurous springs, luxurious villas, and a reputation — noted by moralists such as Seneca — for pleasure and vice.

History

Baiae took its name, according to tradition, from Baius, the helmsman of Odysseus. Its volcanic hot springs made it a spa from early times; from the 2nd century BC wealthy Romans built sprawling maritime villas there, and Julius Caesar, Cicero, Pompey, and later emperors from Augustus to Nero all had residences at Baiae. The town was the scene of imperial intrigue: Agrippina the Younger was murdered nearby in 59 AD on Nero's orders, and Hadrian died at Baiae in 138 AD. Roman engineers pioneered hydraulic concrete and great domed bath halls there, including the so-called Temples of Mercury, Venus, and Diana.

Submergence

Because of bradyseism — the slow rising and sinking of the ground in the Phlegraean Fields caldera — much of the lower town slid beneath the sea in late antiquity and the early Middle Ages, when malaria and Saracen raids further emptied the site. Today the sunken streets, mosaics, and statues of Baiae can be visited by glass-bottomed boat or by diving in the Parco Archeologico Sommerso di Baia, one of the world's notable underwater archaeological parks, while the monumental bath complexes survive on the hillside above.
