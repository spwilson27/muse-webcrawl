John Graves Simcoe (25 February 1752 – 26 October 1806) was a British Army general and the first lieutenant governor of Upper Canada, serving from 1791 to 1796. He founded York, which became the city of Toronto, and is remembered for measures that limited slavery in Upper Canada and for planning the new province's towns, roads and institutions.

American War of Independence

Simcoe served in America from 1775, took command of the Queen's Rangers, a Loyalist light infantry corps, in 1777, and led it with distinction in raids and skirmishes in Virginia and elsewhere. Wounded and captured at Yorktown in 1781, he returned to England and married Elizabeth Posthuma Gwillim.

Upper Canada

Appointed lieutenant governor of the new province of Upper Canada under the Constitutional Act of 1791, Simcoe arrived at Newark (Niagara-on-the-Lake) in 1792 and in 1793 moved the capital to York on Lake Ontario. He promoted immigration, ordered military roads such as Yonge Street and Dundas Street, and introduced English common law and courts. His administration passed the Act Against Slavery of 1793, the first legislation in the British Empire to restrict slavery, providing for gradual abolition by barring further importation and freeing the children of enslaved people at age 25.

Later life

Recalled to England in 1796, Simcoe later served in Saint-Domingue and commanded the Western District against a feared French invasion. He died at Exeter in 1806. In Ontario, the August civic holiday is known as Simcoe Day in Toronto, and the town of Simcoe and Lake Simcoe bear his name.
