Golden syrup

TypeInvert sugar syrup
Place of originLondon, England
Created1880s (Lyle's Golden Syrup)
Main ingredientsSugar, water, acid

Golden syrup, or light treacle, is a thick, amber-coloured form of inverted sugar syrup made by refining sugar or by treating a sugar solution with acid. It has a rich, buttery, caramel-like flavour and the consistency of honey, and is a common ingredient in British baking and desserts.

History

The best-known brand, Lyle's Golden Syrup, was created in the 1880s by Abram Lyle's sugar refinery in Plaistow, London. It was originally a by-product of sugar refining, sold cheaply in wooden casks, and soon marketed in the now-iconic green and gold tin bearing the image of a lion surrounded by bees — one of Britain's oldest unchanged brand packagings, recognised by Guinness World Records.

Production and properties

Golden syrup consists of sucrose partially hydrolysed into glucose and fructose, which gives it resistance to crystallisation and a smooth texture. It is roughly as sweet as ordinary sugar syrup but with a distinctive toasted flavour.

Uses

It is used in treacle tart, flapjacks, sponge puddings, gingerbread, and as a topping for porridge and pancakes. It is a staple in the United Kingdom, Ireland, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa, and is less common in North America, where corn syrup or honey may be substituted.
