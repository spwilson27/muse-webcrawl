En plein air

A painter working en plein air

En plein air (French for "in the open air"), or plein air painting, is the act of painting outdoors. This method contrasts with studio painting or academic rules that might create a predetermined look. The practice goes back for centuries but was truly made into an artistic technique by the French Impressionists and the Barbizon School. The popularity of painting en plein air increased in the 1870s with the introduction of paints in metal tubes and the portable box easel.

History

Sketching outdoors in oil was practised by artists such as Pierre-Henri de Valenciennes in the late eighteenth century, who urged students to make oil studies directly from nature. In the nineteenth century, John Constable in England and the Barbizon painters in France — Théodore Rousseau, Charles-François Daubigny and others — made landscape studies out of doors, while usually finishing exhibition pictures in the studio. The Impressionists, above all Claude Monet, took the decisive step of completing entire paintings outside, working quickly to capture transient effects of light and atmosphere. Practical innovations made this possible: John Goffe Rand's collapsible tin paint tube (1841), premixed colours, and lightweight easels.

Practice

Plein air painters contend with shifting light, weather, insects and curious onlookers, often completing works in a single session (alla prima). The approach spread worldwide — to the Heidelberg School in Australia, the Group of Seven in Canada, Russian and American landscape schools — and remains popular today through art festivals ("plein air paint-outs"), societies, and portable equipment for oils, watercolour and pastel. Contemporary practitioners range from traditional landscapists to urban sketchers.
