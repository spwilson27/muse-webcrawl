Defensin

Defensins are small cysteine-rich cationic proteins across cellular life, including vertebrate and invertebrate animals, plants and fungi. They are antimicrobial peptides active against bacteria, fungi and many viruses, forming part of the innate immune system.

Structure and classes

Vertebrate defensins fall into alpha, beta and theta subfamilies distinguished by the pairing pattern of their six conserved cysteine residues, which form disulfide bonds stabilising a beta-sheet structure. Alpha-defensins are abundant in neutrophil granules and in intestinal Paneth cells; beta-defensins are produced by epithelial cells at skin and mucosal surfaces. Theta-defensins, found in some primates, are circular peptides assembled from two truncated alpha-like precursors.

Mechanism

Being positively charged, defensins are attracted to negatively charged microbial membranes, into which they insert to form pores or otherwise disrupt membrane integrity, killing the microbe. Some also act as signalling molecules, recruiting immune cells and modulating inflammation, and certain defensins can enhance or block viral entry into host cells.

Clinical relevance

Altered defensin expression is associated with conditions including Crohn's disease, cystic fibrosis complications and skin disorders, and defensins are studied as templates for new anti-infective drugs.
