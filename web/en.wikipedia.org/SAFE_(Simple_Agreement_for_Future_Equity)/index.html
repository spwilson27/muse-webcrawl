SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity)

A Simple Agreement for Future Equity (SAFE) is a financing contract used by startups to raise seed capital. Introduced by the accelerator Y Combinator in 2013, a SAFE gives an investor the right to receive equity in the company at a future priced round or liquidity event, in exchange for an investment made today.

Structure

Unlike a convertible note, a SAFE is not a debt instrument: it carries no interest rate and no maturity date, and it is typically not repayable. Instead, when the company later raises a priced equity round, the SAFE converts into shares, usually at a price reflecting a valuation cap, a discount on the round price, or both, depending on the variant used.

Common variants include the cap-only SAFE, the discount-only SAFE, the cap-and-discount SAFE, and the "MFN" (most favored nation) SAFE, which entitles the holder to adopt better terms given to later SAFE investors.

Post-money SAFE

In 2018 Y Combinator introduced the post-money SAFE, which defines the investor's ownership by reference to the company's post-money valuation cap. Because all SAFEs and convertible instruments are factored into that cap, founders and investors can calculate the dilution from each SAFE more precisely than under the original pre-money formulation.

Usage and criticism

SAFEs became the dominant seed-stage instrument in Silicon Valley because they are short, standardized, and inexpensive to execute compared with priced equity rounds. Critics note that stacking many SAFEs can obscure a company's true capitalization, and that investors hold neither debt protections nor current shareholder rights until conversion.
