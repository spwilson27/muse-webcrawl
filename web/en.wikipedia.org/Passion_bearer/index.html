Passion bearer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Saints Boris and Gleb, protomartyrs and passion bearers of Rus'

In Eastern Orthodox Christianity, a passion bearer (Russian: страстотерпец, strastoterpets) is a saint who was not killed directly for professing the Christian faith, as a martyr was, but who suffered death at the hands of others — often political assassins or family rivals — and accepted it with humility, meekness and without resistance, in imitation of Christ's suffering. The title is especially characteristic of the Russian Orthodox tradition.

Distinction from martyrdom

The martyr dies for confessing Christ; the passion bearer may die for other reasons — dynastic murder, political intrigue or personal hostility — but is glorified for the manner of the death: refusing to raise a hand against a brother, forgiving the killers, and meeting the end in prayer. The category thus recognises sanctity in non-resistance and Christ-like endurance rather than in confession under persecution.

Examples

The first and most famous passion bearers are the brothers Boris and Gleb, sons of St Vladimir of Kiev, murdered in 1015 on the orders of their elder brother Sviatopolk during the succession struggle after Vladimir's death. Rather than resist with arms, the young princes accepted death, and their cult became foundational for Rus' Christianity. Other celebrated passion bearers include Prince Michael of Tver, killed at the Horde in 1318, the Tsarevich Dmitry of Uglich, and — following their canonisation by the Russian Orthodox Church in 2000 — Emperor Nicholas II and his family, executed by the Bolsheviks in 1918 and glorified as royal passion bearers.

Veneration

Passion bearers are commemorated in services with hymns stressing their meekness and voluntary suffering. The category has no exact parallel in Western canonisation practice, where such figures would more typically be classed simply as martyrs or confessors.
