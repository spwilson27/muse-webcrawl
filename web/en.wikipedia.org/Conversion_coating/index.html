Conversion coating

A conversion coating is a chemical or electro-chemical treatment applied to a manufactured part that superficially converts the material into a thin adhering coating of an insoluble compound. These coatings are commonly applied to metals to protect them against corrosion, to improve the adhesion of paints and other coatings, or to provide lubricity and decorative finishes.

Types

The best-known conversion coatings include chromate conversion coatings on aluminium and zinc, phosphate coatings (such as zinc and manganese phosphate, also called parkerizing) on steel, and bluing, a controlled oxidising treatment for steel. Anodizing aluminium is an electrolytic conversion process that thickens the natural oxide layer.

Mechanism

Unlike paints, which merely cover the surface, a conversion coating is formed by a chemical reaction with the substrate itself, so the coating is integral to the surface and cannot peel in the same way. Phosphate coatings also provide a crystalline surface that retains oils and promotes paint adhesion, which is why they are widely used in the automotive industry before painting.

Environmental concerns

Traditional chromate processes use hexavalent chromium, which is toxic and heavily regulated, driving adoption of trivalent chromium and chromium-free alternatives. See also chromate conversion coating and anodizing.
