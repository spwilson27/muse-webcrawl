Biryani

Biryani is a mixed rice dish originating from the Indian subcontinent, made with basmati rice, meat (such as chicken, goat, lamb, or beef), seafood, eggs, or vegetables, and a blend of spices including saffron, cardamom, cloves, cinnamon, bay leaves, and cumin. It is traditionally cooked by layering partially cooked rice over marinated meat and sealing the pot so the dish finishes in its own steam — a method called dum pukht. Biryani is one of the most popular dishes in South Asian cuisine and is eaten across the region's diaspora worldwide.

History

The word derives from the Persian birinj (rice) or beryān (roasted/fried). The dish developed in the royal kitchens of the Mughal Empire, combining Persian pilaf traditions with Indian spicing and local rice cookery. Regional courts refined distinct styles: the Nizams of Hyderabad patronised Hyderabadi biryani, famous for its kacchi method in which raw marinated meat cooks together with the rice; the Nawabs of Awadh developed the gentler, aromatic Lucknowi style; and in Kolkata, biryani made with potato — said to date from the exile of Nawab Wajid Ali Shah in 1856 — became a defining local version.

Preparation

In the pakki method, meat and rice are cooked separately and then layered; in kacchi biryani, raw marinated meat is layered with parboiled rice and cooked together. The pot (handi) is sealed with dough or a tight lid and cooked over low heat (dum), allowing saffron-tinted and plain grains to mingle. Marinades typically combine yogurt, fried onions (birista), ginger, garlic, green chillies, mint, and coriander. Biryani is commonly served with raita, mirchi ka salan (a chilli-peanut curry in Hyderabad), or a boiled egg, and it differs from pulao in its stronger spicing and layered construction — a distinction debated passionately by enthusiasts.

Regional varieties

Notable versions include Hyderabadi, Lucknowi, Kolkata, Sindhi (with dried plums and extra chilli), Bombay, Thalassery (Malabar, made with short-grained kaima rice and often seafood), Dindigul, and Ambur styles, as well as Sri Lankan and diaspora adaptations. Vegetarian and egg biryanis are widespread, and biryani is a customary centrepiece at weddings and festivals such as Eid.
