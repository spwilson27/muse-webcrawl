Expropriation

Expropriation is the action by which a government or state agency takes privately owned property for a public purpose, typically with the payment of compensation. In the United States the power is known as eminent domain; in the United Kingdom, Canada, and Australia the corresponding concept is compulsory purchase or compulsory acquisition. The power is regarded as inherent in sovereignty but is limited by constitutional protections of property in most jurisdictions.

Legal basis

The Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution provides that private property shall not "be taken for public use, without just compensation." Similar guarantees appear in Article 17 of the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789) and in the European Convention on Human Rights. Disputes typically center on what counts as a public use and how compensation should be valued; the U.S. Supreme Court's decision in Kelo v. City of New London (2005), allowing takings for economic development, provoked widespread legislative reform.

International law

In international law, expropriation of foreign investors' property — including outright nationalization, as in the Mexican oil expropriation of 1938 or the nationalization of the Suez Canal in 1956 — is lawful if it serves a public purpose, is non-discriminatory, follows due process, and is accompanied by compensation. The required standard of compensation (the "prompt, adequate, and effective" Hull formula versus "appropriate" compensation) was fiercely debated during the twentieth century and is now commonly governed by bilateral investment treaties and investor-state arbitration.
