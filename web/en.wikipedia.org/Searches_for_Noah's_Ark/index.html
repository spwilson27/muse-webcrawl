Searches for Noah's Ark

Mount Ararat, the traditional resting place of Noah's Ark.

Searches for Noah's Ark have been made since at least the 3rd century AD, when Eusebius reported attempts. According to the Book of Genesis, the Ark came to rest on the "mountains of Ararat" after the flood, and numerous expeditions — largely from the 19th century onward — have sought its remains, principally on Mount Ararat in modern Turkey.

Historical accounts

Ancient and medieval writers, including Josephus, Epiphanius of Salamis and Marco Polo, repeated traditions that timbers of the Ark were preserved and shown to visitors in the Ararat region. Local Armenian and Kurdish traditions likewise treated the mountain as sacred and its summit as inaccessible.

Modern expeditions

Documented ascents of Ararat began in 1829 with Friedrich Parrot. In the 20th century, claims proliferated: the Russian aviator Vladimir Roskovitsky reportedly sighted a structure in 1917; French industrialist Fernand Navarra recovered carved wood in 1955 that he attributed to the Ark; and hobbyist Ron Wyatt promoted the boat-shaped Durupınar site near Doğubayazıt as the Ark's remains. Aerial photographs and satellite images of the so-called "Ararat anomaly" have also attracted attention.

Scientific assessment

Geologists regard the Durupınar formation as a natural rock structure, and no expedition has produced verifiable scientific evidence of the Ark. Radiocarbon tests on wood samples offered as Ark material have yielded inconsistent or medieval dates. Academic consensus treats searches as expressions of religious belief rather than archaeology, and Turkish authorities have at times restricted access to the mountain for security reasons.
