Organum

Organum
Period: Medieval, 9th–13th centuries
Type: Early polyphony based on plainchant

Organum is, in general, a plainchant melody with at least one added voice to enhance the harmony, developed within the tradition of Western European religious chant of the early Middle Ages. It is the earliest documented form of polyphony in Western music.

Development

The treatise Musica enchiriadis (c. 895) describes the first organum: a chant voice (vox principalis) shadowed by another voice (vox organalis) singing in parallel at the fourth or fifth. Over the following centuries organum grew freer: contrary and oblique motion were admitted, the added voice began to move in extended melismas above a slowly sustained chant note (florid or melismatic organum), as cultivated at the Abbey of Saint-Martial in Limoges and in the Notre-Dame school of Paris.

Notre Dame organum

The composers Léonin and Pérotin, associated with Notre Dame Cathedral in the late 12th and early 13th centuries, brought organum to its grandest scale, with works in three and four voices such as Pérotin's Viderunt omnes. Rhythmic modes imposed measured rhythm on the upper voices, and the short repeated passages called clausulae led directly to the development of the motet.
