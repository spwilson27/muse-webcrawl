Modernism

Modernism was an early-20th-century movement in literature, visual arts, performing arts, and music that emphasised innovation, experimentation, and a deliberate break with traditional forms. Arising amid rapid industrialisation, urbanisation, and the shocks of the First World War, modernists sought — in Ezra Pound's famous phrase — to "make it new." The movement encompassed diverse schools including Imagism, Cubism, Surrealism, Expressionism, and Futurism, united less by a single style than by self-conscious experimentation and skepticism toward inherited certainties.

Origins

Modernism grew out of late-19th-century developments: the paintings of the Impressionists and Post-Impressionists, the symbolist poetry of Baudelaire and Mallarmé, and the philosophical challenges posed by Nietzsche, Marx, Darwin, and Freud to conventional views of the self, society, and religion. The phrase "on or about December 1910, human character changed," coined by Virginia Woolf, is often taken as a symbolic marker of the modernist moment.

Literature

Modernist writers experimented with narrative perspective, stream of consciousness, fragmentation, and mythic parallelism. Landmark works include James Joyce's Ulysses (1922), T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land (1922), Virginia Woolf's Mrs Dalloway (1925), Marcel Proust's In Search of Lost Time (1913–1927), Franz Kafka's novels, and the poetry of Ezra Pound and H.D. Faulkner later adapted modernist techniques to the American South. "High modernism" is conventionally centred on the years immediately after the First World War.

Visual art and architecture

Modernist art moved decisively away from representation: Fauvism's arbitrary colour, Cubism's fractured geometry (Picasso and Braque, from 1907), Kandinsky's and Mondrian's abstraction, Duchamp's readymades, and the politically charged experiments of Dada and Surrealism. In architecture, modernism meant the International Style — functional design, industrial materials (steel, glass, reinforced concrete), and rejection of ornament — exemplified by the Bauhaus, Le Corbusier, and Ludwig Mies van der Rohe ("less is more").

Music and theatre

Modernist composers broke with tonality: Stravinsky's The Rite of Spring (1913) caused a famous premiere riot, while Schoenberg developed atonality and the twelve-tone technique. In theatre, Stanislavski's system, Brecht's epic theatre and alienation effect, and later the Theatre of the Absurd (Beckett's Waiting for Godot, 1953) reshaped dramatic form.

Legacy

By the later 20th century critics were describing a successor sensibility — postmodernism — characterised by irony, pastiche, and skepticism toward grand narratives, though the boundary between late modernism and postmodernism remains debated. Modernist works continue to anchor the Western canon of the 20th century.
