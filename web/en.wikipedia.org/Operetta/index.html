Operetta

An operetta is a form of theatre and a genre of light opera. It includes spoken dialogue, songs, and dances. Operettas are shorter than operas and tend toward light, amusing, or romantic subjects, frequently with satirical treatments of social conventions. The form emerged in Paris in the mid-19th century and spread to Vienna, London, and New York.

History

Jacques Offenbach is regarded as the creator of the French operetta (opéra bouffe), with works such as Orpheus in the Underworld (1858). In Vienna, Johann Strauss II's Die Fledermaus (1874) and Franz Lehár's The Merry Widow (1905) defined the Viennese style. In England, Gilbert and Sullivan's Savoy operas combined witty libretti with tuneful scores, while in the United States composers such as Victor Herbert and Sigmund Romberg bridged operetta and the emerging musical theatre.

Characteristics

Operettas typically alternate sung numbers with spoken dialogue, include waltzes and couplets, and end happily. Although the genre declined in popularity after the Second World War, staples such as Die Fledermaus, The Merry Widow, and the Gilbert and Sullivan works remain in regular performance.
