Cement kiln

Cement kilns are the heart of Portland cement manufacture, used for the pyroprocessing stage in which raw materials (mainly limestone and clay or shale) are heated to about 1,450 °C to form cement clinker. Most modern plants use large rotary kilns, often preceded by cyclone preheaters and a precalciner.

Rotary kiln

A rotary kiln is a steel cylinder, typically 3–6 m in diameter and up to about 100 m long, lined with refractory brick and inclined slightly from horizontal. Raw meal enters the upper end while flame from a burner at the lower end heats it; rotation slowly conveys the material toward the flame. In the burning zone, calcium oxide reacts with silica, alumina, and iron oxide to form the clinker minerals alite, belite, aluminate, and ferrite.

Process chemistry

As the feed travels down the kiln it dries, is preheated, and undergoes calcination (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂) before final clinkering. The hot clinker drops into a cooler, which recuperates heat as secondary air. Calcination makes cement production a major industrial source of carbon dioxide emissions.

Fuels and environment

Kilns burn coal, petroleum coke, natural gas, and increasingly alternative fuels including waste materials; the high temperatures and alkaline environment can destroy many hazardous wastes, though emissions of NOx, dust, and CO₂ require careful control.
