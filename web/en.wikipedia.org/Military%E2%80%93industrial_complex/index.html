Military–industrial complex

The military–industrial complex describes the relationship between a country's military and the defense industry that supplies it, viewed together as a vested interest that influences public policy. The term gained widespread use after its inclusion in the farewell address of US President Dwight D. Eisenhower in 1961.

Origin

Eisenhower warned that "the potential for the disastrous rise of misplaced power exists and will persist" and urged citizens to guard against the influence of the complex, which he saw as a permanent armaments industry of vast proportions that had not existed before the Second World War.

Concept

The concept holds that defense contractors, military services, and government agencies form an "iron triangle" with congressional committees, each profiting from large defense budgets regardless of actual strategic need. Critics argue this encourages arms races and foreign interventions; defenders argue a strong industrial base is essential to national security.

Related concepts

Later writers extended the idea to the military–industrial–congressional complex, and by analogy to the prison–industrial complex and other sectoral alliances between industry and the state.
