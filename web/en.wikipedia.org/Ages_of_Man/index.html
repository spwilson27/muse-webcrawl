Ages of Man

The five ages of man in Hesiod's Works and Days.

The Ages of Man are the stages of human history as described in ancient mythology, most famously by the Greek poet Hesiod in his poem Works and Days (c. 700 BC). Hesiod's account divides history into five successive ages — Golden, Silver, Bronze, Heroic and Iron — each created by the gods and progressively more degenerate than the last, with the exception of the Heroic Age, which interrupts the pattern of decline.

Hesiod's five ages

In the Golden Age, under Cronos, humans lived like gods, free from toil, grief and old age, with the earth providing food spontaneously. The Silver Age was much worse: humans remained children for a hundred years, lived briefly as adults, and were destroyed by Zeus for their impiety. The Bronze Age produced a violent race of men devoted to war, who destroyed themselves. The Heroic Age was an exception — a generation of demigod heroes, many of whom died in the wars at Thebes and Troy, after which the blessed among them were sent to the Isles of the Blessed. Hesiod placed himself in the fifth, Iron Age, an age of labour, misery, moral decline and strife, in which might makes right and shame and nemesis eventually abandon the earth.

Later versions

The Roman poet Ovid, in the Metamorphoses, retold the myth with four ages (Golden, Silver, Bronze and Iron), omitting the Heroic Age. Similar schemes of world ages appear in other traditions: Hindu cosmology divides time into four yugas of declining virtue, and the biblical Book of Daniel describes a statue of gold, silver, bronze, iron and clay representing successive kingdoms. The three-age system of archaeology (Stone, Bronze and Iron Ages) borrowed its terminology from the mythic scheme but describes technological stages instead.
