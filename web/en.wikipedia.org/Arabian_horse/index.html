The Arabian horse is one of the oldest and most recognisable horse breeds in the world, developed on the Arabian Peninsula by Bedouin tribes. It is characterised by a distinctive dished (concave) head profile, wide forehead, arched neck, short back — Arabians typically have one fewer lumbar vertebra than most breeds — and a high-carried tail. Renowned for stamina, the breed excels in endurance riding.

History

Horses recognisable as Arabian type appear in ancient Egyptian art and on the Arabian Peninsula for thousands of years. Bedouin breeders maintained strict oral pedigrees (the breed's purity tradition, asil) and prized mares, keeping bloodlines within tribal families. Arabian horses spread to Europe through war and trade; Ottoman and later European importations established famous studs, notably in Poland, Russia, England and Egypt.

Influence on other breeds

Arabian blood contributed to the foundation of most modern light horse breeds. All English Thoroughbreds descend in the male line from three imported Arabian or Arabian-type stallions — the Darley Arabian, the Godolphin Arabian and the Byerley Turk. Arabians also influenced the Morgan, the American Quarter Horse, and many European warmbloods.

Characteristics and uses

Arabians are intelligent, spirited and strongly bonded with people, traits bred in by close association with Bedouin families. Genetically, all Arabians carry the grey gene in some lines but no Arabian is pinto-patterned; purebreds are solid-coloured, sometimes with white markings. The breed dominates competitive endurance riding, is popular in the show ring, and is used for racing in many countries.
