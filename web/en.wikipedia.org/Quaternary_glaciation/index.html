The Quaternary glaciation, also known as the Pleistocene glaciation, is an alternating series of glacial and interglacial periods during the Quaternary period that began 2.58 million years ago and continues to the present day. During this period, immense ice sheets advanced repeatedly over much of North America, northern Europe, and Asia, dramatically lowering global sea levels and reshaping landscapes.

We are currently in the Holocene interglacial, which began about 11,700 years ago at the end of the Last Glacial Maximum. Glaciers nevertheless persist: the Antarctic and Greenland ice sheets are remnants of the glacial world, and geologists consider the present interglacial an interval within an ongoing ice age, since permanent ice remains at the poles.

Causes

The alternation of glacial and interglacial periods is driven primarily by Milankovitch cycles — variations in Earth's orbital eccentricity, axial tilt, and precession that change the seasonal distribution of sunlight. These effects are amplified by feedbacks involving atmospheric carbon dioxide, ice albedo, ocean circulation, and vegetation.

Effects

At the Last Glacial Maximum, global sea level was about 120–130 metres lower than today, exposing land bridges such as Beringia, which allowed humans to migrate into the Americas. Glaciation carved fjords, deposited moraines and till, created the Great Lakes of North America, and forced species to migrate. The weight of ice depressed the crust, and its removal is still causing post-glacial rebound in Canada and Scandinavia.

Evidence

Evidence for past glaciations comes from glacial landforms and sediments, ice cores from Greenland and Antarctica, marine oxygen-isotope records, lake varves, and loess deposits. These records show roughly fifty glacial cycles over the Quaternary.
