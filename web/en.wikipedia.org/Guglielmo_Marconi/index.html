Guglielmo Marconi

Guglielmo Giovanni Maria Marconi, 1st Marquis of Marconi (25 April 1874 – 20 July 1937) was an Italian inventor and electrical engineer known for his creation of a practical radio wave-based wireless telegraph system. In 1901 he succeeded in sending the first wireless signal across the Atlantic Ocean, and in 1909 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for contributions to wireless telegraphy. The companies he founded built the first commercial radio industry.

Early experiments

Educated privately in Bologna and Florence, Marconi built on Heinrich Hertz's demonstration of electromagnetic waves and the work of predecessors including Augusto Righi and Oliver Lodge. At his family's estate at Pontecchio in 1895 he transmitted signals over distances approaching two kilometres using a coherer receiver and elevated antennas, increasing range by grounding the transmitter.

Commercial development

Finding little official interest in Italy, Marconi moved to Britain in 1896, filed his famous patent for wireless telegraphy, and founded the Wireless Telegraph & Signal Company in 1897. His system was adopted for ship-to-shore communication and proved its value in maritime rescues. In December 1901, at St John's, Newfoundland, he reported receiving the Morse letter "S" transmitted from Poldhu, Cornwall — the first transatlantic radio transmission. Regular transatlantic commercial service followed from 1907.

Later life

Marconi continued research into shortwave and beam systems, served Italy as a senator and diplomat, and was made a marquis in 1929. He died in Rome in 1937; radio stations around the world observed two minutes of silence in his honour.
