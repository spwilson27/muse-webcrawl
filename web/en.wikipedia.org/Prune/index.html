Prune

Dried prunes.

A prune is a dried plum, most commonly from the European plum (Prunus domestica). Fresh plums destined for prunes are varieties with firm, sweet flesh and high soluble solids that can be dried whole without fermenting around the stone. Prunes are wrinkled in appearance, deep blue-black in colour, and chewy, and they keep for many months without refrigeration.

Production

Prunes have been produced for centuries around the Mediterranean and in western Asia; dried plums were carried by traders and armies as a compact, durable food. Modern commercial production is concentrated in California, which supplies a large share of the world's prunes, and in France, where the pruneau d'Agen from the south-west is a protected speciality. Fruit is harvested mechanically, washed, and dehydrated in tunnels at controlled temperatures, then graded, processed with steam, and sometimes pitted before packing.

Nutrition and digestion

Prunes are rich in dietary fibre and contain significant amounts of vitamin K, potassium, and phenolic compounds. They are well known for a gentle laxative effect: prunes contain sorbitol, a sugar alcohol that draws water into the bowel, together with fibre and phenolic compounds such as chlorogenic acids. Prune juice, made by extracting water-soluble components from dried prunes, is widely used for the same purpose.

Culinary uses

Prunes are eaten as a snack, stewed and served with custard or yoghurt, and used in baking and cooking: examples include the Moroccan tajine with lamb and prunes, the Ashkenazi stew tzimmes, compotes, and the French far breton. In some marketing contexts in the United States, prunes were rebranded as "dried plums" to broaden their appeal.
