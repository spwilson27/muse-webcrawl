Robert Walpole, 1st Earl of Orford (1676–1745), was a British Whig statesman generally regarded as the first prime minister of Great Britain. His dominance of the government from 1721 to 1742 — still the longest tenure of any British prime minister — shaped the office itself.

Rise to power

Walpole entered Parliament in 1701, served as Secretary at War and Treasurer of the Navy, and was imprisoned in the Tower in 1712 by a Tory ministry on corruption charges widely seen as political. Restored under George I, he resigned in 1717 but returned in 1720. His deft handling of the South Sea Bubble crash — shielding the court and restoring confidence with a rescue scheme — made him indispensable, and in April 1721 he became First Lord of the Treasury and Chancellor of the Exchequer, effectively chief minister.

Government

Walpole's system rested on patronage, management of the House of Commons, low land taxes funded by customs and excise, and a foreign policy of peace that kept Britain out of major wars for two decades. The collapse of his Excise Bill in 1733 — attacked as an intolerable inquisition on trade — showed the limits of his power, though he survived it. King George II's reliance on Walpole, and Queen Caroline's support, underpinned his position; his residence at 10 Downing Street, given to him by the King, became the prime minister's official home.

Fall and legacy

Forced reluctantly into war with Spain (the War of Jenkins' Ear, 1739), Walpole saw his majority erode and resigned in February 1742 after losing a vote in the Commons. He was created Earl of Orford and died in 1745. Historians credit him with stabilising the Hanoverian succession, the public finances, and cabinet government itself.
