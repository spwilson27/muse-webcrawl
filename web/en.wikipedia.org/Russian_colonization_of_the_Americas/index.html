Russian colonization of the Americas

Period1732 / 1741 – 1867
TerritoryRussian America (Alaska, Aleutian Islands, outposts in Hawaii and California)
Administered byRussian-American Company (from 1799)
CapitalNew Archangel (Sitka)
EndedAlaska Purchase, 1867

The Russian colonization of the Americas covers the period from 1732 to 1867, when the Russian Empire laid claim to northern Pacific coastal territories of North America. Russian colonial possessions in the Americas are collectively known as Russian America. The venture was driven chiefly by the maritime fur trade, especially in sea otter pelts, and was administered from 1799 by the chartered Russian-American Company.

Exploration

The first Russian to sight Alaska may have been Semyon Dezhnev in 1648. In 1741 the Second Kamchatka Expedition under Vitus Bering and Aleksei Chirikov reached the Alaskan coast; Bering died on the island now named for him, but survivors brought back sea otter pelts that ignited a rush of Siberian fur traders (promyshlenniki) to the Aleutian Islands. The traders violently exploited and subjugated the Aleut (Unangan) people, whose population declined catastrophically from disease, enslavement-like labour demands and massacres, including the 1784 Awa'uq massacre by Grigory Shelikhov's men.

Colonial era

Shelikhov founded the first permanent Russian settlement at Three Saints Bay on Kodiak Island in 1784. Under Alexander Baranov, manager and later governor, the capital was moved to New Archangel (Sitka) in 1804 after Tlingit resistance destroyed the earlier post nearby; the Tlingit retook Sitka in 1802 and were defeated again in the Battle of Sitka in 1804, but remained effectively independent. Southern outposts included Fort Ross in California (1812–1841), established to supply food to Alaska, and briefly Fort Elizabeth in Hawaii. At its peak the Russian population of Russian America numbered only about 700–800 colonists, supplemented by Creoles of mixed Russian and Native ancestry, for whom schools and an Orthodox Church presence were established.

End of Russian America

By the mid-nineteenth century the fur trade had declined through overhunting, the colony was difficult to supply and defend, and after the Crimean War Russia preferred to sell rather than risk losing the territory to Britain without compensation. The Alaska Purchase of 1867 transferred the territory to the United States for $7.2 million (about two cents per acre). The transfer ceremony took place at Sitka on 18 October 1867, a date still commemorated as Alaska Day. Russian cultural legacies remain in Alaskan place names, Orthodox churches, and the Russian-influenced dialect of Ninilchik.
