Pole (complex analysis)

In complex analysis (a branch of mathematics), a pole is a certain type of singularity of a complex-valued function of a complex variable. It is the simplest type of non-removable singularity. By definition, a pole of a function f is an isolated singularity at which |f(z)| tends to infinity as z approaches the point — formally, a point a such that f(z) → ∞ as z → a.

Definitions

A point a is a pole of order (or multiplicity) m if the Laurent series expansion of f about a has only finitely many negative-power terms, with the lowest term being (z − a)−m. Equivalently, a is a pole of order m if (z − a)m f(z) has a removable singularity at a with a nonzero limit there. A pole of order 1 is called a simple pole; a pole of order 2 a double pole, and so on.

A function that is holomorphic everywhere in a domain except for poles is called meromorphic. Rational functions are meromorphic on the whole Riemann sphere, with a pole at infinity of order equal to the difference of degrees when the denominator's degree is smaller.

Residues

The residue of a function at a pole is the coefficient of (z − a)−1 in its Laurent expansion. For a simple pole, the residue equals the limit of (z − a) f(z) as z → a. Poles and residues are central to the residue theorem, which evaluates contour integrals by summing residues at enclosed singularities.

Examples

  • The function 1/z has a simple pole at z = 0 with residue 1.
  • The gamma function has simple poles at 0, −1, −2, ...
  • The Riemann zeta function has a single simple pole at s = 1 with residue 1.
  • The function 1/sin²z has double poles at each integer multiple of π.
