Psychological warfare

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Psychological warfare (PSYWAR), or the basic aspects of modern psychological operations (PSYOP), has been known by many other names or terms, including Military Information Support Operations (MISO), Psy Ops, political warfare, "winning hearts and minds", and propaganda. It is the planned use of communications and other means to influence the perceptions, attitudes, and behavior of target audiences — enemy forces, foreign populations, or one's own public — in support of political or military objectives.

Methods

Psychological warfare employs leaflets dropped from aircraft, radio and television broadcasts, loudspeaker appeals on the battlefield, posters, and, in the digital age, social media and other online channels. Messages may encourage surrender or desertion, undermine enemy morale, bolster friendly populations, or shape neutral opinion. Demoralizing content ranges from factual announcements of defeats to threats, misinformation, and appeals to homesickness; "safe conduct passes" promising good treatment to surrendering soldiers were used extensively in the Second World War and the Korean War. Practitioners distinguish "white" (openly attributed), "grey" (unattributed), and "black" (falsely attributed) propaganda.

History

The use of psychological tactics is as old as warfare itself — from Gideon's trumpets in the Book of Judges to Mongol feigned retreats and terror. Modern organized psywar dates to the First World War, when Britain and Germany established propaganda organizations and dropped millions of leaflets over enemy lines. In the Second World War, all major belligerents operated large psychological warfare units: the Allied Psychological Warfare Division, German propaganda companies, radio broadcasters such as "Tokyo Rose" and "Lord Haw-Haw", and figures like Sefton Delmer running black propaganda stations. Cold War practice expanded into peacetime political warfare, and U.S. PSYOP forces played prominent roles in Vietnam, the Gulf War, Afghanistan, and Iraq, where leaflet campaigns preceded and accompanied combat operations.

Organization and debate

In the United States, psychological operations are conducted by specialized units, principally in the Army (including the 4th and 8th Military Information Support Groups), and are subject to legal and policy constraints: U.S. law and policy generally prohibit directing such operations at American audiences. The effectiveness of psychological warfare is debated; its results are hard to isolate from battlefield realities, and backfiring campaigns can damage credibility. Contemporary concerns include state-sponsored disinformation and computational propaganda online, which blur the boundary between psychological operations, public diplomacy, and covert influence.
