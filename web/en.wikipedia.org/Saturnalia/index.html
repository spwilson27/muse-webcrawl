Saturnalia

Saturnalia was an ancient Roman festival in honour of the god Saturn, held on 17 December of the Julian calendar and later extended with festivities until 23 December. The holiday was celebrated with a sacrifice at the Temple of Saturn in the Roman Forum and a public banquet, followed by private gift-giving, continual partying, and a carnival atmosphere that overturned Roman social norms: gambling was permitted, and masters provided table service for their slaves, as it was seen as a time of liberty for both slaves and freedmen alike. A common custom was the election of a "King of the Saturnalia" (Latin: Saturnalicius princeps), who would give orders to people and preside over the merrymaking.

Origins

The Romans identified Saturn with the Greek god Cronus, whose reign was associated with a mythical Golden Age of abundance. The festival may have originated as a celebration marking the end of the agricultural sowing season. The Temple of Saturn, dedicated in 497 BC, anchored the rite in the Forum, and the statue of the god—whose feet were bound with wool the rest of the year—was unbound for the festival, symbolizing Saturn's liberation.

Celebration

Catullus called Saturnalia "the best of days" (Saturnalia, optimo dierum). Schools closed, courts did not sit, and the usual purple-striped togas were exchanged for colourful informal dress (synthesis) and the felt cap of freedom, the pilleus. Gifts exchanged included candles (cerei), clay figurines (sigillaria), and joke presents accompanied by verses. The greeting of the season was "Io Saturnalia!"

The social inversion of the festival—slaves dining before or even being served by their masters—echoed the Roman belief that under Saturn's rule all men had been equal. Gambling with dice, normally frowned upon, was openly permitted. The satirist Lucian and the poet Martial left vivid accounts of the noise, drinking, and licence of the season.

Legacy

Saturnalia is frequently cited in discussions of the origins of later midwinter celebrations. Its dates, feasting, gift-giving, and greenery have invited comparison with Christmas, though the extent of direct influence is debated among historians. The festival remained popular well into the Christian era, and its customs of role reversal survived in later European traditions such as the Feast of Fools and, arguably, in the election of a "Lord of Misrule."
