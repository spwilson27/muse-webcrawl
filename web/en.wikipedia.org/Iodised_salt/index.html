Iodised salt

Iodised salt (also spelled iodized salt) is table salt mixed with a minute amount of various salts of the element iodine. The ingestion of iodine prevents iodine deficiency. Worldwide, iodine deficiency affects about two billion people and is the leading preventable cause of intellectual and developmental disabilities. In many countries, iodising salt is a mandatory or widespread public-health measure.

History

The connection between iodine and goitre was established in the 19th century, and iodine was added to salt in Switzerland and the United States in the 1920s — famously in Michigan, where goitre rates among schoolchildren fell dramatically within a decade. Salt was chosen as the vehicle because it is consumed by nearly everyone in roughly constant amounts and is cheap to fortify. Universal salt iodisation was endorsed by the WHO and UNICEF in the 1990s; by the 2010s roughly 88% of the world's households used adequately iodised salt.

Chemistry and production

Iodine is added as potassium iodide, potassium iodate, sodium iodide, or sodium iodate, typically at 15–40 mg of iodine per kilogram of salt. Iodate is preferred in hot, humid climates because iodide oxidises and evaporates more readily; dextrose or other stabilisers and anti-caking agents are often added. Sea salt and most gourmet finishing salts are usually not iodised.

Health effects

Preventing goitre and cretinism in iodine-poor regions is the central benefit; even mild deficiency in pregnancy can reduce a child's IQ. Excess iodine from multiple sources can occasionally trigger thyroid dysfunction in susceptible individuals, but iodised salt at standard levels is considered safe. Fortification programmes are monitored by urinary iodine surveys.
