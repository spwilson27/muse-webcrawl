Evolutionary algorithm

TypeMetaheuristic optimization method
Inspired byBiological evolution

An evolutionary algorithm (EA) is a subset of evolutionary computation, a generic population-based metaheuristic optimization algorithm inspired by biological evolution. Candidate solutions to a problem are treated as individuals in a population, and a fitness function determines the quality of each solution. Over successive generations, the population evolves through mechanisms analogous to reproduction, mutation, recombination, and selection.

Mechanism

A typical EA begins with a randomly generated population of candidate solutions, each encoded as a chromosome-like structure. Each individual is evaluated by the fitness function. Fitter individuals are more likely to be selected as parents; offspring are produced by crossover (recombining parts of two parents) and mutation (small random changes). The process repeats until a termination condition — a satisfactory fitness level or a maximum number of generations — is met.

Families and applications

The main historical families are genetic algorithms (developed by John Holland), evolution strategies (Ingo Rechenberg and Hans-Paul Schwefel), evolutionary programming (Lawrence Fogel), and genetic programming (John Koza), which evolves computer programs. EAs are used where the search space is large, rugged, or poorly understood: engineering design, scheduling, antenna and circuit design, machine learning hyperparameter tuning, and automated program synthesis. They are especially useful for multi-objective optimization, where a Pareto front of trade-off solutions is sought. See also genetic algorithm, metaheuristic, and simulated annealing.
