Firms arise when organizing production internally costs less than using the market.

In economics and related disciplines, a transaction cost is a cost in making any economic trade when participating in a market. Such costs include the time, effort, and resources spent searching for a trading partner and information, bargaining and drafting agreements, and monitoring and enforcing contracts — beyond the price of the good itself. The concept became the foundation of transaction cost economics, a central branch of new institutional economics.

Origins: Coase and the theory of the firm

Ronald Coase introduced the idea in "The Nature of the Firm" (1937), asking why, if markets allocate efficiently, firms exist at all. His answer: using the price mechanism is itself costly, and entrepreneurs substitute internal direction for market contracts when the costs of internal organization are lower than the costs of transacting. In "The Problem of Social Cost" (1960), Coase showed that with zero transaction costs, bargaining would reach an efficient outcome regardless of the initial allocation of legal rights (the Coase theorem) — implying that real-world institutions matter precisely because transaction costs are positive.

Williamson's framework

Oliver E. Williamson operationalized the theory, emphasizing bounded rationality, opportunism, and three dimensions of transactions: asset specificity (investments tied to a particular relationship, creating hold-up risk), uncertainty, and frequency. Transactions are matched to governance structures — market, hybrid (long-term contracts, alliances), or hierarchy (vertical integration) — so as to economize on transaction costs. Williamson shared the 2009 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics for this work.

Categories and examples

Common classifications distinguish ex ante costs (search and information, bargaining, drafting) from ex post costs (monitoring, enforcement, haggling over adjustments, and the losses from misalignment). Kenneth Arrow described transaction costs as the "costs of running the economic system". Their magnitude explains phenomena from the make-or-buy decision and franchising to the persistence of intermediaries and the design of legal systems and property rights.
