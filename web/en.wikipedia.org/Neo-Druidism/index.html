Neo-Druidism, also known as modern Druidry, is a form of modern Paganism and spirituality that seeks to emulate, reconstruct, or draw inspiration from the practices of the druids, the priestly class of the ancient Celtic peoples of Iron Age Europe. Unlike the ancient druids, whose beliefs were never written down, modern Druidry is a consciously created tradition assembled from archaeology, folklore, Romantic-era revivalism and contemporary ecological spirituality.

History

Interest in the druids revived in the 17th and 18th centuries with antiquarians such as John Aubrey and William Stukeley, who (incorrectly) associated druids with monuments such as Stonehenge. Fraternal and cultural druid orders appeared in the late 18th century, including the Ancient Order of Druids (1781). In the 1960s, Ross Nichols founded the Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids, while in North America the Reformed Druids of North America emerged from a 1963 college protest at Carleton College. Isaac Bonewits later founded Ár nDraíocht Féin (ADF) in 1983, emphasizing scholarly reconstruction of Indo-European religion.

Beliefs and practices

Neo-Druidism has no central dogma. Common threads include reverence for nature and the land, veneration of ancestors, belief in a spiritual dimension to the world (often expressed through Celtic deities or a generalized divine), and celebration of the eightfold Wheel of the Year — the solstices, equinoxes and cross-quarter festivals such as Samhain, Imbolc, Beltane and Lughnasadh. Many groups hold ceremonies at dawn on the summer solstice at Stonehenge and other ancient sites. Creative inspiration, called awen in Welsh tradition, is a central spiritual concept.

Recognition

In 2010, the Charity Commission for England and Wales granted The Druid Network charitable status, effectively recognizing Druidry as a religion in the United Kingdom. Modern Druids also participate in environmental activism, including campaigns around sacred sites and rewilding.
