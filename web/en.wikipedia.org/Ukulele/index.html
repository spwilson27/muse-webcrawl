Ukulele

The ukulele is a member of the lute family of instruments. Generally employing four nylon strings, it is a small, guitar-like instrument originating in 19th-century Hawaii as an adaptation of the Portuguese machete, cavaquinho, and rajao brought to the islands by Madeiran immigrants. The ukulele is closely associated with Hawaiian music but gained enormous popularity in the mainland United States during the early 20th century and again in a revival beginning in the 1990s.

History

Portuguese immigrants arriving in Hawaii from 1879 brought small stringed instruments; cabinet makers such as Manuel Nunes, José do Espírito Santo, and Augusto Dias began producing the hybrid instrument locally. According to tradition, the name ukulele — roughly "jumping flea" — alluded to the player's fast-moving fingers. King Kalākaua championed the instrument, incorporating it into royal performances. It spread to the mainland through the Panama–Pacific International Exposition of 1915 and became a craze of the Jazz Age.

Types and tuning

Ukuleles are commonly made in four sizes: soprano (the original standard), concert, tenor, and baritone. Standard tuning for soprano, concert, and tenor is g'–c'–e'–a' ("my dog has fleas"), a reentrant tuning in which the first string is higher than expected; the baritone is tuned like the top four strings of a guitar. Traditional woods include koa; modern instruments are also built from mahogany, spruce, and plastic.

Cultural presence

Notable players include George Formby in Britain, Arthur Godfrey and later Tiny Tim on American television, and virtuosos such as Jake Shimabukuro, whose arrangement of "While My Guitar Gently Weeps" became an early viral video. Israel Kamakawiwoʻole's medley of "Somewhere Over the Rainbow/What a Wonderful World" is among the most recognisable ukulele recordings.
