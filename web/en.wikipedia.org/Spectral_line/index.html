Spectral line

Spectral line
TypeEmission or absorption feature at a discrete wavelength
OriginTransitions between quantised atomic/molecular energy levels
Famous examplesHydrogen Balmer series, H-alpha 656.3 nm, sodium D lines, 21 cm hydrogen line
UsesElement identification, redshift, temperatures, magnetic fields, radial velocities

A spectral line is a dark or bright line that appears in an otherwise continuous spectrum, produced when photons of a specific energy are emitted or absorbed as electrons (or molecules) jump between quantised energy levels. Each chemical element and molecule has a characteristic pattern of lines — a fingerprint — which makes spectroscopy the principal tool for determining the composition, temperature, density, motion and magnetic fields of everything from flames to distant galaxies.

Formation

An emission line forms when an excited atom decays to a lower energy state and releases a photon; an absorption line forms when background light passes through cooler gas and photons of the transition energy are absorbed. By Kirchhoff's laws, a hot dense object gives a continuous spectrum, a hot diffuse gas gives emission lines, and a cool diffuse gas in front of a hotter source gives absorption lines. Real lines are broadened by thermal motion (Doppler broadening), collisions (pressure broadening), turbulence and magnetic splitting (Zeeman effect).

History

Joseph von Fraunhofer mapped hundreds of dark lines in the solar spectrum in 1814; Kirchhoff and Bunsen showed in 1859–60 that each element produces unique lines, identifying caesium and rubidium and explaining the Fraunhofer lines. Analysis of hydrogen's lines led Balmer (1885) and Rydberg to empirical formulas, and Bohr's 1913 atomic model to their explanation. Helium was discovered in the Sun's spectrum (1868) before it was found on Earth.

Astronomical applications

Doppler shifts of spectral lines measure radial velocities and revealed the expansion of the universe through galactic redshifts. The 21 cm spin-flip line of neutral hydrogen maps the structure of the Milky Way and the early universe, while line strengths diagnose stellar temperatures, surface gravities and chemical abundances.
