Quebec Agreement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Quebec Agreement was a secret agreement between the United Kingdom and the United States outlining the terms for the coordinated development of the science and engineering related to nuclear energy, and specifically nuclear weapons. It was signed by Winston Churchill and Franklin D. Roosevelt on 19 August 1943, during the First Quebec Conference of the Second World War.

Terms

The agreement merged the British Tube Alloys project into the American Manhattan Project, established the Combined Policy Committee to coordinate the work, and stipulated that neither country would use nuclear weapons against the other, nor against a third party without mutual consent, nor communicate information to third parties without consent. It also addressed post-war industrial and commercial advantages, on terms that favoured the American position.

Aftermath

The cooperation it enabled was substantial — British scientists joined the Manhattan Project — but after the war the United States' McMahon Act of 1946 ended most collaboration, to British frustration, and the agreement's joint-consent clause complicated Anglo-American relations when the US used neither weapon with formal British concurrence procedures in 1945 (consent was recorded at a July 1945 meeting). The Quebec Agreement was superseded by later arrangements, notably the 1958 US–UK Mutual Defence Agreement after Britain developed its own weapons.
