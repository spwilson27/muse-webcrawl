Inclusionary zoning

Inclusionary zoning (IZ), also known as inclusionary housing, is a land-use regulation that requires or incentivizes developers of new residential housing to set aside a percentage of units at prices affordable to low- and moderate-income households. The policy is widely used in the United States, Canada, and several other countries as a tool for producing affordable housing within market-rate developments and for promoting economically integrated neighborhoods.

Mechanisms

Programs typically require that between 5 and 30 percent of units in a new development be affordable, with affordability defined relative to area median income (AMI). In exchange, developers may receive compensating benefits such as density bonuses, expedited permitting, reduced parking requirements, or fee waivers. Some jurisdictions allow developers to pay an in-lieu fee into a housing trust fund, build the affordable units off-site, or dedicate land instead of constructing the units directly.

History

One of the earliest and most influential programs was adopted by Montgomery County, Maryland, in 1974, whose Moderately Priced Dwelling Unit (MPDU) ordinance produced thousands of affordable homes. Cities such as New York, San Francisco, Boston, Denver, and Washington, D.C. later adopted their own versions. In the United Kingdom, related obligations are negotiated through Section 106 agreements.

Debate

Supporters argue that inclusionary zoning harnesses private development to produce affordable housing without direct public subsidy and prevents the concentration of poverty. Critics contend that mandatory set-asides can act as a tax on development, reducing overall housing supply or raising market-rate prices, and that programs produce relatively few units compared with the scale of need. Empirical studies find outcomes depend heavily on local market conditions and program design.
