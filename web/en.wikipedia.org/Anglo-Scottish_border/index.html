Anglo-Scottish border

The Anglo-Scottish border is the border between England and Scotland, running for 96 miles (154 km) from Marshall Meadows Bay on the east coast to the Solway Firth on the west. It is Scotland's only land border. Although largely fixed by the Treaty of York in 1237, the border was for centuries a contested frontier zone of warfare and raiding, only becoming a peaceful administrative boundary after the Union of the Crowns in 1603.

Course

From the North Sea near Berwick-upon-Tweed, the border follows the River Tweed westward for much of its length, crosses the Cheviot Hills (passing just south of the summit of The Cheviot), and descends via the Kershope Burn to the River Liddel, the River Esk, and the Scots' Dike to the Solway Firth at Gretna. The main crossings are at Berwick (A1), Coldstream, and the A68 at Carter Bar in the east, and the M6/A74(M) at Gretna in the west.

History

The Roman frontier of Hadrian's Wall lies well south of the modern border. The medieval border was formalized in 1237, excepting Berwick-upon-Tweed, which changed hands repeatedly before being finally taken by England in 1482. The surrounding "Border country" was divided into English and Scottish Marches, governed by wardens, and notorious for the Border Reivers — raiding clans such as the Armstrongs, Elliots, and Kerrs — whose feuds inspired the Border ballads. Between the marches lay the "Debatable Lands", claimed by neither crown. After James VI of Scotland inherited the English throne in 1603, the region was pacified and renamed the "Middle Shires".

The border today

Since Scottish devolution in 1999, differences in law, education, and health policy between Scotland and England have given the border renewed practical significance. During the 2014 Scottish independence referendum and the COVID-19 pandemic (when differing rules applied on each side) it attracted wide attention, though it remains an open internal border of the United Kingdom.
