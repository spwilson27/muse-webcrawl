Sacred Mountains of China

Temples crown the summits of China's sacred mountains, destinations of pilgrimage for over two thousand years.

The Sacred Mountains of China are divided into several groups, the best known being the Five Great Mountains (五岳, Wǔyuè) associated with Taoism and imperial cult, and the Four Sacred Mountains of Buddhism (四大佛教名山). Together they have been sites of religious pilgrimage, imperial sacrifice, and poetry for more than two millennia.

Five Great Mountains

The Five Great Mountains correspond to the five cardinal directions of Chinese cosmology: Mount Tai in the east (Shandong), Mount Hua in the west (Shaanxi), Mount Heng in the south (Hunan), Mount Heng in the north (Shanxi), and Mount Song in the center (Henan). Emperors performed the feng and shan sacrifices on Mount Tai, the most revered of the five, to report their accession to Heaven. Mount Song is home to the Shaolin Temple, famed for Chan (Zen) Buddhism and martial arts.

Four Sacred Mountains of Buddhism

The four Buddhist mountains are each the earthly abode of a bodhisattva: Mount Wutai (Shanxi), associated with Manjushri, bodhisattva of wisdom; Mount Putuo (Zhejiang), an island mountain of Guanyin, bodhisattva of compassion; Mount Emei (Sichuan), associated with Samantabhadra; and Mount Jiuhua (Anhui), associated with Ksitigarbha. Mount Wutai and Mount Emei are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

Other sacred mountains

Taoism also recognizes sacred ranges such as the Wudang Mountains, Mount Qingcheng, and Mount Longhu, while Mount Kailash in Tibet — sacred to Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Bön — draws pilgrims who circumambulate its peak. The mountains inspired a vast tradition of landscape painting and poetry, including the works of Li Bai and Du Fu.
