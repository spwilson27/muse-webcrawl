Symbiosis

Symbiosis (from Greek συμβίωσις, "living with") is any type of a close and long-term biological interaction between two biological organisms of different species, termed symbionts, be it mutualistic, commensalistic, or parasitic. The term was coined in 1879 by Heinrich Anton de Bary. Symbiotic relationships are an important component of life in the biosphere; endosymbiotic theory holds that mitochondria and chloroplasts originated as symbiotic bacteria within early eukaryotic cells.

Types

  • Mutualism — both partners benefit, e.g. clownfish and sea anemones, bees and flowers, and the fungi and algae (or cyanobacteria) composing lichens.
  • Commensalism — one partner benefits and the other is neither helped nor harmed, e.g. barnacles on whales.
  • Parasitism — one partner benefits at the other's expense, e.g. tapeworms in a host's intestine.
  • Amensalism — one organism is harmed while the other is unaffected.

Endosymbiosis

In endosymbiosis, one organism lives inside the cells or tissues of another. Primary endosymbiosis gave rise to mitochondria and plastids; secondary endosymbiosis spread plastids among diverse eukaryotic lineages. The human body itself hosts trillions of symbiotic microorganisms — the microbiota — which aid digestion, synthesize vitamins, and help train the immune system.
