Li (Confucianism)

Li (Chinese: 禮; pinyin: lǐ) is a classical Chinese concept central to Confucianism, usually translated as "rites", "ritual propriety", or "propriety". Li encompasses the norms, ceremonies, etiquette and social conventions that structure harmonious interaction, from ancestral sacrifices and court ceremonies to everyday manners.

Meaning

For Confucius, li was not mere outward formality: performed with reverence and sincerity, ritual practice cultivates virtue and aligns social life with the Way (Dao). Together with ren (benevolence or humaneness), li forms the core of Confucian ethics. Confucius taught that a ruler should govern through virtue and li rather than through laws and punishments alone.

Texts and development

The classical ritual canon includes the Yili (Etiquette and Rites), the Liji (Book of Rites), and the Zhouli (Rites of Zhou). Later thinkers such as Xunzi developed elaborate defences of ritual as a means of reshaping human dispositions. Li is debated in contrast with fa (law) emphasised by Legalism. See also ren.
