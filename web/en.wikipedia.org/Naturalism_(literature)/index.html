Naturalism (literature)

Naturalism is a literary movement that began in the late 19th century, initially in France and later across Europe and the United States. Building on literary realism, naturalist writers sought to apply scientific principles of objectivity and detachment to their study of human beings, depicting characters whose lives are governed by heredity, environment and social conditions rather than by free will.

Origins and theory

The French novelist Émile Zola was the movement's chief theorist. In his essay Le Roman expérimental (1880), adapting the methods of physiologist Claude Bernard, Zola argued that the novelist should function as an "experimental" scientist, observing characters placed in specific milieux and tracing the deterministic consequences. His twenty-volume Les Rougon-Macquart cycle applied this programme to a family under the Second French Empire.

Characteristics

  • Determinism — characters are products of heredity and environment, often depicted as helpless before larger forces.
  • Objectivity — a detached, quasi-scientific narrative tone, avoiding moral commentary.
  • Pessimism — plots frequently trace decline, poverty, addiction or violence.
  • Detailed documentation — extensive research into settings such as mines, slums, railways and department stores.

Major writers

In France: Émile Zola, Guy de Maupassant and the Goncourt brothers. In the United States: Stephen Crane (Maggie: A Girl of the Streets), Frank Norris (McTeague, The Octopus), Theodore Dreiser (Sister Carrie, An American Tragedy) and Jack London. In Germany, Gerhart Hauptmann carried naturalism into the theatre; in Scandinavia, August Strindberg's later plays show its influence.
