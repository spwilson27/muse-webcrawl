Run-flat tire

A run-flat tire is a pneumatic vehicle tire designed to resist the effects of deflation when punctured, allowing the vehicle to continue to be driven at reduced speeds — typically up to 80 km/h (50 mph) for limited distances, often around 80 km (50 miles) — so that the driver can reach a repair facility without changing the tire at the roadside.

Types

Self-supporting run-flat tires have reinforced sidewalls, usually stiffened with thick rubber inserts, which carry the load of the vehicle when air pressure is lost. Support-ring systems use a rigid ring attached to the wheel inside the tire that supports the vehicle after deflation. Self-sealing tires contain a layer of sealant that closes small punctures in the tread area.

Advantages and disadvantages

Because they allow continued travel after a puncture, run-flats let manufacturers omit the spare tire and jack, saving weight and cargo space and avoiding potentially dangerous roadside stops. Disadvantages include a typically harsher ride, higher cost, limited repairability after being driven deflated, and the need for a tire pressure monitoring system, which is required so that the driver knows the tire has lost pressure.

History

Early run-flat designs appeared in the 1930s and 1970s — including Dunlop's Denovo system of 1972 — but the technology entered the mainstream in the 1990s and 2000s with systems such as Michelin's PAX, Bridgestone's RFT and Continental's SSR, fitted as original equipment by manufacturers including BMW and Mini.
