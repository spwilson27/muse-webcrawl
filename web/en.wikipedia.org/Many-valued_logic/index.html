Many-valued logic

Many-valued logic (also multi- or multiple-value logic) is a propositional calculus in which there are more than two truth values. Traditionally, in Aristotle's logic, there were exactly two possible values (i.e., "true" and "false") for any proposition. Classical two-valued logic may be extended to n-valued logic for n greater than 2. Those most popular in the literature are three-valued logic (e.g., Łukasiewicz's and Kleene's), four-valued logic, and infinitely valued logics such as fuzzy logic.

History

The possibility of a third truth value was discussed by Aristotle himself in the problem of future contingents in De Interpretatione, but systematic many-valued logics were first developed independently in the 1920s by Jan Łukasiewicz in Poland and Emil Post in the United States. Łukasiewicz introduced a third value, "possible", intended for statements about the future; Stephen Cole Kleene proposed a three-valued logic in which the third value means "unknown" or "undefined", now widely used in databases (SQL NULL semantics) and programming-language theory. In 1922 Ivan Bochenski and later logicians extended the scheme to four and more values, and Dunn and Belnap's four-valued logic for handling inconsistent and incomplete information became influential in computer science.

Types

  • Three-valued logics — values true, false and indeterminate/unknown (Łukasiewicz Ł3, Kleene K3, Priest's logic of paradox LP).
  • Four-valued logic — Belnap's values True, False, Both and Neither, used in paraconsistent reasoning.
  • Fuzzy logic — truth values are real numbers in the interval [0, 1], formalised by Lotfi Zadeh in 1965 and developed by Hájek into basic fuzzy logic.
  • Probability logic — treats truth values as probabilities, bridging logic and probability theory.

Applications

Many-valued logics are applied in the semantics of natural language, the design of digital circuits with multiple states, database query languages, verification of hardware and software, and artificial intelligence, where fuzzy control systems became a commercially successful technology in consumer electronics and industrial automation.
