Constructive trust

Constructive trust
FieldEquity; law of trusts
NatureRemedial device imposed by law
ArisesBy operation of law, to prevent unjust enrichment or fraud
Contrasted withExpress trust; resulting trust

A constructive trust is an equitable remedy in common law jurisdictions by which a court treats a person who holds legal title to property as a trustee for another, because it would be unconscionable for the holder to keep the property for themselves. Unlike an express trust, it is not created by the intention of the parties; it is imposed by law — "constructed" by the court — whenever the circumstances demand it.

When it arises

Typical situations include property obtained by fraud, theft or breach of fiduciary duty; profits made by a fiduciary from their position (as in Boardman v Phipps); specifically enforceable contracts for the sale of land, under which the seller becomes a constructive trustee for the buyer; and property transferred under a common intention in domestic relationships, the basis of many cohabitation disputes.

Remedial and institutional forms

English law traditionally treats the constructive trust as "institutional": it arises automatically when the relevant facts occur, and the court merely declares it. Many other Commonwealth and United States courts recognise a fully "remedial" constructive trust, imposed at the court's discretion as of the date of judgment to reverse unjust enrichment. The distinction matters in insolvency, because a beneficiary under a trust takes priority over the trustee's unsecured creditors.

Effects

The constructive trustee holds the property for the beneficiary and must transfer it as directed. The device is one of equity's principal tools against unconscionable retention of property and secret profits.
