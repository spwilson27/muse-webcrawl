In numerical analysis, Richardson extrapolation is a sequence acceleration method used to improve the rate of convergence of a sequence, such as the approximation of an integral or derivative computed with a step size h. It is named after Lewis Fry Richardson, who introduced the technique in the early 20th century for hand computation of finite-difference solutions — work he described as the "deferred approach to the limit".

The idea: if a numerical method gives an approximation A(h) with an error expansion in known powers of h,

A(h) = Aexact + c1hp + c2hp+1 + …

then combining values computed at two step sizes, h and h/2, cancels the leading error term and yields a higher-order estimate:

R = (2p A(h/2) − A(h)) / (2p − 1) = Aexact + O(hp+1).

Iterated Richardson extrapolation organises estimates into a tableau, each column cancelling another error term.

Applications

  • Romberg integration applies Richardson extrapolation repeatedly to trapezoidal-rule estimates, generating highly accurate integrals.
  • Numerical differentiation gains accuracy by combining centred differences at different step sizes.
  • Ordinary differential equations: the Bulirsch–Stoer method and extrapolated implicit midpoint rules build on the same principle.
  • Grid convergence studies in computational fluid dynamics use Richardson extrapolation with successively refined meshes to estimate the exact (grid-independent) solution and quantify discretisation error (Grid Convergence Index).

Limitations

The method requires that the error actually possesses a smooth asymptotic expansion in the assumed powers of h; nonsmooth problems, singularities and round-off error at very small step sizes can destroy the expected improvement. Richardson extrapolation is a special case of the general theory of extrapolation methods and of Aitken-type sequence acceleration.
