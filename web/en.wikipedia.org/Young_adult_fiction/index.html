Young adult fiction

Young adult fiction (YA) is a category of fiction written for readers roughly 12 to 18 years old. Although aimed at adolescents, about half its readership is adult. YA typically features a teenage protagonist, first-person or close third-person narration, and themes of identity, first love, friendship, family conflict, and coming of age.

History

Precursors include The Catcher in the Rye (1951) and Maureen Daly's Seventeenth Summer (1942). The modern category crystallised with S. E. Hinton's The Outsiders (1967) and the gritty "problem novels" of Judy Blume and Robert Cormier in the 1970s. In the 2000s the market exploded with series fiction — Harry Potter (straddling children's and YA), Twilight, The Hunger Games, and The Fault in Our Stars — driving film adaptations and a huge adult crossover audience.

Characteristics and debates

YA spans every genre — fantasy, dystopia, romance, contemporary realism, verse novels, and graphic novels. Since the 2010s the field has debated diversity of authorship and representation (movements such as We Need Diverse Books and #OwnVoices), the boundary with "new adult" fiction for readers in their twenties, and periodic controversies over content in school libraries. Award recognition includes the Michael L. Printz Award and National Book Award Young People's Literature category.
