Neptune (Latin: Neptūnus) is the Roman god of freshwater and the sea in Roman religion. He is the counterpart of the Greek god Poseidon. In the Greek-influenced tradition, Neptune was the brother of Jupiter and Pluto, and the three brothers presided over the realms of heaven, the earthly world, and the sea (including the underworld, in Pluto's case) respectively. Salacia was his wife.

Depictions of Neptune in Roman mosaics, especially those of North Africa, are strongly influenced by Hellenistic conventions. Neptune likely was associated originally with freshwater springs: indeed, in contrast to Poseidon, who had been known to the Greeks since Mycenaean times under the name Po-se-da-o, Neptune appears to have no Indo-European etymology, and the Roman name is traditionally connected with the root meaning "moist" or "damp". Edward Kehoe has suggested that the name might derive from the Latin nebula, meaning fog.

Worship

The theology of Neptune is limited by his close identification with the Greek god Poseidon, one of many members of the Greek pantheon whose theology was later applied to a Roman deity. Originally, Neptune was a god of freshwater, but by the time of his identification with Poseidon, he became a god of the sea as well, and his mythology was assimilated accordingly.

Festivals

The Neptunalia was the festival of Neptune, celebrated in Rome on 23 July, at the height of summer drought (and likely in association with the protection of water resources). During the festival, people built huts of branches and foliage (umbrae) and spent the day drinking and feasting in the open air. The location of Neptune's temple in Rome, near the Circus Flaminius in the southern Campus Martius, suggests an early connection with the Tiber and riverine waters.

Mythology

Neptune's mythology is largely that of Poseidon. The division of the world among the three sons of Saturn — sky to Jupiter, sea to Neptune, and underworld to Pluto — mirrored the Greek division among Zeus, Poseidon, and Hades. Neptune was said to wield a trident and to ride a chariot drawn by hippocamps or horses across the waves; his anger was associated with storms and earthquakes, although the earthquake aspect was more prominent in the Greek Poseidon as "Earth-shaker".

Neptune was also closely linked with horses. He was considered the creator of the horse and the patron of horse racing, an association that survives in his titles and in the location of his altars near Roman racetracks.

Etymology

The Roman theologian Varro derived the name Neptunus from nuptus, "covering", from the Greek-derived verb obnubo ("to veil"), referring to the covering of the earth by water. Modern scholars have proposed derivations from Indo-European roots relating to moisture, dampness, and mist, reflecting his original character as a god of freshwater and springs.

Legacy

The planet Neptune, discovered in 1846, was named after the god, in keeping with the tradition of naming planets after Roman deities. The name was proposed because of the planet's deep blue colour, reminiscent of the sea.
