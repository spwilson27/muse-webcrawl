The Grand Duchy of Lithuania was a European state that existed from the 13th century until 1795. At its height in the 15th century it was the largest state in Europe, stretching from the Baltic Sea to the Black Sea and encompassing the territories of modern Lithuania, Belarus, Ukraine, and parts of Poland, Russia, Latvia, Estonia, and Moldova. From 1386 it was joined in a dynastic and then political union with the Kingdom of Poland, culminating in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth (1569–1795).

Formation and expansion

Lithuania emerged as pagan Baltic tribes were united in resistance to German crusading orders. Mindaugas was crowned King of Lithuania in 1253, but after his assassination the state reverted to grand-ducal rule. Under Gediminas (r. 1316–1341), who founded Vilnius in 1323, and his sons Algirdas and Kęstutis, the duchy expanded deep into the lands of the former Kievan Rus', absorbing much of present-day Belarus and Ukraine; Algirdas's victory at the Battle of Blue Waters (1362/63) brought Kyiv under Lithuanian control.

The ruling elite was Lithuanian and, until 1387, pagan, but most subjects were Orthodox Slavs, and the state's chancery language was Ruthenian (Old Belarusian). This made the Grand Duchy an unusually pluralistic polity, later home to Catholics, Orthodox, Jews, Karaites, Tatars, and, after the Reformation, Protestants.

Union with Poland

Threatened by the Teutonic Order and Muscovy, Grand Duke Jogaila married Queen Jadwiga of Poland and accepted Catholic baptism and the Polish crown under the Union of Krewo (1385). The joint Polish–Lithuanian armies crushed the Teutonic Knights at the Battle of Grunwald in 1410, with Jogaila's cousin Vytautas ruling Lithuania in practice. The Union of Lublin (1569) transformed the personal union into a single Commonwealth, transferring the Ukrainian palatinates to Poland but preserving Lithuania's separate law (the Lithuanian Statutes), army, and treasury.

Decline and partitions

Wars with Sweden and Russia in the mid-17th century (the "Deluge") devastated the Commonwealth, and the 18th century brought growing Russian domination. The Grand Duchy disappeared with the Third Partition of Poland in 1795, its lands divided between the Russian Empire and Prussia. Its memory became central to the modern national historiographies of Lithuania, Belarus, and Ukraine alike.
