Vedanta

Vedanta (Sanskrit: वेदान्त, Vedānta), also known as Uttara Mīmāṃsā, is the most prominent of the six orthodox (āstika) schools of Hindu philosophy. Literally "the end of the Vedas," the name originally referred to the Upanishads, the concluding portions of the Vedic texts, and came to denote the philosophical traditions that interpret them. Vedanta is the most influential school of Hindu thought and provides the theological foundations for much of Hinduism as practised today.

Foundations

All Vedanta schools base their doctrines on three canonical sources, the Prasthanatrayi ("three sources"): the Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita, and the Brahma Sutras attributed to Badarayana (roughly 200 BCE–200 CE), which summarise and systematise Upanishadic teaching in aphorisms. Commentaries on these texts by the founders of each sub-school are the principal vehicles of Vedantic argument. Vedanta accepts the Vedas as a valid source of knowledge (pramāṇa) and centres on the relation between Brahman (ultimate reality), Ātman (the self), and the world (jagat), and on the means to liberation (moksha).

Sub-schools

Vedanta traditions differ chiefly over how the self relates to Brahman:

  • Advaita Vedanta (non-dualism), taught by Gaudapada and Adi Shankara (c. 8th century CE), holds that Ātman is identical with Brahman, that the world of multiplicity is maya (appearance), and that liberation is the recognition of this identity.
  • Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism), taught by Ramanuja (11th–12th century), teaches that individual selves and the world are real modes or attributes of Brahman, as the body is to the soul.
  • Dvaita (dualism), taught by Madhvacharya (13th century), asserts an eternal, real distinction between God (Vishnu) and individual souls.
  • Later schools include Nimbarka's Dvaitadvaita (dualistic non-dualism), Vallabha's Shuddhadvaita (pure non-dualism), and Chaitanya's Achintya Bheda Abheda (inconceivable difference and non-difference).

Epistemology and practice

Vedanta commonly accepts six pramāṇas (means of valid knowledge), including perception, inference, and scriptural testimony (śabda). Liberation is pursued through knowledge (jñāna) in Advaita, through devotion and self-surrender (bhakti and prapatti) in the Vaishnava schools, and is supported by ethical disciplines and meditation. In the 19th and 20th centuries, figures such as Swami Vivekananda presented a modernised "Neo-Vedanta" to global audiences, notably at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago in 1893, and Vedanta societies were founded in the West.
