The Paris Commune (French: Commune de Paris) was a revolutionary government that ruled Paris from 18 March to 28 May 1871. It arose in the aftermath of France's defeat in the Franco-Prussian War and the siege of Paris, when the national government of Adolphe Thiers, based at Versailles, attempted to disarm the Paris National Guard. Parisians resisted, elected a municipal council — the Commune — and governed the city for two months until the Commune was crushed by the French Army in the fighting of the Semaine sanglante ("Bloody Week").

Origins

Paris had endured a four-month Prussian siege marked by hunger and bombardment. The armistice of January 1871 and the election of a conservative, monarchist-leaning National Assembly angered republican and working-class Paris. When government troops tried on 18 March to seize the cannons of the National Guard parked at Montmartre, soldiers fraternised with the crowd and two generals were killed; the government withdrew to Versailles. Elections on 26 March installed the Commune's council, dominated by Jacobins, Blanquists, Proudhonists and members of the International Workingmen's Association.

Policies

The Commune enacted a programme of social and democratic reforms: separation of church and state, remission of rents owed during the siege, abolition of night work for bakers, postponement of commercial debts, the right of workers to take over abandoned workshops, free secular education, and the election and revocability of officials, whose salaries were capped. The red flag was adopted, the Vendôme Column was pulled down, and women — notably through the Union des Femmes associated with Louise Michel — played a prominent role in the city's defence and public life.

The Bloody Week and aftermath

After weeks of skirmishing, Versailles troops under Marshal MacMahon entered Paris on 21 May 1871. A week of street fighting followed; Communard forces and their prisoners, including the Archbishop of Paris, were killed, and fires destroyed landmarks such as the Tuileries Palace. Estimates of Communards killed during the Bloody Week and subsequent executions range from about 10,000 to 20,000. Tens of thousands were arrested; thousands were deported to New Caledonia. An amnesty came only in 1880.

Legacy

The Commune became a foundational symbol for socialist, anarchist and communist movements. Karl Marx praised it in The Civil War in France as the political form of working-class government; Lenin and later revolutionaries drew lessons from its fall. The Communards' Wall in Père Lachaise Cemetery, where 147 defenders were shot, remains a site of commemoration.
