The Pritzker Architecture Prize is an international award presented annually "to honor a living architect or architects whose built work demonstrates a combination of those qualities of talent, vision and commitment, which has produced consistent and significant contributions to humanity and the built environment through the art of architecture." Founded in 1979 by Jay A. Pritzker and his wife Cindy through the Hyatt Foundation, it is often called "architecture's Nobel Prize" and is considered the profession's highest honour.

History

The Pritzkers, owners of the Hyatt hotel chain, established the prize to encourage greater awareness of architecture and to honour lifetime achievement in the field. The first laureate, in 1979, was Philip Johnson — the architect who had helped define the International Style, designed the Glass House and later became a leading figure of postmodern architecture. The prize includes a grant of US$100,000 and, since 1987, a bronze medallion based on designs by Louis Sullivan.

Selection

Laureates are chosen by a jury of architects, academics, critics and patrons appointed by the Hyatt Foundation, which accepts nominations from any source. Unlike the Nobel Prizes, the Pritzker may be awarded to a partnership when the work is jointly authored, as with Jacques Herzog and Pierre de Meuron (2001) and SANAA's Kazuyo Sejima and Ryue Nishizawa (2010). Nationality, race, creed and ideology are not considerations. The prize was first awarded to a woman, Zaha Hadid, in 2004.

Ceremony and reception

The award ceremony is held each year at an architecturally significant site around the world, from the Palace of Versailles and the White House to the Great Wall of China region and the Acropolis Museum. The prize has been credited with elevating the public profile of architecture and directing attention to social and environmental practice, though the jury has occasionally been criticised — notably in 2013, when a public campaign asked it to retroactively recognise Denise Scott Brown, partner and wife of 1991 laureate Robert Venturi; the jury declined to revisit its decision.
