Postmodern architecture is a style or movement which emerged in the late 1950s as a reaction against the austerity, formality and lack of variety of modern architecture, particularly the International Style advocated by Le Corbusier and Ludwig Mies van der Rohe. The movement was introduced by architect and urban planner Joseph Hudnut in 1940s writing and given theoretical force by Robert Venturi's Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture (1966) and Venturi, Denise Scott Brown and Steven Izenour's Learning from Las Vegas (1972). It flourished from the 1980s through the 1990s.

Origins and theory

Postmodernists argued that modernism's universal, machine-inspired forms had produced sterile and alienating buildings and cities. Venturi answered Mies's dictum "less is more" with "less is a bore," advocating architecture that embraced ambiguity, contradiction, historical memory and popular culture. Charles Jencks, the movement's leading historian, popularised the claim that modern architecture "died" with the demolition of the Pruitt–Igoe housing project in St. Louis in 1972.

Characteristics

Postmodern buildings combine modern construction with references to historical styles — classical columns, pediments, arches and keystones used freely, often at exaggerated scale or in unexpected materials. Features include bright or contrasting colour, applied ornament, playful and ironic quotations, asymmetry combined with classical motifs, and attention to context and streetscape. Venturi distinguished the "duck" (a building that is itself a symbol) from the "decorated shed" (a plain building with applied signs), preferring the latter's communicative richness.

Notable buildings

Early landmarks include Venturi's Vanna Venturi House in Philadelphia (1964) and the Guild House (1963). The movement's most famous monument is Philip Johnson and John Burgee's AT&T Building in New York (1984, now 550 Madison Avenue), a granite-clad skyscraper topped by a giant broken pediment resembling a Chippendale cabinet. Other notable works are Michael Graves's Portland Building (1982), Charles Moore's Piazza d'Italia in New Orleans (1978), James Stirling's Neue Staatsgalerie in Stuttgart (1984), and Terry Farrell's SIS Building in London (1994).

Philip Johnson's role

Johnson, who had helped codify the International Style in 1932 and built the Glass House as its domestic exemplar, became postmodernism's most prominent elder statesman: his AT&T Building was hailed on the cover of Time magazine, and in 1988 he co-curated the Museum of Modern Art exhibition Deconstructivist Architecture, signalling the next turn of the avant-garde.

Criticism and legacy

Critics have charged postmodern architecture with superficiality, pastiche and corporate populism; by the late 1990s it had fallen from fashion as deconstructivism, high-tech and new modernist idioms rose. Yet its advocacy of context, preservation and pluralism left durable marks on planning and conservation, and many postmodern landmarks are now themselves listed and protected as historic buildings.
