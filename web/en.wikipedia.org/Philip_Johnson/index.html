Philip Cortelyou Johnson (July 8, 1906 – January 25, 2005) was an American architect who designed modern and postmodern architecture. Among his best-known designs are his modernist Glass House (1949) in New Canaan, Connecticut; the postmodern 550 Madison Avenue (AT&T Building, 1984) in New York; 1050 Brickell Avenue in Miami; and the 32-story PPG Place in Pittsburgh. His early career at the Museum of Modern Art helped introduce European modernism to America.

Early career and MoMA

Born in Cleveland to a wealthy family, Johnson studied classics at Harvard. After travels in Europe, he founded the Department of Architecture and Design at the Museum of Modern Art in New York in 1930. With Henry-Russell Hitchcock he curated the landmark 1932 exhibition "The International Style," which introduced modernist architects such as Le Corbusier, Walter Gropius and Ludwig Mies van der Rohe to the American public. Johnson's 1930s involvement with populist and far-right politics, including a period of Nazi sympathizing, has since drawn extensive scrutiny and remains a controversial part of his biography.

Architecture

Johnson earned his architecture degree at Harvard in 1943, studying under Gropius and Marcel Breuer. His Glass House (1949), indebted to Mies's Farnsworth House, became an icon of modernism. He collaborated with Mies van der Rohe on the Seagram Building (1958). From the 1960s, in partnership with John Burgee, Johnson moved toward postmodernism; the AT&T Building, with its Chippendale-style broken pediment, became postmodernism's most famous monument. Other works include the New York State Theater at Lincoln Center, the Crystal Cathedral in California, and the Lipstick Building in New York.

Legacy

Johnson was the first recipient of the Pritzker Architecture Prize in 1979. He died in 2005 at the Glass House, which he bequeathed to the National Trust for Historic Preservation.
