The Glass House, also known as the Johnson House, is a historic house museum on Ponus Ridge Road in New Canaan, Connecticut. Designed by architect Philip Johnson as his own residence and completed in 1949, it is an important example of early modernist architecture in the United States and of the International Style. The house consists of a single rectangular room enclosed entirely in glass, framed by black steel piers, set in a landscaped 49-acre estate.

Design

The house is a glass rectangle 55 feet long, 33 feet wide and 10 feet 6 inches high, resting on a brick floor laid in a herringbone pattern. Eight black-painted steel H-beams form the structural frame. The interior is a single open space divided only by low walnut cabinets and a brick cylinder containing the bathroom and fireplace, which is the sole element rising to the ceiling. A kitchen counter, dining area and sleeping area occupy the undivided volume; views of the surrounding landscape serve as the house's "wallpaper."

Johnson acknowledged debts to Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, whose Farnsworth House (designed 1945–46, completed 1951) pursues a similar ideal of a transparent pavilion, as well as to Le Corbusier, Karl Friedrich Schinkel and the De Stijl movement. The Glass House was completed two years before the Farnsworth House, though Johnson had seen Mies's unbuilt design.

The estate

Over five decades Johnson added a series of structures to the property, turning the grounds into a portfolio of his changing styles. These include the Brick House (guest house, 1949–50), a pavilion in the lake (1962), the Painting Gallery (1965) — a partially buried structure whose plan derives from an ancient Greek tomb — the Sculpture Gallery (1970), a library/study (1980), a gatehouse known as Da Monsta (1995) and other follies. Johnson lived in the Glass House with his partner, curator David Whitney, until his death in 2005.

Preservation

The house was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1997. Following Johnson's death, the property passed to the National Trust for Historic Preservation, which opened it to the public in 2007. The estate is now operated as a museum and study centre, with tours of the Glass House and the surrounding structures and landscape.

Reception

The Glass House is widely regarded as a masterpiece of modernist domestic architecture and one of the purest realisations of the International Style's ideals of transparency, structural clarity and minimal enclosure. Critics have noted its debts to Mies, but its radical simplification — architecture reduced to frame, glass and landscape — made it an enduring icon, frequently compared with Mies's Farnsworth House and with Johnson's later work in postmodern architecture.
