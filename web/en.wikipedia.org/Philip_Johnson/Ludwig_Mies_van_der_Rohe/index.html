Ludwig Mies van der Rohe (born Maria Ludwig Michael Mies; March 27, 1886 – August 17, 1969) was a German-born American architect and furniture designer. Regarded as a pioneer of modern architecture alongside Le Corbusier, Walter Gropius and Frank Lloyd Wright, he was the last director of the Bauhaus and a leading figure of the International Style. He sought an architecture of minimal structure and maximal spatial openness, summarised in his aphorisms "less is more" and "God is in the details."

Early career in Germany

Born in Aachen, the son of a stonemason, Mies received no formal architectural training but worked in his father's shop and as a draughtsman before joining the Berlin studio of Peter Behrens, where Le Corbusier and Gropius also worked. Early independent works included the Riehl House (1907) and traditional-style villas for wealthy clients. After the First World War he turned decisively to modernism, designing visionary glass skyscraper projects (1921–22) and joining avant-garde groups. His five built and unbuilt projects of the 1920s — including the Concrete Country House and Brick Country House — became canonical statements of modern spatial planning.

Barcelona Pavilion and interwar work

In 1927 Mies directed the architecture section of the Weissenhof housing exhibition in Stuttgart. In 1929 he designed the German Pavilion for the Barcelona International Exposition — the Barcelona Pavilion — a composition of marble, onyx, travertine and glass planes that became an icon of modern architecture (demolished in 1930 and reconstructed on the site in 1983–86). The same period produced the Villa Tugendhat in Brno (1930) and the Barcelona chair, designed with Lilly Reich. Mies directed the Bauhaus from 1930 until its closure under Nazi pressure in 1933.

American career

Emigrating to the United States in 1937, Mies became head of architecture at Chicago's Armour Institute, later the Illinois Institute of Technology, whose campus he planned and largely designed. American masterpieces followed: the Farnsworth House (1951), a transparent glass-and-steel weekend house in Plano, Illinois, whose purity influenced Philip Johnson's Glass House; the Lake Shore Drive apartment towers in Chicago (1951); Crown Hall at IIT (1956); and with Johnson, the Seagram Building in New York (1958), a bronze-and-glass tower that set the template for corporate modernism.

Late work and legacy

Mies's final building, the Neue Nationalgalerie in West Berlin (1968), placed a universal glass hall on a granite podium. He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1963 and died in Chicago in 1969. His disciplined approach to structure, proportion and materials profoundly influenced postwar architecture, and his students and followers carried the "Miesian" idiom into office towers and universities worldwide. He is also remembered for furniture designs such as the Barcelona, Brno and MR chairs, still in production.
