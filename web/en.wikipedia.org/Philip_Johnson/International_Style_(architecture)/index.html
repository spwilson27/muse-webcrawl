The International Style is a major architectural style that developed in Europe and the United States in the 1920s and 1930s and became the dominant style of corporate and institutional building for several decades. The term was coined in 1932 by historian Henry-Russell Hitchcock and architect Philip Johnson in connection with their exhibition Modern Architecture: International Exhibition at the Museum of Modern Art in New York, and their accompanying book The International Style: Architecture Since 1922.

Principles

Hitchcock and Johnson identified three defining principles: the expression of volume rather than mass — buildings conceived as thin planes enclosing space; regularity and a standardised grid rather than axial symmetry; and the avoidance of applied ornament, with elegance depending instead on the inherent qualities of materials and proportions. Typical features include flat roofs, smooth white or glass surfaces, steel and reinforced-concrete construction, cantilevered planes, ribbon windows and open interior plans.

European origins

The style grew out of European modernism of the 1920s: the Bauhaus under Walter Gropius, Le Corbusier's white villas of the decade (codified in his "Five Points of Architecture"), the De Stijl movement in the Netherlands, and the work of Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, whose Barcelona Pavilion (1929) epitomised the new architecture. Housing exhibitions such as the Weissenhof Estate in Stuttgart (1927) displayed the style to the public.

Spread to America and after

With the rise of Nazism, many leading modernists emigrated to the United States: Gropius and Marcel Breuer to Harvard, Mies to Chicago's Armour Institute (later IIT), and Josef Albers to Black Mountain College. After the Second World War the style became the default language of the corporate skyscraper, exemplified by Lever House (1952) and Mies and Johnson's Seagram Building (1958) in New York, and of glass-curtain-wall office buildings worldwide. Johnson's own Glass House (1949) applied the style to domestic architecture.

Criticism and legacy

From the 1960s the International Style was criticised for monotony, disregard of climate and context, and association with insensitive urban renewal. Critics such as Jane Jacobs and later postmodernists attacked its universalising claims. Nevertheless it shaped the skylines of cities on every continent, and renewed scholarly and preservation interest has recognised major works as landmarks of twentieth-century architecture.
