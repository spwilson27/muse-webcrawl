The High Court of Justiciary is the supreme criminal court in Scotland. It has jurisdiction over the most serious crimes, including murder, rape and treason, which cannot be tried elsewhere, and it also sits as the Court of Criminal Appeal, the final court of appeal in Scottish criminal cases.

History and composition

The court was established in its modern form in 1672, developing from the medieval office of Justiciar and the Justice Court that accompanied the king. Its judges are the senators of the College of Justice — the same judges who sit in the Court of Session, Scotland's supreme civil court — holding the additional office of Lords Commissioners of Justiciary. The court's heads are the Lord Justice General (an office held along with the Lord Presidency of the Court of Session) and the Lord Justice Clerk. The permanent seat is in Edinburgh, at the Lawnmarket on the Royal Mile, with regular sittings in Glasgow and Aberdeen and circuits elsewhere.

Jurisdiction and procedure

Prosecutions are brought in the public interest by the Lord Advocate through the Crown Office and Procurator Fiscal Service. Trials are held before a single judge and a jury of fifteen, which may convict by a majority of eight; Scots law retains the three-verdict system of guilty, not guilty, and not proven (the Scottish Government has legislated to abolish "not proven"). The court has exclusive competence for murder and rape and shares competence with sheriff courts for other solemn cases. It can impose any sentence authorised by law, including life imprisonment; there is no appeal from the High Court to the UK Supreme Court on the merits of a criminal case, preserving the independence of Scots criminal law guaranteed at the Union of 1707, although compatibility issues under the Scotland Act and human rights law may reach the Supreme Court.

Notable cases

The High Court tried Abdelbaset al-Megrahi for the 1988 Lockerbie bombing at a special sitting of the Scottish Court in the Netherlands at Camp Zeist (2000–2001). It also heard the appeal that led to the Cadder ruling's aftermath on suspects' access to solicitors, prompting emergency legislation in 2010.
