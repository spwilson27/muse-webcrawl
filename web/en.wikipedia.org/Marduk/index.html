Marduk

Marduk, chief god of Babylon, accompanied by his dragon Mušḫuššu.

Marduk (Akkadian: AMAR.UTU, often rendered "calf of Utu"; in the Hebrew Bible, Merodach) was the chief god of Babylon and the national god of Babylonia. Originally a local deity of the city of Babylon, Marduk rose with his city to become head of the Mesopotamian pantheon by the first millennium BC, absorbing the functions and epithets of older gods such as Enlil and Ea. He is celebrated in the creation epic Enūma Eliš as the champion of the gods who defeats the chaos monster Tiamat and fashions the ordered cosmos from her body.

Rise to supremacy

When Hammurabi (r. 1792–1750 BC) made Babylon the capital of an empire, he elevated Marduk in royal ideology, though Marduk's formal supremacy came later. By the Kassite and post-Kassite periods Babylonian theologians systematised Marduk's fifty names, recited in the final tablets of Enūma Eliš, each encoding an aspect of his power. When Nebuchadnezzar I recovered Marduk's statue from Elamite captivity in the twelfth century BC, Babylonian scribes celebrated the god as king of the gods.

Mythology

In Enūma Eliš, the young god Marduk agrees to battle the primordial sea Tiamat on condition that the assembled gods grant him supreme authority. Armed with winds, a net, bow and mace, he kills Tiamat, splits her body to form heaven and earth, organises the stars and calendar, and creates humankind from the blood of the rebel god Qingu to serve the gods. Babylon is founded as his city, with his temple Esagila and its ziggurat Etemenanki — plausibly the inspiration for the Tower of Babel story. Marduk's statue was carried in procession during the New Year (Akitu) festival, in which the epic was recited and the king's mandate ritually renewed.

Cult and later history

The statue of Marduk (often simply called "Bēl", "the Lord") was a palladium of Babylonian sovereignty; its capture by Hittites, Assyrians and Elamites marked national catastrophes, and its restoration occasions of triumph. Assyrian kings who ruled Babylon handled the cult carefully, and Sennacherib's destruction of Babylon in 689 BC promoted a rival Assyrian version of the epic with Ashur in Marduk's role. The cult survived into the Hellenistic period, fading only in the first centuries AD.
