English law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

English law is the common law legal system of England and Wales, comprising mainly criminal law and civil law, each branch having its own courts and procedures. It is one of the two legal systems in the United Kingdom (the other being Scots law, a mixed system), and its principles are exported to many other Commonwealth jurisdictions and former British colonies, forming the basis of the common law world in contrast to civil law systems derived from Roman law.

Sources and character

English law has three principal sources: statute law enacted by Parliament, retained European law of historical application, and the common law created by judges through precedent (stare decisis). Alongside the common law proper there developed equity, administered historically by the Court of Chancery, which tempered the rigidity of common law rules with principles of fairness; the two streams were fused administratively by the Judicature Acts of 1873–75. There is no single written constitution; constitutional law rests on statutes, conventions and case law.

Courts and profession

The senior courts are the Court of Appeal, the High Court and the Crown Court, with the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom (since 2009) as the final court of appeal. Legal practice is a divided profession of solicitors and barristers. Trial by jury survives chiefly in serious criminal cases; most civil disputes are decided by judges alone.

Influence

Through colonisation and commerce, English common law became the foundation of the legal systems of the United States, Canada, Australia, India, and many other countries, and English commercial law — particularly in contract, shipping and insurance — remains a frequent choice to govern international contracts, with London a leading centre for arbitration.
