Long Parliament

The Commons during the Long Parliament

The Long Parliament was an English Parliament which lasted from 1640 until 1648, when it was purged by the New Model Army, and effectively until 1660. It was summoned by King Charles I after the failure of the Short Parliament in the wake of the Bishops' Wars, because only the richer landowners in Parliament could raise taxes on a scale large enough for the king's needs.

Early reforms

Led in the Commons by John Pym, the Parliament secured its own survival by an act forbidding its dissolution without its own consent, passed the Triennial Act, abolished the prerogative courts of Star Chamber and High Commission, and impeached the king's ministers, the Earl of Strafford (executed in May 1641) and Archbishop Laud. The Grand Remonstrance of late 1641 listed grievances against the king's rule.

Civil War and purge

Charles's attempt to arrest five members in January 1642 precipitated the First English Civil War. Parliament raised its own army, allied with the Scottish Covenanters, and after its victory the army's political role grew. In December 1648, in Pride's Purge, Colonel Thomas Pride excluded members hostile to the army, leaving the Rump Parliament, which tried and executed Charles I in January 1649 and established the Commonwealth. Cromwell expelled the Rump in 1653; the surviving members were briefly recalled in 1659–1660 before the Restoration.
