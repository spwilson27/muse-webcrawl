Alluvial fan

An alluvial fan is an accumulation of sediments that fans outwards from a concentrated source of sediments, such as a narrow canyon emerging from an escarpment. They are characteristic of mountainous terrain in arid to semiarid climates, but are also found in more humid environments subject to intense rainfall and in areas of modern glaciation. Typical examples occur where a stream leaves a mountain range and slows abruptly on entering a plain, depositing its load in a cone- or fan-shaped body.

Formation

As a stream flows out of a confined channel onto a flatter surface, its velocity and carrying capacity drop sharply. Sediment carried as bed load and suspended load is deposited, beginning with the coarsest material near the apex. Over time the active channel shifts across the fan surface through avulsion, distributing sediment over the whole cone. Fans built mainly by debris flows are steep and coarse-grained, while those built by sheet floods and stream flows are broader and gentler.

Size and significance

Alluvial fans range from a few metres across to tens of kilometres in radius; the largest, such as the Kosi megafan in the Himalayan foreland, span tens of thousands of square kilometres. Fans are important aquifers, fertile agricultural sites, and hazards: flooding and debris flows on fan surfaces threaten settlements built on them. Similar fan deposits have been identified on Mars and Titan, indicating past flowing liquids.
