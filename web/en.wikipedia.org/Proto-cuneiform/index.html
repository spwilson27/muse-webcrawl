Proto-cuneiform

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The proto-cuneiform script is the earliest known stage of cuneiform, used in ancient Mesopotamia (modern Iraq) during the late 4th millennium BCE, roughly 3400–2900 BCE, in the Uruk period (Uruk IV and Uruk III strata). It consisted of pictographic and abstract signs impressed or incised into wet clay tablets, and was used chiefly for administrative records — accounts of commodities such as grain, beer, livestock, and land — and for lexical lists used in scribal training. Whether proto-cuneiform encoded a specific spoken language (usually assumed to be Sumerian) remains debated, since the script recorded numbers and commodities with little or no phonetic or grammatical indication.

Development

Proto-cuneiform evolved from earlier accounting devices: clay tokens used for millennia in the Near East, numerical tablets, and bullae (hollow clay envelopes containing tokens). Around 3400 BCE, scribes at Uruk began drawing the tokens' shapes on tablets and then impressing signs directly. The sign repertoire eventually numbered over a thousand signs, many of them pictographs: a head, a jar, an ear of barley. Over time the signs became more abstract as scribes rotated pictographs and drew them with the cut end of a reed stylus, producing the characteristic wedge-shaped (cuneiform) strokes of later periods.

Corpus and decipherment

About 6,000 proto-cuneiform tablets and fragments are known, the great majority from Uruk, with smaller numbers from other sites in southern Iraq and southwestern Iran. The numerical systems are comparatively well understood — the texts employed several different counting systems depending on what was being measured (sexagesimal for discrete objects, bisexagesimal for grain products, and special systems for area and capacity). The Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative has published photographs and transliterations of most of the corpus, enabling ongoing research into the sign lists and administrative practices of the world's first cities.

Significance

Proto-cuneiform is, together with Egyptian hieroglyphs, one of the earliest writing systems in the world. Its decipherment and study illuminate the invention of writing as an administrative technology of the early state: writing appears first not as literature, but as bookkeeping. During the Early Dynastic period the script gradually acquired phonetic values and began to record the Sumerian language fully, becoming true cuneiform.
