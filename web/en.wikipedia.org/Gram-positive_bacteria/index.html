Gram-positive bacteria

Gram-positive bacteria retain the crystal violet stain, appearing purple under the microscope

Gram-positive bacteria are bacteria that give a positive result in the Gram stain test, which is traditionally used to quickly classify bacteria into two broad categories according to their type of cell wall. Gram-positive bacteria take up the crystal violet stain used in the test, and then appear to be purple-colored when seen through an optical microscope.

Cell wall structure

The Gram-positive cell wall consists of a thick layer of peptidoglycan, typically 20 to 80 nanometres thick, surrounding the cytoplasmic membrane. Embedded in this layer are teichoic acids and lipoteichoic acids, which contribute to cell rigidity and ion regulation. Unlike Gram-negative bacteria, Gram-positive bacteria lack an outer membrane, though many possess a protective S-layer or capsule. The thick peptidoglycan retains the crystal violet–iodine complex during the alcohol decolorization step of Gram staining.

Classification

Most Gram-positive bacteria belong to the phyla Bacillota (Firmicutes) and Actinomycetota (Actinobacteria). They are further characterized by the guanine-cytosine content of their DNA: the Bacillota are generally low-GC, while the Actinomycetota are high-GC. Shapes include cocci (spheres), such as Staphylococcus and Streptococcus, and bacilli (rods), such as Bacillus, Clostridium, and Listeria.

Medical significance

Many Gram-positive species are human pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Bacillus anthracis. Because they lack an outer membrane, they are generally more susceptible to antibiotics such as penicillin and vancomycin that target peptidoglycan synthesis, although resistant strains such as MRSA have become a major clinical problem. Some species, such as Lactobacillus, are beneficial members of the human microbiome and are used in food fermentation.
