Moon rock

Moon rock or lunar rock is rock originating from the Moon. The term also applies loosely to materials formed during lunar impact events. Rocks collected from the Moon during human and robotic exploration are held in laboratories around the world and are among the most valuable scientific materials known.

Sources

Three kinds of missions have returned lunar material: the six crewed Apollo landings (1969–1972) brought back 382 kilograms (842 lb) of rock, core samples, and soil; three Soviet Luna spacecraft returned about 301 grams robotically; and China's Chang'e 5 returned 1,731 grams in 2020, with Chang'e 6 bringing the first samples from the far side of the Moon in 2024. Separately, more than 370 meteorites found on Earth are recognised as lunar in origin, ejected from the Moon by impacts.

The "Genesis Rock", an anorthosite collected by Apollo 15, dates to the Moon's early crust.

Composition

Lunar rocks fall into two broad types: the dark basalts of the maria (ancient lava plains) and the pale anorthosites of the highlands. They are distinguished from Earth rocks by their dryness — a near-total absence of water and volatile compounds — and by the presence of impact-produced glass (agglutinates) in the regolith. Radiometric dating shows most lunar surface rocks are 3 to 4.5 billion years old, far older than most terrestrial rocks.

Value and display

Apollo samples are generally considered the property of the United States government and cannot legally be sold, though tiny fragments were presented as goodwill gifts to nations and US states. A few legal sales and some notorious thefts and losses of goodwill samples have occurred. Lunar meteorites, by contrast, trade on the open market at high per-gram prices.
