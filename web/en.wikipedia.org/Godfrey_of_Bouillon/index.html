Godfrey of Bouillon

Godfrey of Bouillon (French: Godefroy de Bouillon; c. 1060 – 18 July 1100) was a Frankish knight and nobleman from the Duchy of Lower Lorraine who became one of the leaders of the First Crusade and the first ruler of the Kingdom of Jerusalem. According to later tradition, he refused the title of king, preferring to be called Advocatus Sancti Sepulchri — "Advocate (Defender) of the Holy Sepulchre".

Bornc. 1060, Boulogne-sur-Mer, County of Flanders
Died18 July 1100, Jerusalem
HouseFlanders
Reign1099–1100 (Jerusalem)

Life

Godfrey was the second son of Eustace II, Count of Boulogne, and inherited the lordship of Bouillon and, through imperial service, influence in Lower Lorraine; he fought for Emperor Henry IV in Italy and against the Saxons. To finance his crusading expedition he sold or mortgaged much of his land, including Bouillon itself.

The First Crusade

Godfrey led one of the main crusader armies through Hungary and Anatolia, fought at Dorylaeum and Antioch, and was among the first crusaders to enter Jerusalem when it fell on 15 July 1099, after which the crusaders massacred much of the population. Elected ruler of the new kingdom, he secured its position by defeating a Fatimid army at Ascalon. He died in July 1100, possibly of illness or, by some accounts, poisoning, and was succeeded by his brother Baldwin, who took the royal title Godfrey had declined. In medieval legend Godfrey became one of the Nine Worthies, the paragons of chivalry.
