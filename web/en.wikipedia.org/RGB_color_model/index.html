RGB color model

RGB color model
Primaries: Red, Green, Blue
Type: Additive
Typical depth: 24 bits per pixel ("true color")
Standard space: sRGB

The RGB color model is an additive color model in which red, green and blue light are combined in varying intensities to reproduce a broad range of colours. It is the standard model for electronic displays — monitors, televisions, cameras and scanners — because human colour vision is trichromatic, based on three types of cone cells in the eye.

How it works

With no light of any primary, the result is black; with all three at full intensity, the result is white. Each channel is typically stored in 8 bits, allowing 256 levels per channel and about 16.7 million distinct colours in a 24-bit image. Colours are often written in hexadecimal form, as in #FF5733, and the sRGB colour space defines the primaries and gamma used on the web and in most consumer devices.

History

The model rests on the Young–Helmholtz theory of trichromatic vision. James Clerk Maxwell produced the first colour photograph using red, green and blue filters in 1861. RGB became practical with colour television broadcasting in the 1950s and underlies later technologies from CRTs to LCD and OLED screens.

Limitations

RGB is device-dependent and cannot reproduce every colour visible to the eye. For printing, colours are usually converted to the subtractive CMYK model.
