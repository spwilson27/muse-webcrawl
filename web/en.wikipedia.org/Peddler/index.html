Peddler


Peddlers carried goods to customers who had no access to shops.

A peddler, in British English also spelled pedlar, is a travelling salesperson who carries goods from place to place, selling door to door or in the street. Related terms include hawker, chapman, and (historically, in England) petty chapman. Before the spread of shops and mail order, peddlers were a vital link in distribution, bringing needles, cloth, books, medicines, and news to rural households.

History

Peddling is ancient and worldwide. In early America, "Yankee peddlers" from New England toured the countryside with tinware and clocks; in Britain, pedlars required licences from the 17th century onward, and licensing under the Pedlars Acts continued into modern times. Some peddlers built substantial businesses — a number of major retail firms began as peddling rounds.

Regulation and decline

Peddlers were frequently regulated, taxed, and suspected of fraud or of spreading disease and sedition. The occupation declined with railways, department stores, and mail-order catalogues, but survives in street markets, food trucks, and seasonal selling. The word "peddle" also carries a figurative sense, as in peddling influence or rumours.
