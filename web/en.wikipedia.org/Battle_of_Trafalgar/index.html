Battle of Trafalgar

British ships of the line breaking the Franco-Spanish line in two columns, as painted in contemporary accounts.

The Battle of Trafalgar was a naval engagement fought on 21 October 1805 between the British Royal Navy and the combined fleets of the French and Spanish navies during the Napoleonic Wars. As part of Napoleon's planned invasion of the United Kingdom, the Franco-Spanish fleet was ordered to break out of Cádiz and link up with other forces, but was intercepted off Cape Trafalgar, on the southwest coast of Spain.

The British fleet of 27 ships of the line, commanded by Vice-Admiral Lord Nelson aboard HMS Victory, faced 33 allied ships under French Admiral Pierre-Charles Villeneuve. Nelson rejected the conventional parallel line of battle: instead his fleet advanced in two perpendicular columns to cut the enemy line into segments — the celebrated "Nelson Touch" — accepting raking fire during the approach in exchange for overwhelming local superiority once through.

Outcome

The allied fleet lost some 22 ships captured or destroyed; the British lost none. Allied casualties were roughly 4,400 killed and 2,500 wounded, against about 450 British killed and 1,200 wounded. Nelson himself was fatally wounded by a French sharpshooter aboard the Redoutable and died below decks, having signalled before the battle the immortal message "England expects that every man will do his duty."

Significance

Trafalgar ended any realistic prospect of a French invasion of Britain and confirmed British naval supremacy for more than a century; the Royal Navy's dominance of trade routes in the following decades shaped conflicts from Carthage-era Mediterranean rivalries' successors to 20th-century campaigns such as the response to the attack on Pearl Harbor. Trafalgar Square and Nelson's Column in London commemorate the victory, and the battle is still toasted annually in the Royal Navy on Trafalgar Night (21 October).
