Folk psychology

Folk psychology is the set of intuitive, everyday assumptions that ordinary people use to explain and predict human behaviour in terms of mental states such as beliefs, desires, intentions, emotions and perceptions. It is the commonsense framework by which people understand one another, as in explaining that someone opened the fridge because they wanted a drink and believed one was inside.

Theory of mind

Folk psychology is closely associated with the concept of theory of mind, the cognitive capacity to attribute mental states to oneself and others. Developmental psychologists study when children acquire this capacity, often using false-belief tasks such as the Sally-Anne test, which most children pass around the age of four or five.

Philosophical debate

Philosophers disagree about the status of folk psychology. Theory theorists hold that it constitutes an implicit theory, a body of law-like generalisations about how mental states cause behaviour. Simulation theorists argue instead that people predict others by imaginatively putting themselves in the other's position. Eliminative materialists, notably Paul and Patricia Churchland, have argued that folk psychology is a radically false theory that will eventually be displaced by mature neuroscience, while others defend it as an indispensable and largely accurate framework.

Applications

Understanding folk psychology matters in clinical psychology, artificial intelligence, law and economics, wherever systems must model how people reason about minds. Research on autism has explored differences in folk-psychological reasoning, and cross-cultural studies examine how mental-state concepts vary between communities.
