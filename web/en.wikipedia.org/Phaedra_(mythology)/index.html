Phaedra (mythology)

In Greek mythology, Phaedra (Ancient Greek: Φαίδρα, "bright") was a Cretan princess, the daughter of King Minos and Pasiphaë, sister of Ariadne, and the wife of Theseus, king of Athens. She is famed for her consuming, forbidden love for her stepson Hippolytus, which led to both their deaths. Her story is the subject of Euripides' tragedy Hippolytus and Seneca's Phaedra, and of Racine's Phèdre — often regarded as the summit of French classical tragedy.

Myth

Phaedra married Theseus and bore him sons, but fell desperately in love with Hippolytus, Theseus' son by the Amazon Hippolyta (or Antiope). In Euripides' surviving Hippolytus (428 BC), Aphrodite, slighted by Hippolytus' devotion to Artemis and his disdain for love, inflicts this passion as her revenge. Phaedra, wasting with shame, confides in her nurse, who reveals the secret to Hippolytus; bound by oath, he cannot speak of it but denounces her. Phaedra hangs herself, leaving a letter falsely accusing Hippolytus of rape. Theseus, returning, curses his son with one of the three curses granted by Poseidon; a sea-monster startles Hippolytus' horses and he is fatally dragged. Artemis then reveals the truth, and father and dying son are reconciled.

In Euripides' earlier, lost version, Phaedra apparently propositioned Hippolytus directly, scandalising audiences; Seneca's Roman version likewise makes Phaedra bolder. Later tradition adds details such as Phaedra's temple of Aphrodite at Athens, whence she watched Hippolytus exercising, and her burial at Troezen.

Interpretation and legacy

Phaedra embodies the conflict between desire and honour (sōphrosynē), and the destructive power of the gods' indifference. Major treatments include Euripides' two plays, Seneca's Phaedra, Racine's Phèdre (1677), and modern versions by poets and playwrights; operas such as Rameau's Hippolyte et Aricie rework the wider myth.
