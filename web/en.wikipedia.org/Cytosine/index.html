Cytosine (symbol C) is one of the four main nucleobases found in the nucleic acids DNA and RNA, the others being adenine, guanine, and thymine (uracil in RNA). It is a pyrimidine derivative with a heterocyclic aromatic ring and two substituents attached (an amine group at position 4 and a keto group at position 2). In Watson–Crick base pairing, cytosine forms three hydrogen bonds with guanine.

History

Cytosine was discovered and named in 1894 by Albrecht Kossel and Albert Neumann, who isolated it from calf thymus tissue; its structure was proposed in 1903 and confirmed by laboratory synthesis the same year. In 1997 cytosine was used in an early demonstration of quantum information processing in NMR-based quantum computing experiments.

Chemical reactions

Cytosine can be converted to uracil by spontaneous deamination; if unrepaired, this produces a point mutation. The enzyme cytidine deaminase performs the equivalent reaction on the nucleoside cytidine. Methylation of cytosine at the 5 position gives 5-methylcytosine, an important epigenetic mark; in mammals this occurs mainly at CpG sites and is associated with gene silencing. Cytosine also forms part of the high-energy nucleotide cytidine triphosphate (CTP), used in lipid synthesis.

In nucleic acids

The nucleoside of cytosine is cytidine, and in DNA cytosine pairs with guanine via three hydrogen bonds, making GC-rich regions of DNA more thermostable than AT-rich regions. The proportion of guanine plus cytosine (GC content) is a widely used descriptor of genomes.
