Election

An election is a formal group decision-making process by which a population chooses an individual or multiple individuals to hold public office. Elections have been the usual mechanism by which modern representative democracy has operated since the 17th century. They may fill offices in the legislature, sometimes in the executive and judiciary, and for regional and local government.

History

Elections were used in ancient Athens — though only a minority of the population were citizens permitted to vote — and in the Roman Republic, early India, and medieval institutions such as the election of popes and Holy Roman Emperors. The modern era of mass elections began as suffrage was extended from propertied men to all adult men and then to women during the 19th and 20th centuries.

Process

An election typically involves a campaign period, registration of voters, voting (in person at polling stations, by post, or increasingly by electronic means), counting, and certification of results. Key features of free and fair elections include a secret ballot, an independent electoral administration, universal and equal suffrage, and access for observers. Elections can be undermined by fraud, voter suppression, gerrymandering and disinformation.

Electoral systems

Systems differ in how votes translate into seats: first-past-the-post, two-round systems, proportional representation, and ranked-choice methods such as the single transferable vote. The study of elections and voting behaviour is called psephology; social choice theory studies properties of voting rules, including results such as Arrow's impossibility theorem.
