Big-game hunting

Big-game hunting is the hunting of large game animals for trophies, taxidermy, meat, and commercially valuable animal by-products (such as horns, antlers, tusks, bones, fur, body fat, or special organs). The term is often associated with the hunting of Africa's large mammals, including the "Big Five": lion, leopard, rhinoceros, elephant, and African buffalo.

History

Hunting large animals is as old as humanity, but big-game hunting as a sport grew with nineteenth-century colonial expansion. Figures such as Frederick Selous became famous as professional hunters, and expeditions like Theodore Roosevelt's 1909 Smithsonian safari popularised the safari in the public imagination. The era's largely unregulated hunting contributed to sharp declines in many species.

Methods and regulation

Modern big-game hunting is typically conducted on foot with high-powered rifles under licence and quota systems, often accompanied by professional hunters and trackers. Hunts are regulated by national law and, for international trade in trophies, by CITES. Record-keeping organisations such as Safari Club International and the Boone and Crockett Club maintain scoring systems based on horn or antler measurements.

Controversy

Trophy hunting is controversial. Supporters argue that fees fund conservation and anti-poaching and give local communities an economic stake in wildlife; critics argue that it is unethical, that revenues rarely reach communities, and that selective removal of prime males harms populations. The 2015 killing of Cecil the lion in Zimbabwe drew worldwide attention to the debate.
