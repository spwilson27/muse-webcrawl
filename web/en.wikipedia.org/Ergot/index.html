Ergot

Scientific nameClaviceps purpurea
KingdomFungi
OrderHypocreales
HostsRye, wheat and other cereals and grasses
ToxinsErgot alkaloids (e.g. ergotamine)

Ergot is a plant disease caused by the fungus Claviceps purpurea, which infects rye and other cereals. The fungus replaces grains with dark, purple-black resting bodies called sclerotia — the ergots — which contain potent alkaloids. When milled into flour and eaten, these cause the poisoning known as ergotism.

Life cycle

Spores of C. purpurea infect the flowers of the host plant in spring, mimicking the path of a pollen grain to the ovary. The fungus consumes the developing grain and eventually forms a hard sclerotium, which falls to the ground, overwinters, and in spring produces small fruiting bodies that release new spores.

Ergotism

Ergot poisoning in humans and livestock manifests in two main forms: gangrenous ergotism, in which vasoconstriction starves the extremities and causes them to blacken and die — known in medieval Europe as "St Anthony's fire" — and convulsive ergotism, marked by spasms, hallucinations and seizures. Epidemics repeatedly devastated rye-eating communities in the Middle Ages.

Medical uses

Despite their toxicity, ergot alkaloids have important pharmaceutical uses. Ergotamine has been used for migraine; ergometrine (ergonovine) to control postpartum haemorrhage; and the semi-synthetic derivative bromocriptine for Parkinson's disease and pituitary tumours. The chemist Albert Hofmann, working on ergot derivatives at Sandoz in 1938, synthesised lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD). See also: Ergotism, Claviceps purpurea, Rye, Ergotamine.
