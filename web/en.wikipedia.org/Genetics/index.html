Genetics is the study of genes, genetic variation and heredity in organisms. It seeks to understand how traits are passed from parents to offspring through DNA, how genes are expressed, and how they change over generations — questions central to biology, medicine, agriculture and evolution.

History

The science began with Gregor Mendel's experiments on pea plants in the 1860s, which revealed discrete "factors" (genes) segregating in predictable ratios; his work was rediscovered in 1900. Thomas Hunt Morgan's fruit-fly studies established that genes lie on chromosomes. In 1944 Oswald Avery's group showed DNA is the hereditary material, and in 1953 James Watson and Francis Crick, using Rosalind Franklin's X-ray diffraction data, deduced the double-helix structure of DNA. The genetic code was cracked in the 1960s, recombinant DNA technology followed in the 1970s, and the Human Genome Project completed a reference human genome in 2003.

Core concepts

Genes are DNA sequences encoding proteins or functional RNAs. Variants of a gene are alleles; an organism's genetic makeup is its genotype and its observable traits its phenotype. DNA is packaged into chromosomes; most human cells carry 46. Mutation generates new variation, while processes such as crossing over in meiosis reshuffle it. Gene expression — transcription to RNA and translation to protein — is regulated in response to cell type and environment, and epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylation modify expression without changing sequence.

Applications

Genetics underlies the diagnosis of inherited disorders such as Down syndrome and cystic fibrosis, selective breeding and genetically modified crops, forensic DNA profiling, ancestry testing, and modern genome editing with CRISPR–Cas9.
