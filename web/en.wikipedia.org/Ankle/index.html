Ankle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The ankle, or the talocrural region, is the area where the foot and the leg meet. The ankle joint proper (talocrural joint) is a hinged synovial joint formed by the tibia and fibula of the leg and the talus of the foot. It primarily allows dorsiflexion and plantarflexion — pointing the foot up and down — while the neighbouring subtalar joint permits side-to-side movement.

Structure

The lower ends of the tibia and fibula form a socket (mortise) that grips the talus. The bony prominences on either side are the medial malleolus (tibia) and lateral malleolus (fibula). The joint is stabilised by strong ligaments, including the deltoid ligament on the medial side and the anterior talofibular, calcaneofibular and posterior talofibular ligaments on the lateral side. Tendons of the leg muscles, the Achilles tendon chief among them, cross the ankle to move the foot.

Clinical significance

Ankle sprains — usually injury to the lateral ligaments when the foot rolls inward — are among the most common musculoskeletal injuries. Fractures often involve the malleoli. The ankle also bears the body's weight in walking and running and is affected by arthritis, tendinitis and conditions such as tarsal tunnel syndrome.
