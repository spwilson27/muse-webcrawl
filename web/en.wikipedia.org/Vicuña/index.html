Vicuña

Vicuña (Vicugna vicugna)
Family: Camelidae
Habitat: high Alpine grasslands of the central Andes, 3,200–4,800 m
Conservation status: Least Concern (recovered)

The vicuña is one of two wild South American camelids, along with the guanaco, living in the high alpine areas of the Andes. It is the smaller, more gracile relative of the llama and alpaca, and produces an extremely fine, soft wool that was prized by Inca royalty.

Biology

Vicuñas graze on tough puna grasses and must drink daily, unlike their relatives. Family groups consist of a territorial male with several females and their young; surplus males form bachelor herds. Their large hearts and lungs and unusually efficient haemoglobin adapt them to thin mountain air.

Conservation

Poaching for wool reduced vicuñas to about 6,000 animals by the 1960s. Strict protection and the Vicuña Convention of 1979, permitting regulated shearing of live animals by Andean communities, restored numbers to several hundred thousand. Vicuña cloth remains among the most expensive natural fibres in the world.
