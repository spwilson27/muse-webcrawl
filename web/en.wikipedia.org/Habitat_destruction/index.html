Habitat destruction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Habitat destruction (also termed habitat loss and habitat reduction) is the process by which a natural habitat becomes incapable of supporting its native species. The organisms that previously inhabited the site are displaced or dead, reducing biodiversity and species abundance. Habitat destruction is the leading cause of biodiversity loss and species extinction worldwide, ahead of overexploitation, invasive species, pollution and climate change, although the drivers interact.

Causes

The principal human causes are land conversion for agriculture, urbanisation, mining and resource extraction, deforestation for timber and fuel, and infrastructure development. By some estimates, around half of the world's habitable land has been converted to agriculture. Coastal development, bottom trawling and the drainage of wetlands destroy marine and freshwater habitats, while fragmentation by roads and clearings breaks continuous habitat into isolated patches that cannot support viable populations.

Effects

When a habitat is destroyed, the carrying capacity for indigenous flora, fauna and other organisms is reduced so that populations decline, sometimes to the point of extinction. Fragmentation increases edge effects, exposes species to predators and invasive competitors, and disrupts migration and pollination. The Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES) reported in 2019 that around one million species were threatened with extinction, many within decades, with habitat loss a central driver.

Hotspots

Conservation biologists prioritise biodiversity hotspots — biogeographic regions with exceptional concentrations of endemic species undergoing exceptional habitat loss. Regions such as Madagascar, the Atlantic Forest of Brazil, and the forests of Southeast Asia have lost the great majority of their original vegetation cover.
