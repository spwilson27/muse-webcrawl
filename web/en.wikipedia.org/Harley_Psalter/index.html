The Harley Psalter (British Library, Harley MS 603) is an Anglo-Saxon illuminated manuscript psalter produced at Christ Church, Canterbury, in the first decades of the 11th century. It is the earliest and most faithful of three medieval copies of the Carolingian Utrecht Psalter, and it is celebrated for its energetic pen drawings, which transpose the animated Reims style of its model into an English idiom of outline and coloured wash.

Description

The manuscript contains the Latin text of the Psalms in the Roman version, each psalm accompanied by a literal illustration of its imagery placed within or above the text column, exactly as in the Utrecht Psalter, which had been made near Reims around 816–835 and was present at Canterbury by about the year 1000. More than one hundred drawings accompany the psalms. The Harley Psalter was executed in several campaigns by multiple artists; parts of the book were left unfinished, and work resumed intermittently, with some drawings added later in the 11th and 12th centuries.

History

The book remained in England through the Middle Ages. It takes its name from Robert Harley, 1st Earl of Oxford (1661–1724), the statesman and bibliophile whose vast collection of manuscripts was purchased for the nation in 1753 and became one of the foundation collections of the British Museum library, now the British Library.

Significance

Together with its sister copies, the Eadwine Psalter (c. 1155–1160) and the Anglo-Catalan Psalter (c. 1180s–1200), the Harley Psalter documents the extraordinary afterlife of the Utrecht Psalter's picture cycle, the most influential programme of psalm illustration in medieval art. Its sketch-like tinted drawings are a landmark of Anglo-Saxon draughtsmanship.
