Arithmetica (Greek: Ἀριθμητικά) is an Ancient Greek text on mathematics written by Diophantus of Alexandria in the 3rd century AD. It is a collection of problems giving numerical solutions of both determinate and indeterminate equations, and it became one of the foundational works in the history of algebra and number theory. Equations whose solutions are sought in integers or rational numbers are still called Diophantine equations.

Contents

The work originally comprised 13 books, of which six survive in Greek and four more were recovered in a 10th-century Arabic translation identified in 1968. The problems typically ask for numbers satisfying given conditions — for example, dividing a square into two squares — and Diophantus presents ingenious particular solutions rather than general methods. He introduced an abbreviated, "syncopated" notation for unknowns and powers, an important step between rhetorical algebra and modern symbolism.

Influence

The Arithmetica was translated into Latin by Xylander (1575) and edited by Claude Gaspard Bachet de Méziriac (1621). It was in the margin of Bachet's edition, beside Problem II.8, that Pierre de Fermat wrote his famous note claiming that no three positive integers a, b, c satisfy an + bn = cn for n > 2 — Fermat's Last Theorem — adding that the margin was too narrow to contain his "truly marvellous proof". The theorem resisted proof until 1994.
