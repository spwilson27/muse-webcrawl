Poisson point process

FieldProbability theory, spatial statistics
Named afterSiméon Denis Poisson
Key propertyIndependent increments; Poisson counts

A Poisson point process is a type of random mathematical object that consists of points randomly located on a mathematical space such as the real line or the plane. It is a fundamental model in probability theory for phenomena in which events occur at random positions or times, with no interaction between them.

The process is characterized by two defining properties. First, the number of points falling in any bounded region follows a Poisson distribution whose mean is proportional to the size of the region. Second, the numbers of points in disjoint regions are independent random variables. The constant of proportionality is called the intensity of the process.

Definitions

In the homogeneous case on the real line with intensity λ > 0, the number N(a, b] of points in an interval of length L is Poisson distributed with mean λL, and counts over disjoint intervals are independent. The inhomogeneous (or nonhomogeneous) Poisson process generalizes this by allowing the intensity to vary with position through an intensity function λ(x).

History

The process takes its name from the French mathematician Siméon Denis Poisson, who introduced the related distribution in 1837. The point-process formulation was developed in the early twentieth century through applications in telephone traffic by A. K. Erlang and in the work of William Feller and Aleksandr Khinchin.

Applications

Poisson point processes model the locations of trees in a forest, the arrivals of calls at a call centre, radioactive decay events, stars in a galaxy, and users in a wireless network. In spatial statistics, the process serves as the benchmark model of complete spatial randomness, against which clustering or regularity in data is tested.

Related processes

Important generalizations include the Cox process (a Poisson process with a random intensity), the renewal process, and marked point processes, in which each point carries an additional random quantity or "mark". The Poisson process in one dimension is also the canonical example of a counting process with independent increments.
