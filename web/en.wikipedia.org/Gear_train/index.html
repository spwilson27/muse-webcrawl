Gear train

A gear train is a mechanical system formed by mounting two or more gears on a frame so that their teeth engage and transmit rotation from an input shaft to an output shaft. Gear trains are used to change the speed, torque and direction of a power source, and are found in clocks, automobiles, machine tools and countless other machines.

Types

A simple gear train has one gear on each shaft; gears between the first and last, called idlers, reverse the direction of rotation but do not change the overall gear ratio. In a compound gear train, two gears of different sizes are fixed together on the same shaft, allowing large ratios to be achieved in a compact space. An epicyclic (planetary) gear train has one or more planet gears orbiting a central sun gear, often inside a ring gear; planetary systems are used in automatic transmissions and hub gears because they offer several ratios and high torque density.

Analysis

The overall ratio of a gear train is the product of the tooth-count ratios of each meshing pair, with a sign change for each external mesh. Because power (minus friction losses) is conserved, reducing output speed by a factor increases output torque by approximately the same factor. Gear trains known since antiquity include those of the Antikythera mechanism, an ancient Greek astronomical calculator built from dozens of bronze gears.
