Diaphragm (optics)

In optics, a diaphragm is a thin opaque structure with an opening (aperture) at its center. Its role is to block most of the light, except the light passing through the aperture. It is usually placed in the light path of a lens or objective to regulate the amount of light passing through the system.

Aperture stop and field stop

The diaphragm that controls the brightness of the image by limiting the cone of light is called the aperture stop. A diaphragm that limits the size of the field of view is called a field stop. In a camera lens, the aperture stop is commonly an adjustable iris diaphragm made of overlapping metal leaves whose opening can be varied continuously or in steps, measured in f-numbers.

Effects

Stopping down a diaphragm (reducing the aperture) increases the depth of field and generally improves sharpness by masking off-axis aberrations, at the cost of requiring longer exposure times; very small apertures degrade sharpness again through diffraction. The shape of the diaphragm also shapes out-of-focus highlights, an effect known as bokeh.
