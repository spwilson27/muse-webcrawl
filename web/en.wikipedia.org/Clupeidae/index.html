Clupeidae

Clupeidae is a family of ray-finned fishes, commonly known as herrings, sardines, shads, and menhadens. It comprises roughly 200 species in more than 50 genera, found in marine, brackish, and fresh waters worldwide, with the greatest diversity in tropical seas. Clupeids are typically small, silvery, schooling fishes that feed on plankton and form a crucial link between plankton production and larger predators in aquatic food webs.

Description

Clupeids have streamlined, laterally compressed bodies covered in large, easily shed cycloid scales, a single dorsal fin, forked tail fins, and often a series of sharp scutes along the belly. Most species lack teeth on the jaws and feed by filtering plankton through elaborate gill rakers, or by picking individual prey. Many have a silvery lateral band produced by guanine crystals in the scales, an adaptation for camouflage in open water.

Ecology

Most clupeids are pelagic schooling fish of coastal waters, migrating in vast shoals that may number in the millions. They convert plankton into biomass eaten by seabirds, marine mammals, and predatory fish such as tuna and cod, making them classic forage fish. Some species, such as the shads of the genus Alosa, are anadromous, ascending rivers to spawn, while the alewife has become naturalised in the North American Great Lakes.

Fisheries

The family includes some of the most heavily fished species on Earth. Atlantic herring, sardines (pilchards), and menhaden support major fisheries for human food, fish meal, and oil; clupeids collectively have accounted for a substantial share of the world marine catch. Their populations are prone to dramatic boom-and-collapse cycles driven by oceanographic conditions, as seen in the historic collapses of the California sardine and North Sea herring fisheries, which prompted modern quota management.

Genera

Well-known genera include Clupea (true herrings), Sardina and Sardinella (sardines), Alosa (shads and alewives), Brevoortia (menhadens), and Sprattus (sprats). Recent classifications place the family within the order Clupeiformes alongside the anchovies (Engraulidae).
