Theosophy is an esoteric religious and philosophical movement established in the United States during the late 19th century. It was founded principally by the Russian émigrée Helena Blavatsky, who claimed that its teachings derived from an ancient, universal wisdom-religion preserved by a hidden brotherhood of adepts ("Masters" or "Mahatmas"). The Theosophical Society was founded in New York in 1875 by Blavatsky, Henry Steel Olcott, and William Quan Judge.

Teachings

Theosophy draws heavily on Hindu and Buddhist concepts such as karma and reincarnation, combined with Western esotericism including Neoplatonism, Hermeticism, and Kabbalah. In Blavatsky's major work The Secret Doctrine (1888), the universe is presented as the emanation of an impersonal Absolute, evolving cyclically through vast ages; humanity itself develops through a succession of "root races" on a spiritual journey toward perfection. The Society's stated objects are to form a nucleus of universal brotherhood, encourage the comparative study of religion, philosophy, and science, and investigate unexplained laws of nature and latent human powers.

History and influence

In 1882 the Society's headquarters moved to Adyar, Madras (now Chennai), India, where it became entangled with Indian cultural and nationalist revival. After Blavatsky's death in 1891 the movement split into rival organizations; under Annie Besant it promoted the young Jiddu Krishnamurti as a coming World Teacher, a role he repudiated in 1929. Theosophy profoundly influenced modern Western esotericism, the New Age movement, and artists and writers including W. B. Yeats, Wassily Kandinsky, and Piet Mondrian.
