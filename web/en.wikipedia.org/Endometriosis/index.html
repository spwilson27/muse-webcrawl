Endometriosis is a disease in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus (the endometrium) grows outside the uterus. It occurs in women and girls of reproductive age, most commonly affecting the ovaries, fallopian tubes and the tissue lining the pelvis; rarely, endometrial-like tissue may be found beyond the pelvic organs. The displaced tissue continues to act as it normally would — it thickens, breaks down and bleeds with each menstrual cycle — but because it has no way to exit the body it becomes trapped, causing irritation, scar tissue and adhesions.

Signs and symptoms

The primary symptoms are pelvic pain and infertility. Pain is often associated with menstrual periods (dysmenorrhoea) and may include pain with intercourse, bowel movements or urination, as well as excessive bleeding and fatigue. Nearly half of those affected have chronic pelvic pain, and up to half experience difficulty conceiving. The severity of pain does not reliably correspond to the extent of the disease.

Causes and diagnosis

The cause is not fully understood. Leading hypotheses include retrograde menstruation, transformation of peritoneal cells, immune dysfunction and genetic predisposition; having a mother or sister with the condition increases risk. Diagnosis has traditionally required surgical visualisation by laparoscopy, but imaging and clinical diagnosis are increasingly used. Average delays to diagnosis of several years are commonly reported.

Treatment

There is no cure, but symptoms can be managed. Pain is treated with NSAIDs and hormonal therapies — combined oral contraceptives, progestins and GnRH analogues — which suppress lesion activity. Surgery can remove lesions and improve fertility in some patients, and hysterectomy with oophorectomy is a last resort. Endometriosis affects an estimated 10% of women of reproductive age worldwide, with substantial effects on quality of life, work and relationships.
