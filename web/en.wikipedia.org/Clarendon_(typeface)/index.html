Clarendon (typeface)

Clarendon

Clarendon is a slab-serif typeface created in England by Robert Besley for the Fann Street Foundry. It was published in 1845 and became the archetype of the slab-serif style: bold letterforms with heavy, bracketed serifs and relatively even stroke weight, designed originally as a bold companion to ordinary roman text faces for emphasis.

History

Clarendon was the first typeface to be registered under the British Ornamental Designs Act of 1842, giving it a measure of legal protection against copying—an early forerunner of typeface design protection. Its robust character made it a staple of 19th-century posters, handbills, and "Wanted" notices, and it remains strongly associated with the visual language of the American Old West.

Revivals and use

Twentieth-century revivals include Hermann Eidenbenz's Clarendon for Haas and Linotype (1953), and versions by Monotype, URW, and Adobe. Freeman Craw's Craw Clarendon adapted the style for American use. Clarendon and its relatives appear in logos, signage, and book design; the German railway Deutsche Bahn and many publishers have used Clarendon-style faces for display work.
