Mascarpone

Italian acid-set cream cheese

Mascarpone is an Italian acid-set cream cheese made by coagulating cream with citric or acetic acid. It is ivory-coloured, exceptionally smooth and spreadable, with a mild, slightly sweet flavour and a very high fat content — typically around 44 percent, and up to 75 percent of dry matter. It is best known internationally as an essential ingredient of the dessert tiramisu.

Origin and production

Mascarpone originated in Lombardy, in the area between Lodi and Abbiategrasso south-west of Milan, probably in the late 16th or early 17th century. The name may derive from mascarpa, a local dialect word for an unrelated milk product, or from the Spanish phrase más que bueno, "better than good."

Traditionally, mascarpone was made from the cream skimmed from milk destined for Parmesan production. In modern manufacture, heavy cream is heated to about 85–90 °C and acidified; the curd is then drained in cloth, giving a dense, spoonable product with no rind and a short shelf life. Unlike most cheeses, it is made without rennet and is not aged.

Uses

Beyond tiramisu, mascarpone is used in cheesecakes, as a filling for pastries, stirred into risotto and pasta sauces, and served simply with fruit or honey. It spoils quickly once opened and should be used within a few days.
