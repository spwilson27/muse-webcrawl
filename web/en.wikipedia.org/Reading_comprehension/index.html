Reading comprehension

Reading comprehension is the ability to process text, understand its meaning, and integrate what the reader has read with what the reader already knows. It depends on two interrelated abilities: word reading (decoding written words accurately and fluently) and language comprehension (understanding spoken language). An individual's ability to comprehend text is influenced by their skills and their ability to process information.

Processes

Comprehension involves constructing a coherent mental representation of a text. Readers draw on vocabulary knowledge, background knowledge, inference-making, working memory, and comprehension monitoring — noticing when understanding breaks down and applying repair strategies such as re-reading. The "simple view of reading" frames reading comprehension as the product of decoding and linguistic comprehension: weakness in either component produces poor comprehension.

Levels

Instruction commonly distinguishes literal comprehension (recalling facts stated in the text), inferential comprehension (drawing conclusions not explicitly stated), and evaluative or critical comprehension (judging quality, accuracy and worth). More complex models describe levels of processing from surface code through a textbase to a situational model of the events described.

Instruction and assessment

Evidence-based strategies include reciprocal teaching, summarisation, question generation, graphic organisers, and vocabulary instruction. Comprehension is assessed through standardised tests, cloze tasks, retelling, and open-ended questions, and forms a core outcome in international studies such as PIRLS and PISA. Difficulties with comprehension, distinct from decoding problems, are a focus of reading research and intervention.
