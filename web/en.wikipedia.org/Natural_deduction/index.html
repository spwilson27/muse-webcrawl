Natural deduction

In logic and proof theory, natural deduction is a kind of proof calculus in which logical deduction is expressed by inference rules closely related to the "natural" way of reasoning. Natural deduction grew out of the dissatisfaction with axiomatic systems common in early 20th-century formal logic.

History

The system was developed independently by Gerhard Gentzen in Germany and Stanisław Jaśkowski in Poland, both publishing in 1934. Gentzen presented natural deduction as a means of studying how proofs proceed in mathematical practice, and went on to develop the sequent calculus as a tool for proving consistency results.

Rules

In natural deduction, each logical connective is governed by introduction and elimination rules. For example, to prove an implication A → B, one assumes A and derives B (implication introduction); from an implication and its antecedent, one may derive the consequent (implication elimination, modus ponens). Assumptions can be discharged as subproofs are completed. Dag Prawitz later developed normalization theory for natural deduction, a cornerstone of proof theory and, through the Curry–Howard correspondence, a foundation for the semantics of functional programming languages.
