Alexandre Gustave Eiffel (15 December 1832 – 27 December 1923) was a French civil engineer and architect. A graduate of the École Centrale des Arts et Manufactures, he built an international reputation designing railway bridges and metal structures across Europe and beyond. He is best known for the world-famous Eiffel Tower, designed by his company and built for the 1889 Universal Exposition in Paris, and for the internal framework of the Statue of Liberty in New York.

Career

Eiffel founded his construction company in 1868. His works include the Maria Pia Bridge in Porto, the Garabit Viaduct in France (completed 1884, then the world's highest bridge), the Budapest Nyugati railway station, and the dome of the Nice Observatory. His firm also designed the internal armature supporting Bartholdi's Statue of Liberty (1886).

Eiffel Tower

The tower, designed by engineers Maurice Koechlin and Émile Nouguier with architect Stephen Sauvestre, was built between 1887 and 1889 as the entrance arch to the 1889 World's Fair marking the centenary of the French Revolution. At 300 metres (330 with its antenna) it was the tallest human-made structure in the world until 1930. Initially criticised by many artists, it became a global icon of France.

Later life

After the Panama Canal locks scandal of 1893, in which he was convicted and later cleared on appeal, Eiffel withdrew from business and devoted himself to scientific research in meteorology and aerodynamics, using the tower for experiments. His name is one of the 72 names inscribed on the Eiffel Tower. He died in Paris in 1923.
