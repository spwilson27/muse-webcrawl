Supermarine Spitfire

The Supermarine Spitfire is a British single-seat fighter aircraft used by the Royal Air Force and many Allied countries before, during, and after the Second World War. Designed by R. J. Mitchell of Supermarine, it first flew on 5 March 1936 and is famed for its elliptical wing and its role, alongside the Hawker Hurricane, in the Battle of Britain (1940).

Design and development

The Spitfire combined a Rolls-Royce Merlin engine with a thin elliptical wing that gave low drag and excellent maneuverability, plus a monocoque airframe of light alloys. Mitchell died in 1937 and his colleague Joseph Smith carried development forward. Through the war the type was progressively re-engined with the larger Griffon engine, gaining speed, armament, and roles: interceptor, fighter-bomber, and photographic reconnaissance. Navalized versions served as the Seafire.

Service

More than 20,300 Spitfires of all variants were built, and the aircraft remained in production throughout the war — the only British fighter to do so. It served in every theatre, and front-line RAF use continued into the 1950s. Its blend of beauty and performance made it an enduring symbol of British resistance; public "Spitfire Funds" raised money to sponsor individual aircraft during the war.
