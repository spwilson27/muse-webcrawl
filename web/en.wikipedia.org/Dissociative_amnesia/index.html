Dissociative amnesia

Dissociative amnesia

Specialty: Psychiatry, clinical psychology

Symptoms: Inability to recall important autobiographical information

Causes: Psychological trauma or stress

Classification: Dissociative disorder (DSM-5)

Dissociative amnesia is a mental disorder characterised by an inability to recall important autobiographical information, usually of a traumatic or stressful nature, that is inconsistent with ordinary forgetting. It is classified among the dissociative disorders in the DSM-5.

Presentation

The memory loss in dissociative amnesia is typically localised to a specific event or period, though it may be selective (some aspects of an event) or, rarely, generalised to the person's entire life history and identity. Unlike amnesia caused by brain damage, dissociative amnesia is thought to arise as a psychological defence against overwhelming experience, and the lost memories are often recoverable.

Dissociative fugue

In the most dramatic cases, the amnesia is accompanied by dissociative fugue: sudden, unexpected travel away from home or work, with an inability to recall one's past and sometimes the assumption of a new identity. In the DSM-5, dissociative fugue became a specifier of dissociative amnesia rather than a separate diagnosis.

Causes

Dissociative amnesia is most often associated with severe stress or trauma, including combat, natural disasters, and physical or sexual abuse. The condition has been reported at elevated rates during wartime; cases among soldiers in the First and Second World Wars were described under labels such as shell shock.

Treatment

Treatment generally involves psychotherapy aimed at gently recovering the lost memories and integrating them, together with support and treatment of co-occurring conditions such as depression or post-traumatic stress disorder. There is no medication specific to the condition.
