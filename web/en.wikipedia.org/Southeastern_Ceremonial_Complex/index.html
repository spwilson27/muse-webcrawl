Southeastern Ceremonial Complex

Southeastern Ceremonial Complex (S.E.C.C.)
Also called: the Southern Cult
Period: c. 1200–1650 CE
Culture: Mississippian culture
Key sites: Cahokia, Moundville, Spiro, Etowah

The Southeastern Ceremonial Complex (S.E.C.C.), often called the Southern Cult, is the name given by archaeologists to a shared body of religious imagery, motifs and ritual objects that spread across the Mississippian world of the American Southeast and Midwest roughly between 1200 and 1650 CE. Artefacts in a distinctive style — engraved marine shell gorgets and cups, repoussé copper plates, carved stone palettes and effigy pipes — have been found at major mound centres including Cahokia, Moundville (Alabama), Spiro (Oklahoma) and Etowah (Georgia).

Motifs and cosmology

Recurring motifs include the falcon warrior or "Birdman", the cross-in-circle sun symbol, the hand-and-eye motif, long-nosed god maskettes, chunkey players, and serpent and underworld imagery. Scholars interpret these through a three-tiered cosmology (Above World, This World, Beneath World) reconstructed from later Southeastern Native American mythology, particularly Muskogee, Cherokee and Natchez traditions.

Interpretation

Early researchers imagined a pan-regional religious cult; modern archaeologists generally see the S.E.C.C. as an elite prestige-goods and ritual network — sometimes divided into regional "horizons" such as the Braden style associated with Cahokia — through which chiefdoms shared ideology and legitimised hereditary elites.
