Unconventional oil

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Unconventional oil is petroleum produced or extracted using techniques other than the conventional methods of drilling vertical wells into free-flowing reservoirs. It includes oil shales, oil sands (tar sands), extra-heavy oil, tight oil (shale oil), and oil derived from coal or gas conversion. Unconventional sources generally require more energy, water, and capital to produce than conventional oil, and often produce greater greenhouse-gas emissions per barrel.

Types

  • Oil sands — mixtures of sand, clay, water, and bitumen, surface-mined or produced in situ with steam (for example in Alberta, Canada, and Venezuela's Orinoco Belt)
  • Tight oil — light crude oil contained in low-permeability shale or tight sandstone formations, produced using horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing (for example in the Bakken and Permian basins)
  • Oil shale — sedimentary rock containing kerogen, which must be heated (retorted) to produce synthetic crude
  • Extra-heavy oil — highly viscous oil that does not flow readily at reservoir conditions
  • Coal-to-liquids and gas-to-liquids — synthetic fuels manufactured from coal or natural gas

History and development

Production of unconventional oil expanded rapidly in the 2000s and 2010s as high oil prices and technological advances — especially horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing — made it economic. The tight-oil boom in the United States reversed decades of declining American output and made the country the world's largest crude oil producer by 2018. Canada's oil sands became one of the largest sources of crude imports to the United States.

Environmental concerns

Unconventional oil production typically has a larger environmental footprint than conventional production: higher carbon intensity (particularly for oil sands, because of the energy used in extraction and upgrading), large water consumption and tailings ponds in mining operations, land disturbance, and, for tight oil, concerns over hydraulic fracturing, wastewater disposal, and induced seismicity. These impacts have made unconventional oil a focus of climate policy debate.
