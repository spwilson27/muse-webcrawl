3D computer graphics

3D computer graphics, sometimes called CGI (computer-generated imagery), are graphics that use three-dimensional representations of geometric data stored in a computer to perform calculations and render digital images, usually 2D images but sometimes 3D models. They are central to video games, film and television visual effects, architectural visualisation, simulation, and product design.

Pipeline

The creation of 3D graphics generally proceeds through modelling, layout and animation, and rendering. In modelling, objects are built from polygons (usually triangles), spline surfaces, or volumetric data. Surfaces are given materials and textures that describe how they respond to light. In rendering, the scene is converted into an image from a chosen viewpoint: rasterisation projects geometry onto the screen in real time, while ray tracing simulates the paths of light rays to produce physically realistic reflections, refractions and shadows at much greater computational cost.

History

The field began in the 1960s with Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad and the work of researchers at the University of Utah, who developed foundational algorithms for hidden-surface removal, shading (Gouraud and Phong shading) and texture mapping. The Utah teapot became a standard test model. By the 1990s, dedicated graphics processing units (GPUs) made real-time 3D widely available, and fully computer-animated feature films such as Toy Story (1995) demonstrated the maturity of offline rendering.

Software and APIs

Popular 3D applications include Blender, Autodesk Maya, Cinema 4D and Houdini, while real-time graphics APIs such as OpenGL, Direct3D, Vulkan and Metal interface between applications and GPU hardware.
