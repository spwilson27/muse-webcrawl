Sixties Scoop

The Sixties Scoop is a term for the practice in Canada, from the 1950s through the 1980s, of removing (“scooping up”) Indigenous children from their families and communities and placing them in foster care or adoptive homes, usually with non-Indigenous families in Canada, the United States, and abroad.

History

Amendments to the Indian Act in 1951 allowed provincial child-welfare authorities to operate on reserves. Social workers, often applying middle-class standards and misunderstanding Indigenous extended-family caregiving, apprehended children in large numbers. Researcher Patrick Johnston coined the term in his 1983 report Native Children and the Child Welfare System. Estimates of the number of children removed range from about 20,000 to higher figures. Many adoptees lost contact with their birth families, languages, and cultures, and many reported abuse and identity struggles.

Response and redress

In Manitoba, the 1985 Kimelman Report (No Quiet Place) condemned the practice, and Manitoba became the first province to apologize. In 2017 the federal government agreed to a settlement of about $875 million for survivors, and in 2018 Minister of Crown-Indigenous Relations Carolyn Bennett delivered a formal apology in the House of Commons. Several provinces have also apologized. The Sixties Scoop is now discussed alongside the residential school system as part of Canada's assimilationist policies toward Indigenous peoples.
