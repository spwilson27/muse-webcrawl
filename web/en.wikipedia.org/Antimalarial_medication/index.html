Antimalarial medication

Antimalarial medications or simply antimalarials are a type of antiparasitic chemical agent, often naturally derived, that can be used to treat or to prevent malaria, in the latter case, most often aiming at two susceptible target groups, young children and pregnant women. As of 2018, modern treatments, including for severe malaria, continued to depend on therapies deriving historically from quinine and artesunate, both parenteral (injectable) drugs.

History

Quinine, an alkaloid from the bark of the South American cinchona tree, was the first effective treatment for malaria and was used from the 17th century. Synthetic derivatives such as chloroquine were developed in the 20th century but widespread resistance reduced their effectiveness. Artemisinin, isolated from sweet wormwood (Artemisia annua) by Tu Youyou and colleagues in China in the 1970s, became the basis of modern artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs).

Main classes

  • Quinine and related agents — quinine, chloroquine, mefloquine, primaquine.
  • Antifolates — proguanil, pyrimethamine, sulfadoxine combinations.
  • Artemisinin derivatives — artemether, artesunate, usually combined with a partner drug.
  • Antibiotics with antimalarial activity — doxycycline, clindamycin.

Resistance

Drug resistance in Plasmodium falciparum has spread successively against chloroquine, sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine, and, in Southeast Asia, partial resistance to artemisinin has emerged, motivating combination therapy and ongoing drug development.
