The Gulf of Suez (Arabic: خليج السويس, Khalīj as-Suways) is a gulf at the northern end of the Red Sea, to the west of the Sinai Peninsula. Situated to the east of the Gulf of Suez is the smaller Gulf of Aqaba; together the two gulfs form the northern twin arms of the Red Sea. The Gulf of Suez was formed within a relatively young but inactive Gulf of Suez Rift dating to the Oligocene–Miocene (roughly 28 million years ago), as the African and Arabian plates began to separate. Stretching about 314 km from the Strait of Jubal in the south to the city of Suez in the north, it is the arm through which the Suez Canal — opened in 1869 — reaches the Red Sea and thus the world's shipping routes to the Indian Ocean.

Geography and geology

The gulf is comparatively shallow, with depths generally under 100 m, unlike the deep axial trough of the Gulf of Aqaba. Its rift basin, with thick Miocene sediments and evaporites, hosts Egypt's oldest and historically most important oil province: fields around Ras Gharib, Belayim and the Morgan field have produced oil since the early 20th century, and offshore production remains significant. Strong northwesterly winds funnelled between the mountain ranges on either shore make the gulf's western coast — around Zafarana and Ras Ghareb — one of Egypt's prime wind-energy regions, with some of the largest wind farms in Africa.

Settlements and economy

Beyond Suez at its head, settlements include the oil towns of Ras Gharib and Ras Shukheir, the resort and port developments at Ain Sokhna (serving Cairo), and El Tor on the Sinai shore. The SUMED pipeline carries crude oil from Ain Sokhna on the gulf to Sidi Kerir on the Mediterranean, bypassing the canal. Tourism is less developed than on the Gulf of Aqaba coast (Sharm El Sheikh) but the gulf's northern shores near Ain Sokhna are a popular weekend destination from Cairo.

History

The gulf and its deserts were known in antiquity for mining (turquoise and copper in Sinai) and Red Sea trade; the crossing of the Red Sea in the Exodus narrative is traditionally associated by some with the gulf's northern reaches. Control of the gulf was strategically vital in the Arab–Israeli wars; Egyptian and Israeli forces contested its shores in 1956, 1967 and 1973, and Israel occupied the Sinai shore until the peace treaty and withdrawal completed in 1982.
