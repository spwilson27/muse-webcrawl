Greyfriars Kirkyard

Greyfriars Kirkyard surrounds Greyfriars Kirk in the Old Town of Edinburgh.

Greyfriars Kirkyard is the graveyard surrounding Greyfriars Kirk in Edinburgh, Scotland. It takes its name from the Franciscan friary that once stood on the site ("Greyfriars" after the grey habits of the friars). Land was granted for burial in 1561–62, after St Giles' churchyard became overcrowded, and the kirkyard has been in use since the 16th century. It contains many notable monuments and the graves of famous Edinburgh figures.

History

The kirk was the first built in Edinburgh after the Reformation; construction began in 1602 and it opened for worship on Christmas Day 1620. In 1638 the National Covenant was signed in or near the kirk, and after the Battle of Bothwell Bridge in 1679 some 1,200 captured Covenanters were imprisoned in a section of the kirkyard known as the Covenanters' Prison. Many died there, and those executed are commemorated by the Martyrs' Monument.

The kirkyard became notorious in the 18th and 19th centuries for body snatching — graves were protected by "mortsafes" (iron cages) and a watch house. The Covenanters' Prison area later acquired a reputation for paranormal activity associated with the "Mackenzie poltergeist", named for Sir George Mackenzie of Rosehaugh, the lord advocate buried in the elaborate Black Mausoleum, whose prosecution of the Covenanters earned him the name "Bluidy Mackenzie".

Greyfriars Bobby

The kirkyard is inseparably linked with Greyfriars Bobby, a Skye Terrier who, according to the famous story, guarded the grave of his master, John Gray (buried here in 1858), for 14 years until his own death in 1872. Bobby is buried in the kirkyard near Gray's grave, and a statue of him, erected in 1873, stands outside the kirkyard gates opposite the pub that bears his name. The story has been retold in books and films, though some historians regard parts of it as embellished.

Literary associations

Names on gravestones in the kirkyard — including William McGonagall, Ebenezer Scroggie, and a Thomas Riddell — are popularly said to have inspired characters in J. K. Rowling's Harry Potter books; the author wrote parts of the series in nearby cafés overlooking the graveyard. Nearby George Heriot's School is also associated in popular lore with Hogwarts. Notable burials include the poet Allan Ramsay, architect William Adam, and physician James Hutton (whose grave marks the founder of modern geology).
