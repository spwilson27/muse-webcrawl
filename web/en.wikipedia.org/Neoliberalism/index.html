Neoliberalism

Neoliberalism is a political and economic philosophy and policy model that emphasizes the value of free market competition, deregulation, privatization, and reductions in government spending. The term originated in the 1930s among European liberal scholars seeking a middle way between classical laissez-faire liberalism and socialist central planning, and was revived in the late twentieth century to describe the market-oriented reforms associated with governments such as those of Augusto Pinochet in Chile, Margaret Thatcher in the United Kingdom, and Ronald Reagan in the United States. Today the term is used predominantly by critics of those policies, and scholars continue to debate its precise meaning and usefulness.

Neoliberal policies promote market mechanisms in areas previously run or regulated by the state.

Origins

The word was coined in 1938 at the Colloque Walter Lippmann in Paris, where Alexander Rüstow and others outlined a liberalism that accepted a role for the state in framing markets. The ideas were developed after the Second World War by the Mont Pelerin Society, founded in 1947 by Friedrich Hayek and bringing together economists including Milton Friedman and Ludwig von Mises. For decades these ideas remained on the margins of policy, which was dominated by Keynesian economics and embedded liberalism.

Policy implementation

Following the economic crises of the 1970s, neoliberal ideas moved into government. In Chile after 1973, the "Chicago Boys" implemented radical privatization and deregulation under Pinochet. Thatcher's government after 1979 privatized state industries, curbed trade unions, and deregulated finance; Reagan's administration cut taxes, reduced regulation, and emphasized monetary restraint. In the 1980s and 1990s, international institutions such as the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank promoted similar reforms — the package John Williamson summarized as the "Washington Consensus" — in Latin America, Africa, Asia, and the post-communist states.

Commonly associated policies include privatization of public assets, fiscal austerity, free trade, floating exchange rates, central bank independence, and labor market flexibility.

Criticism and debate

Critics associate neoliberalism with rising income inequality, financial instability culminating in the 2007–2008 financial crisis, and the erosion of public services and democratic accountability. Supporters credit market reforms with lowering inflation, increasing efficiency, and lifting hundreds of millions out of poverty through trade-led growth, particularly in East Asia. Some scholars caution that the term has become so elastic that it functions more as a pejorative than as a precise analytical category.
