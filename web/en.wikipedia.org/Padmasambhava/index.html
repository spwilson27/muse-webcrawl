Padmasambhava ("the Lotus-Born"), known in Tibet as Guru Rinpoche, was a tantric Buddhist master of the 8th century who is credited, alongside the scholar Shantarakshita and King Trisong Detsen, with establishing Buddhism in Tibet. Venerated as a "second Buddha" in the Himalayan world, he is the central figure of Tibet's oldest school, the Nyingma, and the focus of an immense body of legend, ritual and pilgrimage.

Life and legend

Historical details are scarce and heavily overlaid with hagiography. Tradition holds that Padmasambhava was miraculously born from a lotus in Lake Dhanakosha in the land of Oddiyana (often identified with the Swat Valley), was adopted by King Indrabhuti, renounced a kingdom, and mastered tantric practice in charnel grounds and retreat caves across India and Nepal. Invited to Tibet by Trisong Detsen to overcome the local spirits obstructing the founding of Samye — Tibet's first monastery (c. 779) — he is said to have subdued and bound the indigenous deities as protectors of Buddhism, ordained the first Tibetan monks alongside Shantarakshita, and supervised the translation of scriptures. He is closely associated with consorts including Yeshe Tsogyal, remembered as the mother of Tibetan Buddhism.

Terma and legacy

Padmasambhava is believed to have concealed religious "treasures" (terma) — texts, images and ritual objects — in caves, lakes and even in the minds of disciples, to be discovered by later "treasure revealers" (tertöns) when the times require them. The most famous terma cycle includes the work known in the West as the Tibetan Book of the Dead (Bardo Thodol). His mantra, Om Ah Hung Vajra Guru Padma Siddhi Hung, is recited throughout the Tibetan Buddhist world, and his feast day, the tenth of the lunar month (Guru Rinpoche Day), structures Nyingma practice. Sites associated with him, from Rewalsar (Tso Pema) to the Tiger's Nest (Paro Taktsang) in Bhutan, draw pilgrims across the Himalayas.
