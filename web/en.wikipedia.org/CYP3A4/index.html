Cytochrome P450 3A4
GeneCYP3A4 (chromosome 7q22.1)
FamilyCytochrome P450, family 3, subfamily A
Main sitesLiver, small intestine

Cytochrome P450 3A4 (abbreviated CYP3A4) is one of the most important enzymes involved in the metabolism of xenobiotics in the human body. It is a member of the cytochrome P450 superfamily of monooxygenases and is found predominantly in the liver and in the small intestine, where it forms a first line of defence against orally ingested foreign compounds.

Function

CYP3A4 catalyses the oxidation of a chemically diverse range of substrates, including roughly half of all clinically used medicines. Because the enzyme is concentrated both in hepatocytes and in the enterocytes lining the gut wall, many orally administered drugs are substantially metabolised before they ever reach the systemic circulation, a phenomenon known as first-pass metabolism.

Substrates, inhibitors and inducers

Well-known substrates include many statins, calcium-channel blockers, benzodiazepines, macrolide antibiotics, immunosuppressants such as ciclosporin and tacrolimus, and numerous HIV protease inhibitors. Potent inhibitors of CYP3A4 — including ketoconazole, ritonavir and the furanocoumarins in grapefruit juice — can raise plasma concentrations of substrate drugs to toxic levels. Conversely, inducers such as rifampicin, carbamazepine and St John's wort accelerate metabolism and may cause therapeutic failure.

Variability

Expression of CYP3A4 varies widely between individuals, owing to genetic polymorphisms, age, sex, disease state and environmental exposure. Unlike some other drug-metabolising enzymes, CYP3A4 shows few common loss-of-function alleles, and much of the variability is regulatory rather than structural.
