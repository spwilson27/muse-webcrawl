The G20 or Group of Twenty is an intergovernmental forum comprising 19 sovereign countries, the European Union (EU), and the African Union (AU). It works to address major issues related to the global economy, such as international financial stability, climate change mitigation, and sustainable development. Together the members account for around 85% of global gross domestic product, about 75% of international trade, and roughly two-thirds of the world's population.

History

The G20 was founded in 1999 in the aftermath of the Asian financial crisis, initially as a meeting of finance ministers and central bank governors. In November 2008, amid the global financial crisis, it was elevated to a leaders' summit format, beginning with the Washington, D.C. summit hosted by U.S. President George W. Bush. At the 2009 Pittsburgh summit, leaders designated the G20 the "premier forum for international economic cooperation," partly displacing the narrower G7/G8 in that role.

Members

The 19 country members are Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Canada, China, France, Germany, India, Indonesia, Italy, Japan, Mexico, Russia, Saudi Arabia, South Africa, South Korea, Turkey, the United Kingdom, and the United States. Spain is a permanent guest invitee. The African Union joined as a permanent member at the 2023 New Delhi summit, a change championed by host India.

Structure and summits

The G20 has no permanent secretariat; its presidency rotates annually among members, which set the agenda, host the leaders' summit, and coordinate ministerial "tracks" — the Finance Track and the Sherpa Track — along with engagement groups for business, labor, civil society, and think tanks. Notable summits include London (2009), Toronto and Seoul (2010), Hamburg (2017), Bali (2022), and New Delhi (2023).

Agenda

Early summits focused on financial regulation, stimulus coordination, and IMF reform. The agenda has since widened to climate finance, pandemic preparedness, food and energy security, digital taxation, and debt relief for low-income countries. Consensus communiqués are non-binding, and the forum is often criticized for slow implementation and for the exclusion of smaller states, though defenders note its representativeness relative to the G7.
