Galician language

Galician (galego)
Native to: Galicia and neighbouring areas, Spain
Speakers: ~2.4 million
Family: Indo-European → Romance → Western Romance → Galician-Portuguese
Official status: Co-official in Galicia
Regulated by: Real Academia Galega

Galician (Galician: galego) is a Western Ibero-Romance language spoken by around 2.4 million people, principally in Galicia, an autonomous community in northwestern Spain, where it is co-official with Spanish. It is also spoken in adjacent parts of Asturias and Castile and León, and by the worldwide Galician diaspora.

Galician and Portuguese descend from a common ancestor, medieval Galician-Portuguese, the language of the lyric poetry (cantigas) of the Iberian Peninsula. The two diverged as Portugal became an independent kingdom and Galician came under growing Castilian influence; today they remain partially mutually intelligible, and a "reintegrationist" minority movement advocates spelling Galician according to Portuguese norms.

History

Galician-Portuguese flourished in the 13th and 14th centuries, when even Castilian kings such as Alfonso X composed poetry in it. From the late 15th century Galicia's language entered centuries of decline in writing and public life (the Séculos Escuros, "Dark Centuries"), though it survived as the spoken language of most of the population. A 19th-century literary revival, the Rexurdimento, was led by poets such as Rosalía de Castro. The 1936 Statute of Autonomy recognized Galician, but the Franco dictatorship again repressed it. Since 1981 Galician has been co-official, used in education, the regional parliament, and media such as the broadcaster TVG, though surveys show declining use among the young in cities.

Linguistic features

Galician has seven oral vowels plus nasalization in some dialects, a distinction between open and close mid vowels, and characteristic features such as the gheada (pronunciation of /g/ as [ħ] or [x]) in western varieties. Its vocabulary and phonology stand between Spanish and Portuguese but are historically closer to the latter. The standard, codified by the Real Academia Galega and the Instituto da Lingua Galega, is written with the Latin alphabet; the letters ñ and ll usage follows Spanish-influenced conventions, while reintegrationists write nh and lh as in Portuguese.
