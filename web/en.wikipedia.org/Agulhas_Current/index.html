Agulhas Current

The Agulhas Current is the western boundary current of the southwest Indian Ocean. It flows southward along the east coast of South Africa from roughly 27°S to 40°S, carrying warm tropical water from the Mozambique Channel. It is one of the strongest currents in the world ocean, with transports on the order of 70 sverdrups and surface speeds that can exceed 2 metres per second.

Retroflection and leakage

Near the southern tip of Africa the current turns sharply back on itself — the Agulhas Retroflection — and returns eastward into the Indian Ocean as the Agulhas Return Current. Some water escapes around the Cape of Good Hope into the Atlantic as rings and filaments, a transfer known as Agulhas leakage, which forms part of the global thermohaline circulation and influences Atlantic climate.

Rogue waves

The current's opposition to prevailing swell from Southern Ocean storms produces exceptionally steep seas off the South African coast, historically associated with rogue waves that have damaged or sunk ships along the busy Cape shipping route.

Biological importance

The current shapes the marine ecosystems of the Agulhas region, driving upwelling and the famous sardine run along the east coast, when huge shoals of sardines migrate northward followed by predators.
