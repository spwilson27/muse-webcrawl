The James Caird is a whaler and former lifeboat of the expedition ship Endurance. She is famous for the open-boat voyage of 1916 in which Ernest Shackleton and five companions sailed 800 nautical miles (1,500 km) across the Southern Ocean from Elephant Island to South Georgia to organise the rescue of their stranded expedition — a feat regarded as one of the greatest small-boat journeys ever accomplished.

The James Caird, preserved at Dulwich College, Shackleton's old school

Background

The boat was named after Sir James Key Caird, a Dundee jute manufacturer and philanthropist who helped finance Shackleton's Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition (1914–1917). Built as a double-ended whaler of about 22 feet 6 inches, she was one of Endurance's boats. After Endurance was crushed by ice in the Weddell Sea in 1915, the expedition reached Elephant Island in the ship's boats in April 1916.

The voyage

With no hope of rescue on Elephant Island, Shackleton chose the strongest boat, had ship's carpenter Harry McNish raise her sides and deck her over with canvas and packing-case wood, and set out on 24 April 1916 with Frank Worsley (navigating), Tom Crean, John Vincent, Timothy McCarthy and McNish. Ballasted with boulders and shingle, the boat survived mountainous seas, hurricane-force winds and freezing spray. Guided by Worsley's sextant sights taken in brief breaks in the cloud, they reached the south coast of South Georgia on 10 May 1916 after sixteen days at sea. Shackleton, Crean and Worsley then crossed the island's unmapped mountains on foot to reach the whaling station at Stromness, and all 28 men of the expedition were eventually rescued alive.

Preservation

The James Caird was brought back to Britain in 1919 and presented to Dulwich College, Shackleton's old school, where she is displayed in the James Caird Hall. She is cared for with support from the James Caird Society, founded to preserve the boat and Shackleton's memory.
