Cauchy integral theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Cauchy integral theorem, named after Augustin-Louis Cauchy, is a central statement in complex analysis. It says that the line integral of a holomorphic function around a closed curve vanishes, provided the function is holomorphic on and inside the region bounded by the curve. In symbols, if f is holomorphic on an open set containing the closed disc bounded by the piecewise smooth closed curve γ, then:

∮γ f(z) dz = 0

Statement and proof

Cauchy first presented the theorem in 1814 (published 1825) under additional assumptions, notably continuity of the derivative f′. His original proof used what amounts to Green's theorem. In 1900, Édouard Goursat removed the continuity assumption, producing the result sometimes called the Cauchy–Goursat theorem: holomorphicity alone — mere complex differentiability — suffices. Standard proofs proceed by triangulating the region and showing the integral around each small triangle tends to zero.

Conditions

The theorem requires the domain to be simply connected (or, more precisely, that the contour wind zero times around every point outside the domain). If the function has singularities inside the contour the conclusion fails: for example, ∮ 1/z dz around the unit circle equals 2πi, not zero, because 1/z is not holomorphic at the origin.

Consequences

The theorem is the engine of most of classical complex analysis. From it follow: path independence of integrals of holomorphic functions; the Cauchy integral formula, which recovers a function's values (and all its derivatives) inside a contour from its values on the contour; the power-series (Taylor) expansion of every holomorphic function; the residue theorem used to evaluate difficult real integrals; Morera's theorem (a converse); and Liouville's theorem. Cauchy's formula also shows that holomorphic functions are automatically analytic and infinitely differentiable — a rigidity with no real-variable analogue.
