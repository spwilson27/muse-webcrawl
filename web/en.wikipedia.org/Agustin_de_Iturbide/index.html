Agustin Cosme Damian de Iturbide y Aramburu (27 September 1783 – 19 July 1824), better known as Agustin de Iturbide, was a Mexican army general and statesman. During the Mexican War of Independence, he built a successful political and military coalition that wrested control of Mexico City from the Spanish colonial government, securing Mexican independence in 1821. In 1822 he was proclaimed Emperor of Mexico as Agustin I, reigning briefly until his abdication in March 1823; he was executed the following year after returning from exile.

Career

Born to a wealthy Spanish landowning family in Valladolid (present-day Morelia, Michoacan), Iturbide joined the royalist army as a young man and fought against the insurgents of Miguel Hidalgo and Jose Maria Morelos, earning a reputation as a capable and ruthless commander. In 1820, as revolutionary sentiment shifted in Spain, Iturbide changed course: at Iguala he issued the Plan of Iguala (24 February 1821) with insurgent leader Vicente Guerrero, promising independence, equality of peninsulares and creoles, and protection of the Catholic Church — the "Three Guarantees".

Independence and empire

The Army of the Three Guarantees swept the country, and on 27 September 1821 Iturbide entered Mexico City in triumph; the Treaty of Cordoba formalised independence from Spain. When no European prince accepted the Mexican crown, Iturbide's supporters proclaimed him emperor in May 1822. His reign was marked by conflict with Congress, financial strain and growing opposition; Antonio Lopez de Santa Anna's revolt under the Plan of Casa Mata forced his abdication on 19 March 1823.

Exile, return and death

Iturbide went into exile in Italy and then London, where he wrote his memoirs. Unaware that the Mexican Congress had declared him a traitor and outlaw, he sailed back to Mexico in 1824 hoping to help against a feared Spanish reconquest. He was arrested on landing at Soto la Marina and executed by firing squad at Padilla on 19 July 1824. His remains were later honoured and now rest in Mexico City Cathedral; he remains a contested figure — liberator to some, opportunist usurper to others — and the green-white-red colours of his Army of the Three Guarantees survive in the modern Mexican flag.
