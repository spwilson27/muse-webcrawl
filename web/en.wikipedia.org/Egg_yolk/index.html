Egg yolk

An egg yolk is the nutrient-bearing portion of an egg whose primary function is to supply food for the development of the embryo. In bird eggs it is the familiar yellow, roughly spherical mass suspended in the egg white (albumen) by the chalazae. Yolks are rich in lipids, proteins, fat-soluble vitamins and minerals, and in hens' eggs owe their colour largely to carotenoid pigments such as lutein and zeaxanthin derived from the diet.

Composition

A typical chicken egg yolk weighs about 17 grams and contains roughly 55 kilocalories, about 4.5 g of fat and 2.7 g of protein, together with choline, vitamin A, vitamin D, B vitamins, phosphorus and selenium. Yolk lipids are present largely as lipoproteins; the low-density lipoproteins of yolk give it emulsifying properties exploited in mayonnaise and hollandaise sauce.

Structure and development

The yolk is enclosed by the vitelline membrane. In fertilised eggs the germinal disc on the yolk surface is the site from which the embryo develops, while the yolk itself is progressively consumed as food. Eggs of different species vary greatly in the proportion of yolk, reflecting developmental strategy.

Culinary use

Yolks are used as emulsifiers, thickeners in custards and sauces, enrichments in pasta and pastry, and as a glaze. Separated yolks feature in dishes from zabaglione to lemon curd; concerns about dietary cholesterol historically limited consumption advice, though yolk remains a concentrated source of nutrients such as choline.
