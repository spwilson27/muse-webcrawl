Land compensation

Land compensation is the payment made to owners and occupiers when land is compulsorily acquired by a public authority for infrastructure or development. In most legal systems the power of compulsory purchase (or eminent domain) is conditioned on the payment of fair compensation, typically based on the market value of the land taken, plus allowances for disturbance, severance, and injurious affection to retained land.

Principles

The governing principle in Commonwealth jurisdictions is equivalence: the claimant should be left, so far as money can do it, in the same position as if the land had not been taken. Valuation is usually made under a statutory "no-scheme" rule, disregarding increases or decreases in value caused by the acquiring authority's own project — the Pointe Gourde principle. Additional payments may include home-loss payments for displaced residents, basic and occupier's loss payments, and advance payment of up to 90 percent of the assessed value before entry.

By jurisdiction

In the United States, the Fifth Amendment requires "just compensation," generally interpreted as fair market value. In the United Kingdom, the compensation code derives chiefly from the Land Compensation Acts 1961 and 1973 and the Compulsory Purchase Act 1965. India's Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act 2013 mandates compensation at multiples of market value in rural areas plus structured rehabilitation for displaced families.

Disputes

Disagreements over value are referred in England and Wales to the Upper Tribunal (Lands Chamber). Compensation disputes are common sources of delay and litigation in major infrastructure projects such as railways, airports, and road schemes.
