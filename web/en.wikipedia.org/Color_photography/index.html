Color photography

Color photography is photography that uses media capable of capturing and reproducing colors. By the 1970s color imagery had become dominant in most forms of photography, displacing black-and-white film for snapshots, photojournalism and advertising, although monochrome remained an artistic and specialist medium.

Early experiments

The three-color principle was demonstrated by James Clerk Maxwell in 1861, when a photograph of a tartan ribbon, taken through red, green and blue filters and projected through the same filters, produced a recognisable color image. Early practical processes included the Lumière brothers' Autochrome (1907), which used dyed potato-starch grains as filters on a glass plate and became the first commercially successful color process.

Film era

Modern color film arrived with Kodak's Kodachrome (1935) and Agfa's Agfacolor (1936), using multilayer emulsions with separate layers sensitive to blue, green and red light. Chromogenic films and papers, in which dye couplers form cyan, magenta and yellow dyes during development, made color printing routine after the Second World War. Instant color film followed with Polaroid's Polacolor in 1963.

Digital era

Digital cameras capture color with a single sensor covered by a color filter array (usually the Bayer pattern), interpolating full color at each pixel. Color is managed through color spaces such as sRGB and Adobe RGB, white-balance adjustment and ICC profiles, allowing consistent reproduction across cameras, monitors and printers.
