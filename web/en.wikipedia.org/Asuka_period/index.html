Asuka period

The Asuka period (飛鳥時代, Asuka jidai) in Japanese history spans 538 to 710 CE, named after the Asuka region in the modern Nara Prefecture where the imperial court was frequently located. It was a time of profound transformation: Buddhism was introduced from the Korean kingdom of Baekje, Chinese-style political reforms reshaped the state, and the name of the country changed from Wa to Nihon (Japan).

Introduction of Buddhism

Buddhism was officially presented to the Yamato court in 538 (or 552) by King Seong of Baekje, who sent a gilt-bronze Buddha image and scriptures. The Soga clan championed the new faith against the conservative Mononobe and Nakatomi clans; after the Soga victory of 587, Buddhism was promoted by the regent Prince Shōtoku and Empress Suiko, and great temples such as Asuka-dera and Hōryū-ji were founded.

Political reforms

Prince Shōtoku's Seventeen-article constitution (604) stressed Confucian and Buddhist ideals of harmony and meritocratic government. Embassies to Sui and Tang China (kentōshi) brought back continental law, calendrics, writing and city planning.

The Taika Reforms

After the Isshi Incident of 645 destroyed the Soga, the Taika Reforms proclaimed land nationalisation, a census and tax registers, and a centralised bureaucracy modelled on Tang China — laying foundations for the ritsuryō state completed in the following Nara period. The Jinshin War of 672 settled the imperial succession by force.

Art and architecture

Asuka art shows strong Korean and Chinese influence: the bronze Shaka triad by Tori Busshi at Hōryū-ji, the Kudara Kannon, and the Tamamushi Shrine. The five-storey pagoda of Hōryū-ji is among the oldest surviving wooden structures on Earth.
