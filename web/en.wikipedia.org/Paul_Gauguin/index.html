Eugène Henri Paul Gauguin (7 June 1848 – 8 May 1903) was a French Post-Impressionist artist. Unappreciated until after his death, Gauguin is now recognised for his experimental use of colour and Synthetist style that were distinct from Impressionism. Toward the end of his life, he spent ten years in French Polynesia; his paintings from this period depict its people and landscapes.

Life

Gauguin spent part of his childhood in Peru, his mother's native country, later worked as a merchant seaman and then as a successful Paris stockbroker, painting only in his spare time. The stock-market crash of 1882 pushed him to pursue art full-time, a decision that led to poverty and the breakdown of his marriage to the Danish Mette-Sophie Gad. He painted in Brittany, notably at Pont-Aven, where he developed Synthetism with Émile Bernard, and in 1888 spent a turbulent nine weeks with Vincent van Gogh in Arles, ending with van Gogh's breakdown.

Seeking a more "primitive" life, Gauguin sailed for Tahiti in 1891. There he painted works such as Ia Orana Maria and the monumental Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We Going? (1897), painted after the death of his daughter Aline and his own failed suicide attempt. After a brief return to France he settled permanently in Polynesia, moving to the Marquesas Islands in 1901, where he died in 1903.

Legacy

Gauguin's bold colour, simplified forms and primitivism influenced the Nabis, Fauvism and modern art generally; a 1906 retrospective in Paris helped secure his posthumous fame. His life and conduct in Polynesia — particularly his relationships with young Tahitian girls — have been the subject of sustained modern re-evaluation and controversy.
