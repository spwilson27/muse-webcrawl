The Tokyo subway sarin attack was an act of domestic terrorism perpetrated on 20 March 1995 by members of the doomsday cult Aum Shinrikyo. In a coordinated attack during the morning rush hour, five cult members released sarin, a highly toxic nerve agent, on three lines of the Tokyo subway system. Thirteen people died (a fourteenth victim died in 2020 after decades in a coma-like state) and thousands were injured, many suffering lasting vision damage. It remains the deadliest terrorist attack in modern Japanese history.

The attack

The attackers boarded trains on the Chiyoda, Marunouchi and Hibiya lines, all converging on Kasumigaseki, the district housing Japan's government ministries and police headquarters. Each carried plastic bags of liquid sarin wrapped in newspaper; at pre-arranged stations they dropped the bags and punctured them with sharpened umbrella tips before escaping to waiting getaway cars. As the impure sarin evaporated, passengers began choking, vomiting and losing vision; trains continued running for some time as casualties mounted, and two station staff who handled the bags at Kasumigaseki died. Hospitals received more than 5,500 people, although most had mild exposure or anxiety symptoms; about 1,000 suffered significant poisoning.

Background

Aum Shinrikyo, led by Shoko Asahara, had built a chemical weapons programme directed by Seiichi Endo and others, and had already carried out the Matsumoto sarin attack of June 1994, which killed eight people. Police were closing in on the cult over the kidnapping of a Tokyo notary official, and Asahara ordered the subway attack in part to divert and disrupt the impending police raids, timing it to maximise casualties among officials in Kasumigaseki.

Aftermath

Police raided Aum facilities across Japan within days, seizing large stocks of chemical precursors. Asahara was arrested in May 1995, hiding at the cult's Kamikuishiki complex. Trials stretched over two decades; Asahara and six followers were executed by hanging on 6 July 2018, and six more were executed later that month, after the final appeals concluded. The cult was declared bankrupt; a successor group, Aleph, remains under official surveillance. The attack profoundly changed Japanese public life, prompting counter-terrorism reforms, new laws restricting the successor organisations, and lasting scrutiny of religious corporations; it is commemorated annually, and victims' groups have long campaigned for compensation and recognition.
