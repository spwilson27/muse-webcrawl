A generic single-component phase diagram.

A phase diagram in physical chemistry, engineering, mineralogy, and materials science is a type of chart used to show the conditions (pressure, temperature, volume, composition) at which thermodynamically distinct phases (such as solid, liquid, and gaseous states) occur and coexist at equilibrium.

Single-component diagrams

The most familiar phase diagrams plot pressure against temperature for a pure substance. Lines on the diagram mark phase boundaries where two phases coexist. The triple point is where solid, liquid, and gas coexist; for water it lies at 0.01 °C and 611.7 Pa. The liquid–gas boundary ends at the critical point, beyond which the substance is a supercritical fluid. Water's diagram is unusual in that its solid–liquid line slopes backwards (negative slope), because ice is less dense than liquid water.

Binary and ternary diagrams

For mixtures, composition becomes an additional axis. Binary diagrams show features such as eutectic points, peritectics, and solid-solution ranges, and are indispensable in metallurgy for understanding alloy solidification. The Gibbs phase rule, F = C − P + 2, relates the degrees of freedom to the numbers of components and phases.
