The radian, denoted by the symbol rad, is the SI unit for measuring angles. One radian is the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc whose length equals the radius of the circle. There are 2π radians in a full circle, so one radian equals 180/π degrees, approximately 57.2958°. The radian is the standard unit of angular measure used in most branches of mathematics and physics.

Definition

An angle θ in radians is defined as θ = s/r, where s is the arc length and r is the radius. Because this is a ratio of two lengths, the radian is dimensionless. A quarter circle subtends π/2 radians (90°), a half circle π radians (180°), and a full circle 2π radians (360°).

Advantages

Radians simplify many formulas. In calculus, the derivative of sin(x) is cos(x) only when x is measured in radians. The arc length formula s = rθ, the sector area formula A = ½r²θ, and the Taylor series of the trigonometric functions all take their simplest forms in radians. For small angles, sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ θ when θ is in radians.

History

The concept of the radian emerged in the work of Roger Cotes in the early 18th century and was named in the 1870s by James Thomson, brother of Lord Kelvin. It was adopted as an SI supplementary unit in 1960 and reclassified as a derived unit in 1995.

Related units

Related angular units include the degree, the gradian (gon), the turn, and the milliradian, used in artillery and optics. In physics, the steradian is the corresponding two-dimensional (solid) angle unit.
