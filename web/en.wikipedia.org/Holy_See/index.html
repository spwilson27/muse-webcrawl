The Holy See (Latin: Sancta Sedes, "holy seat") is the jurisdiction of the Pope in his role as head of the worldwide Catholic Church. It is the central government of the Church, operating from Vatican City, and is recognised under international law as a sovereign entity in its own right — distinct, both legally and conceptually, from the Vatican City state that provides its territorial base.

Distinction from Vatican City

Vatican City was created by the Lateran Treaty of 1929 to guarantee the Holy See's independence; but the Holy See itself is far older, and its diplomatic relations and international personality continued through the period (1870–1929) when it held no territory at all. Ambassadors are accredited to the Holy See, not to Vatican City, and it is the Holy See that concludes concordats and participates in international organisations.

Government

The Pope exercises supreme legislative, executive and judicial authority. He is assisted by the Roman Curia, principally the Secretariat of State, and by dicasteries (congregations and councils) administering the Church's affairs. Papal representatives abroad — nuncios and apostolic delegates — form one of the world's oldest diplomatic services.

International role

The Holy See maintains diplomatic relations with the great majority of states, holds permanent observer status at the United Nations, and is a member or observer of numerous international bodies. Its diplomacy traditionally emphasises mediation, humanitarian relief and freedom of religion.
