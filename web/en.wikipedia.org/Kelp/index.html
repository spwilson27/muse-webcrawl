Kelp

Kelps are large brown algae (seaweeds) belonging to the order Laminariales. There are about 30 genera. Despite their plant-like appearance, kelps are heterokonts, not true plants. They grow in shallow, nutrient-rich coastal waters in temperate regions, forming dense underwater "forests" that are among the most productive and dynamic ecosystems on Earth.

Structure and growth

A kelp body (thallus) consists of a holdfast anchoring it to the seabed, a stem-like stipe, and leaf-like blades. In giant kelp (Macrocystis pyrifera), gas-filled bladders (pneumatocysts) buoy the fronds toward the light. Giant kelp can grow up to 30–50 cm per day and reach lengths over 45 metres, making it one of the fastest-growing organisms known.

Ecology

Kelp forests shelter fish, invertebrates, sea otters and marine mammals, and protect coastlines from wave energy. Sea urchins graze on kelp, and where their predators (such as sea otters) are removed, urchin barrens can replace the forest. Kelp forests also absorb significant amounts of carbon dioxide and are studied as a tool for climate mitigation.

Uses

Kelp has long been harvested as food (for example kombu in Japan), as a source of alginates used as thickeners in food and industry, and historically for potash and iodine production. Modern interest includes kelp aquaculture for food, biofuel and carbon sequestration.
