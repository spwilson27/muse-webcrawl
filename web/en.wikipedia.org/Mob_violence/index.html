Mob violence

Mob violence is violent action carried out by a large, disorderly crowd against people or property, outside the control of lawful authority. It includes lynching, pogroms, communal riots, and vigilante attacks, and is studied in sociology, criminology, and political science as a form of collective behavior.

Characteristics

Mob violence typically involves a perceived grievance or triggering incident, rapid spread of rumor, and a sense of anonymity and shared identity within the crowd that lowers individual inhibitions — a process psychologists call deindividuation. Targets are often members of minority or stigmatized groups, alleged criminals, or symbols of authority. Unlike organized armed conflict, mob violence is usually spontaneous or loosely organized, though it can be incited or tolerated by political actors for strategic ends.

Examples

  • Lynching in the United States — thousands of extrajudicial killings, principally of African Americans, from the late 19th to mid-20th century.
  • Pogroms — organized or tolerated massacres of Jewish communities, notably in the Russian Empire and Kristallnacht in Nazi Germany (1938).
  • Communal riots in South Asia — recurrent Hindu–Muslim and other inter-group violence, including the violence of Partition in 1947.
  • Witch-hunts and vigilante killings — including modern incidents fueled by rumors spread through social media and messaging apps.

Prevention and response

Responses range from policing and riot control to legal measures against incitement and lynching, rapid rumor rebuttal, and prosecution of participants. Long-term prevention focuses on impartial law enforcement, intergroup trust, and reducing the impunity that historically allowed mobs to act.
