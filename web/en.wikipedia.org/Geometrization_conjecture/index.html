The geometrization conjecture, proposed by the American mathematician William Thurston in 1982, states that every closed three-dimensional manifold can be decomposed into pieces, each of which carries one of eight types of geometric structure. It is a far-reaching generalization of the uniformization theorem for surfaces, and its most famous consequence is the Poincaré conjecture.

The eight geometries

Thurston identified eight model geometries that can occur in three dimensions: Euclidean geometry, spherical geometry, hyperbolic geometry, the geometry of S² × R, the geometry of H² × R, the geometry of the universal cover of SL(2,R), Nil geometry, and Sol geometry. Each corresponds to a maximal simply connected Riemannian manifold with a transitive group of isometries. A closed 3-manifold satisfying the conjecture is cut along tori into pieces each admitting a complete metric modelled on one of these geometries.

Background

For surfaces, every closed surface admits a metric of constant curvature: positive for the sphere, zero for the torus, and negative for higher-genus surfaces. Thurston's programme asked whether an analogous classification could govern three-manifolds, which are far more varied. The conjecture unified many disparate results and suggested that hyperbolic geometry, Thurston's own speciality, plays the dominant role.

Proof

In 2002–2003 the Russian mathematician Grigori Perelman posted three preprints using Richard Hamilton's Ricci flow with surgery to prove the geometrization conjecture. Because the Poincaré conjecture follows as a special case — a simply connected closed 3-manifold must be the 3-sphere — Perelman's work resolved a problem that had stood since 1904. He was awarded the Fields Medal in 2006 and the Millennium Prize in 2010, declining both.
