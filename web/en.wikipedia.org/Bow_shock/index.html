In astrophysics, a bow shock is the region between a magnetosphere and an ambient flowing plasma, analogous to the bow wave in front of a moving ship. For Earth, it is the boundary at which the solar wind abruptly slows as it approaches the magnetopause: supersonic solar wind plasma is heated, compressed, and deflected around the magnetosphere, flowing through the turbulent region downstream called the magnetosheath.

Earth's bow shock

Because the solar wind typically flows at 400 km/s or more — faster than the speed at which magnetohydrodynamic waves can carry information in the plasma — a standing shock must form where the flow meets the obstacle of Earth's magnetic field. The shock stands roughly 13–14 Earth radii from Earth on the sunward side (about 90,000 km), moving inward when the solar wind pressure is high. Spacecraft crossings show the shock to be a thin, collisionless transition: energy is dissipated not by particle collisions but by electric and magnetic fields, making it a natural laboratory for plasma physics. Crossings by missions such as ISEE, Cluster, and THEMIS have mapped its shape and dynamics.

Other bow shocks

Bow shocks occur wherever a plasma flow meets an obstacle: at Venus and Mars (which lack global magnetic fields, so the shock forms near the ionosphere), at comets, and around the moons of the outer planets. In stellar astronomy, runaway massive stars produce glowing bow shocks in the interstellar medium, and the Sun itself creates a bow wave — long inferred from the heliosphere's motion through the local interstellar cloud, though IBEX data suggest the Sun's bow structure may be a slower "bow wave" rather than a true shock. The term is also used in aerodynamics for the detached shock wave in front of a blunt body in supersonic flow.
