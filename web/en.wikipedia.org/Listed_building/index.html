Listed building

In the United Kingdom, a listed building is a structure of particular architectural or historic interest that has been placed on one of the four statutory lists maintained by the national heritage bodies of England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. Listing gives a building legal protection: it may not be demolished, extended or altered in a way that affects its character without listed building consent from the local planning authority.

Grades

In England and Wales, listed buildings are graded to indicate their relative importance:

  • Grade I — buildings of exceptional interest; only about 2.5% of listings.
  • Grade II* — particularly important buildings of more than special interest; about 5.5% of listings.
  • Grade II — buildings of special interest, warranting every effort to preserve them; the great majority of listings.

Scotland uses Categories A, B and C, and Northern Ireland uses Grades A, B+ and B. There are around 400,000 listed building entries in England alone, some covering groups of structures.

Criteria

Buildings are assessed on architectural interest, historic interest, close historical association with significant people or events, and group value. In general, all buildings built before 1700 that survive in anything like their original condition are listed, as are most built between 1700 and 1840; post-1945 buildings must be exceptionally important to qualify.

History

The system originated in the aftermath of the Second World War, when the Town and Country Planning Acts of 1944 and 1947 required lists of buildings of architectural or historic interest to guide reconstruction. The first surveys were compiled hurriedly and are sometimes known as the "Salvage Lists".
