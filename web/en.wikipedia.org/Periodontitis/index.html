Periodontitis, also called gum disease, is a serious gum infection that damages the soft tissue and, without treatment, destroys the bone that supports the teeth. It is a set of inflammatory conditions affecting the periodontium — the tissues surrounding and supporting the teeth — and can cause teeth to loosen or lead to tooth loss. Periodontitis is common but largely preventable, and it is a major cause of tooth loss in adults worldwide.

Causes and progression

The disease usually develops from gingivitis, a milder and reversible gum inflammation caused by plaque accumulation. In periodontitis, the inner layer of the gum pulls away from the teeth and forms pockets that become infected; the immune response and bacterial toxins break down the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone. Bacteria such as Porphyromonas gingivalis are implicated, but tissue destruction is driven largely by the host's own inflammatory response.

Risk factors and links

Smoking is the most significant environmental risk factor, along with diabetes, poor oral hygiene, genetic susceptibility, and hormonal changes. Epidemiological studies associate periodontitis with cardiovascular disease, adverse pregnancy outcomes, and worse glycemic control in diabetes, though causal pathways are still debated.

Treatment

Treatment aims to control infection: thorough scaling and root planing, improved oral hygiene, smoking cessation, and in advanced cases surgical reduction of pockets or regenerative procedures. Maintenance therapy is essential because the condition is chronic.
