László Lovász (born March 9, 1948) is a Hungarian mathematician and professor emeritus at Eötvös Loránd University, best known for his work in combinatorics, graph theory, and theoretical computer science. He received the Abel Prize in 2021, jointly with Avi Wigderson, "for their foundational contributions to theoretical computer science and discrete mathematics, and their leading role in shaping them into central fields of modern mathematics". He served as president of the International Mathematical Union (2007–2010) and as president of the Hungarian Academy of Sciences (2014–2020).

Early life and career

Lovász was a prodigy: he won gold medals at the International Mathematical Olympiads of 1964, 1965, and 1966, and published significant results while still a teenager. He studied at Eötvös Loránd University, earning his doctorate in 1971 under Tibor Gallai. He taught in Hungary and held positions at Yale University and at Microsoft Research, where he was a senior researcher for several years, before returning to Budapest, where he has led research groups at the Alfréd Rényi Institute of Mathematics and Eötvös Loránd University.

Mathematical work

Lovász's contributions include the proof of Kneser's conjecture in 1978, which launched the use of topological methods in combinatorics; the Lovász local lemma (with Paul Erdős), a fundamental tool of the probabilistic method; the Lenstra–Lenstra–Lovász (LLL) lattice basis reduction algorithm, with applications from integer programming to cryptanalysis; the Lovász number (theta function), which determined the Shannon capacity of the pentagon; the ellipsoid method's application to combinatorial optimization (with Grötschel and Schrijver); and, later, the theory of graph limits ("graphons"), which studies very large networks through continuous limits. His textbooks, including Combinatorial Problems and Exercises and Large Networks and Graph Limits, are standard references.

Awards

In addition to the Abel Prize, Lovász received the Wolf Prize in Mathematics (1999), the Knuth Prize (1999), the Kyoto Prize (2010), the Fulkerson Prize, the Gödel Prize (2001, shared), and Hungary's Széchenyi Prize. He is a member of numerous national academies.
