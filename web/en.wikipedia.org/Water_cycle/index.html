Water cycle

The water cycle, also known as the hydrologic cycle, is the continuous movement of water on, above and below the surface of the Earth. Water changes state between liquid, vapour and ice at various points in the cycle, which is driven by solar energy and gravity. The total mass of water on Earth remains fairly constant over time, but its partitioning into reservoirs — oceans, ice, groundwater, lakes, rivers, soil moisture and atmospheric vapour — varies.

Processes

Evaporation transforms liquid water from oceans, lakes and land surfaces into water vapour; plants contribute through transpiration, the two often combined as evapotranspiration. Rising air cools the vapour, which condenses into cloud droplets and falls as precipitation — rain, snow, sleet or hail. On land, precipitation may flow over the surface as runoff into streams and rivers, infiltrate into the soil and percolate down to recharge groundwater aquifers, or be stored in glaciers and ice caps. Groundwater can return to the surface through springs or be discharged into streams and the sea, completing the cycle.

Reservoirs and residence times

About 96.5 per cent of Earth's water is in the oceans. Of the roughly 2.5 per cent that is fresh water, most is locked in ice sheets and glaciers or held deep underground; readily accessible surface fresh water in lakes and rivers is a tiny fraction. Residence times vary enormously: a water molecule spends on average about nine days in the atmosphere, thousands of years in deep groundwater, and potentially hundreds of thousands of years in the Antarctic ice sheet.

Human influence

People alter the cycle through damming and diverting rivers, extracting groundwater, irrigation, deforestation and urbanisation, which increases runoff and reduces infiltration. Climate change intensifies the cycle: a warmer atmosphere holds more moisture, tending to make wet regions wetter, dry regions drier, and extreme precipitation events heavier — trends already observed in many regions.
