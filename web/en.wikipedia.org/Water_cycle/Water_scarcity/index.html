Water scarcity

Water scarcity (closely related to water stress or water crisis) is the lack of fresh water resources to meet the standard water demand. There are two types of water scarcity: physical water scarcity, where there is not enough water to meet all demands, including environmental flows; and economic water scarcity, where water exists but the infrastructure or institutions to deliver it are lacking.

Scale of the problem

Billions of people experience severe water scarcity during at least part of the year. The Falkenmark indicator classifies a country as water-stressed below 1,700 cubic metres of renewable water per person per year, and in absolute scarcity below 500. Agriculture accounts for roughly 70 percent of global freshwater withdrawals, so scarcity is tightly linked to irrigation demand and food production.

Causes

Causes include population growth, expanding consumption, groundwater over-extraction, pollution that renders water unusable, and climate change altering precipitation and glacier melt. Famous examples include the shrinking of the Aral Sea through irrigation diversion and the depletion of the Ogallala Aquifer in North America.

Responses

Responses range from supply measures — dams, desalination, wastewater reuse, and rainwater harvesting — to demand management such as efficient irrigation, leakage reduction, pricing, and virtual-water trade. The United Nations recognizes access to safe water as a human right and tracks it under Sustainable Development Goal 6.
