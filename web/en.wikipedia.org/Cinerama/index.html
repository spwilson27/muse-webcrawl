Cinerama

Cinerama is a widescreen process that originally projected images simultaneously from three synchronized 35 mm projectors onto a huge, deeply curved screen, creating an aspect ratio of about 2.59:1 and an immersive field of view approaching 146 degrees. Invented by Fred Waller, it was unveiled to the public on 30 September 1952 in New York City with the travelogue This Is Cinerama.

History

Waller had earlier developed the eleven-projector Vitarama system for gunnery training and the 1939 World's Fair. Wartime flight-simulator work convinced him that peripheral vision was the key to realism. The commercial Cinerama process used a three-lensed camera to record three panels at once, accompanied by seven-track magnetic stereophonic sound developed with engineer Hazard E. Reeves.

Films

The first Cinerama productions were travelogues showcasing roller coasters, Niagara Falls, and aerial tours. Narrative features shot in the original three-strip process were few, most famously How the West Was Won (1962) and The Wonderful World of the Brothers Grimm (1962). Because of its cost and complexity, the brand later shifted to single-strip 70 mm presentations marketed as Cinerama, including 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968).

Legacy

Only a handful of venues worldwide, including the Cinerama Dome in Los Angeles and the National Science and Media Museum in Bradford, England, retain three-projector capability. Cinerama pioneered the widescreen revolution and the idea of cinema as an immersive event.
