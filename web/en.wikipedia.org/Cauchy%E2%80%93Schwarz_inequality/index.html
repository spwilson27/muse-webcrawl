Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (also called the Cauchy–Bunyakovsky–Schwarz inequality) is a fundamental inequality in mathematics, used in linear algebra, analysis, probability theory, and other fields. It states that for all vectors u and v of an inner product space, the absolute value of their inner product is at most the product of their norms:

|⟨u, v⟩| ≤ ||u|| · ||v||

Equality holds if and only if u and v are linearly dependent.

Special cases

For vectors in Euclidean space Rn, the inequality takes the form (Σ aibi)² ≤ (Σ ai²)(Σ bi²). For square-integrable functions it becomes an inequality about integrals, and in probability it implies that the absolute value of the correlation coefficient between two random variables is at most 1, and that covariance is bounded by the product of standard deviations.

History

The inequality was published by Augustin-Louis Cauchy in 1821 for finite sums. Viktor Bunyakovsky extended it to integrals in 1859, and Hermann Schwarz gave the modern general proof in 1888.

Proof idea

A standard proof considers the non-negative quadratic function f(t) = ||u − tv||² ≥ 0 and observes that its discriminant must be non-positive, which yields the inequality directly.
