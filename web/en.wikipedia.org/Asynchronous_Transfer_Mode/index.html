Asynchronous Transfer Mode

Asynchronous Transfer Mode (ATM) is a telecommunications standard defined by the International Telecommunication Union for digital transmission of multiple types of traffic — voice, video and data — over fixed-size 53-byte cells. Standardised in the late 1980s as the networking technology for Broadband ISDN, ATM was widely deployed in carrier backbones and DSL access networks during the 1990s and 2000s.

Cell structure

An ATM cell consists of a 5-byte header and a 48-byte payload. The header carries the virtual path identifier (VPI) and virtual channel identifier (VCI) used for switching, a payload-type field, cell-loss priority bit and header error control. Small, fixed-size cells allow fast hardware switching and predictable latency, suiting real-time voice.

Virtual circuits and QoS

ATM is connection-oriented: before data flows, a virtual circuit is established through signalling or provisioning. Service categories — constant bit rate (CBR), variable bit rate (VBR), available bit rate (ABR) and unspecified bit rate (UBR) — let carriers guarantee quality of service with traffic contracts enforced by policing.

History and deployment

The ATM Forum, founded in 1991, accelerated standardisation. Carriers built ATM cores for frame relay, voice and early broadband aggregation; DSL networks commonly carried customer traffic over ATM between the DSLAM and the ISP. Adaptation layers (AAL1–AAL5) mapped voice, video and IP packets onto cells.

Decline

Ethernet and IP/MPLS ultimately displaced ATM: Ethernet offered simpler, cheaper scaling to gigabit speeds without ATM's roughly 10–20 percent "cell tax" overhead. ATM equipment has largely been retired, though concepts such as label switching influenced MPLS.
