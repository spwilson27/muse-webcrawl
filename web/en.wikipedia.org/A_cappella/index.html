A cappella

A cappella (Italian for "in the style of the chapel") music is a performance by a singer or a singing group without instrumental accompaniment, or a piece intended to be performed in this fashion. The term originally distinguished Renaissance polyphony written for unaccompanied choir from concertato music with instruments, and in the 19th century it acquired its modern meaning of vocal music without instruments.

History

Unaccompanied singing is the oldest form of music-making: Gregorian chant, Byzantine and other liturgical chant traditions, and polyphonic motets and madrigals of composers such as Palestrina were composed for voices alone. In the 19th and 20th centuries, new idioms flourished, including barbershop quartets, gospel and doo-wop groups, and collegiate a cappella ensembles originating at Yale and other American universities.

Modern practice

Contemporary a cappella groups use voices to imitate instruments, employing vocal percussion ("beatboxing") and bass lines sung by low voices. Notable acts include the Swingle Singers, Bobby McFerrin, Take 6, Naturally 7, the King's Singers, and Pentatonix, whose commercial success in the 2010s helped popularize the style, aided by competitions such as NBC's The Sing-Off, the ICCA (the real-life competition behind the film Pitch Perfect), and the Contemporary A Cappella Society's recording awards. Technology — close miking, loop pedals, and pitch correction — has expanded what live groups can do.

Religious traditions

Several traditions require or favor a cappella worship music, among them Eastern Orthodox chant, traditional Jewish Shabbat singing, Islamic anasheed, and the Churches of Christ, which historically do not use instruments in worship. Historically the Sistine Chapel choir exemplified the Catholic a cappella ideal that gave the term its name.
