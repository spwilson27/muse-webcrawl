Air superiority

Air superiority is the degree of dominance of one side's air forces over another's in a conflict, permitting operations without prohibitive interference from the opposing air force. The highest degree, air supremacy, denotes total control of the skies. Air superiority is usually established through fighter aircraft, suppression of enemy air defences, and attacks on airfields and command systems.

Degrees of control

Doctrine distinguishes air supremacy (opposing air force incapable of effective interference), air superiority (operations possible without prohibitive interference), a favourable air situation, air parity, and air denial. Since the Second World War, no army has conducted large-scale ground operations successfully while under sustained enemy air attack.

History

The concept emerged in the First World War, when fighters fought for control over the trenches. The Battle of Britain in 1940 showed an air defence system preventing an enemy from gaining superiority. The Allies achieved air supremacy over Normandy in 1944, and coalition forces did so rapidly in the 1991 Gulf War. Dedicated air superiority fighters such as the F-15 Eagle and F-22 Raptor were designed specifically to win and hold control of the air.

Modern challenges

Modern integrated air defence systems, long-range surface-to-air missiles, stealth aircraft, and drones complicate the contest for superiority, and some conflicts have settled into mutual air denial rather than control by either side.
