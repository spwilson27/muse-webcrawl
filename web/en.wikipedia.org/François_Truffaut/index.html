François Truffaut

François Truffaut (1932–1984), a founder of the French New Wave.

François Roland Truffaut (6 February 1932 – 21 October 1984) was a French filmmaker, actor and critic who was one of the founders of the French New Wave. In a career spanning over a quarter of a century he directed more than twenty feature films and left a lasting mark on cinema worldwide.

Life and career

Truffaut had a lonely Paris childhood and found refuge in cinema; as a teenager he was taken under the wing of critic André Bazin. Writing for Cahiers du cinéma, his 1954 essay "Une certaine tendance du cinéma français" attacked the literary "Tradition of Quality" and helped lay the groundwork of auteur theory. After the short Les Mistons (1957), his feature debut The 400 Blows (1959) — drawn from his own childhood and introducing Jean-Pierre Léaud as his alter ego Antoine Doinel — won Best Director at Cannes and launched the New Wave.

He followed the Doinel character through four more films ending with Love on the Run (1979). Other major works include Shoot the Piano Player (1960), Jules and Jim (1962), Fahrenheit 451 (1966), The Wild Child (1970), Day for Night (1973), which won the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film, The Last Metro (1980) and Confidentially Yours (1983). He also acted, playing a scientist in Steven Spielberg's Close Encounters of the Third Kind (1977), and wrote a celebrated book-length interview with Alfred Hitchcock. Truffaut died of a brain tumour in 1984, aged 52.
