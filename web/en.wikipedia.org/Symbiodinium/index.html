Symbiodinium

DomainEukaryota
PhylumMyzozoa
ClassDinophyceae
OrderSuessiales
FamilySymbiodiniaceae
GenusSymbiodinium

Symbiodinium is a genus of dinoflagellates that encompasses the largest and most prevalent group of endosymbiotic dinoflagellates known. These unicellular microalgae are commonly known as zooxanthellae, and they live in symbiosis with a wide range of marine invertebrates, including reef-building corals, sea anemones, jellyfish, nudibranchs, and giant clams, as well as various species of sponges and foraminiferans.

The relationship is generally mutualistic: the dinoflagellate photosynthesizes within the host's tissues, supplying the host with sugars, amino acids, and oxygen, while the host provides shelter and inorganic nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and carbon dioxide. In corals, the symbionts supply the majority of the energy needed for calcification and reef growth.

Taxonomy

Symbiodinium belongs to the family Symbiodiniaceae. Historically, members of the group were treated as a single pandemic species, Symbiodinium microadriaticum, but molecular work revealed enormous genetic diversity, with lineages historically designated by clade letters (A–I). A major taxonomic revision in 2018 elevated several clades to separate genera, including Breviolum, Cladocopium, Durusdinium, and Effrenium, restricting Symbiodinium proper to the former clade A.

Life cycle and morphology

Symbiodinium cells are typically spherical, roughly 6–13 micrometres in diameter, with a large, condensed, U- or V-shaped chromosome structure (the dinokaryon). In host tissues they exist mainly as non-motile coccoid cells; in culture and in the free-living state they can produce motile zoospores with two flagella characteristic of dinoflagellates. Reproduction in hospite is mainly asexual, by binary fission.

Coral bleaching

When marine heatwaves raise water temperatures, corals may expel their symbionts in a stress response known as coral bleaching. Because the pigmented algae give corals much of their colour, loss of Symbiodinium leaves the white skeleton visible. Prolonged bleaching starves the coral and can lead to death; the type of symbiont a coral hosts—such as the heat-tolerant Durusdinium—strongly influences its susceptibility.
