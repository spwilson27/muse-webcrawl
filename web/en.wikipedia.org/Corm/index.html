Corm

A corm is a short, vertical, swollen underground plant stem that serves as a storage organ used by some plants to survive winter dormancy, drought or other unfavourable conditions, and as a structure for vegetative reproduction. Corms consist of solid stem tissue, distinguishing them from true bulbs, which are composed mainly of layered modified leaves (scales).

Structure

A corm is covered by a dry, papery tunic of dead leaf bases. At its base is the basal plate, from which roots grow, and at the top are one or more growing points protected by the tunic. Internally the corm is made of starch-rich parenchyma tissue. Many corms produce small offset corms, called cormels, around the basal plate, which can be separated to propagate the plant.

Growth cycle

In a typical season, the corm's stored reserves fuel the growth of leaves and flowers in spring. During the growing season a new corm forms on top of the old one, which gradually shrivels as its reserves are transferred. Plants bearing corms include crocus, gladiolus, freesia, crocosmia, and the aroid known as taro (Colocasia esculenta), whose corms are an important starchy food in tropical regions.

Corms versus bulbs, tubers and rhizomes

Unlike a bulb, a corm is solid rather than layered. Unlike a tuber, which is a swollen stem or root without a basal plate or tunic, a corm has both. Rhizomes differ in growing horizontally rather than vertically.
