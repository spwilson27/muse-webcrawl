The Dashavatara (Sanskrit: दशावतार, daśāvatāra) are the ten primary avatars of Vishnu, the Hindu god of preservation. Vishnu is said to descend to earth in these forms to restore cosmic order (dharma) when it is threatened by chaos. The word Dashavatara derives from daśa, meaning "ten", and avatāra, meaning "descent". Lists of the ten vary by source and region, but the most widely accepted sequence — celebrated in Jayadeva's twelfth-century Gita Govinda — is:

  • Matsya, the fish, who saved the sage Manu and the Vedas from a great flood.
  • Kurma, the tortoise, who supported Mount Mandara during the churning of the ocean of milk.
  • Varaha, the boar, who rescued the earth goddess Bhudevi from the demon Hiranyaksha.
  • Narasimha, the man-lion, who slew the demon king Hiranyakashipu to protect his devotee Prahlada.
  • Vamana, the dwarf, who humbled the king Bali and regained the three worlds in three strides.
  • Parashurama, the warrior Brahmin with an axe, who repeatedly destroyed corrupt Kshatriya rulers.
  • Rama, the prince of Ayodhya, hero of the Ramayana, who defeated the demon king Ravana.
  • Krishna, the cowherd-god, central to the Mahabharata and speaker of the Bhagavad Gita.
  • Buddha, the enlightened teacher, included in many lists as an avatar who deluded demons or preached compassion.
  • Kalki, the prophesied future avatar who will appear at the end of the Kali Yuga riding a white horse.

Variations

Some regional traditions substitute Balarama, Krishna's brother, for Buddha, or list both Balarama and Krishna with Buddha omitted; in some lists Krishna replaces Buddha and Balarama is counted as the eighth. The Bhagavata Purana gives longer enumerations — twenty-two or more descents — while noting that the avatars are in fact innumerable, of which the ten are the most celebrated. The sequence of animal and human forms has sometimes been compared, anachronistically, to a progression from aquatic to human life.

Worship and culture

Dashavatara imagery adorns countless temples, where the ten figures appear on doorways and panels, and the set is a staple of Indian miniature painting, dance-drama such as Kathakali and Odissi, and the card game ganjifa, whose Mysore and Mughal suits depict the avatars. Each avatar has dedicated festivals — Rama Navami, Krishna Janmashtami, and Narasimha Jayanti among them.
