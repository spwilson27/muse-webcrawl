Homonymy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Homonymy is the relation between words (lexemes) that share the same spoken or written form but have unrelated meanings — that is, they are homonyms. In the strict sense, a homonym is simultaneously a homograph (spelled the same) and a homophone (pronounced the same), as with bank (a financial institution) and bank (the edge of a river), or bat (the animal) and bat (sports equipment). Homonymy is distinguished from polysemy, where a single word has multiple related senses, as with mouth of a person and mouth of a river.

Origin and analysis

Homonyms arise through sound change collapsing distinct words (Old English brid "young bird" and unrelated forms converging as bird is not homonymy, but fair "just" and fair "festival" converged from distinct Old English and Old French sources), through borrowing into an occupied form, or through the divergence of senses so complete that speakers no longer perceive a connection — the boundary between homonymy and polysemy is a classic problem in lexicography, often decided by etymology or by speaker intuition, which do not always agree. Dictionaries conventionally give homonyms separate headwords (bank¹, bank²) and polysemous senses within one entry.

Homonymy in other domains

The concept extends beyond linguistics. In biology, a homonym is a name for a taxon that is spelled identically to another, which the codes of nomenclature generally forbid; the junior homonym must be replaced. In philosophy and logic, homonymy (Aristotle's term, in the opening of the Categories) frames questions about equivocal predication — things said to "be" in many ways. In everyday life, homonymy is a rich source of puns and ambiguity, and a practical challenge for speech recognition and word-sense disambiguation in computational linguistics.
