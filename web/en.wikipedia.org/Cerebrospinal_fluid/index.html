Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is a clear, colourless body fluid found within the tissue surrounding the brain and spinal cord of vertebrates. It is produced mainly by the choroid plexuses in the ventricles of the brain, flows through the ventricular system and subarachnoid space, and is absorbed back into the venous blood through arachnoid granulations.

Functions

CSF cushions the brain against mechanical shock, providing buoyancy that reduces the effective weight of the brain from about 1,400 g to roughly 50 g and preventing it from compressing its own lower structures. It also stabilises the intracranial pressure, transports nutrients and hormones, and removes metabolic waste — during sleep, the glymphatic system uses CSF flow to clear solutes including beta-amyloid.

Composition and circulation

CSF is an ultrafiltrate of plasma with very low protein (15–45 mg/dL), glucose at about two-thirds the plasma level, and few cells (normally fewer than 5 white cells per microlitre, mostly lymphocytes). From the lateral and third ventricles it passes through the cerebral aqueduct to the fourth ventricle, exits via the foramina of Luschka and Magendie into the subarachnoid space, and circulates around the cord and cerebral hemispheres.

Clinical significance

Analysis of CSF obtained by lumbar puncture is central to diagnosing meningitis, subarachnoid haemorrhage, multiple sclerosis (oligoclonal bands), and Guillain–Barré syndrome (raised protein with normal cell count). Obstructed flow or absorption causes hydrocephalus, treated with shunts or endoscopic ventriculostomy. CSF leaks after trauma or procedures can cause low-pressure headaches; idiopathic intracranial hypertension raises pressure without a mass lesion.
