Om, the most fundamental Hindu mantra.

A mantra (Sanskrit: मन्त्र, romanized: mantra) is a sacred utterance — a word, syllable, sound, or phrase — believed by practitioners to have religious, magical, or spiritual powers. Mantras originate in the Vedic tradition of ancient India and are central to Hinduism; variant forms are used in Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism, and mantra-like practices (such as the Jesus Prayer or dhikr) occur in other religions as well. The earliest mantras are at least 3,500 years old, composed in Vedic Sanskrit as the hymns of the Rigveda.

Nature and use

Mantras are typically chanted or recited repeatedly (japa), often counted on a string of prayer beads (mala) in rounds of 108. Traditions variously emphasise the sound of the mantra, its meaning, or the meditative focus it produces. In Hindu practice, a mantra is often received from a guru at initiation (mantra-diksha), and seed syllables (bīja), such as Om, are held to concentrate the essence of a deity or truth. The Gayatri mantra (Rigveda 3.62.10), addressed to the solar deity Savitr, is among the most revered.

In Buddhism and other traditions

In Vajrayana Buddhism mantra is so central that the tradition is sometimes called Mantrayana ("the way of mantra"); the best-known Buddhist mantra is the six-syllable Om mani padme hum of Avalokiteshvara, inscribed on prayer wheels and stones across Tibet and the Himalayas. Jain mantras include the Namokar Mantra, a salutation to the five supreme beings. In Sikhism, the Mul Mantar opens the Guru Granth Sahib, and repetition of God's name (nam simran) is a core practice. In the twentieth century, mantra meditation was popularised globally by movements such as Transcendental Meditation, and "mantra" entered everyday English to denote any oft-repeated slogan or phrase.

Scholarly views

Scholars note that many mantras are not primarily semantic texts: Frits Staal famously argued that mantras resemble birdsong in functioning as sound structures governed by rules rather than meaning, while other Indologists stress the interplay of sound, meaning, and ritual context. Linguistic theory within India (especially the doctrine of sphoṭa and the goddess Vak, speech personified) developed sophisticated accounts of sacred sound.
