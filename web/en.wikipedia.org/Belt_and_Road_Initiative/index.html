Belt and Road Initiative

The Belt and Road Initiative (BRI; Chinese: 一带一路), previously known in English as the One Belt One Road initiative, is a global infrastructure development strategy adopted by the government of China in 2013 to invest in more than 150 countries and international organisations. It is considered the centrepiece of the foreign policy of Chinese leader Xi Jinping, who announced the "Silk Road Economic Belt" in Kazakhstan in September 2013 and the "21st-century Maritime Silk Road" in Indonesia the following month.

Scope

The initiative aims to strengthen infrastructure networks and connectivity across Asia, Europe, Africa, and beyond. Its flagship projects include the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC), the Port of Piraeus in Greece, the Hambantota Port in Sri Lanka, railways in Laos, Kenya, Hungary and Serbia, and the China–Europe freight train network. Financing has come principally from Chinese policy banks — the China Development Bank and the Export–Import Bank of China — supplemented by the Silk Road Fund and the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB), which opened in 2016 with 57 founding members. Cumulative BRI engagement in its first decade has been estimated at around US$1 trillion in construction contracts and investment.

History and forums

China has signed Belt and Road cooperation documents with more than 150 countries. Three Belt and Road Forums for International Cooperation were held in Beijing in 2017, 2019, and 2023. Italy, which joined in 2019 as the only G7 member, declined to renew its memorandum in 2023. Over time the initiative's branding has expanded to include a "Digital Silk Road," a "Health Silk Road," and polar and space silk roads, while official rhetoric has shifted toward "small and beautiful" projects with greater emphasis on green finance and debt sustainability.

Reception and criticism

Supporters argue the BRI fills an enormous infrastructure gap in the developing world, where the Asian Development Bank has estimated needs of US$1.7 trillion per year, and has built ports, railways, power plants, and industrial parks that Western finance had not funded. Critics — notably the United States, India, and some European governments — have raised concerns about opaque lending terms, unsustainable debt burdens (the so-called "debt-trap diplomacy" thesis, which many scholars dispute), environmental and labour standards, and the strategic dual-use potential of ports. Several projects have been renegotiated or scaled back, including in Malaysia, Pakistan, Zambia, and Sri Lanka.

Alternative programmes launched partly in response include the G7's Partnership for Global Infrastructure and Investment and the EU's Global Gateway.
