Public distribution system

The public distribution system (PDS) is a food security system in India, operated jointly by the central and state governments, under which essential commodities — principally wheat, rice, sugar, and kerosene — are distributed at subsidised prices through a network of more than 500,000 "fair price shops" to households holding ration cards. It is one of the largest food distribution networks in the world.

History and structure

The system grew out of wartime rationing in Bombay in 1939 and interwar food controls, and was formalized after independence to manage scarcity and stabilize prices. The Food Corporation of India (established 1965) procures grain from farmers at minimum support prices, stores it, and allocates it to states, which issue ration cards and run the fair price shops. In 1997 the system was restructured as the Targeted Public Distribution System (TPDS), dividing households into below-poverty-line (BPL) and above-poverty-line categories with different entitlements and prices. The National Food Security Act of 2013 converted many entitlements into legal rights, covering up to about two-thirds of the population with highly subsidised grain.

Criticism and reform

The PDS has long been criticized for leakages and diversion of grain to open markets, inclusion and exclusion errors in beneficiary lists, and high fiscal cost. Reforms have included digitization of ration cards, Aadhaar-based authentication, electronic point-of-sale machines in shops, and the "One Nation One Ration Card" scheme allowing portability of entitlements across states. States such as Tamil Nadu, which retained a near-universal system, and Chhattisgarh, which reformed its delivery chain, are frequently cited as better-performing models.
