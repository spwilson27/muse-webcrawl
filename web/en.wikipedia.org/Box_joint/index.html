Box joint

A box joint is a woodworking joint made by cutting a series of interlocking rectangular "fingers" into two pieces of wood, which are then glued together at right angles. Also called a finger joint when used at corners (the term finger joint also denotes a different end-to-end joint), it is a simpler, more mechanical-looking relative of the dovetail joint.

Characteristics

The straight-sided, square fingers provide a large long-grain gluing area, making the joint very strong, though it lacks the mechanical self-locking wedge action of dovetails and relies on glue for resistance to being pulled apart. Box joints are typical of boxes, drawers, crates, beehives, and utility furniture where appearance is secondary to strength and ease of manufacture.

Cutting

Box joints are commonly cut on a table saw with a stacked dado blade, a router table, or a dedicated jig that indexes the workpiece by the width of one finger for each successive cut. CNC routers and laser cutters also produce them precisely. Finger widths are usually matched to the thickness of the stock or made uniform for a regular pattern.
