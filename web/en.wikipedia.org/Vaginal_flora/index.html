Vaginal flora

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In most reproductive-age women the vaginal flora is dominated by lactobacilli.

Vaginal flora, also called the vaginal microbiota or vaginal microbiome, is the community of microorganisms that colonize the vagina. These bacteria were first described by the German gynaecologist Albert Döderlein in 1892 ("Döderlein's bacilli"). A healthy vaginal flora in most women of reproductive age is dominated by species of Lactobacillus, which protect against infection by producing lactic acid and maintaining a low vaginal pH (about 3.8–4.5).

Composition

The vaginal epithelium, under estrogen's influence, accumulates glycogen; lactobacilli ferment glycogen breakdown products to lactic acid, which (in combination with hydrogen peroxide and bacteriocins produced by some strains) inhibits pathogens. Common species include Lactobacillus crispatus, L. gasseri, L. iners and L. jensenii. Molecular surveys group vaginal communities into "community state types" (CSTs), most dominated by a single Lactobacillus species; a diverse community rich in anaerobes such as Gardnerella, Prevotella and Atopobium (CST IV) is characteristic of bacterial vaginosis, though it also occurs in asymptomatic women, with prevalence varying by ancestry.

Before puberty and after menopause, when estrogen levels are low, lactobacilli are less dominant and the flora resembles that of skin and bowel, with a near-neutral pH.

Functions

  • Acidifying the vagina and producing antimicrobial compounds, reducing colonization by urinary and sexually transmitted pathogens;
  • Competing for nutrients and adhesion sites on epithelial cells;
  • Modulating local immunity; a lactobacillus-dominant flora is associated with lower risk of HIV and other STI acquisition, and with lower rates of preterm birth;
  • Seeding the newborn's microbiome during vaginal delivery.

Disturbances

Antibiotics, douching, menstruation, semen (alkaline), hormonal changes and new sexual partners can disturb the flora. Loss of lactobacilli with overgrowth of mixed anaerobes produces bacterial vaginosis; imbalance also predisposes to candidiasis, aerobic vaginitis, urinary tract infection and pelvic inflammatory disease.

Research and therapy

Beyond antibiotics for bacterial vaginosis, researchers are testing live biotherapeutics such as L. crispatus preparations (e.g., LACTIN-V, which reduced BV recurrence in a 2020 trial) and even vaginal microbiota transplantation, still experimental. Sequencing-based studies continue to refine understanding of how community composition varies over the menstrual cycle and across populations.
