Big Mac index

The Big Mac index is a price index published since 1986 by The Economist as an informal way of measuring the purchasing power parity (PPP) between two currencies and providing a test of the extent to which market exchange rates result in goods costing the same in different countries. The index compares the price of a McDonald's Big Mac hamburger in different countries, converted to a common currency.

BasisPrice of a Big Mac in local currency
TheoryPurchasing power parity; "burgernomics"

Methodology

The implied PPP is the exchange rate that would make a Big Mac cost the same in two countries. The percentage difference between this implied rate and the actual market rate indicates whether a currency is under- or over-valued against the benchmark currency, usually the U.S. dollar. The Big Mac is used because it is produced locally in more than 100 countries from largely the same ingredients, combining traded and non-traded inputs.

Adjusted index

Because average prices are expected to be lower in poorer countries — labor is cheaper, per the Balassa–Samuelson effect — The Economist also publishes an adjusted index comparing prices to what would be predicted from GDP per person. This has often shown currencies, such as the Chinese yuan, to be closer to fair value than the raw index suggests.

Criticism and variations

Critics note that a burger is not traded across borders, that prices reflect local rents, taxes, and profit margins, and that demand for Big Macs varies with local tastes and competition. Variants include the Tall Latte index, based on Starbucks coffee, and various national adaptations.
