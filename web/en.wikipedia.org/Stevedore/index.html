Stevedore

A stevedore, also called a longshoreman, docker, or wharfie, is a waterfront manual labourer who loads and unloads cargo from ships in a port. The word comes from the Spanish estibador and Portuguese estivador, meaning "one who stows", ultimately from the Latin stipare, to pack or press together.

Work

Stevedoring involves stowing cargo securely so that a vessel remains stable and cargo is undamaged at sea, operating cranes, winches and forklifts, lashing containers, and handling bulk, break-bulk and roll-on/roll-off freight. Before containerisation, large gangs of men loaded ships by hand, net and hook — dangerous work with high injury rates. The container revolution of the 1950s and 1960s, led by Malcom McLean, drastically reduced the number of stevedores needed while increasing the skill and capital intensity of those who remained.

Labour history

Dock labour was traditionally casual, hired by the day at "shape-ups" on the pier, a system notorious for corruption and kickbacks. Stevedores became some of the most strongly unionised workers in the world: the International Longshoremen's Association on the US East and Gulf coasts, the International Longshore and Warehouse Union on the West Coast, and the Waterside Workers' Federation in Australia. Historic disputes include the 1934 West Coast waterfront strike in the United States and the 1998 Australian waterfront dispute. In Britain, dockers' work was restructured by the National Dock Labour Scheme and its abolition in 1989.

Modern industry

Today stevedoring is usually performed by specialised companies — such as PSA International, DP World, and Hutchison Ports — that operate container terminals under concession from port authorities. The human stevedore increasingly supervises automated cranes and straddle carriers rather than handling cargo directly.
