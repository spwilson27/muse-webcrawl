Spinal cord

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Cross-section of the spinal cord

The spinal cord is a long, thin, tubular structure made up of nervous tissue that extends from the medulla oblongata in the brainstem to the lumbar region of the vertebral column. Together with the brain it forms the central nervous system (CNS). In adult humans it is about 45 cm long in men and 43 cm in women, and it is enclosed within the spinal canal and protected by the vertebrae and meninges.

Structure

A cross-section of the cord shows a butterfly-shaped central region of grey matter, containing neuronal cell bodies, surrounded by white matter composed of myelinated axons organised into ascending (sensory) and descending (motor) tracts. Thirty-one pairs of spinal nerves emerge from the cord and connect it to the peripheral nervous system. Below the conus medullaris, the cord continues as the cauda equina, a bundle of nerve roots.

Function

The spinal cord conveys sensory information from the body to the brain and motor commands from the brain to muscles and glands; it also coordinates many reflexes independently of the brain, such as the knee-jerk reflex. Damage to the cord — from trauma, compression or disease — can cause paralysis and loss of sensation below the level of injury.
