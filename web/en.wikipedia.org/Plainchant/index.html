Plainchant

Plainchant, or plainsong, is a body of chants used in the liturgies of the Western Church. It is monophonic — consisting of a single, unaccompanied melodic line — and sung in free rhythm following the natural accents of the Latin text. The best-known form is Gregorian chant, the chant of the Roman Rite, though other regional traditions include Ambrosian, Mozarabic, and Old Roman chant.

Characteristics

Plainchant melodies are organised in the system of eight church modes. Chants range from simple syllabic settings, with one note per syllable, through neumatic settings, to highly ornate melismatic chants in which long flourishes of notes adorn a single syllable, as in the Alleluia. Chant served every part of the Mass and the Divine Office, from antiphons and hymns to the great responsories of Matins.

History

Chant developed over the first millennium of Christianity, drawing on Jewish and early Christian singing. Tradition credits Pope Gregory I (r. 590–604) with organising the Roman repertoire — hence "Gregorian chant" — though the surviving repertoire was largely codified under the Carolingians, who spread Roman chant through the Frankish kingdom, blending it with Gallican practice. Notation evolved from memory aids (neumes written above text) into the square notation on four-line staves seen in medieval choir books.

Reform and revival

After centuries of local variation and embellishment, the 19th-century monks of Solesmes Abbey led a scholarly restoration of chant, producing the editions adopted by the Vatican in the early 20th century. Although chant lost its central place in Catholic worship after the Second Vatican Council, it remains widely performed and recorded, and chant melodies have influenced composers from the medieval organum of Léonin and Pérotin to modern minimalists.
