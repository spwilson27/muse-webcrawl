Void (astronomy)

Cosmic voids are vast spaces between galaxy filaments (the largest-scale structures in the universe) that contain very few or no galaxies. Voids typically have diameters of 10 to 100 megaparsecs and densities far below the cosmic mean; together with filaments and walls they make up the large-scale structure of the universe, sometimes described as a cosmic web or foam.

Discovery and examples

The first voids were identified in galaxy redshift surveys in the late 1970s, overturning the assumption that galaxies are distributed uniformly. The Boötes void, discovered in 1981, is one of the largest known, roughly 250–330 million light-years across with very few galaxies. Other notable examples include the Local Void adjacent to the Local Group and the giant Eridanus Supervoid, which has been associated with the cosmic microwave background Cold Spot. Surveys such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey have catalogued thousands of voids, which are used to test cosmological models and the behaviour of dark energy.
