Targeted protein degradation

Targeted protein degradation (TPD) is a therapeutic strategy that harnesses the cell's own protein-disposal machinery — chiefly the ubiquitin–proteasome system — to selectively eliminate disease-causing proteins, rather than merely inhibiting their activity. It is widely regarded as a way to drug the large fraction of the proteome considered "undruggable" by conventional small-molecule inhibitors, including scaffolding proteins and transcription factors.

PROTACs

The best-known TPD agents are PROTACs (proteolysis-targeting chimeras), heterobifunctional molecules with one ligand that binds the target protein and another that recruits an E3 ubiquitin ligase (most often cereblon or VHL), joined by a linker. By bringing the two proteins together, the PROTAC induces ubiquitination of the target, marking it for destruction by the proteasome. Because a single PROTAC molecule can catalyse destruction of many target molecules, degraders can act at low, sub-stoichiometric doses and can eliminate all functions of a protein — enzymatic and structural alike — and act on proteins lacking active sites.

Molecular glues and other modalities

Molecular glue degraders are small molecules that stabilize an interaction between an E3 ligase and a target, causing its degradation; the immunomodulatory drugs lenalidomide and pomalidomide were found retrospectively to work this way, degrading zinc-finger transcription factors via cereblon. Other approaches include LYTACs, which route extracellular and membrane proteins to lysosomes; AUTACs and ATTECs, which engage autophagy; and degron-based systems used as research tools.

Clinical development

The first PROTACs entered clinical trials in 2019, targeting the androgen and estrogen receptors in prostate and breast cancer. Challenges include the large size and oral bioavailability of bifunctional molecules, resistance via ligase mutation, and the hook effect at high doses. As of the mid-2020s no PROTAC had yet received regulatory approval, but dozens of degraders were in clinical development.
