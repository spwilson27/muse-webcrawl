Cultural evolution

Cultural evolution is an evolutionary theory in the social sciences that proposes that cultures and societies develop through processes analogous to biological evolution: variation, transmission, and selection acting on ideas, behaviors, technologies, and institutions. Cultural traits are passed between individuals through learning rather than through genes.

History

Nineteenth-century thinkers such as Lewis Henry Morgan and Edward Tylor proposed unilinear schemes of social development, which were later criticized as ethnocentric. The modern quantitative field grew from the 1970s and 1980s with work by Luigi Luca Cavalli-Sforza, Marcus Feldman, Robert Boyd, and Peter Richerson, who applied population-genetic models to cultural transmission.

Mechanisms

Cultural transmission can be vertical (parent to child), horizontal (between peers), or oblique (from elders or media). Learners exhibit biases — toward prestigious individuals, toward conforming to the majority, or toward content that is memorable — which shape which variants spread. Cultural drift, migration, and institutional selection also drive change.

Dual inheritance theory

Dual inheritance theory (gene–culture coevolution) studies the interaction between genetic and cultural evolution: for example, the spread of dairy farming created selection pressure for adult lactase persistence in several populations. Related frameworks include memetics, which treats ideas as replicators called memes.
