The honeycomb lattice of graphene

Graphene is a single layer of carbon atoms arranged in a two-dimensional honeycomb lattice nanostructure. It is an allotrope of carbon, like diamond, graphite, nanotubes, and fullerenes, and can be thought of as an indefinitely large aromatic molecule. The name combines "graphite" and the suffix "-ene," reflecting that graphite itself consists of many stacked graphene sheets.

Graphene's properties are exceptional: it is among the strongest materials ever measured (roughly 200 times stronger than steel by weight), an excellent conductor of heat and electricity, nearly transparent (absorbing about 2.3% of visible light), and impermeable even to helium atoms. Its charge carriers behave as massless Dirac fermions, giving rise to unusual phenomena such as the anomalous quantum Hall effect.

History

The material was theoretical for decades—the band structure was calculated by P. R. Wallace in 1947—but long believed impossible to isolate as a free-standing sheet. In 2004 physicists Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov at the University of Manchester extracted single layers from graphite using adhesive tape (micromechanical cleavage) and demonstrated the field effect in them; they received the 2010 Nobel Prize in Physics for the work. Chemical vapour deposition on metal substrates later enabled larger-area films.

Applications

Proposed and emerging applications include high-frequency transistors and transparent conductive films, composite materials, energy storage in batteries and supercapacitors, water filtration and desalination membranes, biosensors, and coatings. Commercialisation has been gradual, with challenges in mass-producing defect-free sheets at low cost.
