Four-day week

A four-day week is a working-time arrangement in which employees work four days per week instead of the conventional five. It takes several forms: a genuinely reduced schedule, such as the "100-80-100" model (100% of pay, 80% of the time, in exchange for maintaining 100% of output); a compressed schedule, in which the same total hours are worked over four longer days; and four-day schooling, in which schools close for one weekday, usually to save money in rural districts.

Trials and evidence

Interest in the four-day week grew sharply after large-scale pilot programmes in the 2010s and 2020s. Microsoft Japan reported a roughly 40% productivity rise in a 2019 trial. Perpetual Guardian, a New Zealand trust company, made its 2018 trial permanent after finding improved engagement and maintained output. The world's largest trial, run in the United Kingdom in 2022 with 61 companies and about 2,900 workers, found stable or improved revenue, large drops in sick days and resignations, and reduced burnout; most participating companies kept the schedule afterwards. A six-month trial in Iceland (2015–2019), involving about 2,500 public-sector workers, reported maintained or improved productivity and led unions to renegotiate shorter hours for most of the workforce. Coordinated international pilots organised by 4 Day Week Global from 2022 reported similar findings in Ireland, the United States, Australia, and elsewhere.

Arguments

Advocates cite improved well-being, recruitment and retention, lower commuting emissions, and the elimination of low-value work such as unnecessary meetings. Critics note that the model fits some sectors better than others — continuous-coverage services such as health care and customer support must hire additional staff or stagger days off — and that compressed schedules can increase daily fatigue. In education, hundreds of rural US school districts have adopted four-day weeks, with research suggesting savings are modest and effects on achievement mixed.

History

The five-day week itself was a 20th-century achievement of the labour movement, replacing six-day schedules. Proposals for further reductions date back decades: in 1956 US Vice President Richard Nixon predicted a four-day week in the "not too distant future," and economists since Keynes have debated the relationship between productivity growth and leisure.
