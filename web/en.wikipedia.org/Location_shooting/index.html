Location shooting

Location shooting is the practice of filming a movie or television production in a real-world setting rather than on a purpose-built set on a studio soundstage or backlot. The place filmed is called a location, and the work of finding and securing it falls to location scouts and location managers, who obtain permits, arrange logistics and handle relations with property owners and local authorities.

Advantages and disadvantages

Shooting on location lends authenticity that even elaborate sets struggle to match, and it can be cheaper than constructing large sets. Films associated with Italian neorealism and the French New Wave made location work an aesthetic signature, enabled by lighter cameras and faster film stocks. Against this, locations offer little control: weather changes, traffic and crowd noise interfere, daylight limits the shooting day, and transporting a full crew and equipment is expensive. Interiors in particular are often impractical — rooms too small for cameras and lights — so productions commonly combine real exteriors with studio-built interiors.

Doubling and incentives

Locations frequently double for somewhere else: Toronto and Vancouver have long stood in for American cities, while tax incentives offered by governments — from US states to countries such as New Zealand and the United Kingdom — strongly influence where productions choose to shoot, creating local industries of crews and facilities. "Runaway production" is the term used when filming leaves its traditional home base for cheaper or better-incentivised locations.

Related practice

Before principal photography, a unit may shoot establishing shots and background plates at a location while the main scenes are made in the studio; second units commonly handle such work. Advances in LED-volume virtual production have recently offered an alternative, projecting location imagery around actors on a stage.
