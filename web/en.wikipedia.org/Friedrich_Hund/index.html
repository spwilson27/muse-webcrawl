Friedrich Hermann Hund (4 February 1896 – 31 March 1997) was a German physicist from Karlsruhe known for his work on atoms and molecules. He formulated Hund's rules for the ordering of electron energy levels in atoms and was a central figure in the development of molecular orbital theory.

Career

Hund studied mathematics, physics, and geography at Marburg and Göttingen, earning his doctorate under Max Born in 1922. He worked with Niels Bohr in Copenhagen and lectured at Göttingen, Rostock, Leipzig, Jena, Frankfurt, and again Göttingen, where he spent his final decades. Among his students was Edward Teller.

Contributions

Hund's rules (1925–1927) predict the ground-state term of an atom from its electron configuration. With Robert Mulliken he developed the molecular orbital approach to chemical bonding, and the Hund–Mulliken theory became the standard framework of quantum chemistry. Hund was also the first to describe quantum tunnelling, in 1927, in the context of double-well potentials. He received the Max Planck Medal in 1943 and the Otto Hahn Prize in 1974, and lived to the age of 101.
