The European eel (Anguilla anguilla) is a species of eel in the family Anguillidae, native to the Atlantic coast of Europe and North Africa and the rivers that drain into it. It has a famously complex, catadromous life cycle spanning thousands of kilometres of ocean, and its population has collapsed since the 1980s; the species is listed as critically endangered.

Life cycle

European eels spawn in the Sargasso Sea in the western Atlantic. Their leaf-shaped larvae (leptocephali) drift on ocean currents for one to three years towards Europe, where they metamorphose into transparent "glass eels" and enter estuaries and rivers. They pigment into elvers and then "yellow eels", feeding and growing in fresh or brackish water for 5 to 20 years or more. Finally they transform into migratory "silver eels" — with enlarged eyes, dark backs, and silvery bellies — stop feeding, and swim back across the Atlantic to the Sargasso Sea to spawn and die. The spawning itself has never been directly observed in the wild.

Decline and conservation

Recruitment of glass eels fell by roughly 90% or more from 1980s levels. Causes include overfishing (including a lucrative trade in glass eels exported to Asia), migration barriers such as dams and turbines, habitat loss, pollution, the introduced swim-bladder parasite Anguillicoloides crassus, and changes in ocean currents. The EU adopted an Eel Regulation in 2007 requiring member states to implement recovery plans, and the species is listed on CITES Appendix II.

As food

Eels have long been eaten in Europe — as jellied eels in London, smoked eel in northern Europe, and elvers (angulas) in Spain — though consumption is now discouraged for conservation reasons.
