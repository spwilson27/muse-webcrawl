Faraday constant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In physical chemistry, the Faraday constant, denoted by the symbol F, is the magnitude of electric charge per mole of electrons. It is named after the English scientist Michael Faraday and underlies his laws of electrolysis. Since the 2019 revision of the SI, the constant has the exactly defined value:

F = 96,485.33212… C·mol−1

Definition and derivation

The Faraday constant is the product of the elementary charge e and the Avogadro constant NA: F = e × NA. Because both constants have exact defined values in the modern SI, the Faraday constant is also exact. It represents the charge carried by one mole of electrons — roughly 96.5 kilocoulombs.

Uses

The constant appears throughout electrochemistry. In Faraday's laws of electrolysis, the amount of substance altered at an electrode during electrolysis is proportional to the quantity of electricity passed, with F converting between moles of electrons and coulombs. It links the Gibbs free energy of a cell reaction to its potential through ΔG = −nFE, and it appears in the Nernst equation via the factor RT/F. One Faraday of charge is also the total charge required to discharge one mole of a univalent ion, making the constant fundamental to battery capacity calculations and electrosynthesis.
