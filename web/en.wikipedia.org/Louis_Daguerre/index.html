Louis-Jacques-Mandé Daguerre, inventor of the daguerreotype.

Louis-Jacques-Mandé Daguerre (18 November 1787 – 10 July 1851) was a French artist and photographer, recognised for his invention of the eponymous daguerreotype process of photography. He became known as one of the fathers of photography. He is also noted for his invention of the diorama, a theatrical entertainment in which large translucent paintings were animated by changing light.

Biography

Daguerre was born in Cormeilles-en-Parisis, Val-d'Oise, and apprenticed in architecture, theatre design, and panoramic painting. In 1822 he opened the Diorama in Paris with Charles Marie Bouton, presenting spectacular illusionistic scenes that made him famous.

The daguerreotype

In 1829 Daguerre entered partnership with Nicéphore Niépce, who had produced the earliest surviving photograph from nature. After Niépce's death in 1833, Daguerre continued experimenting and discovered that a latent image on a silver-plated copper sheet exposed to iodine vapour could be developed with mercury vapour and fixed with salt solution. The resulting process, the daguerreotype, produced a unique, highly detailed direct-positive image.

The French government purchased the rights and announced the process as a gift "free to the world" on 19 August 1839, a date regarded as the birth of practical photography. Daguerre received a pension, and his manual sold widely. Daguerreotypes spread rapidly through Europe and the United States, especially for portraiture.

Later life and legacy

Daguerre retired to Bry-sur-Marne, where he died in 1851. He is buried there, and a monument marks his grave. Daguerre's name appears on the list of 72 names inscribed on the Eiffel Tower. The lunar crater Daguerre is named after him. The Boulevard du Temple photograph of 1838, showing what are considered the first recognisable figures of people in a photograph, remains one of his most famous images.
