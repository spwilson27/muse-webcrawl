Taxol (Paclitaxel)

Paclitaxel, sold under the brand name Taxol among others, is a chemotherapy medication used to treat a number of types of cancer, including ovarian cancer, breast cancer, lung cancer, Kaposi sarcoma, cervical cancer, and pancreatic cancer. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

History

Paclitaxel was isolated in 1971 from bark of the Pacific yew tree, Taxus brevifolia, by Monroe Wall and Mansukh Wani under a U.S. National Cancer Institute screening program, and its structure was published the same year. Its unusual mechanism — stabilizing microtubules and preventing their dynamic breakdown, thereby blocking cell division — was elucidated by Susan Horwitz in 1979. Because harvesting bark killed the slow-growing trees, supply was a major problem until semisynthesis from baccatin III, a precursor extractable from the needles of the European yew, and later plant-cell culture production, made supply sustainable. The U.S. FDA approved the drug in 1992, and Bristol-Myers Squibb marketed it as Taxol.

Medical use and side effects

Paclitaxel is administered by intravenous infusion, often weekly or every three weeks. Common side effects include hair loss, numbness and tingling in the hands and feet (peripheral neuropathy), joint and muscle pain, diarrhea, and suppression of bone marrow. Serious allergic reactions can occur, partly attributable to the Cremophor EL solvent used in the original formulation; an albumin-bound formulation (nab-paclitaxel, Abraxane) avoids this solvent.
