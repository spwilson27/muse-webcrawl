Slavery in Brazil

Slavery in Brazil began in the 16th century with the Portuguese colonisation and the enslavement of indigenous peoples, then expanded massively through the transatlantic slave trade. Brazil received more enslaved Africans than any other country — roughly 4.9 million of the approximately 12.5 million Africans transported to the Americas — and was the last Western country to abolish slavery, with the Golden Law of 13 May 1888.

Slavery in Brazil

Period: c. 1532–1888
Enslaved Africans imported: ~4.9 million
Abolished: 13 May 1888 (Golden Law)

Economy

Enslaved labour underpinned successive export cycles: sugar in the Northeast, gold in Minas Gerais, and coffee in the Southeast. Indigenous enslavement, bandeirante raiding and African slavery coexisted, and internal slave trading grew after Britain pressured Brazil to end the Atlantic trade in 1850.

Resistance and abolition

Enslaved people resisted through revolt, flight and the formation of quilombos such as Palmares, led in its final years by Zumbi. A gradual emancipation path — the Law of the Free Womb (1871) and the Sexagenarian Law (1885) — preceded full abolition by Princess Isabel in 1888, which contributed to the fall of the monarchy the following year.
