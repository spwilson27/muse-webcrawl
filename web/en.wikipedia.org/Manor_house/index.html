Manor house

A typical English manor house

A manor house is the principal residence of the lord of the manor, usually a large country house forming the administrative centre of a manor in the feudal system, most characteristically in historical Europe. The term is today loosely applied to grand rural houses built from the medieval period through the early twentieth century.

History

Under the manorial system that spread across medieval Europe after the Norman Conquest of England, land was organized into manors held by lords who owed service to the crown. The manor house was where the lord (or his steward) held the manorial court, collected rents and dues, and administered the demesne — the land farmed directly for the lord's household — while tenant farmers worked the remainder.

Early manor houses were frequently fortified, or at least moated, but from the later Middle Ages onward they increasingly took the form of comfortable residences amid their estates. In England, the dissolution of manorial administration left the houses as seats of the landed gentry. Equivalent forms across Europe include the French manoir, the German Gutshaus and Rittergut, the Polish dwór, and the Scandinavian herregård.

Features

Typical elements include a great hall (in medieval examples), a solar or withdrawing rooms, kitchens and service ranges, a chapel in larger houses, and associated estate buildings — stables, barns, dovecotes, and a home farm. The manor house usually stood near the parish church and village, whose tenants owed labour or rent to the lord.

Decline

The legal machinery of manorialism faded over centuries; in England, copyhold tenure was abolished by the Law of Property Act 1922. Many manor houses remain private homes, while others have become hotels, schools, or museums, and the title "lord of the manor" survives in England as an incorporeal property right that can be bought and sold separately from the house itself.
