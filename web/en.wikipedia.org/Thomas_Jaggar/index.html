Thomas Augustus Jaggar Jr. (24 January 1871 – 17 January 1953) was an American volcanologist. He founded and directed the Hawaiian Volcano Observatory, located on the rim of Kīlauea caldera on the island of Hawaii, and is regarded as a pioneer of the systematic scientific observation of volcanoes.

Early life and the call to volcanology

Jaggar was born in Philadelphia, the son of an Episcopal bishop, and studied geology at Harvard University, earning his doctorate in 1897. He taught at Harvard and at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. His commitment to volcanology was crystallised by the catastrophic 1902 eruptions of Mount Pelée on Martinique and La Soufrière on St Vincent, which killed tens of thousands; Jaggar travelled to the disaster sites and resolved that continuous instrumental monitoring of volcanoes was needed to save lives. His motto became Ne plus haustae aut obrutae urbes — "that no more cities be swallowed up or buried".

Hawaiian Volcano Observatory

In 1912 Jaggar arrived in Hawaii and, with funding from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and private donors, established a permanent observatory at Kīlauea. He installed seismographs and tiltmeters, recorded eruptions over four decades, and developed techniques including the use of thermocouples to measure lava temperatures. The observatory later came under the United States Geological Survey, and Jaggar also helped establish Hawaii Volcanoes National Park. He experimented with amphibious vehicles and even proposed bombing lava flows to divert them from the city of Hilo during the 1935 Mauna Loa eruption.

Legacy

Jaggar retired in 1940 and spent his last years at the University of Hawaii, where a museum at the summit of Kīlauea bore his name. His lifelong advocacy established volcano monitoring as a practical science and influenced observatories worldwide.
