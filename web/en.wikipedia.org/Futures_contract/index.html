Futures contract

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In finance, a futures contract (more colloquially, futures) is a standardised legal agreement to buy or sell a particular commodity, asset, or security at a predetermined price at a specified future date. Futures are exchange-traded derivatives, cleared through a central clearing house, and are used both to hedge price risk and to speculate. They differ from forward contracts, which are customised, privately negotiated, and not marked to market daily.

Mechanics

A buyer of futures takes a long position, agreeing to receive the underlying at expiry; a seller takes a short position. Both post initial margin — a fraction of the contract's value — and accounts are settled daily against the closing price, so gains and losses are realised in cash each day. Most positions are closed out before delivery; only a small percentage of contracts go to physical delivery, the rest being cash-settled or offset.

Uses

Farmers and food processors hedge harvest prices; airlines hedge fuel; fund managers hedge equity or interest-rate exposure with index and bond futures. Speculators supply liquidity and take on the transferred risk. Because of leverage, futures gains and losses can be large relative to the margin posted, and exchanges and regulators impose position limits and additional margins in volatile markets.

History

Formalised forward trading in rice at Osaka's Dojima exchange in the early 1700s is an early example, complete with clearing. The Chicago Board of Trade (1848) standardised grain futures in the 1860s. Financial futures — on currencies (1972, at the Chicago Mercantile Exchange), interest rates, and stock indices (1982) — grew to dominate trading volumes in the late twentieth century.
