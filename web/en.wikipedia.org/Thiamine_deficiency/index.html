Thiamine deficiency

Thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency causes beriberi and Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome.

NutrientThiamine (vitamin B1)
DiseasesBeriberi, Wernicke encephalopathy, Korsakoff syndrome
CausesPoor diet, alcoholism, malabsorption, increased requirements
TreatmentThiamine supplementation

Thiamine deficiency is a medical condition of low levels of thiamine (vitamin B1). Thiamine is an essential nutrient involved in carbohydrate metabolism and nervous system function; the body stores little of it, so deficiency can develop within weeks. A severe and chronic form is known as beriberi. The common forms in adults are wet beriberi, which affects the cardiovascular system, and dry beriberi, which affects the nervous system. Advanced symptoms include heart failure and paralysis.

Causes

Deficiency arises from inadequate intake—historically in populations relying on polished white rice, in which the thiamine-rich husk has been removed—as well as from alcohol use disorder, which impairs absorption and storage, malabsorption, dialysis, prolonged vomiting, and conditions that increase metabolic demand such as pregnancy, fever and hyperthyroidism.

Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome

In people with alcohol dependence, thiamine deficiency can produce Wernicke encephalopathy, an acute neurological emergency characterised by confusion, ataxia and abnormal eye movements, and, if untreated, Korsakoff syndrome, a chronic condition of severe memory impairment and confabulation. Suspected Wernicke encephalopathy is treated immediately with high-dose parenteral thiamine, given before glucose.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually clinical, supported by the response to treatment; laboratory measures of thiamine status exist but treatment should not be delayed. Treatment is thiamine supplementation, orally for mild deficiency and intravenously for severe cases or suspected Wernicke encephalopathy. Prevention includes adequate diet and fortification of staple foods.
