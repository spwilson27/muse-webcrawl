Indian logic

Indian logic is the tradition of logical and epistemological inquiry in Indian philosophy, one of the three major independent origins of logic alongside Greek and Chinese traditions. It developed within the Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain schools, primarily as a theory of inference (anumāna) and debate, and flourished for over two thousand years.

Nyāya and the five-membered inference

The Nyāya school, founded on the Nyāya Sūtra attributed to Akṣapāda Gautama (early centuries CE), systematized logic around the means of valid knowledge (pramāṇa): perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. Its canonical inference has five members — thesis (pratijñā), reason (hetu), example (udāharaṇa), application (upanaya), and conclusion (nigamana) — as in: "The hill has fire (thesis), because it has smoke (reason); whatever has smoke has fire, like a kitchen (example); so does this hill (application); therefore the hill has fire (conclusion)." Central is the notion of vyāpti, the invariable concomitance between reason and property to be proved.

Buddhist and later developments

The Buddhist logicians Dignāga (c. 480–540) and Dharmakīrti (7th century) reformed inference, analyzing the three marks of a valid reason (trairūpya), the theory of exclusion (apoha), and reducing the formal inference to fewer members. Jain logicians developed the doctrine of manifold predication (syādvāda) and sevenfold judgment. From the 13th century, the Navya-Nyāya ("New Logic") school founded by Gaṅgeśa created a precise technical language for relations, negation, and quantification, remaining intellectually active into the early modern period. Indian logical ideas also traveled to Tibet and East Asia via Buddhism.
