Niacin

Niacin
Other nameNicotinic acid, vitamin B3
FormulaC6H5NO2
DeficiencyPellagra

Niacin, also known as nicotinic acid, is an organic compound and a form of vitamin B3, an essential human nutrient. It is a precursor of the coenzymes NAD and NADP, central to metabolism. Niacin is obtained from foods including meat, fish, nuts, and fortified cereals, and can be synthesised from tryptophan.

Deficiency and sources

Deficiency causes pellagra, characterised by dermatitis, diarrhoea, dementia, and, if untreated, death — the "four Ds". Pellagra was epidemic in the early 20th-century American South until flour enrichment and the work of Joseph Goldberger established its dietary cause.

Pharmacological use

In gram doses, niacin lowers triglycerides and LDL cholesterol and raises HDL cholesterol, an effect recognised since the 1950s and tested in the Coronary Drug Project. However, AIM-HIGH and HPS2-THRIVE trials found no added cardiovascular benefit when combined with statins, and use declined. Characteristic side effects include intense cutaneous flushing, itching, hyperglycemia, and hepatotoxicity, especially with sustained-release forms.

Chemistry

Niacin is distinct from nicotinamide (niacinamide), the other vitamin B3 vitamer, which does not cause flushing or lipid effects at usual doses.
