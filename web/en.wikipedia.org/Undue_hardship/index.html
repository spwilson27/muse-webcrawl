Undue hardship

Undue hardship determinations are made by courts and administrative bodies.

Undue hardship is a legal concept describing a special or specified circumstance that partially or fully exempts a person or organization from performance of a legal obligation, on the grounds that doing so would impose an unreasonable or disproportionate burden. The term is used in several areas of law in the United States, Canada, and other jurisdictions.

Disability accommodation

Under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), employers must provide reasonable accommodations to qualified employees with disabilities unless doing so would cause undue hardship — defined as an action requiring significant difficulty or expense. Factors include the nature and cost of the accommodation, the employer's overall financial resources, and the effect on operations. Similar standards appear in Canadian human rights law, where an employer must accommodate an employee up to the point of undue hardship.

Bankruptcy

In United States bankruptcy law, a debtor may obtain discharge of a student loan only by showing that repaying it would impose an "undue hardship" on the debtor and the debtor's dependents (11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(8)). Many courts apply the three-part Brunner test, requiring the debtor to show an inability to maintain a minimal standard of living while repaying, additional circumstances indicating the hardship is likely to persist, and a good faith effort to repay.

Religious accommodation

Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 requires employers to reasonably accommodate employees' religious practices unless doing so causes undue hardship on the conduct of the employer's business. In Groff v. DeJoy (2023), the Supreme Court clarified that the employer must show that the burden is substantial in the overall context of its business.
