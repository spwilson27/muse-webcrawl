Golden Law


Princess Isabel signing abolition, 1888.

The Golden Law (Portuguese: Lei Áurea), adopted 13 May 1888, was the law that abolished slavery in Brazil. Signed by Princess Isabel of Braganza, regent for her father Emperor Pedro II, it consisted of only two articles: slavery in Brazil was declared extinct, and all contrary provisions were revoked.

Background

Brazil was the last country in the Western world to abolish slavery, having received more enslaved Africans than any other country in the transatlantic trade. Abolition came gradually: the Law of Free Birth (1871) freed children born to enslaved mothers thereafter, and the Sexagenarian Law (1885) freed the enslaved over sixty. By the 1880s the abolitionist movement — led by figures such as Joaquim Nabuco, José do Patrocínio and André Rebouças — had gained mass support, provinces such as Ceará freed their enslaved people outright, and mass escapes undermined the institution.

Consequences

The Golden Law freed an estimated 700,000 remaining enslaved people without compensation to owners, angering the planter class whose support the monarchy depended on; many historians link abolition to the fall of the Empire in the republican coup of November 1889. Freed people received no land or integration measures, a legacy of inequality that remains debated in Brazil. The date, 13 May, is commemorated as Abolition Day, and Isabel is remembered as "the Redemptress".
