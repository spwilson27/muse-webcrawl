The farad (symbol: F) is the unit of electrical capacitance, the ability of a body to store an electrical charge, in the International System of Units (SI), equivalent to 1 coulomb per volt (C/V). It is named after the English physicist Michael Faraday.

Definition

One farad is defined as the capacitance across which, when charged with one coulomb, there is a potential difference of one volt. Equivalently, it can be expressed as the capacitance that stores one coulomb of charge per volt of potential difference. The farad is a very large unit: most capacitors have values in the microfarad (µF), nanofarad (nF), or picofarad (pF) range, although supercapacitors with capacitances of hundreds or thousands of farads are used in some applications.

History

The term "farad" was proposed by Latimer Clark and Charles Bright in honour of Faraday, and the unit was standardised in the late 19th century. It became an SI derived unit with a special name.
