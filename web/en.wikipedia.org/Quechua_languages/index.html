Quechua languages

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Quechua (also spelled Kichwa in Ecuador) is a family of Indigenous languages spoken in the Andes of South America, principally in Peru, Bolivia, and Ecuador, with smaller communities in Argentina, Colombia, and Chile. With several million speakers it is the most widely spoken Indigenous language family of the Americas. Quechua was the language of administration of the Inca Empire and spread further under Spanish colonial rule as a lingua franca.

Classification and varieties

Linguists classify Quechua into two main branches: Central Quechua (Quechua I), spoken in Peru's central highlands, and Peripheral Quechua (Quechua II), which includes the southern varieties of Cusco and Bolivia and the northern Kichwa of Ecuador. Mutual intelligibility between distant varieties is limited, and whether Quechua is one language with dialects or a family of languages is debated.

Features and status

Quechua languages are agglutinative and suffixing, with subject–object–verb word order, a three-vowel system /i, a, u/, and (in southern varieties) distinctive aspirated and ejective stop series borrowed through contact with Aymara. Quechua is co-official in Peru and Bolivia and recognised in Ecuador; despite large speaker numbers it is considered vulnerable, as many speakers shift to Spanish. Its best-known loanwords in English include puma, condor, llama, quinoa, and coca.
