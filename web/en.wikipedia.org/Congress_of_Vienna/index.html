Congress of Vienna

DatesSeptember 1814 – 9 June 1815
LocationVienna, Austrian Empire

The Congress of Vienna of 1814–1815 was a series of international diplomatic meetings that discussed and agreed on a new layout for the European political and constitutional order after the downfall of the French Emperor Napoleon Bonaparte. Participants were representatives from all European states, and it was dominated by the four great powers — Austria, Britain, Prussia and Russia — plus a rehabilitated France represented by Talleyrand.

Proceedings

The Congress met under the chairmanship of the Austrian statesman Klemens von Metternich. Although formal sessions were rare, the ambassadors negotiated continuously amid an almost unbroken round of balls, banquets and entertainments — prompting Prince de Ligne's quip that "the Congress dances, but it does not march". Negotiations were interrupted by Napoleon's escape from Elba and the Hundred Days, which ended at the Battle of Waterloo days after the Final Act was signed on 9 June 1815.

Decisions

Guided by the principles of legitimacy (restoring deposed dynasties) and balance of power, the Congress redrew Europe's map. Austria regained influence in Italy and Germany; Prussia gained territory in the Rhineland and Saxony; Russia took most of Poland ("Congress Poland"); Sweden kept Norway; and the Netherlands were united under the House of Orange. Switzerland's neutrality was guaranteed, and the German states were organised into a loose German Confederation of 39 states. France was restored to its 1790 borders under the Bourbons.

Legacy

The settlement, maintained through the "Concert of Europe", prevented a general European war for nearly a century, until 1914 — a record that led historians such as Henry Kissinger to study it as a model of stable international order. Critics have long charged that it ignored liberal and nationalist aspirations, storing up the revolutions of 1820, 1830 and 1848.
