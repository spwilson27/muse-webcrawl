TypePhotosynthetic pigment

Chlorophyll is any of several related green pigments found in cyanobacteria and in the chloroplasts of algae and plants. Its name comes from the Greek chloros ("green") and phyllon ("leaf"). Chlorophyll absorbs light most strongly in the blue and red portions of the spectrum and poorly in the green — hence the green colour of plant tissues — and uses the absorbed energy to power photosynthesis.

Structure and types

Chlorophyll molecules are built around a chlorin ring holding a central magnesium ion, with a long hydrophobic phytol tail anchoring them in membranes. Chlorophyll a is the primary pigment in all oxygen-producing photosynthesisers; chlorophyll b is an accessory pigment in land plants and green algae, broadening the absorbed spectrum. Other forms (c in many algae, d and f in some cyanobacteria) absorb still other wavelengths, including far-red.

Function

In photosystems I and II, special pairs of chlorophyll a (P680 and P700) donate electrons when excited by light, driving the production of ATP and NADPH that fix carbon in the Calvin cycle. The pigment was first isolated and named by Joseph Bienaimé Caventou and Pierre Joseph Pelletier in 1817; its structure was largely worked out by Hans Fischer, and Richard Willstätter received the 1915 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for research on plant pigments.

Measurement and uses

Leaf chlorophyll content is estimated non-destructively with SPAD meters and remotely by satellite vegetation indices, informing agriculture and ecology. Chlorophyllin derivatives are used as food colouring (E140/E141) and in some medicines.
