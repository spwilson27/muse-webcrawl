The Forest of Dean is a geographical, historical, and cultural region in the western part of Gloucestershire, England, occupying a wooded plateau between the River Wye to the west and northwest, the River Severn to the south and east, and the city of Gloucester to the northeast. One of England's surviving ancient royal forests, it was reserved as a hunting ground by the Norman kings and later became a major centre of coal mining, iron working, and timber production — including oak for Royal Navy ships.

History

The area was settled in prehistory and mined for iron ore and ochre from Roman times. After the Norman Conquest it became a royal hunting forest administered by a constable based at St Briavels Castle, with its own forest law and courts (the Verderers' Court still meets at Speech House). Local "freeminers" — said by custom to be men born within the forest's bounds who had worked a year and a day in a mine — held the right to dig coal and iron in their own "gales". From the seventeenth century, ironworks and then deep coal mines transformed the forest; the last deep mine, Northern United, closed in 1965.

Geography and nature

The forest covers hills of Carboniferous sandstone and limestone; the Wye Valley on its western edge is an Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty. Ancient oak and beech woods shelter fallow deer, wild boar (re-established since the 1990s), goshawks, and dormice. Popular beauty spots include Symonds Yat Rock above the Wye and the Sculpture Trail near Speech House.

Culture

Foresters have a distinct identity and dialect, and local customs include the freemining tradition, verderers, and commoners' sheep grazing ("sheep badgers"). The forest inspired writers with local roots and associations, and its woodland and industrial ruins are now managed largely by Forestry England for timber, conservation, and recreation.
