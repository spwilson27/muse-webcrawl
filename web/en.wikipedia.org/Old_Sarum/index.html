Old Sarum

Old Sarum is the ruin of a settlement in Wiltshire, England, about two miles north of modern Salisbury. Occupied from the Iron Age, it became a Norman stronghold with a castle and cathedral, before being largely abandoned in the 13th century when the cathedral and city were refounded at Salisbury. It is also notorious as the archetypal "rotten borough", returning two Members of Parliament despite having virtually no resident voters until the Reform Act 1832.

History

The hilltop was fortified as a hillfort around 400 BCE, later used by the Romans (Sorviodunum) and Saxons. After the Norman Conquest, William the Conqueror built a motte-and-bailey castle within the earthworks, and in 1075 the see of Sherborne was moved here; Bishop Osmund completed a cathedral in 1092. Friction between the castle garrison and the clergy, a cramped water-poor site, and the ambition of Bishop Richard Poore led to the cathedral's relocation to the Avon valley in the 1220s, founding New Sarum (Salisbury). The castle was demolished under Henry VIII and the site deserted.

Rotten borough

The depopulated hill nonetheless continued to elect two MPs. The seat passed into the hands of the Pitt family; William Pitt the Elder entered Parliament for Old Sarum in 1735. Its absurdity — famously, a handful of voters owned by one proprietor — made it a symbol in the campaign for parliamentary reform, abolished by the Great Reform Act of 1832.
