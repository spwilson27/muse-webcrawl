Accelerometer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An accelerometer is a sensor that measures proper acceleration—the acceleration experienced relative to free fall—typically expressed in metres per second squared (m/s²) or in multiples of standard gravity (g). Single- and multi-axis accelerometers detect both static forces, such as gravity (used to determine orientation), and dynamic forces caused by movement or vibration.

Principles of operation

Most accelerometers work by sensing the displacement of a small proof mass suspended by springs: when the device accelerates, the mass lags behind, and the displacement is measured capacitively, piezoelectrically, or piezoresistively and converted to an electrical signal. By Einstein's equivalence principle, an accelerometer at rest on Earth's surface reads approximately 1 g upward, while one in free fall reads zero.

Types

Modern consumer devices use MEMS (micro-electromechanical systems) accelerometers etched in silicon, which are tiny, cheap, and low-power. Other types include piezoelectric accelerometers for vibration and shock measurement in industry, piezoresistive devices, and high-precision servo (force-balance) accelerometers used in inertial navigation systems and seismology.

Applications

Accelerometers are ubiquitous: smartphones use them for screen orientation, step counting and gesture control; laptops use them to park hard-disk heads when a fall is detected; vehicles use them to trigger airbag deployment and electronic stability control; and they are essential components of inertial measurement units in aircraft, spacecraft, missiles and drones. In science and engineering they monitor structural vibration, seismic activity, and machine health.
