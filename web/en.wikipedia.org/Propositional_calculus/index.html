Propositional calculus

Propositional calculus is a branch of mathematical logic that studies ways of joining and modifying entire propositions, statements, or sentences to form more complicated propositions, statements, or sentences, as well as the logical relationships and properties that are derived from these methods of combining or altering statements. It is also called propositional logic, statement logic, sentential calculus, or sentential logic. Unlike first-order logic, propositional logic does not deal with non-logical objects or quantifiers, but treats whole propositions as its atoms.

Overview

In propositional logic, atomic propositions are represented by letters such as p, q, and r, and compound formulas are built using logical connectives: negation (¬), conjunction (∧), disjunction (∨), implication (→), and biconditional (↔). A formula is a tautology if it is true under every assignment of truth values to its atoms; validity can be checked mechanically with truth tables, making the system decidable.

Historical development

The study of propositions and their combinations goes back to Aristotle's syllogistic and the Stoic logicians. Its modern formal treatment began with Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) and was developed by Russell and Whitehead in Principia Mathematica (1910–1913). Emil Post proved the completeness of the propositional calculus in 1921, and Wittgenstein popularised the truth-table method in his Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus.

Proof systems

Propositional logic can be presented through several equivalent proof systems, including Hilbert-style axiomatic systems, natural deduction, and the sequent calculus. Two fundamental metatheorems are soundness (everything provable is true) and completeness (everything true under all valuations is provable).
