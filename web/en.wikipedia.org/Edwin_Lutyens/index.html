Sir Edwin Landseer Lutyens OM KCIE RA (29 March 1869 – 1 January 1944) was an English architect known for imaginatively adapting traditional architectural styles to the requirements of his era. He designed many English country houses, war memorials and public buildings. In his biography, the writer Christopher Hussey wrote, "In his lifetime (Lutyens) was widely held to be our greatest architect since Wren if not, as many maintained, his superior."

Country houses

Lutyens rose to prominence designing Arts and Crafts country houses in Surrey, often in collaboration with garden designer Gertrude Jekyll, whose brother he had met in 1889. Works such as Munstead Wood (1896) and Deanery Garden showcased his inventive use of local materials and traditional forms. His 114 commission list includes Castle Drogo in Devon (1911–1930), the last castle built in England.

New Delhi

Lutyens played a central role in designing and building New Delhi, the new capital of British India. Working with Herbert Baker, he laid out the imperial city between 1912 and 1931, designing the Viceroy's House (now Rashtrapati Bhavan), India Gate and the great hexagonal plan of avenues. The area of his bungalows is still known as Lutyens' Delhi.

War memorials

After the First World War, Lutyens became the principal architect to the Imperial War Graves Commission. He designed the Cenotaph in Whitehall (1919–1920), the Thiepval Memorial to the Missing of the Somme in France (1928–1932), and dozens of other memorials embodying his concept of the "Stone of Remembrance".

Later work and legacy

His largest project, Liverpool Metropolitan Cathedral, was left unfinished at his death in 1944; only the crypt was built to his design. Lutyens was knighted in 1918 and received the Royal Gold Medal in 1921. His work bridges the Arts and Crafts movement and Neoclassicism, and he remains one of Britain's most celebrated architects.
