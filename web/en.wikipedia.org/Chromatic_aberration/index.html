Chromatic aberration

Chromatic aberration (CA) is a failure of a lens to focus all colours to the same point. It is caused by dispersion: the refractive index of glass varies with the wavelength of light, so each colour is bent by a slightly different amount. Chromatic aberration manifests as coloured "fringes", typically purple or green, along high-contrast boundaries in an image.

Types

Axial (longitudinal) chromatic aberration occurs when different wavelengths are focused at different distances along the optical axis, producing coloured halos that appear throughout the frame and diminish when the lens is stopped down. Lateral (transverse) chromatic aberration arises when wavelengths are focused at different positions in the focal plane; its fringes are strongest toward the corners of the image and, unlike the axial type, are not reduced by stopping down.

Correction

Optical designers correct chromatic aberration with achromatic doublets, which combine crown and flint glass elements, and with apochromatic lens designs and low-dispersion glass that bring three or more wavelengths to a common focus. Mirrors are free of the defect, one reason reflecting telescopes are preferred for large instruments. In photography, lateral chromatic aberration can also be removed in software, since the colour channels are simply displaced relative to one another.
