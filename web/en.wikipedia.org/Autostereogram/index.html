Autostereogram

A random-dot autostereogram: viewed with diverged eyes, a hidden 3D shape emerges.

An autostereogram is a two-dimensional image designed to create the visual illusion of a three-dimensional scene from a single picture. To perceive the hidden depth, the viewer must decouple the eyes' normal coordination of convergence and accommodation — usually by looking "through" the image (wall-eyed divergence) or by crossing the eyes — so that repeating patterns in the image are fused by the brain into a virtual 3D object floating in front of or behind the page.

History

The principles go back to Charles Wheatstone's 1838 description of binocular vision and to wallpaper patterns noticed to produce depth effects when viewed with diverged eyes. In 1959 Béla Julesz invented the random-dot stereogram, using computer-generated dot pairs viewed through a stereoscope to show that depth perception can arise without monocular cues. Christopher Tyler combined these ideas in 1979, with programmer Maureen Clarke, to produce the single-image random-dot autostereogram. The format became a mass craze in the early 1990s with the Magic Eye books and posters.

How it works

An autostereogram consists of a horizontally repeating pattern. Where the pattern's repeat period is shortened slightly, the brain interprets the fused features as closer to the viewer; lengthening the period pushes them back. A depth map therefore modulates the spacing of a texture or random dots across the image. Techniques include random-dot, textured and "wallpaper" autostereograms, and wiggle or animated variants.

Viewing

Common advice is to hold the image close, relax the focus as if gazing at a distant scene, and slowly move the image away until the pattern locks into depth. Some viewers use a finger or a reflected highlight as a fusion aid. A minority of people with impaired binocular vision cannot perceive the effect.
