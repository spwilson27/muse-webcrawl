The Stark effect splits atomic spectral lines in an applied electric field.

The Stark effect is the shifting and splitting of spectral lines of atoms and molecules due to the presence of an external static electric field. It is the electric-field analogue of the Zeeman effect, in which a spectral line is split by a magnetic field. The effect is named after the German physicist Johannes Stark, who discovered it in 1913 and received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1919 partly for this work.

Description

An applied electric field perturbs the energy levels of an atom. In hydrogen, degenerate levels of the same principal quantum number but different angular momentum mix in the field, producing a linear Stark effect in which the splitting is proportional to the field strength. In most other atoms the leading behaviour is a quadratic Stark effect, in which shifts are proportional to the square of the field. The effect can be described in quantum mechanics by perturbation theory; the complete explanation of hydrogen's linear effect was a celebrated early triumph of the Schrödinger equation in 1926.

Applications

Stark spectroscopy is used to measure dipole moments and local electric fields in physical, chemical and biological systems. In plasma physics, Stark broadening of spectral lines is used to infer electron densities in stars, fusion devices and discharges. The Stark effect also underlies quantum-confined Stark devices used in optical modulators.
