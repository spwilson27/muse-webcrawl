Prohibition in the United States

Prohibition in the United States was a nationwide constitutional ban on the production, importation, transportation, and sale of alcoholic beverages from 1920 to 1933. It was imposed by the Eighteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution and the enabling Volstead Act, and it ended with the ratification of the Twenty-first Amendment, the only amendment to repeal another.

Origins

Prohibition was the culmination of a decades-long temperance movement led by groups such as the Woman's Christian Temperance Union and the Anti-Saloon League, supported by many Protestant churches. "Drys" argued that alcohol caused poverty, crime, domestic violence, and corruption, and they linked the brewing industry to German-American communities during the First World War, adding patriotic pressure.

Effects

Alcohol consumption fell initially, and some public-health indicators improved, but enforcement proved enormously difficult. Illegal production and sale ("bootlegging") and clandestine bars known as speakeasies flourished, and organised crime syndicates led by figures such as Al Capone grew wealthy and powerful on the illicit liquor trade. Homemade and industrial alcohol sometimes poisoned drinkers, and corruption among officials was widespread. The federal government lost substantial tax revenue while spending heavily on enforcement.

Repeal

Public disillusionment grew during the Great Depression, when the loss of jobs and tax income became acute arguments against the ban. The Twenty-first Amendment was ratified on 5 December 1933, returning control of alcohol regulation to the states; a few states maintained prohibition for decades longer, Mississippi being the last to repeal statewide prohibition in 1966.
