Liberal democracy is a form of government combining liberal political ideology with representative democracy. It is characterised by elections between multiple distinct political parties, a separation of powers into different branches of government, the rule of law in everyday life, a market economy with private property, and the equal protection of human rights, civil rights, civil liberties and political freedoms for all citizens.

Features

Essential features include universal suffrage granting all adult citizens the right to vote regardless of wealth, gender or race; free and fair elections held at regular intervals; freedom of speech, the press, assembly and association; an independent judiciary; and constitutional limits on government power, often codified in a bill of rights. Liberal democracies may be republics, such as the United States and France, or constitutional monarchies, such as the United Kingdom, Spain and Japan.

Origins

Liberal democracy emerged from Enlightenment ideas about individual rights and government by consent, developed by thinkers such as John Locke and Montesquieu. Its institutional foundations were laid by events including the English Bill of Rights of 1689, the American Revolution and the French Revolution, though full democratisation — including women's suffrage — generally came only in the late 19th and 20th centuries.

Criticism

Critics have argued that liberal democracies can be distorted by money in politics, low turnout, media concentration and majoritarian abuses — concerns captured by the term "illiberal democracy" for regimes holding elections while eroding rights and checks and balances. Marxist critics have historically characterised liberal democracy as a facade for capitalist class rule, while defenders regard it as the system least prone to tyranny and best able to correct itself peacefully.
