Drive-in theater

A drive-in theater (or drive-in cinema) is a form of cinema structure consisting of a large outdoor movie screen, a projection booth, a concession stand, and a large parking area for automobiles, within which audience members view movies from the privacy and comfort of their cars.

History

Richard M. Hollingshead Jr. patented the drive-in theater and opened the first one on June 6, 1933, in Pennsauken Township, New Jersey (often identified with nearby Camden). Hollingshead marketed the theater to families, noting that children could be noisy without disturbing others and that patrons need not dress up. The concept spread slowly before World War II, then boomed in the postwar years as car ownership surged and suburbs grew. At the peak in the late 1950s there were more than 4,000 drive-ins in the United States.

Operation

Cars park on ramps that tilt them slightly upward toward the screen. Audio was originally delivered by speakers mounted on poles at each space; in later years most theaters adopted low-power AM or FM transmission to car radios. Screenings begin at dusk, and double features were a common attraction. Concession sales of popcorn, hot dogs, and soft drinks provided much of the profit.

Decline and revival

From the 1970s, rising real-estate values, the adoption of daylight saving time (which pushed start times later), indoor multiplexes, and home video caused most drive-ins to close; many sites were redeveloped as shopping centres or housing. By the 2010s only a few hundred remained in the United States, though surviving theaters converted to digital projection. In 2020, drive-ins experienced a notable revival as one of the few forms of public entertainment compatible with pandemic social distancing, and some were used for concerts, graduations, and religious services.
