Mamluk Sultanate

Mamluk Sultanate
Capital: Cairo
Existed: 1250–1517
Language: Arabic (official), Mamluk-Kipchak Turkic
Religion: Sunni Islam

The Mamluk Sultanate (Arabic: sultanat al-Mamalik) was a medieval realm spanning Egypt, the Levant, and western Arabia (the Hejaz) from 1250 until its conquest by the Ottoman Empire in 1517. It was ruled by the Mamluks, a warrior class of manumitted slave-soldiers, most of Turkic (especially Kipchak) and later Circassian origin, who had converted to Islam and received military training.

Origins

The Mamluks rose to power in the Ayyubid state founded by Saladin. After the death of the Ayyubid sultan as-Salih Ayyub in 1249 during the Seventh Crusade, his Mamluk officers defeated the crusaders at Mansura and, in 1250, seized the sultanate itself, installing Shajar al-Durr briefly before Aybak became sultan. Historians divide the era into the Bahri period (1250–1382), dominated by Kipchak Turks, and the Burji period (1382–1517), dominated by Circassians.

Height of power

Sultan Baybars (r. 1260–1277) and his successors repelled the Mongol Ilkhanate at the Battle of Ain Jalut (1260) and progressively eliminated the Crusader states, taking Acre in 1291. Cairo became one of the great cities of the Islamic world, enriched by the spice trade between the Indian Ocean and the Mediterranean. The Mamluks patronized monumental architecture, including mosques, madrasas, and mausoleums that still define Cairo's skyline, and administered the holy cities of Mecca and Medina.

Decline and fall

From the late 14th century the sultanate suffered from plague, Timur's invasion of Syria (1400–1401), factional strife, and economic strain as the Portuguese opened a sea route around Africa (1498), eroding Egypt's transit trade. War with the rising Ottoman Empire ended in defeat at Marj Dabiq (1516) and Ridaniya (1517); the last sultan, Tuman Bay II, was executed in Cairo. Egypt became an Ottoman province, though Mamluk beys remained a powerful class in Egypt until their massacre by Muhammad Ali in 1811.

Legacy

The Mamluk era profoundly shaped Egyptian and Levantine architecture, historiography, and the furusiyya tradition of cavalry arts. The biographical and chronicle literature produced under Mamluk patronage remains a primary source for medieval Middle Eastern history.
