Greek Orthodox Church

The Greek Orthodox Church (Greek: Ελληνορθόδοξη Εκκλησία) is the body of several churches within the larger communion of Eastern Orthodox Christianity whose liturgy is traditionally conducted in Koine Greek, the original language of the New Testament. The term is sometimes used broadly for the whole Eastern Orthodox Church, and more specifically for the autocephalous churches of Greece, Cyprus, and the ancient Patriarchates of Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem.

History

Christianity spread early in the Greek-speaking world; Saint Paul preached in Athens, Corinth, and Thessalonica in the 1st century. The church formed part of the Byzantine world centred on Constantinople, and its theology was shaped by the Seven Ecumenical Councils. The East–West Schism of 1054 separated it from the Roman Catholic Church.

Under Ottoman rule, the Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople was recognised as leader (millet-bashi) of the Orthodox Christians in the empire. After Greek independence, the Church of Greece declared itself autocephalous in 1833, a status recognised by Constantinople in 1850.

Structure

The Greek Orthodox churches are organised as independent (autocephalous) or autonomous bodies, each governed by a holy synod of bishops. The Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople holds a primacy of honour among Eastern Orthodox patriarchs. Monasteries, notably the monastic community of Mount Athos, occupy a central place in Greek Orthodox spiritual life.

Liturgy and practice

Worship centres on the Divine Liturgy of Saint John Chrysostom, celebrated in Greek with chanting in the Byzantine tradition. Icons, fasting periods such as Great Lent, and the feasts of the liturgical year—above all Easter (Pascha)—structure devotional life. The church claims roughly 10 million members in Greece and a worldwide diaspora, including the Greek Orthodox Archdiocese of America.
