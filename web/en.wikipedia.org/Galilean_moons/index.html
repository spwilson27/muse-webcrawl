The Galilean moons are the four largest moons of Jupiter—Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto—first seen by Galileo Galilei in December 1609 or January 1610, and recognised by him as orbiting Jupiter in March 1610. They are among the largest objects in the Solar System outside the Sun and the eight planets, with radii larger than any of the dwarf planets, and are some of the most massive objects in the Solar System outside the Sun and the eight planets in terms of orbital radius.

Galileo published his observations in the Sidereus Nuncius (1610), naming the moons the "Medicean stars" after his patron Cosimo II de' Medici. The names Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto, proposed by Simon Marius, came into general use in the 20th century. The discovery of bodies orbiting something other than Earth was a major blow to the geocentric model of the Solar System.

The moons

Io, the innermost, is the most volcanically active body in the Solar System, its surface continually resurfaced by silicate volcanoes driven by tidal flexing. Europa has a smooth, fractured ice crust widely believed to cover a global saltwater ocean, making it a prime target in the search for extraterrestrial life. Ganymede is the largest moon in the Solar System—larger than the planet Mercury—and the only moon known to generate its own internal magnetic field; it too is thought to harbour a subsurface ocean. Callisto, the outermost, has the oldest and most heavily cratered surface of any body in the Solar System and may also conceal an ocean.

Orbital resonance

Io, Europa and Ganymede are locked in a 4:2:1 Laplace resonance: for every four orbits of Io, Europa completes exactly two and Ganymede one. The resonance maintains the eccentricity of their orbits and hence the tidal heating that powers Io's volcanism and keeps Europa's ocean liquid.

Exploration

The moons were visited by Pioneer 10 and 11, Voyager 1 and 2, the Galileo orbiter (1995–2003), Cassini, New Horizons and Juno. The European Space Agency's JUICE spacecraft, launched in April 2023, will enter orbit around Ganymede in the 2030s, while NASA's Europa Clipper, launched in October 2024, will study Europa in detail.
