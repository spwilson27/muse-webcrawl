The Sandia Mountains (Southern Tiwa: O:ku:p'į, "green place") are a mountain range in the south-western United States, immediately east of Albuquerque, New Mexico. Their name is Spanish for "watermelon", a reference to the pink colour of the western escarpment at sunset, produced by abundant potassium feldspar in the granite. The range is a fault-block mountain forming the eastern edge of the Rio Grande rift.

Geography

The range runs roughly north–south for about 17 miles (27 km). Its highest point, Sandia Crest, reaches 10,678 feet (3,255 m) and is accessible by road (NM 536) and by the Sandia Peak Tramway, an aerial tramway about 2.7 miles (4.3 km) long — among the longest passenger tramways in the world — which climbs from the north-eastern edge of Albuquerque. The milder eastern slopes support ponderosa pine forest; the steep western face rises abruptly from the Rio Grande valley in rugged canyons.

Geology and ecology

The Sandias are composed largely of Precambrian granite and metamorphic rock about 1.4 billion years old, capped by Pennsylvanian limestone. Uplift along the rift-margin fault began roughly 10 million years ago. Vegetation ranges from desert grassland and piñon–juniper woodland at the base to spruce–fir forest near the crest. The protected Sandia Mountain Wilderness covers much of the range within Cibola National Forest, and the foothills are a popular recreation area for hiking, climbing and paragliding.

History

The mountains contain important archaeological sites, most famously Sandia Cave, excavated in the 1930s–40s, where early artefacts were claimed (controversially) to date to the last Ice Age. Pueblo peoples have long regarded the range as sacred. Today the crest hosts communications towers, a visitor centre and the Sandia Peak ski area.
