American Renaissance (literature)

In American literature, the American Renaissance was the period from the 1820s or 1830s to the Civil War, in which American literature came of age as an expression of a national spirit. The term was popularised by F. O. Matthiessen's 1941 study American Renaissance: Art and Expression in the Age of Emerson and Whitman.

Overview

Centered in New England, the movement is associated with the five authors Matthiessen treated in depth: Ralph Waldo Emerson, Henry David Thoreau, Nathaniel Hawthorne, Herman Melville, and Walt Whitman. Between 1850 and 1855 these writers produced an extraordinary concentration of classics: Emerson's Representative Men, Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter and The House of the Seven Gables, Melville's Moby-Dick, Thoreau's Walden, and Whitman's Leaves of Grass. The period also saw major work by Edgar Allan Poe, Emily Dickinson, Frederick Douglass, Harriet Beecher Stowe, and Longfellow.

Themes and criticism

American Renaissance writing explores democracy, individualism, nature, and the tensions between the young republic's ideals and slavery. Since the 1970s critics have questioned Matthiessen's canon, noting its exclusion of women writers such as Stowe, whose Uncle Tom's Cabin outsold every other book of the era, and of African American voices, and have broadened the field to include popular and sentimental literature.
