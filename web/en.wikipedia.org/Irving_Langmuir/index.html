Irving Langmuir

Irving Langmuir (31 January 1881 – 16 August 1957) was an American chemist and physicist who worked for most of his career at the General Electric research laboratory in Schenectady, New York. He won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1932 for his work in surface chemistry, becoming one of the few industrial scientists so honoured.

Contributions

Langmuir improved the incandescent lamp by filling it with inert gas and developed the gas-filled tungsten lamp, invented the atomic hydrogen welding torch, and contributed to the theory of chemical bonding with the octet concept. In surface chemistry he studied adsorption of molecules in monolayers, giving his name to the Langmuir isotherm and the Langmuir–Blodgett trough. He also conducted pioneering work in plasma physics, coining the term "plasma", and later in atmospheric science, including early experiments in cloud seeding.

Pathological science

In a famous 1953 talk, Langmuir coined the phrase "pathological science" for research in which scientists are misled by self-deception and wishful thinking, citing examples such as N-rays and polywater.
