Multiple organ dysfunction syndrome

Specialty: Critical care medicine
Symptoms: Fever, altered mentation, hypotension, organ failure
Complications: Death
Causes: Sepsis, trauma, burns, pancreatitis, shock

Multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), also known as multiple organ failure, is the progressive, potentially reversible failure of two or more organ systems arising from a life-threatening illness or injury. It represents the severe end of the spectrum of systemic illness and is one of the leading causes of death in intensive care units worldwide.

Causes

MODS may develop as a primary response to an insult such as severe trauma, major burns, or pancreatitis, or secondarily as part of the systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) triggered by sepsis, shock, or massive transfusion. Infection is the most common underlying driver, and sepsis-associated MODS carries a particularly poor prognosis.

Pathophysiology

An overwhelming or dysregulated inflammatory response damages the vascular endothelium, activates coagulation, and impairs tissue perfusion. Mediators such as tumour necrosis factor and interleukins promote microvascular thrombosis and capillary leak, while mitochondria become unable to use delivered oxygen, so organs fail even when blood flow is restored. Gut barrier failure may permit bacterial translocation that sustains the inflammatory cycle.

Diagnosis and scoring

Organ dysfunction is quantified with scoring systems. The Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score grades six systems — respiratory, coagulation, hepatic, cardiovascular, central nervous system, and renal — using variables such as the PaO2/FiO2 ratio, platelet count, bilirubin, blood pressure, Glasgow Coma Scale, and creatinine. The quick SOFA (qSOFA) and earlier MODS and Logistic Organ Dysfunction scores are also used. Rising scores correlate strongly with mortality.

Treatment

There is no specific drug therapy for MODS. Management is supportive and directed at the cause: early antibiotics and source control for infection, haemodynamic resuscitation, lung-protective mechanical ventilation, renal replacement therapy when indicated, glycaemic control, and nutritional support. Prevention through early treatment of the precipitating condition gives the best outcomes; mortality rises steeply with each additional failing organ, exceeding 50–80% when four or more systems fail.
