German Renaissance

The German Renaissance, part of the Northern Renaissance, was a cultural and artistic movement that spread among German thinkers and artists between roughly 1430 and 1550. It developed from the Italian Renaissance through trade, the universities, and the new technology of printing, while retaining strong Gothic and local traditions. Many German courts and free imperial cities became centres of art, humanist scholarship, and science.

Art

The dominant figure was Albrecht Dürer of Nuremberg (1471–1528), whose woodcuts and engravings — including the Apocalypse series, Melencolia I, and Knight, Death and the Devil — and whose treatises on proportion and perspective made German art famous across Europe. Other major artists included Matthias Grünewald, painter of the harrowing Isenheim Altarpiece; Lucas Cranach the Elder, court painter at Wittenberg and portraitist of the Reformation leaders; Hans Holbein the Younger, famed for his portraits and the Dance of Death woodcuts; and the sculptors Tilman Riemenschneider and Veit Stoss.

Humanism and printing

Johannes Gutenberg's movable-type printing press (c. 1440) at Mainz revolutionised the spread of texts and was itself a German Renaissance engine. Humanists such as Johannes Reuchlin, Conrad Celtis, Willibald Pirckheimer, and the visiting Erasmus shaped a scholarly culture that fed directly into the Reformation of Martin Luther — a Wittenberg contemporary of Cranach — which in many accounts marks the movement's end.

Architecture and science

Renaissance forms entered architecture gradually, as in the Fugger Chapel at Augsburg (1509–1512) and Heidelberg Castle's Ottheinrichsbau. In science, Regiomontanus advanced astronomy and printing of mathematical works at Nuremberg, and Nicolaus Copernicus, working in Royal Prussia, published his heliocentric De revolutionibus in 1543.
