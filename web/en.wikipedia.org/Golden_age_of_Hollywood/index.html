Golden age of Hollywood

The golden age of Hollywood was a period in the history of American cinema, generally dated from the late 1920s (the advent of sound) through the late 1940s or 1950s, during which the major film studios dominated production, distribution and exhibition, and the star system made actors into global celebrities. It is associated with a polished, classical style of narrative filmmaking.

The studio system

Five major studios — Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, Paramount, Warner Bros., 20th Century Fox and RKO — together with three smaller ones (Universal, Columbia and United Artists), controlled the industry. Actors, directors and writers worked under long-term contracts; stars such as Clark Gable, Humphrey Bogart, Katharine Hepburn, Bette Davis, James Stewart and Marilyn Monroe were carefully groomed and typecast. Production was governed by the Motion Picture Production Code (the "Hays Code"), enforced from 1934.

Achievements

The era produced many films now considered classics, including Gone with the Wind and The Wizard of Oz (both 1939), Casablanca (1942), It's a Wonderful Life (1946) and the film noir cycle. Weekly cinema attendance in the United States peaked in 1946 at around 90 million admissions.

Decline

The system's pillars crumbled after 1948, when the Supreme Court's decision in United States v. Paramount Pictures, Inc. forced the majors to sell their theatre chains. The rise of television in the 1950s, suburbanisation, and loosening censorship further eroded the studios' dominance, and by the 1960s the classical era had given way to the New Hollywood.
