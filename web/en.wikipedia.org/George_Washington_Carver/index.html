George Washington Carver (c. 1864 – January 5, 1943) was an American agricultural scientist and inventor who promoted alternative crops to cotton and methods to prevent soil depletion. He was one of the most prominent Black scientists of the early 20th century.

Early life

Carver was born into slavery in Missouri. After emancipation he pursued education despite racial barriers, eventually earning bachelor's and master's degrees at Iowa State, where he became the university's first Black faculty member.

Tuskegee

In 1896 Booker T. Washington invited Carver to head the agriculture department at the Tuskegee Institute in Alabama, where he remained for 47 years. He taught poor farmers to rotate crops such as peanuts, soybeans and sweet potatoes to restore nitrogen to soils exhausted by cotton, and published bulletins with recipes and uses for the new crops.

Legacy

Contrary to popular myth, Carver did not invent peanut butter, but he compiled hundreds of uses for peanuts and other crops. He became a celebrated figure in his lifetime, advising presidents and testifying before Congress. He died in 1943 and is buried at Tuskegee.
