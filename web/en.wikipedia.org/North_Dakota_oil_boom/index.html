North Dakota oil boom

The North Dakota oil boom was a period of rapidly expanding oil extraction from the Bakken Formation in the state between about 2006 and 2015. Advances in hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling made the tight oil of the Bakken economically recoverable, and North Dakota rose to become the second-largest oil-producing state in the United States, behind only Texas.

Background

The Bakken Formation, a rock unit of the Williston Basin underlying parts of North Dakota, Montana, Saskatchewan, and Manitoba, was known for decades to hold enormous quantities of oil, but the rock's low permeability frustrated production. The United States Geological Survey estimated billions of barrels of technically recoverable oil. The combination of horizontal wells and multi-stage hydraulic fracturing, pioneered in the early 2000s, unlocked the resource.

Boom and bust

Production climbed from under 100,000 barrels per day in the mid-2000s to more than 1 million barrels per day by 2014. Towns such as Williston, Watford City, and Dickinson grew explosively; housing shortages spawned "man camps," wages soared, and the state accumulated a large budget surplus. The boom slowed sharply when world oil prices collapsed in 2014–2016, though production later recovered to around or above 1.1 million barrels per day.

Effects

The boom brought jobs and wealth but also strains on infrastructure, increased traffic accidents, environmental concerns over flaring and spills, and social pressures on the Fort Berthold Indian Reservation, where much drilling occurred on lands of the Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara Nation.
