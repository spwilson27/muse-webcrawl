Real number

In mathematics, a real number is a number that can be used to measure a continuous one-dimensional quantity such as distance, duration or temperature. Real numbers are thought of as points on the number line and include the rational numbers (fractions and integers) and the irrational numbers (such as √2 and π). The set of real numbers is denoted ℝ.

Properties

The reals form a complete ordered field: they can be added, subtracted, multiplied and divided (except by zero), they are totally ordered, and — the defining completeness property — every non-empty set bounded above has a least upper bound. This property distinguishes ℝ from ℚ and underpins limits, continuity and calculus. The set of real numbers is uncountably infinite, as Cantor's diagonal argument shows, so there are "more" reals than rationals.

Construction

Rigorous definitions were developed in the 19th century: Dedekind constructed the reals as cuts of the rationals, while Cantor and others used equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences of rationals. Historically, quantities corresponding to irrational numbers were known to Greek and Indian mathematicians, but the unified concept of the real line crystallised with the work of Stevin, Descartes, and the arithmetisation of analysis by Weierstrass, Dedekind and Cantor.

Applications and extensions

Real numbers model continuous physical quantities throughout science. Algebraically they extend to the complex numbers by adjoining i = √−1; the reals also have extensions such as the hyperreal numbers used in non-standard analysis.
