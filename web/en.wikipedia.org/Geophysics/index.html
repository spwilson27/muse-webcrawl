Geophysics is a subject of natural science concerned with the physical processes and physical properties of the Earth and its surrounding space environment, and the use of quantitative methods for their analysis. Geophysics is a highly interdisciplinary subject, drawing on physics, mathematics, geology, and computer science.

Geophysicists study phenomena including gravity, geomagnetism, seismic waves, the Earth's internal structure and dynamics, plate tectonics, the generation of the magnetic field in the core (the geodynamo), and the physical properties of rocks and minerals. Geophysics contributes to society's needs in mineral and hydrocarbon exploration, groundwater location, natural hazard mitigation (earthquakes, tsunamis, volcanoes), and understanding climate-relevant processes such as ice-sheet dynamics.

Branches

Major branches include seismology (the study of earthquakes and elastic waves, which revealed the layered structure of crust, mantle, liquid outer core and solid inner core), geodesy (the shape and gravitational field of the Earth), geomagnetism and paleomagnetism (key evidence for seafloor spreading and plate tectonics), gravimetry, geothermics, and exploration geophysics, which applies seismic reflection, resistivity, and potential-field methods to locate resources near the surface.

Methods

Geophysical methods are mostly remote and non-destructive: measurements may be made at or above the surface, from aircraft and satellites, or in boreholes. Inverse theory is used to infer subsurface properties from the observed data. Modern practice relies heavily on large sensor networks, global satellite missions such as GRACE (gravity) and Swarm (magnetic field), and high-performance computing.

History

Although compasses and early seismoscopes were known in ancient China, geophysics emerged as a distinct discipline in the 19th century with systematic magnetic surveys and the mathematical analysis of Earth's gravity and magnetism by figures such as Carl Friedrich Gauss and Alexander von Humboldt. The International Geophysical Year (1957–58) and the plate tectonics revolution of the 1960s transformed the field.
