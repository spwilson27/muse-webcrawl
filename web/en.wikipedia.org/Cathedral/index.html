Cathedral

Named forLatin cathedra, "chair" (of the bishop)
FunctionSeat of a bishop; principal church of a diocese
Notable examplesNotre-Dame de Paris, Cologne, Chartres, St Peter's Basilica (not a cathedral in form but the papal church)

A cathedral is a church that contains the cathedra (Latin for 'chair') of a bishop, thus serving as the central church of a diocese, conference, or episcopate. Cathedrals are found in Christian denominations with an episcopal hierarchy, such as the Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, and Lutheran churches. The cathedral is often among the largest and most architecturally significant buildings of its city or region.


A Gothic cathedral, the characteristic great church of medieval Europe

History

As Christianity was legalised and organised under the Roman Empire, bishops presided from basilicas in the principal cities. In the Middle Ages, cathedral building became one of the largest collective enterprises of European society: Romanesque cathedrals with massive walls and round arches gave way, from the twelfth century, to Gothic cathedrals such as Chartres, Reims and Cologne, with pointed arches, rib vaults, flying buttresses, and vast stained-glass windows. Construction often took centuries and was financed by bishops, chapters, civic authorities, and pilgrims drawn by relics.

Organisation

A cathedral is administered by its dean and chapter of canons. Beyond daily worship, cathedrals historically housed schools and libraries, administered diocesan justice, and served as centres of music — many maintain famous choirs and organs. In the Church of England, parish churches that later became cathedrals retain some parish functions, and some are known as minsters.

Cathedrals today

Many cathedrals are major tourist attractions and concert venues as well as places of worship. New cathedrals continue to be built, including modernist landmarks such as Coventry Cathedral (consecrated 1962) and Gaudí's still-unfinished Sagrada Família in Barcelona.
