Cellular Respiration

Respiration converts fuel energy into ATP — phosphoric acid's most vital role.

Cellular respiration is the set of metabolic processes by which cells extract energy from nutrients and store it in ATP. Its chemistry is at heart phosphate chemistry: energy is captured whenever inorganic phosphate (from phosphoric acid equilibria in the cell) is bonded to ADP, and spent whenever ATP is hydrolysed.

Stages

Glycolysis, in the cytosol, splits glucose to pyruvate, netting 2 ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation. The Krebs cycle in the mitochondrial matrix completes the oxidation to CO2, loading the carriers NADH and FADH2. In oxidative phosphorylation, the electron transport chain pumps protons across the inner membrane, and ATP synthase uses the returning flow to make roughly 26–28 further ATP per glucose.

Efficiency and alternatives

Aerobic respiration yields up to about 32 ATP per glucose. Without oxygen, cells fall back on fermentation, regenerating NAD+ while making only the 2 glycolytic ATP — explaining the burn of lactic acid in sprinting muscle. The phosphate bond economy of respiration links the cell to the planetary phosphorus cycle: every ATP ever made traces its phosphate to weathered rock.
