Fertilizer

TypesSynthetic (mineral) and organic (manure, compost, bone meal)

A fertilizer (American English) or fertiliser (British English) is any material of natural or synthetic origin that is applied to soil or to plant tissues to supply plant nutrients. Fertilizers may be distinct from liming materials or other non-nutrient soil amendments. Modern chemical fertilizers are a foundation of the high crop yields that support roughly half of the world's population.

Nutrients

Plants require three primary macronutrients — nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium — conventionally declared on fertilizer labels as the NPK ratio, plus secondary nutrients (calcium, magnesium, sulfur) and trace micronutrients such as iron, zinc, and boron. Nitrogen promotes leafy growth; phosphorus supports roots, flowering, and energy transfer (it is a component of ATP and of nucleic acids); potassium regulates water and enzyme activity.

Production

Nitrogen fertilizers are made from ammonia synthesized by the Haber–Bosch process from atmospheric nitrogen and hydrogen (usually from natural gas). Phosphate fertilizers are produced by treating mined phosphate rock with sulfuric or phosphoric acid to make superphosphates. Potassium fertilizers come from mined potash deposits. Organic fertilizers include animal manure, compost, and bone meal, which release nutrients more slowly.

Environmental issues

Excess fertilizer runs off fields into waterways, causing eutrophication and algal blooms, and contributes to emissions of the greenhouse gas nitrous oxide. Precision application, slow-release formulations, and nutrient management planning aim to retain the yield benefits of fertilizers while reducing these harms.
