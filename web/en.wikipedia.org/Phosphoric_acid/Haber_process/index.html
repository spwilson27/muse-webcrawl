Haber Process

The Haber plant: nitrogen's partner to phosphoric acid in modern fertilizer.

The Haber process (Haber–Bosch process) synthesizes ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen over an iron catalyst at high pressure and temperature. Its connection to phosphoric acid is the fertilizer bag: modern agriculture applies nitrogen and phosphorus together, and compound NPK fertilizers are made by treating phosphate rock with acids and neutralizing phosphoric acid with Haber ammonia to produce ammonium phosphates such as MAP and DAP — among the most widely used fertilizers on Earth.

The process

Fritz Haber demonstrated ammonia synthesis from the elements in 1909; Carl Bosch scaled it to industry at BASF by 1913. Natural gas is reformed to hydrogen, and the mixture reacts reversibly (N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3) at 150–300 bar and about 450 °C. Roughly 1–2% of world energy consumption feeds the process.

The fertilizer partnership

Nitrogen and phosphorus limit crop growth together. Phosphate rock is digested — often with sulfuric acid to make phosphoric acid directly — and the acid is ammoniated with Haber ammonia to form mono- and diammonium phosphate granules that deliver both nutrients in one product. Mismanaged, the same partnership drives eutrophication, when both nutrients wash into waterways. Vaclav Smil estimated that Haber nitrogen sustains roughly half of humanity; phosphoric acid feeds the other half of the partnership through the phosphorus cycle.
