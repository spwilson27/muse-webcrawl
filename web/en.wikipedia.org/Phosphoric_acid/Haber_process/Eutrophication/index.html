Eutrophication

Eutrophication is a general term describing a process in which nutrients accumulate in a body of water, resulting in an increased growth of microorganisms that depletes the oxygen of water. Eutrophication is often induced by the discharge of phosphate-containing detergents, fertilizers, or sewage into an aquatic system. Depending on the degree of eutrophication, subsequent negative environmental effects such as anoxia (oxygen depletion) and severe reductions in water quality may occur, affecting fish and other animal populations.

Causes

The primary limiting nutrients for eutrophication are phosphorus and nitrogen. In freshwater systems, phosphorus is usually the limiting nutrient, while in many marine systems nitrogen is limiting. Sources include agricultural runoff of synthetic fertilizers — much of the nitrogen supplied by the Haber process — as well as manure, sewage effluent, industrial discharges, and atmospheric deposition.

Effects

Excess nutrients stimulate dense blooms of algae and cyanobacteria. When the blooms die, microbial decomposition consumes dissolved oxygen, creating hypoxic or anoxic conditions that can kill fish and benthic organisms, producing so-called dead zones such as the one in the Gulf of Mexico at the mouth of the Mississippi River. Some algal blooms also produce toxins harmful to animals and humans.

Prevention and reversal

Control strategies include reducing fertilizer application to match crop needs, restoring wetlands and riparian buffers that intercept runoff, improving sewage treatment to remove phosphorus and nitrogen, and restricting phosphate detergents.
