Phosphorus cycle

The phosphorus cycle is the biogeochemical cycle that involves the movement of phosphorus through the lithosphere, hydrosphere and biosphere. Unlike many other biogeochemical cycles, the atmosphere does not play a significant role in the movement of phosphorus, because phosphorus and phosphorus-based compounds are usually solids at the typical ranges of temperature and pressure found on Earth. Therefore, the phosphorus cycle is the slowest of the biogeochemical cycles.

Phosphorus is an essential element for all life; it forms part of DNA, RNA, ATP, and the phospholipids that compose cell membranes. It is also a key component of fertilizer, and shortages or excesses of phosphorus have major agricultural and ecological consequences.

Phosphorus in the environment

The largest reservoir of phosphorus is in sedimentary rocks, particularly apatite-bearing formations. Weathering slowly releases phosphate from rocks into soils and waterways. Plants absorb phosphate from the soil, and animals obtain phosphorus by eating plants or other animals. When organisms die and decompose, phosphorus is returned to the soil or carried into rivers, lakes, and eventually the ocean, where it is deposited as sediment and over geological time uplifted again as rock.

Eutrophication

Because phosphorus is often the limiting nutrient in freshwater ecosystems, even small additions can trigger eutrophication: rapid growth of algae and cyanobacteria, followed by decomposition that depletes dissolved oxygen. Major human sources of added phosphorus include phosphate fertilizer runoff, manure, detergents, and sewage.
