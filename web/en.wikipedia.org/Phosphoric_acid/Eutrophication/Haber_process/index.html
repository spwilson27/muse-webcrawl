Haber process

The Haber process, also called the Haber–Bosch process, is the main industrial procedure for the production of ammonia. It converts atmospheric nitrogen (N2) to ammonia (NH3) by a reaction with hydrogen (H2) using an iron metal catalyst under high temperatures and pressures:

N2 + 3 H2 ⇌ 2 NH3   ΔH° = −92.4 kJ/mol

The German chemist Fritz Haber developed the process in 1909, and Carl Bosch scaled it up to industrial production at BASF by 1913. The ammonia produced is primarily used to make fertilizers, which support the growth of agricultural plants on which roughly half of the world's population depends for food; it is also used in the manufacture of explosives and other chemicals.

Process conditions

The reaction is performed at temperatures of 400–500 °C and pressures of 150–250 atmospheres over an iron catalyst, often promoted with potassium and aluminium oxides. Because the reaction is exothermic and reversible, only around 15% of the nitrogen and hydrogen is converted per pass; unreacted gases are recycled, giving an overall conversion of about 97%. The hydrogen is typically obtained from natural gas by steam reforming, making ammonia production a significant consumer of fossil fuels and a source of carbon dioxide emissions.

Environmental significance

The widespread application of synthetic nitrogen fertilizers from the Haber process, together with phosphate fertilizers, has increased nutrient runoff into waterways. This excess nitrogen and phosphorus contributes to eutrophication of lakes, rivers, and coastal seas.
