Eutrophication

An algal bloom: the visible consequence of too much phosphate in the water.

Eutrophication is the enrichment of an ecosystem with chemical nutrients, most critically phosphate — the ion of phosphoric acid — which in fresh waters is usually the limiting nutrient for plant growth. Runoff from phosphate-fertilized fields, manure, and sewage overwhelms the natural phosphorus cycle, triggering explosive algal growth.

Process and effects

Algal blooms shade submerged plants and, when the algae die, microbial decomposition consumes dissolved oxygen, creating hypoxic "dead zones" that kill fish and invertebrates. In the Gulf of Mexico, Mississippi-borne nutrients sustain a seasonal dead zone thousands of square kilometres in extent; Lake Erie and the Baltic Sea suffer similarly. Some blooms produce toxins harmful to livestock, wildlife, and people.

Control

Because phosphorus, unlike nitrogen, cannot be fixed from the air by organisms, controlling phosphate inputs is decisive: removing phosphate from detergents (a major reform of the 1970s–90s), treating wastewater to precipitate phosphorus, buffering fields with wetlands, and timing fertilizer — much of it manufactured from phosphate rock via processes related to the Haber process era of industrial agriculture — to crop need. Recovery of phosphorus from sewage as struvite both curbs eutrophication and recycles a finite resource.
