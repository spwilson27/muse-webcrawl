Phosphorus

Phosphorus is a chemical element with the symbol P and atomic number 15. Elemental phosphorus exists in two major forms, white phosphorus and red phosphorus, but because it is highly reactive, phosphorus is never found as a free element on Earth. It has a concentration in the Earth's crust of about one gram per kilogram. Phosphorus is essential for life, being a key component of DNA, RNA, ATP, and the phospholipids that form cell membranes.

History

Phosphorus was discovered in 1669 by the German alchemist Hennig Brand, who isolated it from urine while searching for the philosopher's stone. He kept the process secret, but it was later independently discovered by other chemists. The element's name comes from the Greek word phosphoros, meaning "light-bearer", because white phosphorus glows when exposed to oxygen.

Allotropes

Phosphorus occurs in several allotropic forms. White phosphorus consists of tetrahedral P4 molecules; it is waxy, highly toxic, pyrophoric, and glows in the dark. Red phosphorus is polymeric, much less reactive, and is used on the striking surfaces of matchboxes. Black phosphorus, the most thermodynamically stable form at room temperature, has a layered structure resembling graphite.

Biological role

Phosphorus is vital to all known forms of life. Phosphate groups form the backbone of DNA and RNA, and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is the principal carrier of chemical energy in cells. Bones and teeth are largely composed of calcium phosphate in the form of hydroxyapatite.

Production and uses

Phosphorus is obtained from phosphate rock, mined mainly in Morocco, China, and the United States. Most phosphorus produced is converted to phosphoric acid, used in fertilisers, detergents, and food additives. Elemental white phosphorus has military applications in tracer ammunition, smoke screens, and incendiary devices.
