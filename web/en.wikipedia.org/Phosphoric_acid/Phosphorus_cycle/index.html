Phosphorus Cycle

Phosphoric acid manufacture short-circuits the slow geological phosphorus cycle.

The phosphorus cycle is the biogeochemical movement of phosphorus through rock, soil, water, and living tissue. Viewed through the lens of phosphoric acid, the cycle's modern drama is industrial: humanity mines phosphate rock, dissolves it to make phosphoric acid for fertilizer, and thereby doubles the flow of phosphorus from land to sea — with phosphoric acid plants as the valve between the geological reservoir and the living world.

The industrial shortcut

In nature, phosphate reaches soils only as fast as rain and acid weather apatite out of rock — a trickle over millennia — and returns to rock only when ocean sediments are buried and uplifted. The wet process of phosphoric acid manufacture (apatite + sulfuric acid) performs the same chemistry in hours. Its by-product, phosphogypsum, stacks up in vast piles, while the acid goes to fertilizers, food additives, and detergents.

Consequences

Accelerated flows enrich fields but leak: phosphorus runs off into waters, driving eutrophication, and much ends in ocean sediments, effectively lost for millions of years. Because there is no atmospheric reservoir and no substitute for phosphorus in DNA, ATP, and bone, concerns about "peak phosphorus" motivate recycling from manure and sewage.
