Eutrophication

Eutrophication is a general term describing a process in which nutrients accumulate in a body of water, resulting in an increased growth of microorganisms that depletes the oxygen of water. Eutrophication is often induced by the discharge of phosphate-containing detergents, fertilizers, or sewage into an aquatic system. Depending on the degree of eutrophication, subsequent negative environmental effects such as anoxia (oxygen depletion) and severe reductions in water quality may occur, affecting fish and other animal populations.

Phosphorus and eutrophication

In most freshwater lakes, phosphorus is the nutrient in shortest supply relative to algal demand, so it controls the rate of eutrophication. The accelerated movement of phosphorus through the phosphorus cycle — from mined phosphate rock, through fertilizers and livestock manure, into runoff — is the principal reason many lakes and reservoirs have shifted from clear, oligotrophic conditions to turbid, eutrophic ones.

Effects

Excess nutrients stimulate dense blooms of algae and cyanobacteria. When the blooms die, microbial decomposition consumes dissolved oxygen, creating hypoxic or anoxic conditions that can kill fish and benthic organisms, producing so-called dead zones. Some algal blooms also produce toxins harmful to animals and humans.

Prevention and reversal

Control strategies include reducing phosphorus fertilizer and manure application to match crop needs, restoring wetlands and riparian buffers, and improving sewage treatment to remove phosphorus.
