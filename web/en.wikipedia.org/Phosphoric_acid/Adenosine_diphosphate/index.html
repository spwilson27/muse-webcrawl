Adenosine Diphosphate

ADP: adenosine bearing two phosphoric-acid-derived phosphate groups.

Adenosine diphosphate (ADP) is an organic compound central to energy metabolism, consisting of adenosine — adenine joined to ribose — esterified to a chain of two phosphate groups, the anhydrides of phosphoric acid. ADP is the discharged form of the cell's energy currency: ATP synthase recharges it to ATP by attaching a third phosphate, and hydrolysis of ATP back to ADP releases the energy that powers cellular work.

The ATP–ADP cycle

In cellular respiration, oxidation of glucose and other fuels drives ADP phosphorylation; in photosynthesis, photophosphorylation does the same using light. The ratio of ATP to ADP signals the cell's energy state and regulates metabolic pathways: high ADP activates glycolysis and respiration, while accumulating AMP (adenosine monophosphate) triggers still stronger energy-conservation responses through AMP-activated protein kinase.

Other roles

ADP released by damaged cells and activated platelets is a potent signal for platelet aggregation — receptors for ADP (P2Y12) are the targets of antiplatelet drugs such as clopidogrel. ADP-ribosylation, the transfer of ADP-ribose onto proteins, is a regulatory modification exploited by bacterial toxins including cholera and diphtheria toxins.
