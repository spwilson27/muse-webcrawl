Phosphorus cycle

The phosphorus cycle is the biogeochemical cycle that involves the movement of phosphorus through the lithosphere, hydrosphere and biosphere. Unlike many other biogeochemical cycles, the atmosphere does not play a significant role in the movement of phosphorus, because phosphorus and phosphorus-based compounds are usually solids at the typical ranges of temperature and pressure found on Earth. Therefore, the phosphorus cycle is the slowest of the biogeochemical cycles.

Phosphorus is an essential element for all life; it forms part of DNA, RNA, ATP, and the phospholipids that compose cell membranes. It is also a key component of fertilizer, and shortages or excesses of phosphorus have major agricultural and ecological consequences.

Manure in the cycle

Livestock manure is an important vehicle of phosphorus transfer: animals consume phosphorus in feed, and much of it is excreted and returned to fields as manure. Where livestock operations import feed from distant regions, phosphorus accumulates in local soils, and the excess can leak into waterways, contributing to eutrophication.

Phosphorus in the environment

The largest reservoir of phosphorus is in sedimentary rocks, particularly apatite-bearing formations. Weathering slowly releases phosphate from rocks into soils and waterways. Plants absorb phosphate from the soil, and animals obtain phosphorus by eating plants or other animals. When organisms die and decompose, phosphorus is returned to the soil or carried into rivers, lakes, and eventually the ocean, where it is deposited as sediment.
