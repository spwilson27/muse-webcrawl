Manure

Spreading manure closes the phosphorus loop between livestock and cropland.

Manure is animal excrement — often mixed with bedding straw — used as an organic fertilizer. Long before phosphoric acid was manufactured from phosphate rock, manure was humanity's principal means of returning phosphorus to farmed soils: animals concentrate the phosphate of the plants they eat, and spreading their dung recycles it, completing a local loop of the phosphorus cycle.

Composition

Typical cattle manure contains roughly 0.5% nitrogen, 0.25% phosphorus (expressed as P2O5, the convention inherited from phosphoric-acid fertilizer analysis), and 0.5% potassium, plus valuable organic matter that improves soil structure and water retention. Poultry manure is richer still. Availability of the phosphorus to crops rises as microbes mineralize organic phosphates to the orthophosphate ions plants absorb.

Modern challenges

Industrial livestock regions now produce more manure phosphorus than nearby fields can take up. Surplus spreads onto saturated soils and runs off, a leading cause of eutrophication. Responses include transporting manure to phosphorus-deficient regions, composting, anaerobic digestion, and precipitating phosphorus as struvite for use as a slow-release fertilizer — effectively recovering the value that manufactured phosphoric acid (see Haber process for its nitrogen counterpart) would otherwise supply from mined rock.
