ATP Synthase

ATP synthase couples phosphate to ADP, forging life's energy currency.

ATP synthase is a molecular rotary motor that manufactures adenosine triphosphate (ATP) by joining inorganic phosphate — derived ultimately from phosphoric acid chemistry — to adenosine diphosphate (ADP). It sits in the inner mitochondrial membrane, the bacterial plasma membrane, and the thylakoid membrane of chloroplasts, and is the terminal step of oxidative phosphorylation in cellular respiration.

Mechanism

The enzyme has two parts: Fo, a membrane-embedded proton channel, and F1, a catalytic head. Protons flowing down their electrochemical gradient spin the central rotor (the c-ring and γ shaft) at thousands of revolutions per minute; the rotating shaft cycles the three catalytic β subunits through loose, tight, and open conformations — Paul Boyer's binding-change mechanism — binding ADP + phosphate, squeezing them into ATP, and releasing the product. About ten protons yield roughly three ATP.

Significance

A human turns over approximately their own body weight in ATP each day, nearly all of it through this enzyme. Uncouplers such as dinitrophenol waste the proton gradient as heat, while oligomycin blocks the Fo channel. The reversible enzyme can also run backwards, hydrolysing ATP to pump protons — a mode exploited by many bacteria.
