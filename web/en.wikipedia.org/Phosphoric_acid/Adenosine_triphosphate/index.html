Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is a nucleoside triphosphate that provides energy to drive and support many processes in living cells, such as muscle contraction, nerve impulse propagation, condensate dissolution, and chemical synthesis. Found in all known forms of life, it is often referred to as the "molecular unit of currency" of intracellular energy transfer.

Structure

ATP consists of the nitrogenous base adenine, the five-carbon sugar ribose, and a chain of three phosphate groups (alpha, beta, and gamma). It is closely related to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and adenosine monophosphate (AMP), and to the nucleotide building blocks of RNA.

Energy transfer

Energy is released when ATP is hydrolysed to ADP and inorganic phosphate; under cellular conditions this reaction is strongly exergonic, and cells couple it to otherwise unfavourable processes — biosynthesis, active transport (such as the sodium–potassium pump), and mechanical work by motor proteins like myosin.

Production

ATP is regenerated by cellular respiration: glycolysis and fermentation yield a small net gain by substrate-level phosphorylation, while oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria — driven by the proton gradient across the inner membrane and catalysed by ATP synthase — produces the great majority in aerobic organisms. In plants, photophosphorylation during photosynthesis also generates ATP. A human body recycles roughly its own mass in ATP each day.
