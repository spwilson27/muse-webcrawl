Musical film

The musical film is a film genre in which songs by the characters are interwoven into the narrative, sometimes accompanied by dancing. The songs usually advance the plot or develop the film's characters, though in some cases they serve merely as set pieces. Musical films characteristically combine spoken dialogue with sung and choreographed numbers.

History

The genre was born with the arrival of sound: The Jazz Singer (1927) introduced synchronised song, and The Broadway Melody (1929) won the Academy Award for Best Picture. The 1930s brought the backstage musicals of Busby Berkeley and the elegant Fred Astaire–Ginger Rogers films. MGM's Freed Unit defined the golden age in the 1940s and 1950s with Meet Me in St. Louis, An American in Paris and Singin' in the Rain (1952), followed by large-scale adaptations of stage hits such as The Sound of Music (1965) and West Side Story (1961).

Later developments

After a decline in the 1970s, the genre revived through animated films (Disney's Beauty and the Beast and The Lion King), Bollywood's song-centred cinema, and 21st-century successes such as Chicago, Mamma Mia!, La La Land and The Greatest Showman.
