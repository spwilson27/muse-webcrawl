Targeted killing

Targeted killing is a form of assassination carried out by governments against their perceived enemies. The term is usually applied to the deliberate killing, by a state, of a specific individual identified in advance, often outside a formally declared battlefield, frequently using unmanned aerial vehicles (drones), special forces or air strikes. Practitioners distinguish it from assassination on the grounds that the target is a combatant in an armed conflict; critics dispute that distinction and question its legality and effectiveness.

Practice

Israel has openly pursued a policy of targeted killings (called "focused foiling" in Hebrew) against Palestinian militants since the Second Intifada. The United States expanded the practice dramatically after the September 11 attacks, conducting drone strikes against al-Qaeda and Taliban figures in Pakistan, Yemen and Somalia, and killing Iranian general Qasem Soleimani in a 2020 strike in Iraq. Other states, including Russia and Turkey, have also been accused of targeted killings.

Legal and ethical debate

Supporters argue that targeted killing is a lawful act of self-defence against terrorists who cannot be captured, and that precision weapons reduce civilian casualties compared with conventional warfare. Opponents, including United Nations rapporteurs and human-rights organisations, argue that strikes outside recognised war zones amount to extrajudicial execution, violate sovereignty, cause significant civilian deaths, and may be counterproductive by fuelling militancy. The debate centres on the law of armed conflict, the right to life under human-rights law, and the transparency of the criteria by which targets are selected.
