The giant panda (Ailuropoda melanoleuca) is a bear species endemic to China, instantly recognizable by its striking black-and-white coat — black patches around the eyes, ears and legs contrasting with a white body. Despite belonging to the order Carnivora and having the digestive system of a carnivore, the giant panda subsists almost entirely on bamboo, consuming 12–38 kg of it daily. It lives in temperate mountain forests at elevations of 1,200–3,100 metres in central China.

Biology

Giant pandas have several adaptations to their bamboo diet: a "pseudo-thumb" — an enlarged wrist bone (radial sesamoid) — for gripping stems, large molar teeth and powerful jaw muscles for crushing them, and a thick stomach lining to protect against splinters. Because bamboo is low in nutrients, pandas are relatively sedentary, conserving energy. Adults are solitary outside the brief spring breeding season. Females usually give birth to a single surviving cub (twins are common but one is typically abandoned in the wild); newborns are tiny, about 1/900th of the mother's weight, blind and pink.

Classification

After decades of debate, molecular studies confirmed that the giant panda is a true bear, the earliest-diverging member of the family Ursidae. It is not closely related to the red panda, which belongs to its own family, Ailuridae. Two subspecies are recognized: the nominate A. m. melanoleuca and the brownish Qinling panda, A. m. qinlingensis.

Conservation

Habitat loss and fragmentation from agriculture, logging and infrastructure have been the panda's chief threats. Decades of Chinese government conservation — nature reserves, logging bans and reforestation — helped numbers recover, and in 2016 the IUCN reclassified the species from Endangered to Vulnerable; in 2021 China likewise reclassified it. The Giant Panda National Park, established in 2021, consolidates habitat across provinces. The panda is the logo of the World Wide Fund for Nature (WWF) and a global symbol of conservation. China has also loaned pandas to foreign zoos as part of "panda diplomacy."

In captivity

Captive breeding, pioneered at centres such as Chengdu and Wolong, overcame early difficulties through artificial insemination and the "twin swapping" technique for raising both twins. Several hundred pandas live in captivity worldwide, and captive-born individuals have been reintroduced into small wild populations.
