Saint Brigid of Kildare or Brigid of Ireland (Irish: Naomh Bríd; c. 451 – c. 525) is one of Ireland's patron saints, along with Patrick and Columba. An early Irish Christian nun, abbess and founder of several monasteries of nuns, including that of Kildare, she is one of the most revered figures in Irish tradition.

Life and legend

Most of what is told of Brigid comes from hagiographies written a century or more after her death, notably the seventh-century Vita by Cogitosus. Tradition makes her the daughter of Dubthach, a pagan chieftain, and Broccán, a Christian slave. Refusing marriage, she took the veil and founded a monastery at Kildare ("church of the oak") around 470–480, unusual in being a double monastery of nuns and monks, and became its abbess. Legends credit her with miracles of generosity and abundance — multiplying food and ale for the poor, healing the sick, and turning water into beer — and with maintaining a perpetual sacred fire at Kildare, tended by nineteen nuns.

Brigid the goddess?

Scholars have long debated the relationship between the saint and Brigid, a goddess of pre-Christian Ireland associated with fire, poetry, healing and smithcraft, whose festival of Imbolc falls on 1 February — St Brigid's feast day. Some hold that the saint was a historical person whose cult absorbed the goddess's attributes and shrine; others suspect the saint is substantially the goddess euhemerised. The question remains unresolved.

Veneration and customs

Brigid's cult spread across Ireland and, with Irish missionaries, to Scotland and continental Europe. Her most distinctive custom is the weaving of St Brigid's crosses from rushes, hung in homes for protection. In 2023 Ireland made St Brigid's Day (1 February, or the nearest Monday) a public holiday — the first named for a woman — coinciding with the Celtic festival of Imbolc marking the start of spring.
