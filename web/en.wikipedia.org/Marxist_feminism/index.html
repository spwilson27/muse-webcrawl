Marxist feminism is a philosophical variant of feminism that incorporates and extends Marxist theory. Marxist feminism analyzes the ways in which women are exploited through capitalism and the individual ownership of private property. According to Marxist feminists, women's liberation can only be achieved through a radical restructuring of the current capitalist economy, in which, they contend, much of women's labor is uncompensated.

Origins

The foundational text is Friedrich Engels's The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State (1884), which argued that women's subordination arose historically with private property and the monogamous family, rather than being natural. Early Marxist feminists such as Clara Zetkin and Alexandra Kollontai organized working-class women within socialist movements and, in Kollontai's case, helped implement reforms on divorce, maternity provision, and communal childcare in early Soviet Russia.

Key themes

Central concerns include the unpaid domestic labor that reproduces the workforce — the subject of the 1970s "domestic labor debate" and the Wages for Housework campaign — and the analysis of how capitalism and patriarchy interlock, later developed in social reproduction theory. Thinkers such as Silvia Federici, in Caliban and the Witch (2004), connected the witch hunts of early modern Europe to primitive accumulation and the disciplining of women's bodies.

Criticism

Marxist feminism has been criticized by other feminists for subordinating gender to class ("dual systems" debates), and by intersectional theorists, including Angela Davis in Women, Race and Class (1981), for initially neglecting race and slavery in its account of women's oppression.
