Romulus was the legendary founder and first king of Rome. Various traditions attribute the establishment of many of Rome's oldest legal, political, religious, and social institutions to Romulus and his contemporaries. Although many of these traditions incorporate elements of folklore, and it is unclear how much a putative historical figure underlies the mythical Romulus, the events and institutions attributed to him were central to the myths surrounding Rome's origins and cultural traditions.

In Roman mythology, Romulus and his twin brother Remus were the children of Rhea Silvia and the god Mars. Their maternal grandfather was Numitor, rightful king of Alba Longa, who had been deposed by his brother Amulius. Amulius ordered the twins drowned in the Tiber River, but they were saved by a she-wolf who suckled them in a cave, the Lupercal. They were discovered by the shepherd Faustulus, who raised them. Upon reaching adulthood, the twins restored Numitor to the throne and founded their own city on the banks of the Tiber.

Foundation of Rome

Romulus and Remus quarrelled over the location and name of their new city. Romulus favoured the Palatine Hill and Remus the Aventine. The dispute led to Remus's death, either at Romulus's own hand or in the ensuing brawl. Romulus then founded Rome, traditionally dated to 21 April 753 BC, fortifying the Palatine and becoming its first king. He populated the city by offering asylum to fugitives and exiles, and, lacking women, staged the abduction known as the Rape of the Sabine Women, provoking war with the Sabines, which ended in a union of the two peoples.

Reign and institutions

Tradition credits Romulus with creating the Senate of one hundred elders (the patres), dividing the people into thirty curiae, organising the army into legions, and establishing the Roman system of patronage. He waged wars against neighbouring peoples including the Caeninenses, Antemnates, Crustumini, Sabines, Camerini, and Veientes, steadily expanding Rome's territory.

Death and deification

Romulus reigned for some thirty-seven years. According to tradition he disappeared during a storm while reviewing his troops and was taken up to heaven, becoming the god Quirinus, later identified with one aspect of the Capitoline Triad's sphere and worshipped by the Romans as their divine ancestor.
