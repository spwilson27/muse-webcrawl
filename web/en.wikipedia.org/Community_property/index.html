Community property

Community property is a marital property regime under which most property acquired during a marriage is owned jointly by both spouses and divided equally on divorce. It contrasts with the separate property and equitable distribution regimes derived from English common law. The system originates in civil law traditions, notably Spanish and French law.

United States

Nine US states follow community property: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin (where it is called marital property). Alaska allows couples to opt in. In general, all income and assets acquired during the marriage are community property, while property owned before marriage, or acquired by gift or inheritance, remains separate property. On divorce, community property is generally divided equally; on death, a spouse usually receives a full step-up in tax basis for community assets.

Other jurisdictions

Variants of community property are found in many civil-law countries, including forms in France (communauté réduite aux acquêts), Spain (sociedad de gananciales), South Africa, and the Philippines, and in the "community of acquisitions" systems of several Latin American countries.
