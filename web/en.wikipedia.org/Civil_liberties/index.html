Civil liberties

Civil liberties are guarantees and freedoms in a liberal democracy that governments commit not to abridge, either by legislation or judicial interpretation, without due process. Though the scope of the term differs between countries, civil liberties often include the freedom of conscience, freedom of press, freedom of religion, freedom of expression, freedom of assembly, the right to security and liberty, freedom of speech, the right to privacy, the right to equal treatment under the law and due process, the right to a fair trial, and the right to life.

Concepts

Civil liberties are often distinguished from civil rights: liberties are freedoms from government interference (negative rights), whereas civil rights are claims to equal treatment and protection against discrimination, typically requiring government action. Other civil liberties include the right to own property, the right to defend oneself, and the right to bodily integrity.

By jurisdiction

In the United States, civil liberties are chiefly defined by the Bill of Rights — the first ten amendments to the Constitution — as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The United Kingdom protects civil liberties through common law, the Human Rights Act 1998, and (formerly) the European Convention on Human Rights. India guarantees fundamental rights in Part III of its Constitution, including equality before the law and freedoms of speech, assembly, and religion. China's constitution enumerates rights of speech, press, and assembly, though these are limited in practice by law and the leading role of the Chinese Communist Party.

Contemporary debates

Debates about civil liberties recur around surveillance and privacy in the digital age, counterterrorism powers, public-health measures during pandemics, hate speech and online expression, and the rights of suspects and prisoners. Advocacy organisations such as the ACLU in the United States and Liberty in the United Kingdom litigate and campaign in defence of civil liberties.
