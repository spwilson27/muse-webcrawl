Ringer's solution

Ringer's solution is a solution of several salts dissolved in water for the purpose of creating an isotonic solution relative to the body fluids of an animal. It typically contains sodium chloride, potassium chloride, calcium chloride and sodium bicarbonate, in concentrations that mimic the ionic composition of plasma. It is named after Sydney Ringer, the British physiologist who, in the 1880s, determined that a frog's heart perfused with a solution of these inorganic salts would keep beating far longer than one perfused with plain saline — famously after discovering that London tap water worked better than distilled water because of its dissolved salts.

Composition and variants

A common recipe contains about 8.6 g/L sodium chloride, 0.3 g/L potassium chloride and 0.33 g/L calcium chloride. Lactated Ringer's solution (also called Hartmann's solution, after Alexis Hartmann) replaces bicarbonate with sodium lactate, which is metabolised in the liver to bicarbonate; it is widely used for intravenous fluid resuscitation. Acetated variants (such as Plasma-Lyte) use acetate instead.

Uses

Historically a laboratory medium for keeping isolated organs and tissues alive, Ringer's solution and its descendants are today standard intravenous crystalloid fluids for treating dehydration, burns and blood loss, and are also used for wound irrigation and in veterinary medicine. Trials such as SMART (2018) have compared balanced crystalloids like lactated Ringer's against normal saline, finding modest outcome advantages for balanced solutions in some patient groups.
