Austrian school of economics

The Austrian school is a heterodox school of economic thought that advocates strict adherence to methodological individualism, the concept that social phenomena result exclusively from the motivations and actions of individuals. It originated in late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Vienna with the work of Carl Menger, Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk and Friedrich von Wieser, and was later developed by Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek.

Principles

Austrian economists emphasise the subjective theory of value, marginalism, the role of the entrepreneur, and spontaneous order in markets. Mises developed praxeology, a deductive method of economics based on the action axiom. The school is strongly critical of central banking and fractional-reserve banking, offering the Austrian business cycle theory, which attributes booms and busts to artificially low interest rates that induce malinvestment. Austrians generally oppose central planning, arguing — as in Mises's economic calculation problem and Hayek's work on dispersed knowledge — that prices are indispensable signals.

History and influence

Founded with Menger's Principles of Economics (1871), the school participated in the Methodenstreit and the socialist calculation debate. Many of its leading figures emigrated in the 1930s; Hayek received the Nobel Memorial Prize in 1974. The school experienced a revival from the 1970s onward, particularly in the United States, where institutions such as the Mises Institute promote its ideas. Its relationship to mainstream economics remains contested: critics regard its rejection of econometric testing as unscientific, while supporters credit it with insights into entrepreneurship, money and cycles.
