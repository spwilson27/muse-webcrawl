Hemodialysis, also spelled haemodialysis, is a medical procedure that artificially removes waste products and excess fluid from the blood when the kidneys are unable to do so — a form of renal replacement therapy used in acute kidney injury and end-stage kidney disease. The patient's blood is circulated through an external machine containing a dialyzer ("artificial kidney"), where it passes beside a dialysis solution across a semipermeable membrane before returning to the body.

Principles

Cleaning relies mainly on diffusion: small solutes such as urea, creatinine, potassium and phosphate move from blood, where their concentration is high, into the dialysate, which flows counter-currently to maximise the gradient. Excess water is removed by ultrafiltration driven by a pressure gradient. The dialysate's composition is carefully set so that electrolytes and bicarbonate normalise rather than simply deplete.

Procedure and access

A durable access to the circulation is required. The preferred option is a surgically created arteriovenous fistula, usually in the arm, which matures over weeks; alternatives are a synthetic graft or a central venous catheter, the latter carrying higher infection risk. Conventional in-center treatment lasts about four hours three times weekly; home hemodialysis and nocturnal schedules permit more frequent or longer treatments, which can improve fluid and phosphate control. Anticoagulation (usually heparin) prevents clotting in the circuit.

History

The first successful human hemodialysis was performed by Willem Kolff in the Netherlands in 1943–45 using his rotating-drum dialyzer. Chronic dialysis became feasible after Belding Scribner introduced the Teflon shunt in 1960, transforming end-stage kidney failure from a fatal condition into a survivable chronic one.

Complications

Common problems include low blood pressure and cramps during ultrafiltration, access infection or thrombosis, and long-term issues such as cardiovascular disease, anaemia, bone and mineral disorders, and dialysis-related amyloidosis. Hemodialysis sustains life but does not fully replace kidney function; kidney transplantation, when available, generally offers better survival and quality of life.
