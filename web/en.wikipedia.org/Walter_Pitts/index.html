Walter Harry Pitts Jr. (April 23, 1923 – May 14, 1969) was an American logician and self-taught prodigy who worked in computational neuroscience. He is best known for co-authoring, with Warren McCulloch, the 1943 paper "A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity," which introduced the first mathematical model of an artificial neuron.

Early life

Raised in a poor Detroit family, Pitts taught himself logic and mathematics by reading in the public library; at twelve he reportedly wrote to Bertrand Russell pointing out problems in Principia Mathematica. He ran away from home at fifteen to the University of Chicago, where he sat in on lectures by Rudolf Carnap and met the teenage Jerome Lettvin, his lifelong friend.

The McCulloch–Pitts neuron

In 1942 Pitts moved in with the family of neuropsychiatrist Warren McCulloch. Their 1943 paper showed that networks of simple threshold neurons could compute any function computable by a Turing machine, laying a theoretical foundation for artificial neural networks, cybernetics, and cognitive science. Pitts went on to work with Norbert Wiener at MIT on statistical theories of neural networks and three-dimensional visual processing, but never completed his doctorate.

Later life

After a bitter rupture with Wiener in the early 1950s, in which Pitts reportedly burned his dissertation work, he withdrew from many academic pursuits, though he continued research at MIT's Research Laboratory of Electronics. He died in 1969 of bleeding esophageal varices associated with cirrhosis.
