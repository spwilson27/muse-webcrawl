Bisphosphonate

Bisphosphonates are a class of drugs that prevent the loss of bone density, used to treat osteoporosis and other bone diseases. They are the most commonly prescribed drugs for osteoporosis. They share a chemical structure characterized by two phosphonate groups attached to a central carbon atom, an analogue of the naturally occurring pyrophosphate that resists enzymatic breakdown.

The general structure of a bisphosphonate, with P–C–P backbone and two side chains.

Mechanism of action

Bisphosphonates bind strongly to hydroxyapatite in bone and are taken up by osteoclasts during resorption. Nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates, such as alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate and zoledronic acid, inhibit the enzyme farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase in the mevalonate pathway, disrupting osteoclast function and inducing osteoclast apoptosis. Older non-nitrogen compounds such as etidronate act by forming toxic ATP analogues.

Medical uses

They are used for postmenopausal and glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis, Paget's disease of bone, bone metastases and multiple myeloma, hypercalcemia of malignancy, and osteogenesis imperfecta in children. Administration is oral (usually weekly or monthly) or intravenous.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include gastrointestinal irritation and esophagitis with oral dosing, and an acute-phase flu-like reaction after intravenous doses. Rare but serious complications include osteonecrosis of the jaw and atypical femoral fractures, generally after long-term high-dose use.

History

Bisphosphonates were first synthesized in the 19th century and used industrially as water softeners before their biological effects on calcification were recognized in the 1960s, leading to clinical development from the 1970s onward.
