Allied intervention in the Russian Civil War

The Allied intervention in the Russian Civil War (1918–1925) was a multinational military expedition in which the Allied powers of the First World War — principally the United Kingdom, France, the United States, and Japan, together with smaller contingents from many other nations — landed forces in Russia during its civil war. Initially justified by the need to secure war materiel stockpiles, rescue the Czechoslovak Legion, and potentially re-establish an Eastern Front against Germany, the intervention developed into open, though half-hearted and uncoordinated, support for the anti-Bolshevik White armies. It failed to prevent the Bolshevik victory and left a lasting legacy of Soviet distrust of the West.

Background

After the October Revolution of 1917, Lenin's government signed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk with Germany in March 1918, taking Russia out of the war. The Allies feared that huge stockpiles of supplies at Murmansk, Arkhangelsk, and Vladivostok would fall into German or Bolshevik hands, and that some 50,000 men of the Czechoslovak Legion — former prisoners of war fighting their way east along the Trans-Siberian Railway to rejoin the war — would be trapped after clashing with Soviet authorities in May 1918.

The interventions

North Russia

British, American (the "Polar Bear Expedition" from Michigan), French, and other troops occupied Murmansk and Arkhangelsk, fighting Bolshevik forces along the Northern Dvina and the railway to Vologda. They withdrew in autumn 1919.

Siberia

Japan landed the largest force — eventually some 70,000 men — and occupied much of the Russian Far East, supporting Cossack warlords such as Grigory Semyonov. About 8,000 American troops (the American Expeditionary Force, Siberia, under General William Graves) guarded sections of the railway and remained officially neutral between Reds and Whites. Most Allied forces withdrew in 1920; the Japanese stayed until 1922 (and in northern Sakhalin until 1925).

The South

French and Greek troops briefly occupied Odessa and other Black Sea ports in late 1918–early 1919 before evacuating amid mutinies in the French fleet. Britain supplied extensive materiel, advisers, and some air and tank support to General Denikin's Armed Forces of South Russia and later to Wrangel in Crimea.

Outcome and legacy

The intervention was hampered by war weariness at home, conflicting aims (Japan pursued territorial ambitions; the Americans refused an openly anti-Bolshevik crusade), and the Whites' own disunity and unpopularity. Soviet propaganda made enduring use of the intervention as proof of capitalist encirclement, a theme that shaped Soviet foreign policy for decades; the episode remains a touchstone of Russian grievance narratives about Western interference.
