The Boeing 747 is a long-range wide-body airliner designed and manufactured by Boeing Commercial Airplanes in the United States. The first wide-body airplane produced, it was the first aircraft called a "jumbo jet". Its distinctive upper deck and four engines made it one of the most recognizable aircraft in the world; for 37 years it held the passenger capacity record, and it was nicknamed the "Queen of the Skies".

Development

The 747 was conceived in the 1960s at the urging of Pan Am president Juan Trippe, who wanted an aircraft two and a half times the size of the Boeing 707 to reduce seat-mile costs. A design team led by Joe Sutter developed the aircraft at Boeing's Everett factory in Washington — at the time the largest building in the world by volume. Because Boeing expected supersonic transports to make it obsolete as a passenger aircraft, the 747 was designed so it could be readily adapted as a freighter, a decision that accounts for its humped upper deck (which allowed a nose cargo door) and its long second career carrying freight.

Variants and service

The original 747-100 entered service with Pan Am in 1970. Successive variants included the longer-range 747-200, the stretched-upper-deck 747-300, and the highly successful 747-400 (1989) with winglets, a two-crew glass cockpit, and range of about 13,490 km. Special versions served as Air Force One (VC-25), the E-4 airborne command post, and the Shuttle Carrier Aircraft that ferried NASA's Space Shuttles. The final variant, the 747-8, entered service in 2011–2012. The last aircraft, a 747-8 freighter, was delivered to Atlas Air in January 2023, ending a 54-year production run.

Legacy

The 747 democratized long-haul air travel by sharply lowering the cost per seat and remained the backbone of many airlines' long-haul fleets for decades. Two-engine wide-bodies such as the Boeing 777 and Airbus A350 eventually displaced it in passenger service on economic grounds, but large numbers continue to fly as freighters.
