Lighthouse

Lighthouse (general)
PurposeMaritime navigation aid
Light source (modern)LED or rotating beacon; historically oil, gas, electric arc
OpticsFresnel lens (classic)
Typical rangeUp to ~25 nautical miles

A lighthouse is a tower, building, or other type of physical structure designed to emit light from a system of lamps and lenses and to serve as a beacon for navigational aid for maritime pilots at sea or on inland waterways. Lighthouses mark dangerous coastlines, hazardous shoals, reefs, rocks, and safe entries to harbors; they also assist in aerial navigation.

History

The most famous ancient lighthouse was the Pharos of Alexandria in Egypt, built around 280 BC and counted among the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World; it stood perhaps 100 metres tall. Roman towers followed at Dover and elsewhere. Modern lighthouse construction began in the eighteenth century with structures such as John Smeaton's Eddystone Lighthouse (1759). The invention of the Fresnel lens in the 1820s, which concentrated light into a powerful rotating beam, transformed lighthouse optics.

Operation

Each lighthouse has a distinctive light characteristic — a pattern of flashes, occultations, and colours — by which mariners identify it on charts. Historically, lighthouse keepers maintained lamps, lenses, and fog signals, often in remote and hazardous stations. Automation spread through the twentieth century, and the last keepers were withdrawn from most stations by the 1990s, although a few staffed lights remain.

Decline and preservation

With satellite navigation (GPS) ubiquitous, many lighthouses have been decommissioned, but they continue as backup aids, daymarks, and heritage structures. Numerous lighthouses are now protected monuments, museums, or even hotels, and they remain potent cultural symbols of guidance and safety.
