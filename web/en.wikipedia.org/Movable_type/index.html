Movable type is the system of printing and typography that uses movable components — individual pieces of type, each bearing a single character or symbol — to reproduce the elements of a document. Unlike woodblock printing, in which a whole page is carved into a single block, movable type lets a printer assemble a page from a stock of reusable sorts, print as many copies as needed, then redistribute the type for the next job. It is one of the most consequential technologies in the history of communication.

History

Invention in China

Movable type was invented around 1040 by Bi Sheng, an artisan of Song-dynasty China, who made types from baked clay, set them in an iron frame, and printed with them; the system was recorded by the polymath Shen Kuo in his Dream Pool Essays. Wooden type and a revolving type case were developed by Wang Zhen around 1298. Because Chinese script required thousands of characters, woodblock printing remained more economical for many purposes, but movable type was used for large projects.

Metal type in Korea

Printers in Goryeo (Korea) developed cast bronze movable type by the early 13th century, supported by royal foundries. The oldest surviving book printed with metal movable type is the Jikji, a Korean Buddhist text printed in 1377, recognised by UNESCO in the Memory of the World register.

The Gutenberg system

Around 1450, in Mainz, Germany, Johannes Gutenberg independently developed a complete European system: an alloy of lead, tin, and antimony for durable type; a hand mould for casting thousands of identical sorts; an oil-based ink; and an adapted screw press. His 42-line Bible (c. 1455) demonstrated quality rivalling manuscripts at a fraction of the cost. The system spread rapidly — by 1500 presses operated in hundreds of European towns and had printed millions of books, transforming scholarship, religion, and language.

Use and decline

Hand composition with metal type remained the industry standard for four centuries. In the 19th century the Linotype (1884) and Monotype machines mechanised composition by casting fresh type from molten metal ("hot metal" typesetting). Phototypesetting displaced hot metal after the Second World War, and desktop publishing with digital fonts finally retired movable type from commercial printing in the 1980s–90s. The craft survives in letterpress studios and fine-press printing, prized for its tactile impression.
