The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) is a financial regulatory body in the United Kingdom. It operates within the legal framework of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 and is one of the two successor bodies to the Financial Services Authority (FSA), alongside the Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA). The FCA came into existence on 1 April 2013.

Role and objectives

The FCA regulates the conduct of around 50,000 financial services firms in the UK and is the prudential regulator of those firms not regulated by the PRA. Its statutory objectives are to protect consumers, to protect and enhance the integrity of the UK financial system, and to promote effective competition in the interests of consumers. It also supervises financial markets, including the London Stock Exchange, and maintains the UK Listing Authority function.

The FCA has significant powers over the marketing of financial products; it can specify minimum standards and place requirements on products, ban financial products for up to a year, and instruct firms to withdraw or modify misleading advertising. In 2023 the FCA introduced the Consumer Duty, a set of rules requiring firms to deliver good outcomes for retail customers.

History

Following the 2008 financial crisis, the coalition government announced the abolition of the FSA and a new "twin peaks" model of regulation. Under the Financial Services Act 2012, prudential regulation of banks, insurers and major investment firms passed to the PRA (a subsidiary of the Bank of England), while conduct regulation passed to the FCA. In April 2014 the FCA also took over consumer credit regulation from the Office of Fair Trading.
