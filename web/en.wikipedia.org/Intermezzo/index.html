Intermezzo

Intermezzo

OriginItalian opera, 18th century
Meaning"Interlude" or "intermediate" (Italian)
FormsOperatic interlude; short independent piano piece; theatrical interval piece
Notable examplesPergolesi's La serva padrona (1733); Brahms's piano intermezzi, Op. 117–119

In music and theatre, an intermezzo (Italian, literally "interlude") is a composition that fits between other musical or dramatic entities, such as acts of a play or movements of a larger musical work. The term is used in several distinct senses, ranging from the comic operatic interludes of the early 18th century to the short lyrical piano pieces of the Romantic era.

Comic intermezzi performed between the acts of serious operas helped give rise to opera buffa.

Operatic intermezzo

In early 18th-century Naples, short comic pieces in two or three parts were performed between the acts of an opera seria. Usually involving only two singers and a small orchestra, these intermezzi offered light relief from the heroic main plot. The most famous example, Giovanni Battista Pergolesi's La serva padrona (1733), was originally an intermezzo to his opera Il prigionier superbo; when performed in Paris in 1752 it ignited the Querelle des Bouffons and helped establish the independent genre of opera buffa.

Instrumental intermezzo

In the 19th century the term came to denote a short, self-contained instrumental piece, often lyrical in character. Johannes Brahms used the title for many of his late piano pieces, including the three Intermezzi, Op. 117 (1892), which he described as "lullabies of my sorrows." Mendelssohn and Schumann also wrote movements called intermezzo within larger works.

Orchestral intermezzo in opera

Later opera composers used the intermezzo as an orchestral interlude between scenes, allowing stage changes while sustaining the drama. Famous examples include the Intermezzo from Mascagni's Cavalleria rusticana (1890) and the intermezzi in Puccini's Manon Lescaut. Richard Strauss titled an autobiographical comic opera Intermezzo (premiered 1924), in which the interludes themselves carry much of the musical interest.
