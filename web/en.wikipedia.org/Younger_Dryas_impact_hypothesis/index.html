Younger Dryas impact hypothesis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Younger Dryas impact hypothesis (YDIH) proposes that fragments of a large comet or asteroid struck or exploded over North America about 12,900 years ago, at the onset of the Younger Dryas cold interval. Proponents argue that the impact triggered abrupt climate cooling, widespread wildfires, the extinction of many North American megafauna, and the disappearance of the Clovis culture.

Proposed evidence

The hypothesis was first advanced in 2007 by Richard Firestone and colleagues, who reported a thin sedimentary layer — the "Younger Dryas boundary" layer — at numerous sites across North America containing exotic materials interpreted as impact markers: nanodiamonds, magnetic spherules, iridium, charcoal, and fullerenes with extraterrestrial helium. Later work by proponents added claims of platinum anomalies, meltglass, and shocked quartz, and pointed to the Hiawatha crater beneath the Greenland ice sheet as a possible impact site, though its dating has been disputed.

Scientific response

The hypothesis has been highly controversial. Critics have questioned the reproducibility of several marker claims, argued that the evidence can be explained by ordinary terrestrial processes, and noted chronological problems: the Hiawatha crater was later dated to roughly 58 million years ago, far too old. A comprehensive 2023 review concluded that the impact evidence was not reproducible and that the hypothesis should be rejected, while its supporters have continued to publish studies defending an extraterrestrial trigger for the Younger Dryas. The mainstream explanation attributes the Younger Dryas cooling primarily to a disruption of North Atlantic ocean circulation by glacial meltwater.
