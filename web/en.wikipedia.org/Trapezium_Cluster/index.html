Trapezium Cluster

[Image: The Trapezium stars at the heart of the Orion Nebula]
images/trapezium.png

The Trapezium Cluster is a tight open cluster of young, massive stars at the heart of the Orion Nebula (M42), about 1,300 light-years from Earth. Its four principal stars — Theta-1 Orionis A, B, C and D — form the trapezoid shape that gives the cluster its name; hundreds of fainter stars and protoplanetary discs surround them. The cluster is one of the youngest known in the Milky Way, with an age of only about 300,000 years.

Discovery

Galileo Galilei recorded three of the stars in 1617; a fourth was identified in 1673 by the French astronomer Jean Picard's correspondent Abbe. The cluster's name comes from the trapezoidal arrangement of the four bright components as seen from Earth.

The stars

The dominant star, Theta-1 Orionis C, is one of the most luminous stars known — over 200,000 times the Sun's luminosity — and its intense ultraviolet radiation lights the entire Orion Nebula and drives proplyds (protoplanetary discs) in the surrounding region, sculpting their teardrop shapes through photoevaporation. Theta-1 Orionis B is itself a multiple system. The stars' powerful stellar winds clear a central cavity in the nebula.

Significance

Because the Trapezium is so young and nearby, it is a primary laboratory for studying massive star formation, the dynamics of dense clusters, and the formation of planetary systems in hostile ultraviolet environments. Infrared observations continue to reveal new brown dwarfs and planetary-mass objects in the surrounding nebula.
