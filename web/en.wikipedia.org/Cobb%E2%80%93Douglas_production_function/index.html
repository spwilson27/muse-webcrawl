Cobb–Douglas production function

In economics, the Cobb–Douglas production function is a form of production function used to represent the relationship between the amounts of two or more inputs — typically physical capital and labour — and the amount of output produced. It was developed by economist Paul Douglas and mathematician Charles Cobb, who presented it in a 1928 paper studying United States manufacturing.

Y = A · Lβ · Kα

where Y is total production, L is labour input, K is capital input, A is total factor productivity, and α and β are the output elasticities of capital and labour respectively.

Properties

If α + β = 1, the function exhibits constant returns to scale: doubling both inputs doubles output. The exponents also equal the factor income shares under perfect competition, and Douglas found that the values estimated from US data roughly matched the observed shares of labour (about three quarters) and capital (about one quarter) in national income. The elasticity of substitution between inputs is exactly one.

Uses and criticism

The Cobb–Douglas function is widely used in growth theory, including the Solow–Swan model, and in empirical production analysis, because of its simplicity and tractability. Critics have questioned whether its good statistical fit reflects a genuine technological relationship or accounting identities, and generalisations such as the CES production function relax its restrictive assumptions.
