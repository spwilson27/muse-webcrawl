Hoppin' John

Hoppin' JohnOrigin: South Carolina Lowcountry, United States
Main ingredients: black-eyed peas, rice, smoked pork
Tradition: eaten on New Year's Day for luck

Hoppin' John, also known as Carolina peas and rice, is a dish in Southern United States cuisine consisting of black-eyed peas (or field peas), rice, chopped onion and sliced bacon or other smoked pork, seasoned with salt and sometimes hot peppers. It derives from West African rice-and-bean traditions carried to the South Carolina Lowcountry through the Gullah culture of enslaved Africans, influenced also by Caribbean dishes.

New Year tradition

Hoppin' John is traditionally eaten on New Year's Day throughout the South, believed to bring prosperity in the coming year — the peas symbolising coins. A related custom holds that leftovers, called "Skippin' Jenny", eaten the next day demonstrate frugality and increase the chance of wealth. It is often served with collard greens (representing paper money) and cornbread (representing gold).

Name

The etymology of "Hoppin' John" is uncertain; folk explanations include an invitation ("Hop in, John") and a disabled Charleston street vendor remembered in nineteenth-century accounts. Recipes under the name appear in print from the 1840s, notably in Sarah Tyson Rorer's and Mary Randolph's era cookbooks of Southern cooking.
