Law of superposition

The law of superposition is a fundamental principle of stratigraphy and one of the basic axioms of geology. It states that, within a sequence of layers of sedimentary rock that has not been disturbed, each layer is older than the one above it and younger than the one below it. The principle was first clearly set out by the Danish scientist Nicolas Steno in his 1669 work Prodromus, and it allows geologists to determine the relative ages of strata and the fossils they contain.

Statement and basis

Sediment is deposited in horizontal layers over time. A bed cannot be laid down until the surface beneath it exists, so a layer must be younger than the layer on which it rests. Applied to a stack of strata, the principle produces a relative chronology from oldest at the bottom to youngest at the top, unless the sequence has been overturned or faulted after deposition.

Limitations

The principle assumes the strata have not been disturbed. Folding can overturn beds, thrust faults can stack older rocks over younger ones, and intrusions can cut across layers. Geologists therefore combine superposition with other principles — original horizontality, lateral continuity, cross-cutting relationships and faunal succession — and with radiometric dating to build an absolute timescale.

Applications

Superposition underlies archaeological stratigraphy as well: in an undisturbed excavation, deeper artefacts are generally older than shallower ones. It is also used in interpreting landforms such as the layered walls of the Grand Canyon and in petroleum geology, where the order of strata guides exploration.
