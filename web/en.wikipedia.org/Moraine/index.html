Moraine

A moraine is any accumulation of unconsolidated debris (regolith and rock), sometimes referred to as glacial till, that occurs in both currently and formerly glaciated regions, and that has been previously carried along by a glacier or ice sheet. It may consist of partly rounded particles ranging in size from boulders (in which case it is often referred to as boulder clay) down to gravel and sand, in a groundmass of finely divided material.

Formation

Moraines form from debris that the glacier plucks from the valley walls and floor, material that falls onto the glacier from above, and sediment carried within and beneath the ice. When the glacier melts or retreats, this debris is deposited as ridges or sheets.

Types

Lateral moraines form along the sides of a glacier. Medial moraines form down the centre of a glacier where two glaciers merge and their lateral moraines join. Terminal (end) moraines are ridges deposited at the furthest point of a glacier's advance; recessional moraines form behind them during pauses in retreat. Ground moraine is the irregular sheet of till deposited beneath a glacier, producing gently rolling terrain. Supraglacial material travels on the surface of the ice.

Examples

Famous moraines include the terminal moraines that form Long Island and Cape Cod in the United States, the Oak Ridges Moraine in Ontario, Canada, and the moraine-dammed lakes of the English Lake District and the Italian lakes such as Lake Garda.
