Electoral system

An electoral system or voting system is a set of rules that determine how elections and referendums are conducted and how their results are determined. Electoral systems are used in politics to elect governments and in many other settings. They specify the ballot, the counting method, and how votes are translated into seats or outcomes.

Plurality and majority systems

In first-past-the-post, the candidate with the most votes in each single-member district wins; it tends to produce single-party governments but often disproportional results. Majority systems such as the two-round system and the alternative vote (instant runoff) aim to ensure winners command broad support. Duverger's law observes that plurality systems tend toward two-party competition.

Proportional systems

Proportional representation allocates seats in multi-member districts in proportion to votes, using party lists (open or closed) with formulas such as D'Hondt or Sainte-Laguë, or the single transferable vote, in which voters rank individual candidates. PR produces multiparty legislatures and coalition governments, with legal thresholds used to limit fragmentation.

Mixed and other systems

Mixed-member systems combine district and list tiers, either compensating for disproportionality (MMP, as in Germany and New Zealand) or running them in parallel (as in Japan). Other methods studied by social choice theorists include approval voting, score voting and Condorcet methods. No system satisfies all desirable fairness criteria simultaneously, a fact formalised in Arrow's theorem and the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem.
