Limnic eruption

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Degassing pipes installed at Lake Nyos prevent a recurrence of its deadly 1986 limnic eruption.

A limnic eruption, also known as a lake overturn, is a rare type of natural disaster in which dissolved carbon dioxide (CO2) suddenly erupts from deep lake waters, forming a gas cloud capable of suffocating wildlife, livestock, and humans. The gas, denser than air, hugs the ground and displaces breathable air in low-lying areas. Only a few such events have been recorded, but they were catastrophic.

Mechanism

For an eruption to occur, a lake must be saturated with gas—typically CO2 leaking from underlying volcanic sources into the cold, high-pressure bottom waters of a deep, stably stratified (meromictic) tropical lake. A trigger such as a landslide, heavy rain, or volcanic activity disturbs the water column; as gas-laden water rises, pressure drops, CO2 comes out of solution, and the rising bubbles make the process self-sustaining, like opening a shaken bottle of champagne.

Recorded events

At Lake Monoun in Cameroon in 1984, a limnic eruption killed 37 people. On 21 August 1986, Lake Nyos, also in Cameroon, released an enormous cloud of CO2 (estimated at up to 1.6 million tonnes) that flowed down valleys at high speed, killing about 1,746 people and 3,500 livestock in villages up to 25 km away. Survivors reported a smell like gunpowder or rotten eggs and fell unconscious within moments.

Mitigation

Since 2001, engineers have installed degassing columns at Lake Nyos, and since 2011 at Lake Monoun, that siphon gas-rich bottom water to the surface, where the CO2 is released gradually and safely. Rwanda's Lake Kivu, which contains much larger quantities of CO2 and methane near a population of millions, is monitored, and methane is extracted commercially for electricity generation, which also reduces the hazard.
