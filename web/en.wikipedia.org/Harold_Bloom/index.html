Harold Bloom (July 11, 1930 – October 14, 2019) was an American literary critic and the Sterling Professor of the Humanities at Yale University. One of the most influential and controversial critics of his generation, he wrote more than forty books of criticism, championed the Western canon against what he called the "School of Resentment", and argued that Shakespeare "invented the human" as we understand it.

Life and career

Bloom was born in New York City to Jewish immigrants from Eastern Europe and grew up in the Bronx. A prodigious reader, he studied at Cornell under the Romanticist M. H. Abrams and took his doctorate at Yale in 1955, joining its faculty and remaining there for his entire career. His early books on Shelley, Blake, Yeats and Wallace Stevens established his reputation as a defender of Romantic poetry against the reigning New Criticism and the school of T. S. Eliot.

The Anxiety of Influence

In The Anxiety of Influence (1973) and its sequels, including A Map of Misreading (1975) and Kabbalah and Criticism (1975), Bloom proposed that poets are engaged in an Oedipal struggle with their strongest precursors, creatively "misreading" them through six revisionary ratios. Drawing on Freud, Gnosticism and Kabbalah, the theory made Bloom a central, if idiosyncratic, figure beside the Yale deconstructionists, from whom he later distanced himself.

Defence of the canon

The Western Canon: The Books and School of the Ages (1994), listing 26 authors at the core of Western literature, became a bestseller amid the "canon wars" of the 1990s. Bloom attacked feminist, Marxist, New Historicist and multiculturalist approaches, which he lumped together as the "School of Resentment", for subordinating aesthetic value to politics. Shakespeare: The Invention of the Human (1998) argued that Shakespeare's characters, above all Falstaff and Hamlet, created modern personality itself. His later works include How to Read and Why (2000), Hamlet: Poem Unlimited (2003), The Anatomy of Influence (2011) and a series of late studies of individual plays and poets.

Legacy

Bloom's erudition, aphoristic style and unabashed aestheticism made him a rare public intellectual among academic critics; his detractors found his judgements dogmatic and his canon narrow. He taught his last Yale class days before his death in 2019.
