Trajan (Latin: Marcus Ulpius Traianus; 18 September 53 – 8 August 117) was Roman emperor from 98 to 117 AD. Officially declared by the Senate as optimus princeps ("best ruler"), Trajan is remembered as a successful soldier-emperor whose reign marked the greatest territorial extent of the Roman Empire, and a period of extensive public building and welfare programs. He is the second of the Five Good Emperors.

Conquests

Born in Italica (near modern Seville, Spain) to a provincial family, Trajan rose through the army and was adopted by the emperor Nerva. As emperor he conquered Dacia (in modern Romania) in two wars (101–102 and 105–106), commemorated by Trajan's Column in Rome, whose spiral relief narrates the campaigns. In the east he annexed Nabataea and campaigned against Parthia (113–117), capturing Ctesiphon and reaching the Persian Gulf, creating the provinces of Armenia, Assyria, and Mesopotamia. At his death the empire stretched from Britain to Mesopotamia.

Domestic rule

Trajan sponsored large building projects — Trajan's Forum and Market, roads, bridges such as the one at Alcántara, and the restoration of harbours — and expanded the alimenta, a welfare program supporting poor children in Italy. His correspondence with Pliny the Younger, including the famous exchange on the treatment of Christians, survives as a key source for Roman administration.

Death and legacy

Trajan fell ill and died at Selinus in Cilicia in August 117, while returning from the east; his ashes were placed in the base of his Column. He was succeeded by his ward Hadrian, who abandoned some of his eastern conquests. Later tradition celebrated Trajan as the model emperor: the Senate wished each new emperor to be "luckier than Augustus and better than Trajan" (felicior Augusto, melior Traiano).
