Gimbal lock

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gimbal lock is the loss of one degree of freedom in a three-dimensional, three-gimbal mechanism that occurs when the axes of two of the three gimbals are driven into a parallel configuration, "locking" the system into rotation in a two-dimensional space. The term comes from gimbals, pivoted supports that allow rotation about an axis, used since antiquity in compasses and later in gyroscopes and inertial navigation systems.

Mechanical gimbal lock

A gyroscope mounted in three nested gimbals can normally take any orientation. If, however, the pitch angle reaches ±90°, the inner and outer gimbal axes align, and the device can no longer sense or maintain rotation about the third axis — it has lost a degree of freedom and may tumble. Mechanical engineers avoid true lock by using a fourth, redundant gimbal, by keeping mechanisms away from the critical configuration ("driving the gimbals"), or by using alternative mounts.

Apollo 11

Gimbal lock was a known hazard of the Apollo spacecraft's inertial measurement unit (IMU), which used three gimbals. Mission procedures kept the spacecraft's attitude away from the lock region, with a "gimbal lock" warning zone marked on the 8-ball attitude indicator. During Apollo 11 astronaut Michael Collins remarked on the concern, and after the mission engineers concluded that a fourth gimbal would have added complexity without benefit, since procedures successfully avoided the condition.

In mathematics and software

The same phenomenon appears mathematically when rotations are parameterised with Euler angles. At certain angles (for example, a pitch of ±90° in the common yaw–pitch–roll convention), two of the three rotations act about the same axis, so the parameterisation becomes singular and one rotational degree of freedom cannot be represented. This affects 3D graphics, robotics, and aerospace simulation. Software generally avoids the problem by representing rotations with quaternions or rotation matrices, which have no singularities, or by switching between different angle conventions.
