The Partition of India in 1947 was the change of political borders that accompanied the dissolution of the British Raj in the Indian subcontinent and the creation of two independent dominions: India and Pakistan. The partition involved the division of the provinces of Bengal and Punjab along broadly religious lines, and triggered one of the largest migrations in human history, accompanied by mass violence.

Background

The All-India Muslim League, led by Muhammad Ali Jinnah, argued from its 1940 Lahore Resolution that Muslims of the subcontinent constituted a separate nation (the "two-nation theory") entitled to its own state. The Indian National Congress under Jawaharlal Nehru and Mahatma Gandhi sought a united, independent India. Post-war Britain, under Prime Minister Clement Attlee, resolved to withdraw; the failed Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946, escalating communal violence (including the Calcutta killings of August 1946), and the last Viceroy, Lord Mountbatten, accelerated the timetable. The Mountbatten Plan of 3 June 1947 proposed partition, and the Indian Independence Act received royal assent on 18 July 1947.

Boundary and independence

Sir Cyril Radcliffe, a British lawyer who had never visited India, chaired the boundary commissions for Punjab and Bengal and produced the "Radcliffe Line" in a matter of weeks; the awards were published only after independence. Pakistan became independent on 14 August 1947 and India on 15 August, with Nehru's famous "Tryst with Destiny" speech in New Delhi. Over 560 princely states were left to accede to either dominion; disputed accessions — above all Jammu and Kashmir — ignited the first Indo-Pakistani war within months.

Displacement and violence

The partition displaced an estimated 10 to 20 million people along religious lines, creating perhaps the largest refugee crisis of the twentieth century. Hindus and Sikhs fled Pakistan for India and Muslims fled India for Pakistan, particularly across Punjab and Bengal. Communal massacres, abductions and forced conversions accompanied the migration; estimates of the dead range from several hundred thousand to as many as two million. Tens of thousands of women were abducted on both sides, prompting inter-dominion recovery operations that continued for years.

Legacy

Partition shaped the enduring rivalry between India and Pakistan — including wars in 1947–48, 1965, 1971 (which created Bangladesh from East Pakistan) and 1999, and the continuing Kashmir dispute between two nuclear-armed states. Its memory is preserved in literature (Saadat Hasan Manto, Khushwant Singh's Train to Pakistan), oral history projects, and the Partition Museum in Amritsar (opened 2017); India, Pakistan and Bangladesh all observe 14 August ("Partition Horrors Remembrance Day" in India) to commemorate the victims.
