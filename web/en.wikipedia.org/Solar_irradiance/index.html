Solar irradiance

Solar irradiance is the power per unit area received from the Sun in the form of electromagnetic radiation, measured in watts per square metre (W/m²). It may be measured in space or at Earth's surface after atmospheric absorption and scattering. The total solar irradiance (TSI), integrated over all wavelengths, is the amount of solar radiation received at the top of Earth's atmosphere on a surface perpendicular to the incoming rays.

Total solar irradiance

Modern satellite measurements give a TSI of about 1,361 W/m² at one astronomical unit, replacing the older value of about 1,366 W/m². TSI varies slightly with the 11-year solar cycle — by roughly 0.1% — because sunspots darken the Sun while bright faculae brighten it, with faculae dominating at solar maximum. Longer-term changes are debated; reconstructions suggest modest increases from the Maunder Minimum of the 17th century to the present.

Because Earth is a sphere, the average irradiance over the whole planet is one quarter of TSI, about 340 W/m², before accounting for reflection by clouds and the surface (albedo).

Spectral irradiance

Solar radiation spans ultraviolet, visible, and infrared wavelengths. Most energy lies in the visible and near-infrared bands, roughly following the spectrum of a black body at about 5,800 K. The ultraviolet portion varies proportionally much more over the solar cycle and strongly affects stratospheric chemistry and heating.

Irradiance at Earth's surface

At the surface, radiation is divided into direct beam irradiance and diffuse sky radiation; their sum is global horizontal irradiance. Clouds, aerosols, water vapor, and the path length through the atmosphere (air mass) reduce and redistribute sunlight. Surface irradiance is a key input for solar energy systems, agriculture, and climate models.

Measurement

Ground-based instruments include pyranometers and pyrheliometers; the World Radiometric Reference is maintained in Davos, Switzerland. In space, radiometers aboard satellites such as ACRIM, VIRGO/SOHO, TIM/SORCE, and TSIS-1 have measured TSI since 1978. Satellites also provide the data used to study the solar neutrino problem from a different angle — the Sun's total energy output, related to its luminosity.

Role in climate

Solar irradiance is Earth's primary energy source and drives the climate system. Changes in TSI over recent decades are too small to explain observed modern warming, according to assessments by the IPCC, but solar variability is important for understanding pre-industrial climate and phenomena such as the Little Ice Age.
