Cotton

Cotton is a soft, fluffy staple fibre that grows in a boll, or protective case, around the seeds of the cotton plants of the genus Gossypium in the mallow family Malvaceae. The fibre is almost pure cellulose and, under natural conditions, increases water absorbency. Cotton is the most widely used natural fibre cloth in clothing today, and is produced in many countries, with China, India and the United States among the largest producers.

History

Cotton was independently domesticated in the Old and New Worlds — in the Indus Valley, Africa, Mexico and Peru — thousands of years ago. The invention of the cotton gin in 1793 by Eli Whitney dramatically reduced the labour of separating fibre from seeds, transforming cotton production in the American South and entrenching slavery there. Cotton textiles were central to the Industrial Revolution in Britain, where mechanised spinning and weaving began.

Cultivation and processing

The main commercial species are Gossypium hirsutum (upland cotton, about 90% of world production), G. barbadense (extra-long staple, including Pima and Egyptian cotton), G. arboreum and G. herbaceum. After harvesting, bolls are ginned, the fibres are carded and spun into yarn, and woven or knitted into fabrics such as denim, terrycloth and corduroy. Cottonseed is pressed for oil and animal feed.

Environmental issues

Conventional cotton is a water- and pesticide-intensive crop; organic cotton, Bt cotton and better irrigation practices have been promoted in response. The shrinking of the Aral Sea is largely attributed to irrigation for cotton in Central Asia.
