Mandarin orange

OrderSapindales
FamilyRutaceae

The mandarin orange (Citrus reticulata), also known simply as a mandarin, is a small, rounded citrus tree and its fruit. The fruit is smaller and less spherical than a common orange, with a thin, loose, easily peeled skin and segments that separate readily. Mandarins are usually eaten plain or in fruit salads, and are among the ancestral species of modern cultivated citrus.

Description

The mandarin tree grows to about 7.5 metres (25 ft) in height, with slender branches and lanceolate leaves. The flowers are borne singly or in small groups in the leaf axils. The fruit is a hesperidium about 4–8 cm in diameter, oblate in shape, with a deep orange, loosely attached rind. Mandarins tend to be sweeter and less acidic than oranges, and their flavour is often considered richer.

Origins and taxonomy

Citrus originated in a region stretching from northeastern India and Myanmar to southwestern China. The mandarin is one of the original ("fundamental") citrus species, along with the citron, pomelo, and papedas, from which other cultivated types derive by hybridisation: the sweet orange, for example, is an ancient hybrid of mandarin and pomelo. The name "mandarin" is thought to derive from the colour of the robes of mandarins, the officials of imperial China; the fruit reached Europe only in the early nineteenth century, arriving in England from China in 1805, and spread from there to the Mediterranean.

Varieties and hybrids

Important mandarin groups and hybrids include:

  • Satsumas (Citrus unshiu) — hardy, seedless mandarins developed in Japan, named after the former Satsuma Province.
  • Clementines — hybrids of mandarin and sweet orange, named after Brother Clément Rodier, who identified the variety in Algeria in the early twentieth century.
  • Tangerines — a name applied in North America to certain reddish-orange mandarin varieties, originally associated with Tangier, Morocco.
  • Tangelos and tangors — mandarin–pomelo/grapefruit and mandarin–orange hybrids respectively.

Cultivation and uses

China is by far the world's largest producer of mandarins, followed by Spain, Turkey, and Morocco. The fruit is eaten fresh, juiced, and canned; canned mandarin segments are a common dessert item. Mandarins are a traditional symbol of good luck and are given as gifts during Chinese New Year. The peel yields essential oils used in flavourings and perfumery, and the fruit is valued as a source of vitamin C and the carotenoid beta-cryptoxanthin.
