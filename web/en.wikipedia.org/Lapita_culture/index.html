Lapita culture

The Lapita culture is the name given to a Neolithic Austronesian culture that spread across the Pacific around 1500–500 BC. It is believed to be the common ancestral culture of the Polynesian, Micronesian, and some Melanesian peoples, and is identified archaeologically by its distinctive dentate-stamped pottery, obsidian tools, shell ornaments, and a horticultural economy based on taro, yam, banana, coconut, and breadfruit, together with domesticated pigs, dogs, and chickens.

Distribution

Lapita sites were first identified in the Bismarck Archipelago off New Guinea around 1500 BC. Within a few centuries the culture had spread eastward through the Solomon Islands to Vanuatu, New Caledonia, Fiji, Tonga, and Samoa — a maritime expansion of more than 4,000 kilometres. The culture takes its name from the site of Lapita in New Caledonia, where its pottery was first described in the early 20th century.

Pottery

Lapita pottery is characterised by fine dentate (toothed) stamping, incision, and applied relief, forming complex geometric and sometimes anthropomorphic designs. The elaborate decoration declined over time; in the eastern part of the range pottery became progressively plainer and eventually disappeared from Polynesian assemblages altogether in Samoa and Tonga.

Origins and significance

The Lapita expansion represents the movement of Austronesian-speaking seafarers, ultimately out of Taiwan and island Southeast Asia, into Remote Oceania — the first human settlement of the Pacific islands beyond the Solomons. Modern Polynesians, and many coastal Melanesian populations, descend in part from Lapita peoples who intermingled with Papuan groups in Near Oceania. The Lapita culture is thus central to debates about the peopling of the Pacific, the development of Polynesian culture, and the spread of the Oceanic languages.
