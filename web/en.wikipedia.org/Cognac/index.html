Cognac

Cognac (French pronunciation: [kɔɲak]) is a variety of brandy named after the commune of Cognac in western France. It is produced in the surrounding wine-growing region in the departments of Charente and Charente-Maritime. For a distilled brandy to bear the name Cognac, its production methods must meet certain legal requirements: it must be made from specified grape varieties, distilled twice in copper pot stills, and aged at least two years in French oak barrels from Limousin or Tronçais.

Production

The base wine, made mainly from the Ugni blanc grape, is thin and acidic and ill-suited to table use but excellent for distillation. After fermentation it is distilled twice in Charentais copper alembics; the heart of the second distillation, the bonne chauffe, becomes eau-de-vie of up to about 72% alcohol. The spirit is then aged in oak casks, where it extracts tannins and flavour and loses alcohol and volume — the "angels' share" — before being blended by a master blender (maître de chai) from eaux-de-vie of different ages and crus.

Grades and crus

The main quality grades are V.S (Very Special), aged at least two years; V.S.O.P (Very Superior Old Pale), at least four years; and X.O (Extra Old), at least ten years since a 2018 rule change. The region is divided into six crus, with Grande Champagne and Petite Champagne generally considered the finest; a blend of the two with at least half Grande Champagne may be labelled Fine Champagne. Major houses include Hennessy, Martell, Rémy Martin, and Courvoisier.

History

Dutch traders began distilling the region's wines in the 16th century so they would survive sea transport, and double distillation emerged in the 17th century. The trade grew rich through exports, survived the phylloxera devastation of the 1870s, and gained formal appellation protection in the 20th century. The great majority of cognac is exported, with the United States, Singapore, and China among the largest markets.
