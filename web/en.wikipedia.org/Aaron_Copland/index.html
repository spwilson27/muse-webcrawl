Aaron Copland (14 November 1900 – 2 December 1990) was an American composer, critic, writer, teacher, pianist and conductor of orchestras and other ensembles. The open, slowly changing harmonies in much of his music evoked for many listeners the vast American landscape and the pioneer spirit, and he is often called the "Dean of American Music".

Life and career

Copland was born in Brooklyn to Lithuanian Jewish immigrant parents. After early piano studies he travelled to Paris in 1921, becoming one of the first American students of Nadia Boulanger, who championed his early work. Returning to New York in 1924, he first wrote in a modernist, jazz-influenced idiom (for example the Piano Concerto, 1926), then turned in the 1930s to a simpler, more accessible style intended to reach a broad audience — the manner of his most famous works.

Major works

His ballets Billy the Kid (1938), Rodeo (1942) and Appalachian Spring (1944, written for Martha Graham and awarded the Pulitzer Prize) established his sound; the Fanfare for the Common Man (1942), later incorporated into his Third Symphony, became one of the best-known pieces of American orchestral music. He also wrote film scores (Of Mice and Men, The Red Pony, The Heiress, for which he won an Academy Award), the opera The Tender Land, and in later years works using twelve-tone techniques such as the Piano Fantasy, Connotations and Inscape.

Teaching and legacy

Copland was a central figure at Tanglewood, where he taught for decades, and an influential mentor to composers including Leonard Bernstein. His books What to Listen for in Music (1939) remain classics of music appreciation. Despite being investigated during the McCarthy era — his Lincoln Portrait was removed from Eisenhower's inaugural concert in 1953 — he received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1964 and continued conducting and promoting American music worldwide.
