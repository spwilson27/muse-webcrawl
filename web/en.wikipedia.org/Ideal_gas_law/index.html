Ideal gas law

The ideal gas law relates pressure, volume, temperature and amount of gas.

The ideal gas law, also called the general gas equation, is the equation of state of a hypothetical ideal gas. It is a good approximation of the behavior of many gases under many conditions, although it has several limitations. It was first stated by Benoît Paul Émile Clapeyron in 1834 as a combination of the empirical Boyle's law, Charles's law, Avogadro's law, and Gay-Lussac's law.

PV = nRT

Here P is the pressure of the gas, V is its volume, n is the amount of substance in moles, R is the ideal gas constant (8.314 J·mol−1·K−1), and T is the absolute temperature in kelvins.

Derivation and limitations

The law can be derived from the kinetic theory of gases by assuming that gas molecules are point particles that undergo only elastic collisions. Real gases deviate from ideal behavior at high pressures and low temperatures, where intermolecular forces and finite molecular size become important; the van der Waals equation introduces corrections for both effects. A molar form of the law can also be written using density: P = ρRT/M.

Applications

The ideal gas law underlies calculations in thermodynamics, atmospheric science, and chemical engineering, from determining the density of air at altitude to sizing pressure vessels and analyzing internal combustion engine cycles.
