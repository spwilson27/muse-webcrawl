Mass in the Catholic Church

The Mass is the central liturgical service of the Catholic Church, in which the Eucharist is celebrated. The term derives from the Latin dismissal Ite, missa est ("Go, it is the dismissal"). The Catholic Church teaches that in the Mass the sacrifice of Christ on the cross is made present, and that the consecrated bread and wine truly become the Body and Blood of Christ (transubstantiation).

Structure

The Mass comprises two principal parts. The Liturgy of the Word includes readings from Scripture, the homily, the Creed and intercessions. The Liturgy of the Eucharist includes the preparation of the gifts, the Eucharistic Prayer with the consecration, and Holy Communion. Introductory and concluding rites frame the service.

Forms of the Roman Rite

Since the Second Vatican Council, the ordinary form of the Roman Rite is the Mass of Paul VI (promulgated 1969), usually celebrated in the vernacular. The earlier Tridentine Mass, codified in 1570 and last revised in 1962, continues in use under conditions set by the Holy See. Eastern Catholic Churches celebrate their own liturgies, often called the Divine Liturgy.

Theology

The Church teaches that the Mass is both a sacrifice and a sacred meal: it re-presents (makes present) the one sacrifice of Calvary, is a memorial of Christ's death and resurrection, and nourishes the faithful with communion. Attendance at Mass on Sundays and holy days of obligation is obligatory for Catholics.
