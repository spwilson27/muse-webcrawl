Botulism

Specialty: Infectious disease, neurology
Cause: Botulinum toxin from Clostridium botulinum
Onset: 12–72 hours after exposure

Botulism is a rare but serious paralytic illness caused by a neurotoxin, botulinum toxin, produced by the bacterium Clostridium botulinum and related species. The toxin blocks the release of acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction, leading to flaccid paralysis. Botulism can occur in several forms: foodborne, wound, infant, and iatrogenic.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically begin 12 to 36 hours after exposure with double vision, blurred vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, difficulty swallowing, dry mouth, and muscle weakness. In infants, constipation, poor feeding, and a weak cry may appear, with a characteristic floppiness known as "floppy baby syndrome". Paralysis may spread to the muscles of respiration and can be fatal without ventilatory support.

Cause

Clostridium botulinum is an anaerobic, spore-forming bacterium found in soil and sediments. Its spores can survive boiling and germinate in low-acid, oxygen-free environments such as improperly home-canned foods, where the bacteria produce toxin. Foodborne botulism is most often associated with improperly preserved vegetables, fish, and meats. Wound botulism occurs when spores contaminate a wound, notably among people who inject drugs. Infant botulism results from ingesting spores, sometimes from honey, which then produce toxin in the immature gut.

Treatment and prevention

Treatment centers on early administration of antitoxin, which neutralizes circulating toxin but cannot reverse existing paralysis, and intensive supportive care, including mechanical ventilation. Antibiotics are used for wound botulism after antitoxin is given. Prevention relies on proper canning techniques, pressure cooking of low-acid foods, refrigerating infused oils, and not feeding honey to infants under one year of age. Botulinum toxin, in tiny controlled doses, is also used medically and cosmetically as Botox.
