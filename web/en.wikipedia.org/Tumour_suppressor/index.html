Tumor suppressor gene

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tumor suppressor gene (British spelling: tumour suppressor gene), or anti-oncogene, is a gene that regulates a cell during cell division and replication. If the cell grows uncontrollably, it will result in cancer. When a tumor suppressor gene is mutated, it results in a loss or reduction in its function; in combination with other genetic changes, this can allow the cell to grow abnormally.

Two-hit hypothesis

Unlike oncogenes, which act dominantly, tumor suppressor genes generally follow Alfred Knudson's two-hit hypothesis (1971): both alleles must be affected before an effect is manifested. Knudson developed the model from cases of retinoblastoma, in which children inheriting one defective copy of the RB1 gene developed tumours after a second, somatic mutation disabled the remaining copy.

Functions

Tumor suppressor proteins perform varied roles, including:

  • Repression of genes essential for progression through the cell cycle
  • Coupling the cell cycle to DNA damage: cells with damaged DNA do not divide until the damage is repaired
  • Initiation of apoptosis (programmed cell death) if DNA damage is irreparable
  • Cell adhesion and prevention of metastasis
  • DNA repair, keeping the genome stable

Examples

The most famous tumor suppressor is TP53, encoding the p53 protein — often called the "guardian of the genome" — which is mutated in more than half of human cancers. Others include RB1 (retinoblastoma), BRCA1 and BRCA2 (hereditary breast and ovarian cancer), APC (familial adenomatous polyposis), PTEN, NF1, and the cell-cycle inhibitors CDKN2A (p16) and p21.

Clinical significance

Loss of tumor suppressor function is a hallmark of cancer. Therapies cannot easily restore a lost gene, but synthetic-lethality approaches — such as PARP inhibitors in BRCA-mutant cancers — exploit the vulnerabilities created by suppressor loss.
