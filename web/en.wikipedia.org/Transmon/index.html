Transmon

In quantum computing, a transmon is a type of superconducting charge qubit designed to have reduced sensitivity to charge noise. Its name is an abbreviation of transmission line shunted plasma oscillation qubit. The transmon was developed by Robert J. Schoelkopf, Michel Devoret, Steven M. Girvin and collaborators at Yale University in 2007.

Design

The transmon achieves its insensitivity to charge noise by shunting the Josephson junction with an additional large capacitor, which greatly increases the ratio of the Josephson energy EJ to the charging energy EC. In this regime (EJ/EC of roughly 50–100), the qubit energy levels become nearly independent of offset charge, while the anharmonicity decreases only algebraically rather than exponentially. This trade-off allows the device to behave as a weakly anharmonic oscillator whose two lowest levels form the qubit.

Operation

Transmons are fabricated from superconducting materials such as aluminium and niobium using standard lithography, and are operated inside dilution refrigerators at millikelvin temperatures. They are typically coupled to microwave resonators used for control and readout via circuit quantum electrodynamics (circuit QED), with single-qubit gates performed by shaped microwave pulses and readout by dispersive measurement.

Use in quantum processors

Transmons are among the most widely used qubit types in industry: IBM's Quantum processors, Google's Sycamore processor (used in the 2019 quantum supremacy experiment), and Rigetti's chips are all based on transmon-like designs. Reported coherence times have improved from microseconds in early devices to several hundred microseconds in modern implementations.
