Queer theory

Queer theory is a field of critical theory that emerged in the early 1990s out of queer studies and women's studies. It is a critical, interdisciplinary field that centres on challenging heteronormativity and the idea that binary gender categories are natural, fixed, or universal. Queer theorists understand identity not as an essential, fixed property of a person but as something constructed, fluid, and performative. The term "queer theory" was first coined by Teresa de Lauretis for a conference at the University of California, Santa Cruz, in 1990.

History

Queer theory drew heavily on the work of Michel Foucault, whose History of Sexuality (1976) argued that sexuality is a product of discourse rather than a natural given, and on Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick's Epistemology of the Closet (1990), which examined the homo/heterosexual binary as a defining structure of modern Western thought. Judith Butler's Gender Trouble (1990) introduced the idea of gender performativity: gender is not something one is but something one does, enacted through repeated acts, gestures, and stylisations of the body.

Key concepts

Central concepts include heteronormativity — the assumption that heterosexuality is the default and preferred mode of sexual orientation — and the critique of binary oppositions such as male/female, gay/straight, and normal/deviant. Queer theory questions fixed categories of sex, gender, and sexuality and analyses how power and knowledge shape these categories. It also overlaps with the study of lesbian and gay identities, transgender studies, and intersections with race, class, and disability.

Criticism

Critics have argued that queer theory can be overly abstract, disconnected from material politics, and neglectful of the lived experiences of LGBTQ people. Others defend it as an important tool for denaturalising categories and opening political possibilities.
