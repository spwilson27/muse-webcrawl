Hemolytic anemia (or haemolytic anaemia) is a form of anemia due to hemolysis, the abnormal breakdown of red blood cells (RBCs), either in the blood vessels (intravascular hemolysis) or elsewhere in the human body (extravascular, usually in the spleen). This most commonly occurs within the spleen, but also can occur in the reticuloendothelial system or mechanically (prosthetic valve damage).

A red blood cell normally survives about 100–120 days. In hemolytic anemia the lifespan of the cells is shortened, and if bone marrow production cannot compensate, anemia results. Hemolytic anemia accounts for about 5% of all anemias.

Signs and symptoms

General symptoms of anemia include fatigue, pallor, breathlessness, and tachycardia. Features specific to hemolysis include jaundice from raised bilirubin, dark (tea-coloured) urine from hemoglobinuria, enlargement of the spleen (splenomegaly), and an increased risk of pigment gallstones. In chronic hemolysis, leg ulcers and skeletal changes may occur in children.

Causes

Causes are broadly divided into inherited (intrinsic) and acquired (extrinsic) disorders:

  • Membrane defects: hereditary spherocytosis, hereditary elliptocytosis.
  • Enzyme defects: glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, pyruvate kinase deficiency.
  • Hemoglobin defects: sickle cell disease, thalassemias.
  • Immune causes: autoimmune hemolytic anemia (warm and cold types), hemolytic disease of the newborn, transfusion reactions.
  • Other acquired causes: infections (malaria), drugs, toxins, microangiopathic processes (TTP, HUS, DIC), paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, and mechanical destruction from prosthetic heart valves.

Diagnosis

Laboratory findings include low hemoglobin with an increased reticulocyte count, raised lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), raised unconjugated bilirubin, and reduced or absent haptoglobin. A peripheral blood smear may show spherocytes, schistocytes, or bite cells depending on the cause. The direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test detects immune-mediated cases.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the cause and severity. Folic acid supplementation supports increased red cell production. Autoimmune cases may respond to corticosteroids, rituximab, or splenectomy. Blood transfusion is used for symptomatic or severe anemia. In hereditary spherocytosis, splenectomy can greatly reduce hemolysis. Avoiding triggers (such as oxidant drugs or fava beans in G6PD deficiency) is essential.
