Integrated pest management

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Integrated pest management (IPM), also known as integrated pest control (IPC), is a broad-based approach that integrates both chemical and non-chemical practices for the economic control of pests. IPM aims to suppress pest populations below the economic injury level, rather than to eradicate them entirely, and to minimize risks to people, beneficial organisms, and the environment.

Principles

The Food and Agriculture Organization defines IPM as "the careful consideration of all available pest control techniques and subsequent integration of appropriate measures that discourage the development of pest populations and keep pesticides and other interventions to levels that are economically justified and reduce or minimize risks to human health and the environment." Central concepts include monitoring pest populations, identifying pests correctly, and acting only when numbers exceed an action threshold.

Tactics

IPM combines several categories of control: cultural practices such as crop rotation, sanitation, and resistant varieties; mechanical and physical controls such as traps, barriers, and mulches; biological control using predators, parasitoids, and pathogens of the pest; and chemical control, used selectively and as a last resort, favouring targeted products that spare natural enemies. Pheromone traps and mating disruption are widely used in orchards, and sterile insect technique has eradicated pests such as the screwworm from large areas.

History

The concept emerged in the 1950s–1970s as over-reliance on synthetic insecticides produced resistance, pest resurgence, and secondary pest outbreaks. Entomologists including Vernon Stern and Ray F. Smith formalized the "integrated control" concept in 1959, introducing the economic injury level. Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962) heightened public awareness, and governments increasingly promoted IPM in agriculture, forestry, and public health.

Applications

IPM is applied in farming, horticulture, stored products, turf and ornamentals, and in structural and public-health pest control, such as mosquito management programmes.
