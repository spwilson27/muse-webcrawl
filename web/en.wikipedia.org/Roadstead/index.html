Roadstead

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A roadstead (or roads, as in " Hampton Roads") is a body of water sheltered from rip currents, spring tides, or ocean swells where ships can lie reasonably safely at anchor without dragging or snatching. It can be open or natural, usually—though not invariably—estuary-based, or may be created artificially. In maritime law it is defined as a "known general station for ships, notoriously used as such, and distinguished by the name".

Charts and nautical publications often use the word anchorage instead of roads or roadstead. A well-known example is the Downs in the English Channel off the coast of Kent, where sailing ships historically waited for favourable winds or pilots. Famous roadsteads include Spithead near Portsmouth and Hampton Roads in Virginia.

Characteristics

A roadstead differs from a harbour or port in that it typically offers less complete shelter: it may be exposed to some wind directions or swell, and it usually lacks wharves, quays, and cargo-handling facilities. Ships ride at anchor rather than berthing alongside. Roadsteads are nevertheless valuable as assembly points for convoys, as waiting areas for congested ports, and as anchorages where cargo can be transferred to and from lighters.

In law

For the purposes of several maritime conventions, a roadstead that is normally used for the loading, unloading, and anchoring of ships is treated as part of the territorial sea of the adjacent coastal state, even when it lies partly beyond the ordinary limit of that sea. This rule, reflected in the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, ensures that customary anchorages fall within coastal-state jurisdiction.
