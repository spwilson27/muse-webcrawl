Blue supergiant

A blue supergiant is a hot, luminous star, often referred to as an OB supergiant. They are usually of spectral class B (or occasionally O) and luminosity class I, having surface temperatures between about 10,000 and 50,000 K and luminosities from around 10,000 to more than a million times that of the Sun. Blue supergiants are among the most luminous stars known and represent a late stage in the evolution of high-mass stars.

Blue supergiants combine enormous luminosity with relatively compact radii compared to red supergiants.

Formation and evolution

Stars with initial masses above roughly 8–10 solar masses burn their core hydrogen rapidly and leave the main sequence within a few tens of millions of years. As the core contracts and heats, the outer layers expand and cool, carrying the star toward the red supergiant stage; depending on mass loss and internal mixing, a star may move back and forth across the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram, appearing as a blue supergiant one or more times. Blue supergiants are thought to include both stars evolving redward for the first time and stars that have already been red supergiants and are evolving back toward hotter temperatures after heavy mass loss.

Properties

Because of their mass and luminosity, blue supergiants have strong stellar winds, often losing mass at rates of millions of times that of the solar wind. Their spectra are frequently marked by emission features (P Cygni profiles) from these outflows. Radii are typically tens to a few hundred times that of the Sun—large, but much more compact than red supergiants of comparable mass. Many blue supergiants are variable, including the Alpha Cygni variables and some luminous blue variables, which undergo dramatic eruptions.

Examples and supernovae

Rigel (Beta Orionis), the brightest star in Orion, is a blue supergiant about 79 solar radii across and over 100,000 times as luminous as the Sun. Alnilam, the middle star of Orion's Belt, is another prominent example. Notably, the progenitor of Supernova 1987A in the Large Magellanic Cloud, Sanduleak −69° 202, was a blue supergiant—contrary to the prevailing expectation that Type II supernovae came from red supergiants—which spurred revision of late-stage evolution models for massive stars.

Fate

Blue supergiants eventually exhaust their nuclear fuel and collapse, ending their lives as core-collapse supernovae, or in the most massive cases collapsing directly into black holes. Their winds and explosions enrich the interstellar medium with heavy elements.
