Carbon capture and storage (CCS) is a process in which carbon dioxide (CO2) from industrial and energy sources is separated, transported, and stored underground so that it does not enter the atmosphere. It is proposed as one means of mitigating climate change while fossil fuels continue to be used. When the CO2 is used rather than simply stored, the process is called carbon capture and utilisation (CCU); the combined term is CCUS.

Capture

Three main capture routes exist: post-combustion capture, separating CO2 from flue gases (typically with amine solvents); pre-combustion capture, gasifying fuel to produce hydrogen and a concentrated CO2 stream; and oxy-fuel combustion, burning fuel in pure oxygen. Capture is energy-intensive and adds significantly to plant costs — the so-called "energy penalty".

Transport and storage

Captured CO2 is compressed, usually to a supercritical fluid, and moved by pipeline or ship to a storage site. There it is injected deep underground into porous rock formations capped by impermeable layers — depleted oil and gas reservoirs or deep saline aquifers — where it is expected to remain trapped for thousands of years. Norway's Sleipner project, storing CO2 beneath the North Sea since 1996, is a long-running example. Critics point to high costs, slow deployment, leakage risks, and the concern that CCS prolongs dependence on fossil fuels.
