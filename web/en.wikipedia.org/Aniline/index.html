Aniline

Aniline (also known as phenylamine) is an organic compound with the formula C6H5NH2. Consisting of a phenyl group attached to an amino group, aniline is the simplest aromatic amine. It is a colourless to pale yellow oily liquid, industrial in importance as the principal precursor to polyurethane and a versatile starting material for dyes, drugs, and other chemicals. Being a volatile and toxic compound, aniline must be handled with care; it is readily absorbed through the skin and can cause methaemoglobinaemia.

Production

Aniline is produced industrially in two steps from benzene: nitration yields nitrobenzene, which is then reduced (typically by catalytic hydrogenation) to aniline. Global production runs to millions of tonnes annually.

Uses

The largest application of aniline is in the manufacture of methylene diphenyl diisocyanate (MDI), used to make polyurethane foams. Aniline is also a precursor to rubber processing chemicals, herbicides, pigments, pharmaceuticals such as paracetamol (via intermediates), and the conducting polymer polyaniline. Historically, aniline launched the synthetic dye industry: in 1856 the eighteen-year-old William Henry Perkin, attempting to synthesize quinine, oxidized aniline and obtained the purple dye mauveine — the first synthetic organic dye, which founded a vast coal-tar colour industry.

History and structure

Aniline was first isolated from indigo in 1826 by Otto Unverdorben (who called it "Crystallin") and later from coal tar; its name derives from anil, the Portuguese name for the indigo plant. In aniline the nitrogen lone pair is delocalized into the benzene ring, making it much less basic than aliphatic amines and rendering the ring strongly activated toward electrophilic substitution.

Safety

Aniline is toxic by inhalation, skin contact, and ingestion, and is a suspected carcinogen; occupational exposure is strictly regulated.
