Greatest common divisor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, the greatest common divisor (GCD), also called the greatest common factor or highest common factor, of two or more integers is the largest positive integer that divides each of the integers. For example, the GCD of 48 and 180 is 12. Two numbers with GCD 1 are said to be coprime. The GCD is fundamental in number theory and is used for reducing fractions to lowest terms.

Computation

From prime factorisations, the GCD takes each common prime to the minimum exponent — though factorisation is impractical for large numbers. The standard method is the Euclidean algorithm, described in Euclid's Elements (c. 300 BC): repeatedly replace the larger number by the remainder when the larger is divided by the smaller, until the remainder is zero; the last non-zero remainder is the GCD. For example, gcd(48, 180): 180 = 3·48 + 36; 48 = 1·36 + 12; 36 = 3·12 + 0, so the GCD is 12. The extended Euclidean algorithm also finds integers x and y with ax + by = gcd(a, b) (Bézout's identity), which is essential in modular arithmetic and cryptography such as RSA key generation.

Properties and generalisations

The GCD satisfies gcd(a, b) · lcm(a, b) = |ab|, linking it to the least common multiple. The concept generalises to polynomials — where Euclid's algorithm works analogously — and to any Euclidean domain, and abstractly the GCD is characterised as the unique (up to units) common divisor divisible by every other common divisor. In geometry, gcd(m, n) counts lattice points on the diagonal of an m × n rectangle.
