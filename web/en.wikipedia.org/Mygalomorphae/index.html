The Mygalomorphae, or mygalomorphs, are an infraorder of spiders characterised by chelicerae whose fangs point straight down and move parallel to one another (paraxial), striking like pickaxes, in contrast to the pincer-like fangs of araneomorph spiders. The group includes the heavy-bodied ground spiders, trapdoor spiders, funnel-web spiders and the tarantulas.

Characteristics

Mygalomorphs typically have two pairs of book lungs and spinnerets that are generally shorter than those of araneomorphs. Most are burrowers or ground-dwellers, often living for many years in silk-lined burrows; females of some tarantula species can live for several decades. Their silk use is comparatively simple — burrow linings, trapdoors, sheet webs — rather than aerial prey-catching webs.

Families

Well-known families include Theraphosidae (tarantulas), Atracidae (Australian funnel-web spiders, whose venom can be medically significant), Ctenizidae and Idiopidae (trapdoor spiders), and Atypidae (purseweb spiders). The infraorder is ancient, with a fossil record extending back to the Triassic.
