Diuretic

Diuretics

Class: Drugs increasing urine production

Main uses: Hypertension, heart failure, edema

Major types: Thiazides, loop diuretics, potassium-sparing

Examples: Hydrochlorothiazide, furosemide, spironolactone

A diuretic is any substance that promotes diuresis, the increased production of urine. Diuretics work by reducing the reabsorption of sodium and water in the kidneys, so that more fluid is excreted. They are among the most widely prescribed drug classes, used principally to treat hypertension (high blood pressure), heart failure, and edematous conditions such as those caused by liver cirrhosis and kidney disease.

Types

  • Thiazide diuretics (e.g., hydrochlorothiazide) act on the distal convoluted tubule and are first-line therapy for hypertension.
  • Loop diuretics (e.g., furosemide) act on the loop of Henle and are the most potent, used for acute fluid overload in heart failure.
  • Potassium-sparing diuretics (e.g., spironolactone, amiloride) reduce sodium reabsorption in the collecting duct without wasting potassium; spironolactone also blocks aldosterone.
  • Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (e.g., acetazolamide) are weak diuretics used mainly for glaucoma and altitude sickness.
  • Osmotic diuretics (e.g., mannitol) are used to reduce intracranial and intraocular pressure.

Side effects

Common adverse effects include dehydration, electrolyte disturbances — notably low potassium (hypokalemia) with thiazide and loop diuretics, and high potassium with potassium-sparing agents — low blood pressure, and gout precipitated by raised uric acid. Electrolytes and kidney function are monitored during therapy.

Non-drug diuretics

Some foods and drinks have mild diuretic effects, including caffeine, alcohol, and cranberry juice. Alcohol inhibits antidiuretic hormone (vasopressin), contributing to increased urine output. Diuretics are also misused in sport and weight-class activities, and many appear on the World Anti-Doping Agency prohibited list as masking agents.
