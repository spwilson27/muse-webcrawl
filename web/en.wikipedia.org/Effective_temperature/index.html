Effective temperature

The effective temperature of a body such as a star or planet is the temperature of a black body that would emit the same total amount of electromagnetic radiation. It is a fundamental parameter in astrophysics, used to classify stars and to estimate planetary surface conditions.

Stars

For a star of luminosity L and radius R, the effective temperature Teff is defined by the Stefan–Boltzmann law:

L = 4πR2σTeff4,

where σ is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant. The Sun's effective temperature is about 5,778 K. Effective temperature correlates with a star's colour and spectral type — bluer stars are hotter — and together with luminosity it places a star on the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram. It is not a temperature measured at any single layer: the visible surface of a star spans a range of temperatures, and Teff is the characteristic value that reproduces the total emitted flux.

Planets

For a planet, the effective temperature is the equilibrium temperature it would have as a black body absorbing sunlight and re-radiating it as heat, ignoring atmospheric effects. It depends on the star's luminosity, the planet's distance, and its albedo. Earth's effective temperature is about 255 K — well below its actual average surface temperature of about 288 K, the difference being the warming due to the greenhouse effect. Comparing a planet's effective and actual temperatures is a standard way to characterise its atmosphere and its potential habitability.
