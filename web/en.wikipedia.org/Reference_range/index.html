Reference range

A reference range conventionally covers the central 95% of values found in a healthy reference population.

In medicine and health-related fields, a reference range or reference interval is the range of values for a physiological measurement in healthy persons — for example, the concentration of a substance in blood. It serves as a basis for a physician or other health professional to interpret a set of test results for a particular patient. A standard reference range is usually defined as the interval between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of measurements in a reference population, so that by construction about 5% of healthy people have results outside it on any given test.

Establishing reference ranges

Laboratories establish reference intervals by testing samples from carefully selected healthy volunteers, following guidelines such as those of the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry (IFCC) and the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). Ranges often differ by age, sex and other factors, and may vary between laboratories because of differences in instruments and methods, which is why reports print the local reference interval beside each result. Some quantities, such as cholesterol, are interpreted against clinical decision limits derived from outcome studies rather than population percentiles.

Interpretation

A result outside the reference range is not by itself evidence of disease, and a result within it does not guarantee health: the ranges overlap between healthy and diseased populations, and the more tests performed, the more likely some fall outside by chance. Clinicians therefore interpret values in context, considering the degree of deviation, previous results for the same patient, and the sensitivity and specificity of the test.

Examples

Familiar reference ranges include fasting blood glucose (about 3.9–5.5 mmol/L), serum sodium (135–145 mmol/L), haemoglobin (roughly 120–155 g/L in women and 130–170 g/L in men), and thyroid-stimulating hormone (about 0.4–4.0 mIU/L), though exact figures vary by source and laboratory.
