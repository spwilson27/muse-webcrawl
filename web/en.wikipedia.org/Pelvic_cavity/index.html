Pelvic cavity


The pelvic cavity is enclosed by the bones of the pelvis.

The pelvic cavity is a body cavity defined by the bones of the pelvis at the lower end of the trunk. Its sloping walls are formed by the sacrum, coccyx, and hip bones, and its floor is the pelvic diaphragm (levator ani and coccygeus muscles). It is continuous above with the abdominal cavity through the pelvic inlet (superior pelvic aperture) and is bounded below by the pelvic outlet.

Contents

The cavity contains the urinary bladder, the rectum, the internal genital organs — in females the uterus, fallopian tubes, ovaries, and vagina; in males the prostate and seminal vesicles — together with loops of small intestine and the sigmoid colon, and the vessels, nerves, and lymphatics supplying them. The peritoneum drapes over its upper contents, forming pouches such as the recto-uterine pouch (of Douglas) in females.

Clinical significance

The pelvic cavity is examined in gynaecology, urology, and colorectal surgery; fluid collecting in its most dependent recesses can be sampled or imaged, and its bony dimensions are important in obstetrics, where the shape of the female pelvis determines the mechanics of childbirth.
