Drosera, commonly known as the sundews, is one of the largest genera of carnivorous plants, with at least 194 species. Members of the family Droseraceae, sundews lure, capture, and digest insects using stalked mucilaginous glands covering their leaves, the glistening droplets of mucilage resembling morning dew—hence the common name.

Trap mechanism

Each leaf bears tentacles tipped with glands that secrete sticky mucilage, sometimes sweetened to attract prey. When an insect becomes stuck, neighbouring tentacles bend toward it and the leaf may slowly curl around it over minutes to hours. Digestive enzymes and acids secreted by sessile glands dissolve the prey, and the leaf absorbs the released nutrients, supplementing the poor soils in which sundews grow.

Distribution and habitat

Sundews occur on every continent except Antarctica, with the greatest diversity in Australia, which is home to about half of all species, including the pygmy sundews and tuberous sundews. They inhabit wetlands, bogs, and sandy or peaty soils. Forms range from tiny rosettes a centimetre across to climbing species such as Drosera erythrogyne, among the longest carnivorous plants.

Cultivation and uses

Sundews such as Drosera capensis are popular in cultivation. Historically, sundews were used in folk medicine and even to curdle milk in parts of Scandinavia.
