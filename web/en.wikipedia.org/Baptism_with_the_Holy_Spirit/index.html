Baptism with the Holy Spirit

In Christian theology, baptism with the Holy Spirit refers to a religious experience in which a believer is immersed in or filled with the Holy Spirit. The term originates in the New Testament, where John the Baptist contrasts his water baptism with the one who would "baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire" (Matthew 3:11). The event of Pentecost, described in Acts 2, is commonly taken as the first instance of the experience.

Interpretations

Interpretations vary widely between traditions. In Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity, baptism in the Holy Spirit is generally understood as a distinct experience subsequent to conversion, often evidenced by speaking in tongues (glossolalia). In other traditions, including much of evangelical and mainline Protestant theology, the baptism of the Spirit is identified with regeneration and incorporation into the church at conversion, sometimes drawing on 1 Corinthians 12:13. Catholic theology associates the gift of the Spirit primarily with the sacraments of baptism and confirmation, while acknowledging charismatic renewal movements.

History

The modern emphasis on a distinct baptism of the Holy Spirit developed through the Holiness movement of the 19th century and the Pentecostal revival of the early 20th century, including the Azusa Street Revival of 1906. The charismatic movement of the 1960s carried the teaching into mainline denominations.
