International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights

The International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR) is a multilateral treaty adopted by the United Nations General Assembly on 16 December 1966 through Resolution 2200A (XXI). It commits its states parties to work toward granting economic, social and cultural rights to the individuals under their jurisdiction, including labour rights and the rights to health, education and an adequate standard of living. Together with the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, it forms the International Bill of Human Rights. The Covenant entered into force on 3 January 1976.

Contents of the Covenant

Part I recognises the right of all peoples to self-determination. Part II sets out the principle of progressive realisation: each state party undertakes to take steps "to the maximum of its available resources" toward achieving the full realisation of the rights recognised, without discrimination. Part III lists the substantive rights: work and just and favourable conditions of work; trade union rights; social security; family life and protection of mothers and children; an adequate standard of living, including food, clothing and housing, and freedom from hunger; the highest attainable standard of physical and mental health; education, with free and compulsory primary education; and participation in cultural life and enjoyment of the benefits of scientific progress.

Monitoring

Compliance is monitored by the Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, a body of independent experts that examines periodic reports submitted by states parties. An Optional Protocol, adopted in 2008 and in force since 2013, allows the Committee to receive complaints from individuals and conduct inquiries into grave or systematic violations.

Reservations and signatories

As of recent counts, the Covenant has over 170 parties. The United States signed the Covenant in 1977 but has not ratified it. Several states have entered reservations interpreting certain provisions in line with their domestic constitutions or religious law.
