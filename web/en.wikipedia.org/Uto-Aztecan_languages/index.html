The Uto-Aztecan languages are a family of Indigenous languages of the Americas, spoken primarily in the western United States and Mexico, with about thirty living languages. The northernmost language, Shoshoni, is spoken in Idaho, and the southernmost, Pipil, in El Salvador. The family's best-known member is Nahuatl, the language of the Aztec Empire.

Classification

The family divides into northern branches — Numic (Shoshoni, Comanche, Ute, Paiute), Takic (Luiseño, Cahuilla), Tübatulabal and Hopi — and southern branches in Mexico, including Tepiman (O'odham), Taracahitic (Tarahumara, Yaqui, Mayo), Corachol (Cora, Huichol) and Aztecan (Nahuatl and its close relatives).

Homeland and history

Scholars debate whether Proto-Uto-Aztecan was spoken in the southwestern United States or in Mesoamerica. Vocabulary for maize agriculture in the proto-language has led many to favour a homeland in or near Mesoamerica roughly 4,000–5,000 years ago, with northward expansions carrying Numic languages across the Great Basin around a thousand years ago.

Features

Uto-Aztecan languages are typically agglutinative, with subject-object-verb or subject-verb-object order, vowel systems often including a high central vowel, and in Nahuatl a famous absolutive suffix (-tl/-tli). Many of the languages are endangered, though Nahuatl retains well over a million speakers.
