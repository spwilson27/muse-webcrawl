Reaction wheel

A reaction wheel is a type of flywheel used in spacecraft to control their orientation (attitude) without using thrusters or expending propellant. By accelerating or decelerating an electrically driven wheel, the spacecraft rotates in the opposite direction, in accordance with conservation of angular momentum. Reaction wheels allow precise pointing for telescopes, cameras and antennas.

Operation

Three wheels mounted on orthogonal axes provide control about all three axes; a fourth, skewed wheel is often added for redundancy. Torque is proportional to the rate of change of wheel speed, while total stored momentum limits how far the craft can slew before the wheels saturate. Saturated wheels are "desaturated" (momentum dumping) using magnetorquers acting against a planet's magnetic field, or small thrusters. Related devices called momentum wheels run at a near-constant high speed to give gyroscopic stiffness, and control moment gyroscopes tilt spinning rotors for much larger torques.

Failures and reliability

Bearing friction and lubricant degradation make reaction wheels a common failure point on long missions. The Hubble Space Telescope's pointing depends on rate-sensing gyroscopes alongside its wheels; the Kepler space telescope's prime mission ended in 2013 after two of its four reaction wheels failed, though engineers later stabilised it using solar radiation pressure in the K2 mission. Dawn and other deep-space probes have likewise adapted to wheel failures.
