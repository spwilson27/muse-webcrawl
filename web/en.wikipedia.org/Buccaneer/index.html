Buccaneers were a kind of privateer or free sailor peculiar to the Caribbean Sea during the 17th and 18th centuries. First established on the island of Hispaniola as hunters supplying meat to ship crews, they began raiding Spanish shipping and settlements, operating largely against Spain. Their strongholds included the island of Tortuga and later Port Royal in Jamaica. Famous buccaneers include Henry Morgan, François l'Olonnais, and Bartholomew Sharp.

Etymology

The word "buccaneer" derives from the French boucanier, from boucan, a wooden frame used for smoking meat. The term came from the Arawak word buccan for such a frame. The original buccaneers were hunters — often French, English, and Dutch settlers and runaway servants — who hunted feral cattle and pigs on Hispaniola and smoked the meat on boucans to sell to passing ships.

History

Spanish efforts to drive the hunters off Hispaniola pushed many of them to the island of Tortuga and into outright raiding. In the mid-17th century the buccaneers organized into groups sometimes called the Brethren of the Coast, governed by customary articles dividing plunder and compensating injuries. England, France, and the Netherlands at times encouraged them as a cheap weapon against Spain, issuing letters of marque. Henry Morgan's raids on Porto Bello (1668), Maracaibo, and Panama City (1671) were among the most famous exploits. After the Treaty of Ryswick (1697) and changing colonial policy, the buccaneering era wound down, giving way in the early 18th century to the "golden age" pirates, many of whom were distinct from the buccaneers proper.

In popular culture

The buccaneers were romanticized in Alexandre Olivier Exquemelin's De Americaensche Zee-Roovers (1678), one of the earliest and most influential accounts, translated as The Buccaneers of America. Their exploits inspired later pirate fiction, including Rafael Sabatini's Captain Blood.
