Portuguese Macau

Portuguese Macau refers to the period in which Macau was under Portuguese administration, from the establishment of a permanent Portuguese settlement in 1557 until the handover of sovereignty to the People's Republic of China on 20 December 1999. For much of that time Macau was the principal European trading entrepôt in East Asia and the longest-lived European outpost in the region.

Establishment and golden age

Portuguese traders first reached the China coast in 1513; in 1557 they secured permission from Ming officials to settle at Macau, paying an annual ground rent, nominally in return for suppressing piracy. The settlement flourished on the trade between China, Japan, and Europe — Chinese silk exchanged for Japanese silver — and became a centre of Catholic missions, from which figures such as Matteo Ricci entered China. The Senate (Leal Senado), established in 1583, governed municipal affairs. Macau's prosperity declined after the Dutch seizure of Malacca, Japan's expulsion of the Portuguese in 1639, and the rise of Hong Kong in the nineteenth century.

Colonial period and handover

Portugal asserted fuller colonial control in the nineteenth century, ceasing the ground rent in 1849; the 1887 Treaty of Peking recognised Portugal's "perpetual occupation and government" of Macau, though China never accepted it as a full cession. Neutral Macau was a haven of intrigue during the Second World War. After the 1966 "12-3" riots, Portuguese authority was gravely weakened in practice. Following the Carnation Revolution, Portugal offered to return Macau, but China deferred; the 1987 Sino-Portuguese Joint Declaration set the handover for 1999 under the "one country, two systems" formula, making Macau a Special Administrative Region of China. The Historic Centre of Macao, with its blend of Portuguese and Chinese architecture, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
