Learning by doing

Learning by doing is an educational theory and practice in which learners acquire knowledge, skills and understanding through direct experience and active participation rather than through passive reception of information. The idea is closely associated with the American philosopher and educational reformer John Dewey, who argued that education should be grounded in experience and that reflection on experience is what turns activity into learning.

In economics

In economics, "learning by doing" refers to productivity gains that arise from cumulative production experience. Formalised by Kenneth Arrow in a 1962 paper, the concept explains how workers and firms become more efficient as they repeat tasks, driving down unit costs along a "learning curve". It is a standard mechanism in models of endogenous growth and industrial policy, including arguments for temporary protection of infant industries.

In education

Educational applications include experiential learning cycles (such as David Kolb's model of concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualisation and active experimentation), apprenticeships, laboratory work, simulations, internships and maker education. Research generally finds that active engagement improves retention and transfer compared with lectures alone, though unstructured discovery without guidance can be ineffective for novices.
