Witness

A witness taking an oath before testifying in court.

In law, a witness is someone who has knowledge about a matter of relevance to a legal proceeding and gives testimony — a formal statement of what they know — either orally under oath or affirmation, or in a written form such as an affidavit or deposition. A witness might be compelled to attend court by a subpoena. A witness who testifies falsely under oath commits perjury.

Most witnesses are "fact witnesses" who testify about what they personally saw, heard, or otherwise perceived. Expert witnesses, by contrast, are permitted to give opinions within their field of expertise — for example forensic scientists, doctors, or engineers — to help the court understand technical matters. A person who witnesses the signing of a document, such as a will or contract, may also act as an attesting witness, confirming that the signature is genuine.

Competency and compellability

Legal systems set rules about who may testify. Historically, various groups — children, people with certain convictions, interested parties, and spouses — were excluded or restricted; modern law generally presumes competence, with limited exceptions. Children and vulnerable witnesses may give evidence through special measures such as video links, screens, or pre-recorded interviews. Witnesses are usually compellable: they can be required to testify and may face contempt of court for refusing, though privileges (for example, legal professional privilege, spousal privilege in some jurisdictions, and the privilege against self-incrimination) can protect particular communications or answers.

Procedure

At trial, a witness is typically examined first by the party that called them (examination-in-chief or direct examination), then cross-examined by the opposing party, and sometimes re-examined. Rules of evidence restrict leading questions, hearsay, and irrelevant or prejudicial material. The credibility of a witness — their consistency, memory, possible bias, and demeanour — is often central to the outcome of a case.

Reliability of eyewitness testimony

Psychological research, notably by Elizabeth Loftus and others, has shown that eyewitness memory is fallible and reconstructive: it can be distorted by suggestion, post-event information, stress, and the passage of time. Mistaken eyewitness identification is a leading cause of wrongful convictions later overturned by DNA evidence. Reforms proposed or adopted in response include double-blind lineup administration, sequential lineups, and recording of identifications.

Witness protection and intimidation

Witnesses in cases involving organised crime or serious violence may be at risk of intimidation or retaliation. Many jurisdictions operate witness protection programmes — the best known being the United States Federal Witness Security Program (WITSEC), established in 1970 — that can relocate witnesses and provide new identities. Tampering with or intimidating a witness is a criminal offence in most legal systems. Anonymous testimony, in which the witness's identity is withheld, is permitted only exceptionally because of the defendant's right to confront accusers.

Religious and other uses

Outside the courtroom, the word "witness" is used in religion for one who proclaims or testifies to a faith (as in the Christian usage behind the name Jehovah's Witnesses, and in the concept of martyrdom, from the Greek martys, "witness"). More generally, a witness is simply someone who observes an event, as in "witness to history".
