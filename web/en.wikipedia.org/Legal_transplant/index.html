Legal transplant

A legal transplant is the moving of a rule or a system of law from one country to another, or from one people to another. The term was popularised by the comparative legal scholar Alan Watson in his 1974 book Legal Transplants: An Approach to Comparative Law, which argued that borrowing has been the most fertile source of legal change throughout history and that rules often travel easily between very different societies.

Historical examples

The reception of Roman law in medieval and early modern Europe is the classic example: Roman civil law concepts spread from Italy through universities to much of the continent and, through colonisation, to Latin America, parts of Africa, and Asia. In the 19th century, the French Napoleonic Code was exported across Europe and beyond, while the German Civil Code influenced codifications in Japan, China, Brazil, and elsewhere. Turkey's adoption of the Swiss Civil Code in 1926 and Japan's successive borrowing of Chinese, French, German, and American law are frequently studied cases. More recently, transitions in post-communist states and the drafting of commercial laws in developing economies have involved extensive transplantation, often promoted by international institutions.

Debates

Watson's thesis that transplants are "socially easy" provoked criticism. Pierre Legrand argued that legal rules cannot travel at all, because a rule's meaning is inseparable from the culture that produced it; only a "meaningless form of words" crosses the border. Otto Kahn-Freund drew a spectrum from rules that transfer easily (mechanical) to those bound tightly to political structures. Others, such as Gunther Teubner, reframed transplants as "legal irritants" that trigger new, unpredictable developments in the host system. Empirical work on the "transplant effect" suggests borrowed law works best when adapted to local conditions and supported by domestic demand.
