Synecdoche

Synecdoche (pronounced si-NEK-də-kee) is a figure of speech in which a part of something is used to refer to the whole, or the whole is used to refer to a part. It is closely related to metonymy, and the two are often grouped together, but synecdoche specifically exploits a part–whole (or genus–species) relationship, whereas metonymy relies on broader associations.

The word comes from the Greek synekdochē, meaning "simultaneous understanding".

Types and examples

  • Part for the whole: "all hands on deck" (hands = sailors); "new wheels" (wheels = a car); "fifty head of cattle".
  • Whole for the part: "the police" for particular officers; "the law" arriving at a door.
  • Genus for species: "bread" standing for food in general, as in "earn one's bread".
  • Species for genus: calling every photocopy a "Xerox" or every facial tissue a "Kleenex".
  • Material for the object: "ivory" for piano keys, "pigskin" for an American football, "threads" for clothing.

Use in literature and rhetoric

Synecdoche is common in poetry, oratory, and everyday language because the part stands out vividly and compresses the expression. Shakespeare's "Friends, Romans, countrymen, lend me your ears" uses ears for the audience's attention. Critics have debated its boundaries with metonymy since classical rhetoric; Kenneth Burke counted both among his "four master tropes".

In film and visual media

Cinematographers use synecdoche visually: a close-up of marching boots for an army, or of hands on a plough for a farmer's life. The 2008 film Synecdoche, New York, written and directed by Charlie Kaufman, plays on the term throughout, and puns on the name of Schenectady, New York.
