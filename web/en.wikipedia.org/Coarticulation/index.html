Coarticulation

Coarticulation in its general sense refers to a situation in which a conceptually isolated part of an articulated system, such as a phoneme, is influenced by another conceptual part, for instance the preceding or following phoneme in speech. In phonetics and phonology, coarticulation is the overlapping of adjacent articulations in speech, so that a speech sound takes on characteristics of neighbouring sounds because the articulators — lips, tongue, velum, jaw — move smoothly from one target to the next rather than stopping at discrete positions.

Types

Coarticulation may be anticipatory (or right-to-left), where a feature of a later sound appears early — for example, lip rounding for the vowel in "soon" beginning during the initial consonant — or carryover (left-to-right, perseverative), where a feature of an earlier sound persists, such as nasalization of a vowel following a nasal consonant. Coarticulation can involve features such as voicing, place of articulation, nasality, and lip rounding.

Theoretical accounts

Coarticulation is a central challenge for models of speech production, because it shows that speech is not produced as a string of invariant, bead-like segments. Models such as the task-dynamic approach of articulatory phonology treat gestures as the basic units of speech and derive coarticulation from their temporal overlap. The degree of coarticulation varies between languages: some languages, such as Swedish and Russian, permit extensive vowel-to-vowel coarticulation across consonants, a property termed coarticulatory resistance or aggressiveness.

In speech technology

Speech synthesis systems must model coarticulation to sound natural; concatenative synthesizers historically used diphones, which capture transitions mid-segment, precisely to preserve coarticulatory effects. Automatic speech recognition likewise benefits from context-dependent phone models such as triphones.
