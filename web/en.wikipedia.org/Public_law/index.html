Public law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Public law is the part of law that governs relations between legal persons and a government, between different institutions within a government, and between individuals who are of direct concern to society. Public law comprises constitutional law, administrative law, tax law and criminal law, as well as all procedural law. Laws concerning relationships among individuals belong to private law.

The distinction between public and private law dates to Roman law, where Ulpian described public law as that which concerns the Roman state and private law as that which concerns the utility of individuals. The distinction remains important in civil law jurisdictions and in the organisation of legal education and courts.

Branches

Constitutional law

Constitutional law defines the structure of the state, the powers of its branches and the rights of citizens. It is often embodied in a written constitution enforced by a constitutional or supreme court through judicial review.

Administrative law

Administrative law governs the activities of the agencies of government, including rulemaking, adjudication and enforcement, and provides procedures — such as judicial review of administrative action — by which citizens can challenge the exercise of public power.

Criminal law

Criminal law concerns conduct considered harmful to society as a whole; offences are prosecuted by the state and punished by imprisonment, fines or other sanctions. Because the state is a party and liberty is at stake, criminal procedure carries heightened safeguards for the accused.

Tax law

Tax law governs the assessment and collection of taxes by public authorities and the rights of taxpayers in disputes with them.

International public law

Public international law governs relations between states and international organisations, and is distinct from private international law (conflict of laws), which concerns private disputes with a foreign element.
