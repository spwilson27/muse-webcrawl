Longshore drift

Longshore drift, also called longshore transport or littoral drift, is a geological process by which sediment such as sand and shingle is transported along a coast parallel to the shoreline. It depends on incoming wind direction, which determines wave approach, and is driven by the longshore current in the surf zone.

Mechanism

Waves approaching at an angle push sediment up the beach in the swash at that angle; the backwash returns straight down the steepest gradient under gravity. The result is a zigzag movement of material along the shore. Where the coast changes direction, sediment may accumulate as spits, bars and barrier beaches — for example at river mouths and headlands.

Human impact

Groynes are built to interrupt drift and hold beaches in place, though they can starve beaches downdrift, increasing erosion there. Harbour entrances and breakwaters also alter drift patterns, sometimes requiring dredging or sand bypassing.
