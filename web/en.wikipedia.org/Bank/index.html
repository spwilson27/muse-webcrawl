Bank

A bank is a financial institution that accepts deposits from the public and creates a demand deposit while simultaneously making loans. Lending activities can be directly performed by the bank or indirectly through capital markets. Banks are regulated in most countries and typically hold a banking license issued by a national banking regulator or central bank.

History

Modern banking practices evolved in Renaissance Italy, where merchant bankers of Florence, Venice, and Genoa — most famously the Medici — developed double-entry bookkeeping, bills of exchange, and correspondent networks. The Bank of Amsterdam (1609) and the Bank of England (1694) pioneered deposit banking and note issuance on a public model, laying the groundwork for central banking.

Activities and services

Banks take deposits in current/checking and savings accounts, extend credit through loans, mortgages, and overdrafts, process payments by cheque, card, and electronic transfer, and exchange currencies. Investment banks underwrite securities and advise on mergers, while central banks issue currency and conduct monetary policy. Through fractional-reserve lending, banks collectively expand the money supply.

Risks and regulation

Banks face credit risk, liquidity risk, and interest-rate risk; a loss of depositor confidence can trigger a bank run. Governments therefore impose reserve and capital requirements, supervise institutions, provide deposit insurance (such as the U.S. FDIC), and act as lender of last resort through central banks. The 2008 financial crisis prompted stricter global standards under the Basel III framework.
