Cellular respiration is a set of metabolic processes by which cells break down nutrient molecules — principally glucose — to produce adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the main energy currency of the cell. In aerobic respiration, oxygen is the final electron acceptor and the products are carbon dioxide and water; in its absence, cells may carry out fermentation, regenerating NAD⁺ with a much smaller energy yield.

Oxidative phosphorylation

NADH and FADH₂ donate electrons to the electron transport chain, which pumps protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane, creating a proton gradient (chemiosmosis). ATP synthase uses the flow of protons back into the matrix to phosphorylate ADP to ATP — the mechanism elucidated by Peter Mitchell.

Anaerobic alternatives

Some organisms use electron acceptors other than oxygen (e.g., nitrate, sulfate) in anaerobic respiration. Fermentation — lactic acid fermentation in muscle and ethanol fermentation in yeast — yields only the 2 net ATP from glycolysis but is fast and oxygen-independent.

Significance

Cellular respiration links the breakdown of carbohydrates, fats, and proteins to a common energy pool and, together with photosynthesis, forms the basis of the global carbon cycle. Defects in respiration underlie mitochondrial diseases, and the pathway is a target of numerous poisons (cyanide, carbon monoxide) and drugs.
