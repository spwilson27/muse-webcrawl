The Belém Tower (Portuguese: Torre de Belém), officially the Tower of Saint Vincent (Torre de São Vicente), is a sixteenth-century fortification in the parish of Belém, Lisbon, Portugal. Built between 1514 and 1519 to guard the entrance to Lisbon's harbour on the Tagus river, it is the outstanding surviving example of the Portuguese Manueline style and a symbol of Portugal's Age of Discoveries. Together with the nearby Jerónimos Monastery, it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1983.

History

King Manuel I commissioned the tower to replace an anchored carrack that had defended the Tagus estuary. Designed by the military architect Francisco de Arruda, it was originally built on an island in the river; silting and the shifting of the channel after the 1755 earthquake left it attached to the north bank. The tower served as a fortress, a customs post, a lighthouse and, under Spanish rule and later regimes, a political prison with cells in the dungeons that flooded at high tide.

Architecture

The tower consists of a squat hexagonal bastion with gun ports for artillery and a four-storey tower 30 metres high facing inland. Its Manueline ornament — carved armillary spheres, crosses of the Order of Christ, twisted-rope mouldings and rhinoceros gargoyles recalling the rhinoceros sent to Manuel in 1515 — celebrates Portugal's maritime empire. A statue of Our Lady of Safe Homecoming faces the river. Highlights include the loggia of the second floor, the oratory, and the decorated bulwark terrace.

Today

The tower is one of Lisbon's most visited monuments and a national emblem, appearing on euro coinage designs, and it serves as the ceremonial point of departure associated with the voyages of Vasco da Gama, whose fleet departed from Belém in 1497.
