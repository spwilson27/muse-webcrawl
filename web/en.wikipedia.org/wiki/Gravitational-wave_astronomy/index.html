Gravitational-wave astronomy

Gravitational-wave astronomy is the branch of observational astronomy that uses gravitational waves — ripples in spacetime predicted by Einstein's general relativity in 1916 — to collect data about black holes, neutron stars and other massive accelerating objects.

First direct detection14 September 2015 (GW150914)
DetectorsLIGO, Virgo, KAGRA, GEO600
SourcesBinary black holes, neutron star mergers, supernovae

History

Indirect evidence came from the Hulse–Taylor binary pulsar (1974), whose orbital decay matched energy loss to gravitational waves, earning the 1993 Nobel Prize. Joseph Weber's resonant bars in the 1960s made unconfirmed claims. Laser interferometers finally succeeded: LIGO detected GW150914, a black-hole merger, announced February 2016; Rainer Weiss, Barry Barish and Kip Thorne won the 2017 Nobel Prize.

Key events

GW170817 (2017), a neutron-star merger seen also in gamma rays, optical kilonova light and other wavelengths, inaugurated multi-messenger astronomy, measured the speed of gravity, and confirmed heavy-element (r-process) production. Catalogues now list dozens to hundreds of compact-binary mergers.

Future

Planned detectors include LISA in space, Einstein Telescope and Cosmic Explorer on the ground, and pulsar timing arrays — which in 2023 reported evidence for a nanohertz stochastic background. See Gravitational wave and General relativity.
