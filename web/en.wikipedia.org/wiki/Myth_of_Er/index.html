Myth of Er

Souls choosing lots for their next lives in the Myth of Er.

The Myth of Er is the closing tale of Plato's Republic (Book X, 614b–621d). Socrates recounts how Er, a Pamphylian soldier slain in battle, revived on his funeral pyre twelve days later and told what he had witnessed of the afterlife. The myth caps the dialogue's defence of justice by showing that the soul is immortal and that the just and unjust are rewarded and punished after death — and that souls themselves bear responsibility for choosing their next lives.

The story

Er describes a place where souls are judged: the just ascend to rewards and the unjust descend to suffer tenfold punishments for each wrong, over journeys of a thousand years. The incurable tyrants — above all Ardiaeus, a Pamphylian despot — are seized and not allowed to return. Er then watches the souls gather before the spindle of Necessity, the cosmic axis turned by the three Fates, where lots and patterns of lives are laid out: lives of animals and humans, of tyrants, athletes, rich and poor. Each soul chooses its next life in an order fixed by lot; Odysseus, last of all and cured of ambition by suffering, happily chooses the quiet life of a private citizen. The souls drink from the river of Forgetfulness (Lethe) in the plain of Forgetfulness — all but Er, who is forbidden to drink — and are carried up to birth "like shooting stars".

Interpretation

"The blame is his who chooses; god is blameless": the myth makes responsibility for the character of one's life central even within a cosmic order of reincarnation and fate. It influenced later eschatology and literature, from Cicero's Dream of Scipio onward, and scholars debate how literally Plato intended details such as the music of the spheres in the spindle vision.
