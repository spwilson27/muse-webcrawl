Complex number

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The complex plane, with a complex number plotted as a point]

A complex number is a number that can be expressed in the form a + bi, where a and b are real numbers and i is the imaginary unit, satisfying i² = −1. The real part is a and the imaginary part is b. Complex numbers extend the real numbers so that every polynomial equation has a solution (the fundamental theorem of algebra).

They arose in the 16th century when Italian mathematicians (Cardano, Bombelli) found that formulas for cubic equations involved square roots of negative numbers. Euler popularised the notation i and discovered e^(iθ) = cos θ + i sin θ; Gauss gave the geometric interpretation on the complex plane.

Geometry and polar form

A complex number corresponds to a point (a, b) in the complex (Argand) plane; its modulus |z| is its distance from the origin and its argument the angle. In polar form z = r(cos θ + i sin θ) = re^(iθ), multiplication adds angles and multiplies moduli — Euler's identity e^(iπ) + 1 = 0 relates five fundamental constants.

Complex analysis, the study of functions of a complex variable, is famously rich: differentiable complex functions are automatically infinitely differentiable and analytic (Cauchy's theory).

Applications

Complex numbers are essential in electrical engineering (impedance and phasors), signal processing and the Fourier transform, fluid dynamics, quantum mechanics, and control theory.
