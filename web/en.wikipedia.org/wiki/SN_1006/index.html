SN 1006 was a supernova that appeared in the southern constellation of Lupus in the spring of 1006. Recorded by observers in China, Japan, Egypt, Iraq, Yemen and Europe, it is generally considered the brightest stellar event in recorded history, reaching an estimated apparent magnitude of about −7.5 — far outshining Venus and visible in daylight for weeks. At roughly 7,200 light-years away, it was nevertheless a Type Ia supernova: the thermonuclear destruction of a white dwarf star.

Historical records

Chinese astronomers of the Song dynasty described a "guest star" southeast of the Moon that appeared on 30 April 1006 and shone so brightly that objects could be seen by its light at night. The Egyptian physician-astrologer Ali ibn Ridwan, observing from Cairo, estimated it was two and a half to three times the disc of Venus and said its light was a little more than a quarter of moonlight. Japanese records in the Meigetsuki and other chronicles, and reports associated with the monks of St. Gallen in Switzerland, confirm a new star visible for some three years before fading. A petroglyph in Arizona's White Tank Mountain Regional Park has been proposed — controversially — as a depiction of the event by the Hohokam people.

The remnant

The supernova remnant, identified in 1965 as a radio source and later imaged across the spectrum, is a nearly spherical shell about 60 light-years across, still expanding at thousands of kilometres per second. It shows strikingly little iron-rich debris along the line of sight and no surviving companion star has been conclusively identified, evidence used to test models of Type Ia progenitors. The remnant is a bright source of X-rays and TeV gamma rays from accelerated cosmic rays.
