Titus Quinctius Flamininus

Titus Quinctius Flamininus (c. 229 BC – c. 174 BC) was a Roman politician and general who served as consul in 198 BC and led Rome to victory over Philip V of Macedon in the Second Macedonian War. He is famous for his proclamation of the "freedom of the Greeks" at the Isthmian Games of 196 BC and for his philhellenic diplomacy, which made Greece a Roman protectorate while leaving its cities formally free.

Early career

Flamininus, of the plebeian gens Quinctia, served as a military tribune under Marcus Claudius Marcellus and as propraetor governing Tarentum during the Second Punic War. Ambitious and fluent in Greek — unusual assets for a Roman of his generation — he cultivated the Greek cities of Italy and leapfrogged the normal career ladder, being elected consul for 198 BC at about thirty years of age, before holding the praetorship, by special dispensation.

Second Macedonian War

Taking command in Greece, Flamininus combined diplomacy with force: he won over the Achaean League to the Roman side and in 197 BC defeated Philip's phalanx at the Battle of Cynoscephalae in Thessaly, where the flexible Roman legion exploited broken ground on which the Macedonian formation could not maintain cohesion. The peace of 196 BC stripped Philip of his possessions outside Macedon. At the Isthmian Games that year, a herald proclaimed that the Roman Senate and Flamininus "leave the following peoples free, without garrisons and without tribute, to live under their ancestral laws" — a declaration, recorded by Polybius and Livy, that stunned and delighted the Greek crowd. In 194 BC Flamininus withdrew all Roman troops from Greece, fulfilling the proclamation. He also campaigned against the Spartan tyrant Nabis.

Later life

As censor in 189 BC and as a senior diplomat, Flamininus remained Rome's leading authority on Greek affairs. Late in life he was sent to King Prusias I of Bithynia to demand the surrender of the fugitive Hannibal (183 BC), who took poison rather than be handed over. Flamininus died around 174 BC. Plutarch paired his Life with that of Philopoemen, the Achaean statesman who both admired and resisted him.
