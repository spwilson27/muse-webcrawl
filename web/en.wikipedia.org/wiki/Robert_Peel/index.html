Sir Robert Peel, 2nd Baronet (5 February 1788 – 2 July 1850), was a British Conservative statesman who twice served as Prime Minister of the United Kingdom (1834–1835 and 1841–1846). He is regarded as the founder of the modern Conservative Party and of modern policing in Britain.

Early career

The son of a wealthy Lancashire textile manufacturer, Peel entered Parliament in 1809. As Chief Secretary for Ireland he founded the Royal Irish Constabulary, and as Home Secretary (1822–1827, 1828–1830) he reformed the criminal law — consolidating statutes and reducing the number of capital offences — and in 1829 created the Metropolitan Police Force in London; its constables were nicknamed "bobbies" and "peelers" after him.

Prime Minister

Peel's Tamworth Manifesto of 1834 restated Conservatism as accepting moderate reform. In his second ministry he reformed the tariff system, restored the income tax (1842), passed the Bank Charter Act 1844 and the Factory Act 1844. His decision in 1846 to repeal the Corn Laws — prompted by the Irish potato famine and his own conversion to free trade — carried with Whig support but split his party, ending his ministry and reshaping British politics for a generation.

Death

Peel died in 1850 from injuries after being thrown from his horse on Constitution Hill in London. Numerous towns and public institutions bear his name, including many pubs.
