Minimum viable population

Minimum viable population (MVP) is a concept in conservation biology referring to the smallest isolated population of a species that has a specified chance of surviving for a given period despite the foreseeable effects of demographic, environmental, and genetic stochasticity and natural catastrophes. A common definition is a 90–95% probability of persistence for 100 to 1,000 years.

Estimation

MVP is usually estimated through population viability analysis (PVA), computer simulations that model births, deaths, and environmental variation. In 1981 Mark Shaffer proposed the concept formally. A widely cited rule of thumb, the "50/500 rule," holds that an effective population of at least 50 is needed to avoid short-term inbreeding depression and 500 to retain long-term evolutionary potential, though later research suggests these numbers should be roughly doubled. Meta-analyses across many species have found typical MVP estimates in the low thousands of individuals.

Applications and criticism

MVP estimates guide reserve design, captive breeding, and reintroduction programs, and inform assessments of endangered species. Critics note that MVP varies greatly by species and circumstance, that a single threshold number can be misleading, and that habitat quality and threats such as poaching may matter more than population size alone. Small populations face the extinction vortex, in which inbreeding, demographic chance, and environmental shocks reinforce one another.
