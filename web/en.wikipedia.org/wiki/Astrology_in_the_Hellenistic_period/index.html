Astrology in the Hellenistic period

Astrology in the Hellenistic period refers to the form of astrology that developed in the Mediterranean world, especially Hellenistic Egypt, during the centuries following the conquests of Alexander the Great (roughly the 3rd century BCE to the 3rd century CE). Hellenistic astrology was the synthesis from which nearly all later traditions of Western and Indian horoscopic astrology ultimately derive.

Origins and synthesis

Hellenistic astrology arose from the fusion of three older traditions. From Babylonia came the twelve-sign zodiac, the use of celestial omens on behalf of individuals as well as kings, planetary period relations and much of the mathematical astronomy. From Egypt came the thirty-six decans, star-groups used to measure the hours of the night, which became integrated with the zodiac as subdivisions of each sign. Greek contributions included the philosophical framework — especially Stoic ideas of cosmic sympathy and fate — and the geometric scheme of aspects and angular relationships adapted from Greek geometry.

Horoscopic astrology

The defining innovation of the Hellenistic tradition was horoscopic astrology: the casting of a chart (the Greek horoskopos originally denoting the rising sign, the ascendant) for the moment of an individual's birth, from which the course of the life was judged. Houses (called "places", Greek topoi), lots (such as the Lot of Fortune), annual profections and time-lord systems such as zodiacal releasing were all developed in this period.

Major authors and texts

  • Claudius Ptolemy, whose Tetrabiblos (2nd century CE) gave astrology a systematic, naturalistic presentation in line with Aristotelian physics.
  • Vettius Valens, whose nine-book Anthology is the richest surviving practical handbook, drawn from his own case work.
  • Dorotheus of Sidon, author of a didactic poem in five books on natal and electional astrology.
  • Manilius, whose Latin poem Astronomica shows the Stoic colouring of the art in Rome.
  • The legendary figures Nechepso and Petosiris, to whom early treatises were pseudepigraphically attributed.

Transmission

As the Hellenistic world gave way to the Roman and later the Byzantine empires, the tradition was carried eastward into Persia and India — where it profoundly influenced Indian astrology — and was later translated into Arabic in the Abbasid period, whence it returned to medieval Europe. Much of the Greek material survives only in fragments, summaries, or translations.
