Khevsureti (Georgian: ხევსურეთი) is a historical-ethnographic region in the north-east of Georgia, in the eastern Greater Caucasus mountains, along the border with Chechnya. It is inhabited by the Khevsurs, a branch of the Georgian people, known historically for their distinctive dress, weaponry and strongly preserved martial and folk traditions.

Geography

Khevsureti straddles both slopes of the main Caucasus ridge. Its northern part lies in the valleys of the Argun and its tributaries, while the southern part occupies the headwaters of the Aragvi river. Major settlements include the fortified villages of Shatili and Mutso, and the administrative village of Barisakho. The region covers roughly 1,050 square kilometres of high mountain terrain, much of it above 2,000 metres, and is cut off by snow for much of the year.

History

The Khevsurs preserved a semi-autonomous clan society long after the Georgian lowlands were Christianised and integrated into kingdoms. Their religious life blended Georgian Orthodox Christianity with older pagan beliefs centred on local shrines (khati) and sacred banners. In the Middle Ages, Khevsur warriors were famed as crusader-like fighters; folk legends claim descent from European crusaders who settled in the mountains, a theory supported in popular imagination by their use of chain mail, straight swords and cross motifs on their garments.

During the Soviet period much of the population was resettled to the lowlands, and many villages were abandoned. Since the collapse of the USSR some families have returned seasonally. Shatili, with its interconnected stone tower houses, is now one of Georgia's most visited mountain destinations and is tentatively listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Culture

Traditional Khevsur dress is dark wool richly embroidered with colourful crosses and geometric patterns. Men historically carried a full set of arms — sword, dagger, shield and pistol — as everyday attire. The region is known for ritual beer brewing, ballads accompanied by the panduri lute, and elaborate codes of hospitality and blood feud that survived into the 20th century.
