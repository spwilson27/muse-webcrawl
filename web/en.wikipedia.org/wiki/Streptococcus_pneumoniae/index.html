Streptococcus pneumoniae


Scientific classification: Phylum Firmicutes; Family Streptococcaceae
Common name: Pneumococcus

Streptococcus pneumoniae, commonly called the pneumococcus, is a Gram-positive, alpha-haemolytic, lancet-shaped diplococcus bacterium. It is a major human pathogen, the most common cause of community-acquired bacterial pneumonia, and also causes meningitis, otitis media, sinusitis, and bacteraemia.

Microbiology

The organism grows in pairs or short chains, is catalase-negative, and is sensitive to optochin and bile solubility, tests used to distinguish it from other streptococci. Its polysaccharide capsule is its chief virulence factor, interfering with phagocytosis; over 100 capsular serotypes are recognised. Pneumococci commonly colonise the human nasopharynx asymptomatically, especially in children, and spread by respiratory droplets.

Historical importance

The pneumococcus was central to the birth of molecular biology: Frederick Griffith's 1928 experiments showed that a "transforming principle" could convert harmless rough pneumococci to virulent smooth forms, and the 1944 Avery–MacLeod–McCarty experiment identified that principle as DNA.

Prevention and treatment

Polysaccharide and conjugate vaccines (such as PCV13/PCV15/PCV20 and PPSV23) protect against the most invasive serotypes and are recommended for children, older adults, and immunocompromised people. Infections are usually treated with beta-lactam antibiotics such as penicillin or ceftriaxone, though penicillin resistance has emerged worldwide, sometimes requiring vancomycin or other agents.
