Local loop

In telephony, the local loop (also called the subscriber line or, in the United States, the last mile) is the physical link or circuit that connects from the demarcation point of the customer premises to the edge of the common carrier's network, traditionally at the local telephone exchange. Historically it consists of a pair of copper wires (a twisted pair).

Function

The local loop carries the customer's voice and data traffic to the exchange, where signals are switched or handed to broadband equipment such as a DSLAM. The carrier supplies battery voltage (about −48 V DC) over the loop to power simple telephones and provides ringing current for incoming calls.

Technologies over the loop

The copper loop, originally designed for voice frequencies, has been reused for digital services including ISDN and DSL, whose achievable speeds decline with loop length and wire quality. In modern networks the "loop" may instead be optical fibre (fibre to the home), coaxial cable, or a fixed wireless link.

Regulation

Because the incumbent carrier's local loops are hard to duplicate economically, regulators in many countries have required local loop unbundling, allowing competitors to lease loops and install their own equipment in exchanges.
