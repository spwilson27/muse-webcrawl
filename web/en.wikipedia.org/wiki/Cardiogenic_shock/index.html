Cardiogenic shock

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cardiogenic shock is a medical emergency resulting from inadequate blood flow due to the failure of the heart to pump effectively. It is a form of shock characterised by persistent hypotension and signs of poor tissue perfusion (cold, clammy skin, altered mental state, low urine output) despite adequate circulating volume. It is most commonly a complication of acute myocardial infarction and carries a high mortality — historically around 50%, though early revascularisation has improved outcomes.

Causes

Large heart attacks damaging a substantial fraction of the left ventricle are the leading cause. Other causes include mechanical complications of infarction (papillary muscle rupture causing acute severe mitral regurgitation, ventricular septal rupture, free-wall rupture with tamponade), fulminant myocarditis, end-stage cardiomyopathy, severe valvular disease, malignant arrhythmias and, classically, right ventricular infarction. Haemodynamically it is defined by a low cardiac index with elevated filling pressures — the "cold and wet" profile of heart failure.

Treatment

Management takes place in intensive care with continuous monitoring. Key measures include early coronary angiography and revascularisation of the culprit lesion in infarction (the SHOCK trial showed a survival benefit for early revascularisation), inotropic drugs such as dobutamine, vasopressors such as norepinephrine, and treatment of arrhythmias and mechanical complications, which usually requires surgery. Mechanical circulatory support — intra-aortic balloon pump, percutaneous ventricular assist devices (e.g. Impella) or veno-arterial ECMO — is used as a bridge to recovery, decision or transplantation in selected patients, although trial evidence for routine use has been mixed.
