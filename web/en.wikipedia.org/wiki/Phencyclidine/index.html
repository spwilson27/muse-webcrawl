Phencyclidine

Phencyclidine (PCP), known by street names including angel dust, is a dissociative anaesthetic and hallucinogen that has been used recreationally since the 1960s. It was developed in the 1950s as an intravenous anaesthetic (marketed as Sernyl) but was withdrawn from human use because of severe psychological side effects; it remains a Schedule II controlled substance in the United States and was used for a time in veterinary medicine as Sernylan. PCP is typically smoked — often applied to leafy material such as cannabis or mint — but may also be snorted, swallowed, or injected.

Pharmacology

PCP is principally a non-competitive antagonist of the NMDA receptor, blocking glutamate-mediated excitation, and also inhibits dopamine reuptake. Its effects — dissociation, analgesia, euphoria, nystagmus, slurred speech, and at higher doses agitation, psychosis mimicking schizophrenia, seizures, or coma — last hours and can recur because the drug is stored in fat and slowly released. The PCP model of psychosis helped motivate the glutamate hypofunction hypothesis of schizophrenia and guided development of related compounds such as ketamine.[1]

History

First synthesized in 1926 and developed by Parke-Davis in the 1950s, phencyclidine was approved as a human anaesthetic in 1957 under the name Sernyl but discontinued in 1965 after patients emerged from anaesthesia with delirium and hallucinations. Recreational use surged in the 1970s, drawing intense media attention for episodes of bizarre and sometimes violent behaviour and apparent insensitivity to pain; use declined in the 1980s with the rise of crack cocaine but persists at low levels.
