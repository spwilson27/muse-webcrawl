Old Irish, also called Old Gaelic (Old Irish: Goídelc), is the Goidelic language of Ireland in the period roughly from 600 to 900 AD. It is known from glosses written by Irish monks into Latin manuscripts — at Würzburg, Milan, St. Gall and elsewhere — and from a small body of poetry and prose preserved in later manuscripts. Old Irish is the ancestor of modern Irish, Scottish Gaelic and Manx.

Grammar

Old Irish is one of the most morphologically complex Celtic languages. Nouns declined in five cases across numerous classes with three genders and three numbers (singular, dual, plural). Verbs were extraordinarily intricate: they could be simple or compounded with preverbs, had independent (absolute) and dependent (conjunct) forms chosen by syntax, and could incorporate object pronouns infixed between preverb and stem — yielding single words equivalent to whole clauses. Word order was typically verb–subject–object. Initial consonant mutations (lenition and nasalisation), inherited from Primitive Irish, were fully systematic and grammatically meaningful.

Phonology and sources

Between Primitive Irish (known from Ogham inscriptions, c. 4th–6th centuries) and Old Irish, the language lost final syllables and developed a distinctive stress on the first syllable, which triggered extensive syncope and vowel reduction inside words. The great glosses — especially the Würzburg glosses on the Pauline Epistles — provide continuous text; Rudolf Thurneysen's Grammar of Old Irish (1909, English ed. 1946) remains the standard reference.

Legacy

Old Irish literature preserved in Middle Irish copies includes heroic sagas of the Ulster Cycle, early religious poetry such as the hymns attributed to St Patrick's circle, law tracts, and annals. Around 900, Viking disruption and internal change mark the transition to the simpler Middle Irish.
