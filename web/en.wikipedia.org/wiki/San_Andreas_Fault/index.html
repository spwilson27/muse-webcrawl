San Andreas Fault

The San Andreas Fault is a continental right-lateral strike-slip transform fault that extends roughly 1,200 kilometres (750 mi) through the US state of California. It forms the tectonic boundary between the Pacific Plate and the North American Plate.

San Andreas Fault
Length: ~1,200 km (750 mi)
Type: Transform, right-lateral strike-slip
Plates: Pacific / North American
Location: California, US

Zones

The fault is divided into northern, central and southern segments. The northern segment ruptured in the 1906 San Francisco earthquake. The central segment creeps aseismically near Parkfield. The southern segment, ending at the Salton Sea, last ruptured in about 1680 and is considered overdue for a major event, often called "the Big One".

Motion and study

Slip rates average 20–35 mm per year. The fault was identified in 1895 by Andrew Lawson of UC Berkeley, who named it after Laguna de San Andrés. It became central to the development of plate tectonics.

Earthquakes

Major events include 1857 Fort Tejon (approx. magnitude 7.9), 1906 San Francisco (7.8), and 1989 Loma Prieta (6.9).
