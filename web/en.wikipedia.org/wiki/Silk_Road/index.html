Silk Road

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Silk Road was a network of Eurasian trade routes active from the 2nd century BCE until the mid-15th century, spanning more than 6,400 km from Chang'an (Xi'an) in China to Constantinople and the Mediterranean. It connected the economies, cultures, religions, technologies and diseases of East, South, Central and West Asia with Europe and North Africa. Its best-known export, Chinese silk, gave the routes their modern name (coined by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen in 1877), but goods from spices, glassware, gold and paper to ideas, music and gunpowder travelled along them too.

Routes and history

Han-dynasty China opened the route after the envoy Zhang Qian's journeys to Central Asia (138–125 BCE). From Chang'an, caravans crossed the Gobi Desert to Dunhuang, split around the Taklamakan Desert, passed through oasis cities such as Kashgar, Samarkand and Bukhara, and crossed Persia to Antioch and Tyre. Few merchants travelled the whole way; goods changed hands repeatedly. A maritime "Silk Road" through the Indian Ocean linked Chinese ports with India, Arabia and East Africa.

Exchanges

The routes carried Buddhism from India to China, Islam eastward, Nestorian Christianity to the Tang court, and technologies westward: papermaking, printing, the magnetic compass and gunpowder. They also carried catastrophe — the Black Death is believed to have travelled westward along trade routes in the 14th century. Trade declined as European sea routes to Asia opened after 1498 and as the Ming and Ottoman worlds turned inward; the phrase was revived in the 21st century by China's Belt and Road Initiative.
