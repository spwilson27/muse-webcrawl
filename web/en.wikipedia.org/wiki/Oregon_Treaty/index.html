The Oregon Country and the 49th-parallel boundary

The Oregon Treaty is a treaty between the United Kingdom and the United States that was signed on 15 June 1846 in Washington, D.C. It formally resolved the Oregon boundary dispute by dividing the jointly occupied Oregon Country between the two nations along the 49th parallel.

Background

Since the Treaty of 1818, Britain and the United States had jointly occupied the Pacific Northwest. American expansionism in the 1840s raised tensions: the 1844 presidential campaign of James K. Polk popularised the slogan "Fifty-four Forty or Fight!", claiming the entire region up to the southern boundary of Russian Alaska at 54°40′ N. Neither side, however, wished for war — Britain was concerned with European affairs and the United States soon faced war with Mexico.

Terms

The treaty extended the existing 49th-parallel boundary from the Rocky Mountains westward to the middle of the channel separating the continent from Vancouver Island, and thence through the Strait of Juan de Fuca to the Pacific Ocean — leaving Vancouver Island wholly British. Navigation rights through the channels were granted to both parties, and the property rights of the Hudson's Bay Company and its settlers south of the line were to be respected.

Consequences

The United States gained the future states of Washington, Oregon, and Idaho, plus parts of Montana and Wyoming; Britain retained the territory that became British Columbia. Ambiguity over the water boundary through the San Juan Islands led to the bloodless "Pig War" standoff of 1859, resolved by arbitration in 1872.
