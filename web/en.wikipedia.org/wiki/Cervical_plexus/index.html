Cervical plexus

The cervical plexus lies deep to the sternocleidomastoid muscle.

The cervical plexus is a network (plexus) of nerves formed by the ventral rami of the first four cervical spinal nerves (C1–C4). It lies in the neck, deep to the sternocleidomastoid muscle, and supplies sensation to parts of the head, neck and shoulders, as well as motor fibres to several neck muscles and to the diaphragm via its connections.

Structure

The plexus is formed by loops between adjacent cervical rami, resting on the levator scapulae and scalenus medius muscles and covered by the prevertebral layer of deep cervical fascia. Its branches are grouped into superficial (cutaneous) and deep (muscular) groups.

Branches

The superficial branches emerge around the posterior border of the sternocleidomastoid and include the lesser occipital nerve (C2), the great auricular nerve (C2–C3), the transverse cervical nerve (C2–C3) and the supraclavicular nerves (C3–C4). The deep branches supply the infrahyoid muscles via the ansa cervicalis (a loop formed from C1–C3), the prevertebral muscles, and — through the phrenic nerve (C3–C5, predominantly C4), which receives a contribution from the plexus — the diaphragm. A classic mnemonic holds that C3, C4 and C5 "keep the diaphragm alive".

Clinical significance

A cervical plexus block can be performed to provide anaesthesia for procedures such as carotid endarterectomy and superficial neck surgery. Injury to branches of the plexus may cause numbness over the ear, jaw angle or shoulder region.
