Balance of power (international relations)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The balance of power theory in international relations suggests that states may secure their survival by preventing any one actor from gaining enough military power to dominate all others. If one state becomes much stronger, theory predicts others will unite in a defensive coalition, restoring equilibrium; the term also describes the resulting distribution of power itself and the conscious diplomacy that maintains it.

History and theory

Balance-of-power practice runs from Renaissance Italy through the European state system: the Utrecht settlement (1713) invoked it by name; the Concert of Europe after 1815 managed it cooperatively; Bismarck's alliance web and its collapse into rigid blocs before 1914 supply the cautionary tale. In 20th-century theory, Hans Morgenthau made the balance the essence of politics, Kenneth Waltz made balancing (rather than bandwagoning) the systemic prediction of neorealism, and Stephen Walt refined it as balance of threat. Post-Cold War US primacy ("unipolarity") provoked predictions, largely unfulfilled in hard form, of counter-balancing — and renewed debate as China's rise produces bipolar tendencies and middle powers hedge.

Criticism

Critics note the concept's ambiguity (equilibrium? preponderance? policy? system?), its Eurocentrism, the frequency of buck-passing and under-balancing, and whether nuclear weapons and economic interdependence have transformed its mechanisms — debates central to international relations as a discipline.
