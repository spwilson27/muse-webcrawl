Paul Durand-Ruel (31 October 1831 – 5 February 1922) was a French art dealer who became the great champion and commercial architect of Impressionism. Taking over his father's gallery in Paris, he bought the works of Monet, Renoir, Pissarro, Sisley, Degas, and Manet in bulk when few others would, supported them with monthly allowances, and nearly ruined himself several times in the process.

The dealer system

Durand-Ruel pioneered practices now standard in the art market: buying an artist's entire output, staging solo exhibitions, cultivating collectors through catalogues and press coverage, and exporting art to new markets. A decisive moment came in 1886, when he took some 300 Impressionist paintings to New York; their success with American collectors created the market that ultimately made the movement financially viable and filled American museums with Impressionist works.

Legacy

Monet wrote that without Durand-Ruel the Impressionists would have starved. The dealer's archives — stock books and correspondence — remain a fundamental source for provenance research, and major retrospectives, including a 2014–15 exhibition at the Musée du Luxembourg and the Philadelphia Museum of Art, have examined his role in shaping modern art commerce.
