Mexican Americans

Placeholder image: A Mexican American cultural celebration

Mexican Americans (Spanish: mexico-estadounidenses) are Americans of full or partial Mexican descent. In 2021 they accounted for roughly 11% of the United States population and about 60% of the Hispanic and Latino American population, numbering approximately 37 million people, which makes them the largest Hispanic ethnic group in the country and the second largest ethnic group overall after German Americans in some counts. People of Mexican origin have lived in what is now the southwestern United States since the Spanish colonial period, long before the region became part of the United States.

History

The Mexican American community originated in part with the conclusion of the Mexican–American War in 1848. Under the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, Mexico ceded its northern territories — present-day California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, and parts of Colorado and Wyoming — to the United States, and the roughly 80,000–100,000 Mexican nationals living there were offered U.S. citizenship. Further immigration came with the Mexican Revolution after 1910, the Bracero Program of 1942–1964, which brought millions of temporary farm workers, and sustained labour migration throughout the twentieth century.

Despite treaty guarantees, many Mexican Americans lost land through legal and extralegal means, faced segregation in schools and public facilities, and were targets of mass repatriation during the Great Depression. The community organised in response: the League of United Latin American Citizens (LULAC) was founded in 1929, and the landmark case Mendez v. Westminster (1947) struck down school segregation of Mexican children in California, foreshadowing Brown v. Board of Education.

Chicano movement and labour organising

In the 1960s and 1970s the Chicano Movement asserted ethnic pride and demanded civil rights, better education, and political representation. In the fields, César Chávez and Dolores Huerta co-founded the United Farm Workers, leading the Delano grape strike of 1965–1970 and a national boycott that won union contracts for farmworkers. Cultural production flourished through organisations such as El Teatro Campesino and the Chicano mural movement.

Demographics and culture

Mexican Americans are concentrated in California and Texas, which together are home to well over half the population, with significant communities in Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado, Illinois, and increasingly throughout the Midwest and Southeast. Most Mexican Americans are U.S.-born; Spanish remains widely spoken, alongside English and bilingual blends sometimes called Spanglish. Cultural contributions span cuisine (Tex-Mex and regional Mexican food), music (mariachi, norteño, Tejano, Chicano rock), literature, and film. Mexican Americans have served in the U.S. armed forces at high rates in every major conflict since the Civil War, and include dozens of Medal of Honor recipients.
