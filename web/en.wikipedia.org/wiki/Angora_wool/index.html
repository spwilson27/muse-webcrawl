Angora wool is the soft, fluffy fibre harvested from the Angora rabbit, one of the oldest types of domestic rabbit, originating in Ankara, Turkey. The fibres are hollow, giving Angora exceptional warmth and lightness — garments are noticeably warmer and lighter than sheep's-wool equivalents — and a characteristic floating "halo" when knitted. It is not to be confused with mohair, which comes from the Angora goat.

Production

Four rabbit breeds are recognised for wool production by various registries: English, French, German, and Giant Angora (and the Satin Angora). A rabbit yields roughly 300–500 g of wool per year, collected every three to four months when the coat is naturally shed, by combing, shearing, or — in the case of "plucking" — pulling out the loosened fibres. China produces the overwhelming majority of the world's supply. Because pure Angora fibres lack elasticity and felt easily, yarns are usually blended with sheep's wool or nylon, and textile-labelling laws in many countries require a minimum percentage before a product may be called "angora".

Animal welfare controversy

In 2013, undercover footage from Chinese farms showing workers ripping wool from screaming rabbits led many international clothing retailers — including H&M, Inditex (Zara), and others — to suspend or ban Angora products. Industry and producer groups responded that combing and shearing are painless standard methods and that the footage showed abusive practices, but the controversy permanently shrank the mainstream market and increased demand for certified, traceable supply chains.
