Par value

Par value, also called face value or nominal value, is the stated value of a security as set out in its issuing documents. The term has different implications for bonds, preferred stock, and common stock.

Bonds

For a bond, par value is the principal amount the issuer promises to repay at maturity — commonly $1,000 per bond — and the amount on which coupon interest payments are calculated. A bond is said to trade "at par" when its market price equals its face value, "at a premium" when above, and "at a discount" when below. As a bond approaches maturity its price tends to converge to par, an effect known as pull to par.

Stock

For shares, par value is an amount assigned in the corporate charter. Historically it represented the minimum price at which shares could be issued and the legal capital of the corporation. In modern practice, especially in the United States, par values for common stock are typically nominal — fractions of a cent, such as $0.0001 per share — and bear no relation to market value; many jurisdictions also permit "no-par" shares. Par value retains some significance for preferred stock, whose dividend may be expressed as a percentage of par, and in accounting, where amounts received above par are recorded as additional paid-in capital.

Currency

In foreign exchange, "par value" historically referred to the official fixed rate of a currency under systems such as the gold standard or the Bretton Woods agreements, against which market rates were allowed to fluctuate only within narrow bands.
