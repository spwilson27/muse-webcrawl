Epithelium (pl. epithelia) is one of the four basic types of animal tissue, along with connective, muscle, and nervous tissue. Epithelial tissues line the outer surfaces of organs and blood vessels throughout the body, the inner surfaces of cavities in many internal organs, and form the functional tissue (parenchyma) of glands. Epithelial cells are tightly packed, rest on a basement membrane, display apical–basal polarity, and are avascular, receiving nourishment by diffusion from underlying connective tissue.

Classification

Epithelia are classified by the number of cell layers and the shape of the surface cells. Simple epithelium has one layer; stratified epithelium has two or more; pseudostratified epithelium appears layered but all cells contact the basement membrane. By shape, cells may be squamous (flat), cuboidal, or columnar. Examples include simple squamous endothelium lining blood vessels, simple columnar epithelium of the intestine, stratified squamous epithelium of the skin (epidermis), pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium of the airways, and transitional epithelium (urothelium) of the bladder.

Functions

Epithelia serve as protective barriers, regulate absorption and secretion, line ducts and glands, and provide sensory surfaces. Specializations include cilia for moving mucus, microvilli for absorbing nutrients, keratinization for waterproofing, and tight junctions that seal the spaces between cells.

Clinical significance

Cancers arising from epithelial cells are called carcinomas and account for the great majority of human cancers. Diseases of epithelia include cystic fibrosis (a defective epithelial chloride channel) and various blistering disorders caused by mutations in epithelial adhesion proteins.
