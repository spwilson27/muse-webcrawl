Columnar basalt

Columnar basalt is basalt lava that has solidified into regular, typically hexagonal, vertical columns through the process of columnar jointing. When a thick lava flow cools and contracts, stresses build up; because the rock cannot shrink freely, it fractures in a regular pattern of cracks that propagate from the cooling surfaces, producing prismatic columns perpendicular to those surfaces.

Formation

Columns form as the solidified crust of a lava flow or shallow intrusion cools. Each column's cross-section tends toward a hexagon because that geometry most efficiently relieves contraction stress, though columns with three to eight sides occur. Column diameter ranges from a few centimetres to several metres and depends on cooling rate: slower cooling produces wider columns. Lengths can reach tens of metres.

Notable examples

  • The Giant's Causeway in County Antrim, Northern Ireland — some 40,000 interlocking columns formed by a Palaeogene lava flow, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
  • Fingal's Cave on the island of Staffa, Scotland, formed in the same lava flow.
  • Devils Tower in Wyoming, United States, and Devils Postpile in California.
  • Svartifoss in Iceland and the Garni Gorge in Armenia.
  • The Organ Pipes formations found on several continents.

Columnar jointing also occurs in other rapidly cooled rocks and has been identified in lava flows on Mars and the Moon.
