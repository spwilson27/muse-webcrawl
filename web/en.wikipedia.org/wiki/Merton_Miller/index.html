Merton Howard Miller (May 16, 1923 – June 3, 2000) was an American economist, and the co-author of the Modigliani–Miller theorem (1958), which proposed the irrelevance of debt–equity structure for the market value of a firm under certain assumptions. He shared the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1990, along with Harry Markowitz and William F. Sharpe. Miller spent most of his academic career at the University of Chicago's Booth School of Business.

Career

Miller studied economics at Harvard and earned his PhD at Johns Hopkins in 1952, having worked at the U.S. Treasury during the war. He taught first at the Carnegie Institute of Technology (now Carnegie Mellon), where he met Franco Modigliani. Their 1958 paper "The Cost of Capital, Corporation Finance and the Theory of Investment" argued that, in frictionless markets, a firm's value is unaffected by how it is financed. A 1961 follow-up with Modigliani extended the result to dividend policy, and a 1963 correction incorporated corporate taxes. In 1961 Miller moved to the University of Chicago, where he remained until his retirement in 1993, becoming a central figure of the "Chicago school" of finance.

Later work

Miller later studied financial regulation, futures markets and the economics of the securities industry, serving as a public director of the Chicago Mercantile Exchange and writing widely on financial innovation and the 1987 crash and savings-and-loan crisis.
