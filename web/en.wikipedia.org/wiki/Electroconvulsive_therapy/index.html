Electroconvulsive therapy

Other namesElectroshock therapy
SpecialtyPsychiatry
First used1938

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), formerly known as electroshock therapy, is a psychiatric treatment in which a small electric current is passed through the brain under general anaesthesia, intentionally triggering a brief seizure. It is used mainly for severe major depression that has not responded to other treatments, and also for mania and catatonia.

History

ECT was introduced in 1938 in Rome by Ugo Cerletti and Lucio Bini, following earlier convulsive therapies using insulin and metrazol. Early treatments were given without anaesthesia or muscle relaxants, sometimes causing fractures, and the therapy was at times overused and misused — a legacy that gave ECT a lasting stigma, reinforced by depictions such as in the novel and film One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest.

Modern practice

Today ECT is administered under general anaesthesia with muscle relaxants and brief-pulse stimulation, typically in a course of six to twelve treatments. Response rates for severe and psychotic depression are high — often 70–90 percent — making it the most effective acute treatment for that condition. Common side effects are headache, muscle aches, and transient confusion; memory problems, usually temporary but occasionally persistent, are the most significant adverse effect and vary with electrode placement and stimulus dose.

Status and controversy

Major psychiatric bodies regard properly administered ECT as safe and effective for specific indications, while critics and some former patients dispute the balance of benefit and harm. Legal safeguards generally require informed consent, with involuntary treatment permitted only under strict conditions.
