Field ration

A field ration, also called a combat ration or ration pack, is a pre-packaged meal provided to military personnel for use in the field where organised food facilities are unavailable. Modern individual rations — most famously the American Meal, Ready-to-Eat (MRE) — are self-contained, shelf-stable and designed to be eaten without cooking facilities.

TypePre-packaged military meal
Typical energyapprox. 1,200–3,600 kcal per day pack
ExamplesU.S. MRE, British ORP, French RCIR

History

Armies long relied on hardtack, salt pork and canned goods. World War II produced the U.S. C-ration and K-ration and British composite packs; later MCI and then retort-pouched MREs (from 1981) improved shelf life and variety. Flameless ration heaters, introduced in the 1990s, warm entrees without fire.

Types

Individual rations serve one soldier for a meal or a day; group rations (such as Unitized Group Rations) feed units with field kitchens. Specialised variants include cold-weather, long-range patrol, halal, kosher and vegetarian menus, and survival rations for aircrew.

Composition

A typical pack contains a main entree, side dish, crackers or bread, spreads, dessert, powdered drinks, condiments, utensils and an accessory packet. National menus reflect local cuisines — a point of morale and frequent trading among allied troops.
