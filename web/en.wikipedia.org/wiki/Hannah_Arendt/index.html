Hannah Arendt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hannah Arendt (14 October 1906 – 4 December 1975) was a German-born American political theorist and philosopher, known for her analyses of totalitarianism, power, action and judgement. Born in Hanover and educated at Marburg, Freiburg and Heidelberg — where she studied with Martin Heidegger and took her doctorate under Karl Jaspers — she fled Nazi Germany in 1933, worked for Jewish refugee organisations in Paris, and emigrated to the United States in 1941.

Her first major book, The Origins of Totalitarianism (1951), traced antisemitism, imperialism and the collapse of the nation-state into the total domination of Nazi and Stalinist regimes, which she treated as a novel form of government based on ideology and terror rather than mere tyranny. The Human Condition (1958) distinguished labour, work and action, and celebrated action — speech and deed among equals — as the heart of politics. On Revolution (1963) contrasted the American and French revolutions.

Eichmann and controversy

Reporting the 1961 Jerusalem trial of Adolf Eichmann for The New Yorker, Arendt coined the phrase “the banality of evil”: Eichmann struck her not as a monster but as a thoughtless functionary — a reading that, together with her criticism of Jewish council leaders' cooperation, provoked lasting controversy. Later scholarship using fuller records has complicated her portrait of Eichmann's ideology, while her concepts (natality, plurality, the public realm, “right to have rights”) remain central to political theory.
