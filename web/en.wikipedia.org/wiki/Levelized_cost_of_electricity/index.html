Levelized cost of electricity

The levelized cost of electricity (LCOE), also called the levelized cost of energy, is a measure of the average net present cost of electricity generation for a generator over its lifetime. It is used for investment planning and to compare different methods of electricity generation on a consistent basis.

The LCOE is calculated as the ratio between all the discounted costs over the lifetime of an electricity-generating plant divided by a discounted sum of the actual energy amounts delivered. Inputs include capital expenditure, fixed and variable operations and maintenance costs, fuel costs, financing costs (through the discount rate), and the expected lifetime output, which depends strongly on the capacity factor.

Interpretation

Roughly, LCOE is the minimum average price at which electricity must be sold for the project to break even over its life. Analysts such as the investment bank Lazard publish annual LCOE estimates; during the 2010s these showed the costs of utility-scale solar photovoltaics and onshore wind falling below those of new fossil-fuel and nuclear plants in many markets, a trend that reshaped energy investment worldwide.

Criticism and limitations

LCOE is frequently criticized for ignoring properties of the wider electricity system. It does not capture the time-varying value of electricity — a megawatt-hour generated at peak demand is worth more than one generated at night — nor integration costs such as grid connection, balancing, and storage needed by variable renewables. It also says nothing about dispatchability or externalities such as air pollution and carbon emissions unless carbon pricing is explicitly included. Extensions such as the levelized avoided cost of electricity (LACE) and "system LCOE" attempt to address these gaps.
