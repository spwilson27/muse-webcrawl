Bypass ratio

The bypass ratio (BPR) of a turbofan engine is the ratio between the mass flow rate of the bypass stream to the mass flow rate entering the core. A 10:1 bypass ratio, for example, means that 10 kg of air passes through the bypass duct for every 1 kg of air passing through the core.

The bypass ratio is a key parameter distinguishing turbofan designs: engines with a low bypass ratio (around 1:1 or less) are typical of military aircraft, while modern airliner engines have bypass ratios of 8:1 to 12:1 or higher, trading engine diameter for propulsive efficiency and reduced noise.

In a high-bypass turbofan, most air bypasses the core through the fan duct.

Principles

A turbofan generates thrust from two streams: exhaust from the gas turbine core, and air accelerated by the fan which bypasses the core. Accelerating a larger mass of air by a smaller velocity increment is more efficient (higher propulsive efficiency) than accelerating a smaller mass by a larger increment, which is why high-bypass engines are more fuel-efficient at subsonic speeds. The bypass stream also envelops the hot core exhaust, reducing jet noise.

Typical values

  • Turbojet: 0:1 (no bypass).
  • Low-bypass turbofans (e.g. military engines such as the F100): roughly 0.3:1–1:1.
  • Early airliner turbofans (e.g. JT8D): around 1:1–2:1.
  • Modern high-bypass turbofans (e.g. CFM56, Trent 1000): roughly 5:1–11:1.
  • Latest generation (e.g. GE9X, LEAP): around 9:1–12:1. Geared turbofans such as the Pratt & Whitney PW1000G reach about 12:1.

Trade-offs

Higher bypass ratios require larger fan diameters, increasing nacelle drag, weight, and ground-clearance constraints — particularly significant on low-wing airliners. At supersonic speeds, high bypass is disadvantageous, so combat aircraft use low-bypass engines, sometimes with afterburners. The related concept of overall pressure ratio together with bypass ratio largely determines an engine's thermal and propulsive efficiency.
