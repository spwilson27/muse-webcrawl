Volcanoes of Alaska

The U.S. state of Alaska contains more volcanoes than any other part of the United States. The Aleutian island arc and the Alaska Peninsula form the northern segment of the Pacific Ring of Fire, where the Pacific Plate subducts beneath the North American Plate. The Alaska Volcano Observatory (AVO) monitors more than 50 active volcanic centers in the state, and Alaska accounts for a large share of all historically active volcanoes in the United States.

Notable volcanoes

  • Novarupta / Katmai — site of the largest volcanic eruption of the 20th century, in 1912.
  • Mount Redoubt — erupted in 1989–1990 and 2009, disrupting air traffic over Anchorage.
  • Augustine Volcano — an island stratovolcano in Cook Inlet with frequent eruptions, most recently in 2005–2006.
  • Mount Spurr — erupted in 1992; its Crater Peak vent again showed unrest in 2024–2025.
  • Pavlof Volcano — one of the most consistently active Alaskan volcanoes, on the Alaska Peninsula.
  • Shishaldin Volcano — a near-perfect cone on Unimak Island.
  • Akutan, Cleveland, Veniaminof, Aniakchak, Okmok — frequently active Aleutian centers.
  • Bogoslof — a mostly submarine volcano whose 2016–2017 eruption reshaped its island.

Hazards

Because much of rural Alaska is sparsely populated, the principal hazards are volcanic ash clouds, which threaten jet aircraft on North Pacific air routes, as well as ashfall on communities, lahars, and tsunamis from flank collapses. The 1989 eruption of Redoubt famously caused a Boeing 747 (KLM Flight 867) to lose power in all four engines after flying through its ash plume.
