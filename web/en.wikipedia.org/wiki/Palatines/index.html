Palatines

Palatines were German migrants from the Electoral Palatinate (the Pfalz) on the middle Rhine who emigrated in the early 18th century, most notably in the mass migration of 1709 to England and then to the British colonies in North America. In English usage of the period, "Palatine" came to be applied loosely to German emigrants generally.

Background

The Palatinate had been devastated repeatedly by war, including the Thirty Years' War and the Nine Years' War, during which French troops systematically destroyed towns and farmland in 1688–89 and again in 1693. Heavy taxation, religious tensions affecting the largely Protestant population under Catholic electors, and the exceptionally harsh winter of 1708–09, which killed vines and livestock, pushed thousands to leave.

The migration of 1709

In 1709 some 13,000 or more Palatines travelled down the Rhine to Rotterdam and on to London, encouraged by reports of free land in America and by a "Golden Book" promoting Carolina. The sudden influx overwhelmed English relief efforts; the migrants were camped at Blackheath and Camberwell. Some were settled in Ireland, some remained in England, and about 3,000 were sent to New York in 1710, where they were put to work producing naval stores in camps along the Hudson River — a scheme that largely failed, after which many families moved to the Schoharie and Mohawk valleys and later to Pennsylvania.

Legacy

Palatine settlers and their descendants formed a substantial part of the German-American population of New York and Pennsylvania, founding towns such as Germantown and Newburgh and leaving a legacy of Palatine place names and Lutheran and Reformed congregations.
