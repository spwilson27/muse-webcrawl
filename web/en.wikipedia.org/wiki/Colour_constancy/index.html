Color constancy

Color constancy is an example of subjective constancy and a feature of the human color perception system which ensures that the perceived color of objects remains relatively constant under varying illumination conditions. A green apple for instance looks green to us at midday, when the main illumination is white sunlight, and also at sunset, when the main illumination is red. This helps us identify objects.

Mechanism

The effect is achieved through a combination of retinal adaptation and cortical processing. The visual system estimates the spectral content of the illuminant and discounts it, so that perceived surface color depends mainly on surface reflectance rather than on the light reaching the eye. Edwin Land's retinex theory proposed that color is computed from comparisons of lightness across the visual field in three wavelength bands.

Research history

The phenomenon was systematically studied in the 19th century and given quantitative treatment by researchers including Hermann von Helmholtz. Land's Mondrian experiments in the 1970s demonstrated that color appearance depends on the whole scene, not just the light from a single patch.

Computational color constancy

In computer vision and digital photography, color constancy algorithms estimate the illuminant of a scene so that images can be corrected — the basis of automatic white balance. Methods include the gray-world assumption (the average scene color is gray), the white-patch (max-RGB) method, and modern machine-learning approaches trained on large image datasets.

Related phenomena

Color constancy is imperfect; it can fail under narrow-band illumination such as low-pressure sodium lamps. Viral phenomena such as "the dress" (2015), which observers saw as either blue-and-black or white-and-gold, are attributed to individual differences in illuminant discounting.
