The Chinese Type 59 is the licence-built derivative of the Soviet T-54, and the two are routinely treated together in comparisons of Cold War armour because the Type 59 shows both the strengths and the limits of transferring the T-54 design to a less mature industrial base.

Transfer and production

Under the Sino-Soviet alliance, the USSR supplied T-54A production documentation and assistance in the mid-1950s. The first Chinese-assembled vehicles were completed in 1958 at Factory 617 in Baotou, Inner Mongolia, and the type was formally displayed at the National Day parade on 1 October 1959, taking the name Type 59. Early production deleted some Soviet fittings — gun stabilization and infrared night equipment among them — that Chinese industry could not yet reproduce consistently.

Differences from the Soviet original

Outwardly the Type 59 is almost indistinguishable from the T-54A, retaining the hemispherical turret, 100 mm rifled gun and V-type diesel engine (built as the 12150L). Chinese development soon diverged: the Type 59-I added a laser rangefinder and simplified fire control, the Type 59-II (also exported as the Type 59-IIA) adopted a 105 mm rifled gun derived from the British L7, and the heavily modernized Type 59D of the 1990s received explosive reactive armour, new sights and a lengthened 105 mm gun. Pakistan's Al-Zarrar rebuild carried the concept further with a 125 mm smoothbore.

Service

The Type 59 formed the backbone of the People's Liberation Army's armoured force for four decades and was exported widely, fighting in the Indo-Pakistani War of 1971, the Sino-Vietnamese War of 1979, the Iran–Iraq War and the Gulf War. Its fame in the West owes much to the "Tank Man" photograph of June 1989, which showed a column of Type 59s on Chang'an Avenue in Beijing. Upgraded Type 59s remain in service with several armies long after the original Soviet T-54s were retired.
