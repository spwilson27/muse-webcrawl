The T-62 is directly compared with the T-54/55 family because it was developed from the T-55 as an interim answer to new Western tanks — the British 105 mm L7 gun and the American M60 — and because the two Soviet tanks served side by side for decades.

Origins of the T-62

When intelligence revealed the power of the L7-armed Centurions being fielded by NATO, Soviet designers under Leonid Kartsev at Uralvagonzavod lengthened the T-55 hull, enlarged the turret ring and installed the world's first production smoothbore tank gun, the 115 mm U-5TS "Molot", capable of firing APFSDS ammunition at very high muzzle velocity. The resulting Object 166 was accepted for service in 1961 as the T-62.

Gains and losses

Against the T-55, the T-62 offered a significant improvement in armour penetration — its BM-6 APFSDS round could defeat any NATO tank of the early 1960s — together with an automatic spent-case ejector that vented cartridge cases through a turret hatch. In exchange, the early APFSDS rounds were inaccurate at long range, the tank inherited the T-55's cramped interior and −4° gun depression, the rate of fire was slower, and unit cost was markedly higher. The T-54/55's simpler 100 mm rifled gun with effective HE-FRAG ammunition remained superior for infantry support.

Service in parallel

Because of its cost, the T-62 never replaced the T-54/55 in Soviet service; it equipped selected tank divisions while the mass of the fleet remained T-55s, and it was exported widely, seeing heavy combat in the Yom Kippur War (1973), the Iran–Iraq War and Afghanistan. The comparison illustrates a recurring pattern in Soviet armour: the evolutionary T-54/55 line, cheap and numerous, long outlived its intended interim successor.
