Portraits of Shakespeare

No portrait of William Shakespeare is known to have been painted from life with certainty, and the appearance of the playwright has been debated for centuries. The two images most widely accepted as genuine representations — both posthumous — are the engraving by Martin Droeshout in the First Folio of 1623 and the funerary monument in Holy Trinity Church, Stratford-upon-Avon.

The Droeshout portrait

The engraving on the title page of the First Folio was made by the young Martin Droeshout, who was only about 15 when Shakespeare died, and may have worked from a lost original. Ben Jonson's accompanying verse urges readers to look "not on his picture, but his book", and the image's endorsement by Shakespeare's fellow actors gives it authority despite its somewhat stiff, stylised appearance.

The Chandos portrait

The Chandos portrait, now in the National Portrait Gallery in London — of which it was the first acquisition — shows a dark-haired, bearded man wearing a gold earring. Traditionally attributed to John Taylor and dated to about 1610, it is the portrait most often reproduced as Shakespeare, though its identification rests on tradition rather than documentation.

Other candidates

Numerous other paintings have been claimed as portraits of Shakespeare, including the Cobbe portrait (announced in 2009 as a likely life portrait), the Sanders portrait, the Flower portrait (exposed as a 19th-century forgery painted over an earlier religious work), and the funerary bust in Stratford. Scholarly consensus remains cautious: only the Droeshout engraving and the Stratford monument are universally treated as reliable likenesses.
