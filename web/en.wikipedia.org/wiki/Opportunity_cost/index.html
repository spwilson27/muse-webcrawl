In microeconomics, opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative that is forgone when a choice is made. Because resources are scarce, every decision to use resources one way entails the sacrifice of the most valuable alternative use. Opportunity cost is a foundational concept for ensuring that scarce resources are used efficiently, and it applies to individuals, firms, and governments alike.

Definition and examples

If a person spends time and money going to a concert, the opportunity cost is not only the ticket price but also the value of whatever else the time and money could have bought — for example, an evening's wages and a dinner out. For a firm, investing capital in one project carries the opportunity cost of the return the same capital would have earned in its next-best use. Unlike accounting costs, opportunity costs include implicit (non-monetary) costs and need not appear in any ledger.

Opportunity cost is illustrated by the production–possibility frontier: as an economy shifts resources toward producing more of one good, it must give up increasing amounts of another, a relationship known as the law of increasing opportunity cost.

History

The idea was developed by Austrian economists in the late nineteenth century and given its modern name by Friedrich von Wieser in 1914. Earlier writers, including John Stuart Mill and the French economist Frédéric Bastiat (in his 1850 parable of the broken window), had articulated related ideas about unseen, forgone alternatives.

Distinctions

Opportunity cost is distinct from sunk cost: costs already incurred and unrecoverable are irrelevant to forward-looking choices, whereas the opportunity cost of a decision depends only on the alternatives still available. It is also distinct from comparative advantage, though the two are connected: a producer has a comparative advantage in whatever good it can produce at the lowest opportunity cost.
