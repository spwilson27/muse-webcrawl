Uesugi Kenshin (上杉 謙信, 18 February 1530 – 19 April 1578) was a Japanese daimyō of the Sengoku period. He was born Nagao Kagetora and ruled Echigo Province in northern Japan. Known as the "Dragon of Echigo", he is famed for his military prowess and his legendary rivalry with Takeda Shingen, against whom he fought the five Battles of Kawanakajima. Kenshin was a devout worshipper of Bishamonten, the Buddhist god of war, and his followers believed he was the god's avatar.

Early life

Kagetora was the fourth son of Nagao Tamekage, a deputy military governor of Echigo. As a younger son he was sent to a temple as a child, but at fourteen he was recalled to lead the clan after his father's death and his elder brother's incapacity. He defeated rival claimants, united Echigo by 1551, and in 1552 sheltered the fugitive Uesugi Norimasa, who rewarded him by adopting him and transferring the Uesugi name, the office of Kantō Kanrei, and control of the Kantō region.

Rivalry with Takeda Shingen

Between 1553 and 1564 Kenshin and Takeda Shingen fought five times at Kawanakajima in Shinano Province. The fourth battle (1561) was the largest, and legend holds that Kenshin rode into Shingen's command post and struck at him with his sword, Shingen parrying the blows with his iron war fan. When Shingen died in 1573, Kenshin is said to have wept at the loss of so worthy an adversary.

Later campaigns and death

Kenshin campaigned against the Hōjō of Odawara and campaigned in the Kantō repeatedly; in 1577 he defeated the forces of Oda Nobunaga at the Battle of Tedorigawa. He died at Kasugayama Castle in 1578, possibly of illness or, according to persistent legend, assassination. His death left Echigo to be fought over by his adopted sons Kagekatsu and Kagetoyo in the Otate no Ran succession dispute.

A longstanding popular theory holds that Kenshin was actually a woman, based on contemporary descriptions, his devotion, and a record of monthly stomach complaints; historians generally do not accept the claim, but it remains a staple of popular culture in Japan.
