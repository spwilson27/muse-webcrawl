Ancient Greek architecture

Ancient Greek architecture comprises the built structures of the Greek-speaking world from about 900 BCE to the 1st century CE, most famously the temples, theatres, stoas, and civic buildings of the classical period (5th–4th centuries BCE). It is organised around the architectural orders — Doric, Ionic, and Corinthian — systems of proportion governing columns and entablature, and it profoundly influenced Roman and later Western architecture.

The orders — Doric, Ionic, Corinthian — define Greek temple design.

The orders

The Doric order, used on the mainland, has sturdy fluted columns without bases, plain capitals, and a frieze of triglyphs and metopes; the Parthenon is its supreme example. The Ionic order of Asia Minor and the Aegean is slimmer, with scrolled volutes on its capitals and a continuous frieze. The Corinthian order, with capitals carved as acanthus leaves, was rare in Greek buildings but became a favourite of the Romans. Temples were typically peripteral — surrounded by a colonnade — raised on a stepped platform (crepidoma), and built of limestone or marble with precisely fitted, mortarless masonry.

Refinements

Greek architects employed subtle optical refinements: columns swell slightly (entasis), lean inward, and the stylobate curves gently upward, counteracting optical illusions and enlivening the stonework — most famously in the Parthenon (447–432 BCE), designed by Iktinos and Kallikrates.

Building types

Beyond temples, Greek cities built stoas (covered colonnades), theatres exploiting natural hillsides — such as Epidaurus, famed for its acoustics — stadia, gymnasia, council houses (bouleuteria), fountain houses, and tholoi (round buildings). From the Hellenistic period, cities were laid out on grid plans associated with Hippodamus of Miletus, and architecture grew more monumental and decorative.
