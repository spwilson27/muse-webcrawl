Barycenter

In astronomy, the barycenter (from Ancient Greek βαρύς "heavy" + κέντρον "center") is the center of mass of two or more bodies orbiting one another, the point about which the bodies orbit. It is an important concept in astronomy and astrophysics: a planet does not orbit the exact center of its star but a point — the barycenter — determined by both masses.

Two-body problem

For two bodies of masses M₁ and M₂ separated by distance a, the barycenter lies along the line joining them at a distance from the primary given by a·M₂/(M₁+M₂). If the masses are equal, the barycenter lies midway; if one body is far heavier, it lies close to or even inside the heavier body. The Earth–Moon barycenter lies about 4,671 km from Earth's center, inside the Earth; the Sun–Jupiter barycenter lies just outside the Sun's surface, the only Solar System barycenter with a planet that lies outside the Sun.

Applications

Motion around the barycenter produces the stellar "wobble" used by the radial velocity method to detect exoplanets. Coordinates in astronomy are often given in a barycentric frame: the International Celestial Reference System is centered on the Solar System barycenter, and Barycentric Dynamical Time (TDB) is defined relative to it. In binary star systems and in the Pluto–Charon system — whose barycenter lies outside Pluto, making it effectively a double dwarf planet system — the concept shapes classification debates.
