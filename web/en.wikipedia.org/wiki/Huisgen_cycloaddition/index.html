Huisgen cycloaddition

The Huisgen cycloaddition is the reaction of a dipolarophile with a 1,3-dipolar compound to give a five-membered (hetero)cycle. The most studied example is the azide–alkyne Huisgen cycloaddition, in which an organic azide and a terminal alkyne combine to give a 1,2,3-triazole. The reaction is named after Rolf Huisgen, who systematically developed the chemistry of 1,3-dipolar cycloadditions in the mid-twentieth century.

Mechanism

In the classical thermal reaction the azide (the 1,3-dipole) and the alkyne (the dipolarophile) undergo a concerted cycloaddition on heating, giving a mixture of the 1,4- and 1,5-disubstituted triazole regioisomers. The reaction is strongly exergonic but slow, requiring elevated temperatures, because of the high activation barrier associated with distorting the reactants into the transition-state geometry.

Copper and ruthenium catalysis

In 2002 the groups of Meldal, and of Sharpless and Fokin, independently reported that copper(I) catalysis (CuAAC) accelerates the reaction enormously and directs it exclusively to the 1,4-regioisomer at room temperature in water. Under the name "click chemistry" this variant became a near-universal tool for joining molecules in materials science, drug discovery and bioconjugation, and contributed to the 2022 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Ruthenium catalysis (RuAAC) gives the complementary 1,5-regioisomer and accepts internal alkynes, while the strain-promoted azide–alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC), developed by Bertozzi, runs without metal catalysts and is used in living systems.
