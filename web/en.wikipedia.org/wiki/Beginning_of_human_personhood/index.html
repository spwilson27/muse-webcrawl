The beginning of human personhood is the moment when a human is first recognised as a person. There is a long history of disagreement on the matter, reflecting contrasting views from philosophy, theology, science and law. The question is distinct from the biological beginning of human life (fertilisation) and from the beginnings of individuality and consciousness, though the debates overlap. It is central to controversies over abortion, embryonic stem-cell research and the legal status of the fetus.

Proposed points

  • Conception (fertilisation) — personhood begins when sperm and egg unite to form a genetically unique zygote; the position of the Roman Catholic Church and many pro-life advocates.
  • Implantation — the point at which the embryo embeds in the uterine wall (about a week after fertilisation).
  • Individuation — around day 14, when twinning becomes impossible (the "primitive streak" stage), used by some regulatory frameworks such as the Warnock Report limit for embryo research.
  • Brain activity — the onset of detectable electrical activity in the brain, by analogy with brain-death criteria for the end of personhood.
  • Viability — the stage at which the fetus can survive outside the womb (about 23–24 weeks), central to the US Supreme Court's former Roe v. Wade framework.
  • Birth — the traditional common-law point at which legal personhood unambiguously begins.

Philosophical and religious views

Historical positions include Aristotle's theory of delayed ensoulment (hominization at 40 or 90 days), Aquinas's adoption of successive souls, and various traditions tying personhood to quickening (first felt fetal movement). Modern philosophers debate whether personhood depends on properties such as consciousness, self-awareness, rationality or sentience, or on species membership and potentiality. Gradualist positions hold that moral status increases with development rather than appearing at a single instant.

Legal treatment

Legal systems generally confer full legal personhood at live birth, while extending varying protections to the unborn (for example in inheritance, wrongful-death and fetal-homicide laws). International human-rights instruments have mostly avoided defining the point at which personhood begins, leaving the matter to domestic law.
