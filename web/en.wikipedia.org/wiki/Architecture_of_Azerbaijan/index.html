Architecture of Azerbaijan

The architecture of Azerbaijan reflects the country's position at the crossroads of the Caucasus, Persia, and the steppe, combining elements of Islamic, Persian, Turkic, Russian imperial, and Soviet building traditions. Landmarks range from Zoroastrian fire temples and medieval Shirvanshah palaces to the oil-boom mansions of nineteenth-century Baku and the futuristic towers of the present-day capital.

Early and medieval periods

Prehistoric petroglyphs and megalithic structures survive at Gobustan. In antiquity the region of Caucasian Albania raised early Christian churches, among them the church at Kish. After the Arab conquests, Islamic architecture introduced mosques, minarets, caravanserais, and mausoleums. The symbol of Baku, the cylindrical Maiden Tower, dates in its present form to about the twelfth century. Regional schools of the Seljuk and post-Seljuk periods — notably at Nakhchivan, where the master builder Ajami ibn Abubakr raised the Momine Khatun Mausoleum (1186) with its glazed-brick ornament — are celebrated for their refined geometry. Under the Shirvanshahs, the palace complex in Baku's Old City (Icheri Sheher), with its divankhana, mosque, and the mausoleum of Seyid Yahya Bakuvi, reached a high point of stone craftsmanship in the fifteenth century. The palace of the Shaki Khans (1797), famed for its shebeke lattice windows and murals, crowns the Khanate period.

Russian Empire and oil boom

After Azerbaijan's incorporation into the Russian Empire, and especially with the Baku oil boom of the late nineteenth century, European architects built eclectic palaces, theatres, and apartment houses along new avenues — including the Ismailiyya Palace in a Venetian Gothic style and the Philharmonic Hall — while Absheron's oil barons competed in architectural patronage.

Soviet and contemporary periods

Soviet planning brought constructivist experiments, then Stalinist neoclassicism, then mass housing. Following independence in 1991, and accelerating with oil revenues in the 2000s, Baku was transformed by landmark projects: Zaha Hadid's flowing Heydar Aliyev Center (2012), the Flame Towers, and the crescent-shaped Port Baku development, juxtaposed with the UNESCO-listed Walled City. Preservation of the historic fabric amid rapid development remains a central debate in Azerbaijani architecture.
