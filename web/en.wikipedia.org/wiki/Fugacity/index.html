Fugacity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fugacity approaches the pressure as a gas becomes ideal at low pressure.

In chemical thermodynamics, the fugacity of a real gas is an effective partial pressure which replaces the mechanical partial pressure in an accurate computation of the chemical equilibrium constant. It is equal to the pressure of an ideal gas which has the same temperature and molar Gibbs free energy as the real gas.

The concept was introduced by the American chemist Gilbert N. Lewis in 1901, from the Latin fugere, to flee — expressing the tendency of a substance to "escape" from one phase to another. Fugacities are determined experimentally or estimated from models such as the van der Waals equation. The ratio between fugacity and pressure, f/P, is the fugacity coefficient; it equals 1 for an ideal gas.

Definition

Fugacity is defined through the differential of the chemical potential μ:

dμ = RT d ln f

with the limiting condition that f → P as P → 0. For a pure substance, the Gibbs energy is then:

G = Gideal + RT ln(f/P°)

where P° is a chosen standard pressure, usually 1 bar.

Fugacity in mixtures and condensed phases

For a component i in a mixture, the partial molar analogue defines a component fugacity fi, and phase equilibrium requires the fugacity of each component to be equal in every phase. For liquids, fugacity is often estimated from the vapour pressure corrected by the Poynting factor; the related concept of activity is fugacity normalised by a standard-state fugacity.

Calculation

The fugacity coefficient of a pure gas can be computed from volumetric data through:

ln(f/P) = ∫₀P (Z − 1)/P dP

where Z = PV/(RT) is the compressibility factor. In practice, equations of state such as Redlich–Kwong or Peng–Robinson are used, and for moderate pressures the Lewis fugacity rule or corresponding-states correlations give useful estimates.

Applications

Fugacity is a workhorse of chemical engineering: it underpins vapour–liquid equilibrium calculations, distillation design, high-pressure reaction equilibria such as ammonia synthesis, and the geochemical modelling of volatiles in magmas and hydrothermal systems.
