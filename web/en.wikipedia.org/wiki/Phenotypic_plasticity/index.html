Phenotypic plasticity

Phenotypic plasticity
Predator-induced defences in Daphnia are a classic example of plasticity
FieldEvolutionary biology, developmental biology
Key conceptOne genotype producing multiple phenotypes across environments
Graphical modelReaction norm

Phenotypic plasticity is the ability of a single genotype to produce different phenotypes in response to different environmental conditions. It allows an organism to adjust its morphology, physiology or behaviour during its lifetime without any change to its DNA sequence. The set of phenotypes a genotype can express across an environmental gradient is described by its reaction norm.

The concept was developed in the early twentieth century by Richard Woltereck, who coined the term Reaktionsnorm in 1909 while studying water fleas (Daphnia). It later became central to evolutionary developmental biology and to debates about the relationship between genes and environment.

Examples

Familiar examples include the seasonal forms of butterflies, the production of defensive helmets and spines by Daphnia exposed to predator chemicals, temperature-dependent sex determination in many reptiles, the sun and shade leaves of a single plant, and caste determination in social insects, where genetically similar larvae develop into queens or workers depending on diet. Acclimation of human physiology to altitude — increased red blood cell production — is a physiological form of plasticity.

Adaptive plasticity and evolution

Plasticity is adaptive when the environmentally induced phenotype increases fitness in the environment that induced it. Adaptive plasticity itself can evolve by natural selection, since genotypes differ in the slope and shape of their reaction norms. Some theorists argue that plasticity can lead genetic evolution: a trait first produced as an induced response may later become genetically assimilated, a process proposed by Conrad Waddington. Plasticity also has costs and limits — maintaining sensory and regulatory machinery is expensive, and induced phenotypes may lag behind or mismatch rapidly changing environments.
