Bon Odori

Bon Odori (盆踊り)
GenreJapanese folk dance
OriginJapan, Muromachi period onward
OccasionObon festival (summer)

Bon Odori (Japanese: 盆踊り), meaning simply "Bon dance", is a style of dance performed during the Japanese Buddhist festival of Obon, held in summer to honour the spirits of one's ancestors. The dances welcome the spirits of the dead and send them off again at the close of the festival.


Bon Odori dancers around a yagura

Customs

Traditionally, participants dance in concentric circles around a raised wooden scaffold called a yagura, from which musicians play taiko drums, flute and shamisen, and a singer performs folk songs. Dancers wear light summer kimono (yukata) and repeat a sequence of simple hand and foot movements, so that anyone — including visitors — can join in. The dances usually take place in the evening, in temple grounds, schoolyards, and public squares, accompanied by food stalls and, in some places, fireworks.

Regional variations

Each region of Japan has its own Bon Odori songs and choreography, reflecting local history and industries. Famous examples include the Awa Odori of Tokushima, the Gujō Odori of Gifu — danced through the night for weeks — and the Nishimonai no Bon Odori of Akita, in which dancers wear woven straw hats and indigo clothing. Songs such as Tankyō Bushi (the coal miners' song) and Tokyo Ondo are known nationwide.

Outside Japan

Japanese emigrant communities in Hawaii, Brazil, the continental United States and elsewhere maintain Bon Odori traditions at Buddhist temples each summer; in Hawaii the "bon dance season" is a major cultural event open to all communities.
