Kwame Nkrumah (21 September 1909 – 27 April 1972) was a Ghanaian politician, political theorist and revolutionary who led the Gold Coast to independence from Britain in 1957 — the first sub-Saharan African colony to achieve it — and served as Ghana's first prime minister and then its first president. He was a founding figure of pan-Africanism, a founding member of the Organisation of African Unity (1963), and the most influential African leader of the decolonisation era.

Independence and rule

Educated in the United States (Lincoln and Pennsylvania universities) and politicised in London organising the 1945 Manchester Pan-African Congress with figures such as George Padmore and W.E.B. Du Bois, Nkrumah returned home in 1947 and built the Convention People's Party on a platform of "Positive Action" — strikes, boycotts and civil disobedience — winning elections even from a prison cell in 1951. At independence on 6 March 1957 he declared: "The independence of Ghana is meaningless unless it is linked up with the total liberation of the African continent." His government industrialised ambitiously (the Akosombo Dam and Volta aluminium project), expanded education and health, and championed African unity and non-alignment, but grew authoritarian: preventive detention, a one-party state (1964) and a personality cult ("Osagyefo", the Redeemer) accompanied economic decline as cocoa prices fell.

Overthrow and legacy

While Nkrumah was visiting China in February 1966, the army and police seized power — a coup he and many historians attributed partly to Western intelligence encouragement amid Cold War alignments (compare the Cold War patterns elsewhere in Africa). He lived in exile in Guinea, whose president Sékou Touré made him co-president in name, wrote prolifically, and died of cancer in Bucharest in 1972. His body was eventually reburied with honours in Accra, where his mausoleum stands; in a 2000 BBC poll of African listeners he was voted Africa's "Man of the Millennium". Ghana's capital, Accra, remains the showcase of his era's ambitions.
