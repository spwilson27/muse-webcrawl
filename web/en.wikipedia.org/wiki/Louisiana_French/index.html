Louisiana French (French: français de la Louisiane; Louisiana Creole: fransé Lalwizyàn) is an umbrella term for the dialects and varieties of the French language spoken in the U.S. state of Louisiana. It encompasses the French of the colonial settlers of French Louisiana, the Cajun French of the Acadian descendants, and varieties influenced by later French and Belgian immigration, all distinguished from Louisiana Creole, a separate French-based creole language.

History

French has been spoken in Louisiana since the founding of the colony in 1699, and waves of speakers arrived with the Acadian expulsion from Canada (1755–1764), refugees from Saint-Domingue, and nineteenth-century French immigrants. At the beginning of the 20th century a large share of south Louisiana was francophone, but a 1921 state constitutional provision requiring English-only public schooling, enforced with punishment of children for speaking French, precipitated steep decline through the century.

Revival

In 1968 Louisiana created CODOFIL (the Council for the Development of French in Louisiana) to promote the language, and French immersion programmes now operate in many parishes; musician-led cultural movements and groups such as the band Lost Bayou Ramblers have further championed the language. Louisiana French is written in standard French orthography with regional vocabulary — such as char for car and chaoui for raccoon — documented in the Dictionary of Louisiana French (2010).
