Monterey (Spanish: Monterrey; Ohlone: Aacistak) is a city located in Monterey County on the southern edge of Monterey Bay on the U.S. state of California's Central Coast. Founded on June 3, 1770, Monterey served as the capital of Alta California under both Spain (1777–1821) and Mexico (1822–1846). During this period, Monterey hosted California's first theatre, newspaper, and public school.

History

The area was originally inhabited by the Rumsen Ohlone people. Sebastián Vizcaíno charted the bay in 1602 and named it after the Count of Monterrey, viceroy of New Spain. In 1770 Gaspar de Portolá and Junípero Serra established the Presidio of Monterey and the Mission San Carlos Borromeo de Carmelo. The city was the site of the Battle of Monterey in 1818, when Argentine privateer Hippolyte Bouchard sacked the town, and in July 1846 Commodore John D. Sloat raised the American flag over the Custom House during the Mexican–American War, claiming California for the United States. The California constitutional convention met in Monterey's Colton Hall in 1849.

Fishing and Cannery Row

In the early 20th century Monterey became a major sardine-canning centre, its waterfront lined with canneries. The industry collapsed in the 1950s after the sardine fishery crashed from overfishing. The canneries were immortalised in John Steinbeck's novels Cannery Row (1945) and Sweet Thursday (1954); the street, originally Ocean View Avenue, was renamed Cannery Row in 1958 and is now a tourist district. Marine biologist Ed Ricketts, Steinbeck's friend and the model for "Doc", ran his Pacific Biological Laboratories there.

Present day

Modern Monterey is a tourist destination centred on the Monterey Bay Aquarium (opened 1984), Fisherman's Wharf, and the Monterey Bay National Marine Sanctuary. The city hosts the Naval Postgraduate School and the Defense Language Institute at the Presidio, and is known for the Monterey Jazz Festival, first held in 1958.
