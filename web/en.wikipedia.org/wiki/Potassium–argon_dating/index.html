Potassium–argon dating

Potassium–argon dating, abbreviated K–Ar dating, is a radiometric dating method used in geochronology and archaeology. It is based on measurement of the product of the radioactive decay of an isotope of potassium (K) into argon (Ar). Potassium is a common element found in many materials, such as micas, clay minerals, tephra, and evaporites. In these materials, the decay product 40Ar is able to escape from the liquid (molten) rock, but starts to accumulate when the rock solidifies (recrystallizes). The amount of argon sublimation that occurs is a function of the purity of the sample, the composition of the mother material, and a number of other factors.

Physics

The radioactive isotope 40K decays to stable 40Ar by electron capture or positron emission, and to stable 40Ca by beta decay. The decay to calcium is far more common, but the calcium produced is hard to distinguish from calcium originally present in the rock, so the argon branch is used for dating. The branching ratio gives an effective partial half-life for the argon-producing decay of about 1.248 billion years.

When a rock melts, any accumulated argon escapes into the atmosphere. As the rock cools and crystallizes, the argon can no longer escape and begins to build up in the crystal lattice. Measuring the ratio of 40Ar to remaining 40K therefore gives the time elapsed since solidification.

The dating equation

The age of a sample is given by an equation relating the measured 40Ar*/40K ratio (where 40Ar* is radiogenic argon) to the total decay constant of 40K and the branching constant for the argon pathway. Because the calculation requires the isotopic abundance of 40K in total potassium, the method depends on precise mass-spectrometric measurement.

Argon–argon dating

A refinement of the technique, argon–argon (Ar–Ar) dating, irradiates the sample with neutrons to convert 39K into 39Ar, which serves as a proxy for potassium. Both argon isotopes are then measured in a single mass-spectrometer run, improving precision and allowing step-heating experiments that can reveal thermal disturbance of the sample.

Applications and limitations

K–Ar dating has been used to date volcanic layers above and below hominid fossils in East Africa, including deposits at Olduvai Gorge, thereby constraining the ages of early human ancestors. It is also used to date meteorites and lunar rocks. Limitations include argon loss through heating, excess (inherited) argon in some samples, and the unsuitability of the method for very young or potassium-poor rocks.
