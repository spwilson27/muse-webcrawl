Space station

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Space stations compared to scale.

A space station is a spacecraft in orbit that can support a human crew for extended periods, with docking ports for visiting vehicles but no major propulsion or landing capability of its own. Stations serve as laboratories for research in microgravity, Earth observation and technology development, and as waypoints for exploration.

The Soviet Union launched the first station, Salyut 1, in 1971. The United States followed with Skylab in 1973. The modular Soviet Mir station (1986–2001) pioneered long-duration stays and international visits. The International Space Station, assembled from 1998 by five space agencies, is the largest structure ever built in orbit and has been continuously occupied since November 2000. China's Tiangong station was completed in 2022.

Design

Stations are either monolithic — launched in one piece, like Salyut and Skylab — or modular, assembled in orbit from separately launched sections, like Mir, the ISS and Tiangong. All rely on visiting spacecraft for crew rotation and resupply, on solar arrays for power, and on periodic reboosts to counter orbital decay. Several commercial stations are in development to succeed the ISS in the 2030s.
