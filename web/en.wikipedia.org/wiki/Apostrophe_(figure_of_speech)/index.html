Apostrophe (figure of speech)

Apostrophe (Ancient Greek: ἀποστροφή, apostrophḗ, "turning away") is an exclamatory figure of speech. It occurs when a speaker breaks off from addressing the audience (e.g., in a play) and directs speech to a third party, such as an opposing litigant or some other individual, sometimes absent from the scene. Often the addressee is a personified abstract quality or inanimate object. In dramatic works and poetry written in or translated from ancient Greek or Latin, appearances of apostrophe tend to be much more common than in modern English, largely because of the convention of regularly invoking a Muse or other deities to aid the speaker.

A speaker turns aside from the audience to address an absent person or personified abstraction.

Description

In classical rhetoric, apostrophe was classified as a figure of speech in which the orator turned from the judges or audience to address an adversary, an absent person, or an abstraction such as virtue, fortune, or the fatherland. Quintilian discussed it as a device capable of great emotional force, used to express grief, indignation, or appeal. Because it stages a direct confrontation with something that cannot answer, apostrophe often produces heightened pathos and a sense of immediacy.

Examples

Poets frequently address inanimate objects or abstractions as if they could hear and respond. John Keats opens his "Ode on a Grecian Urn" by addressing the urn itself:

Thou still unravish'd bride of quietness,
Thou foster-child of silence and slow time...

Percy Bysshe Shelley's "Ode to the West Wind" begins with an address to the wind: "O wild West Wind, thou breath of Autumn's being". William Wordsworth exclaims, "Milton! thou shouldst be living at this hour", addressing the long-dead poet. In drama, Mark Antony's funeral oration in Shakespeare's Julius Caesar turns from the crowd to address Caesar's corpse, and Hamlet apostrophises the skull of Yorick: "Alas, poor Yorick! I knew him, Horatio."

Relation to prosopopoeia

Apostrophe is closely related to, but distinct from, prosopopoeia (personification), in which an absent or imaginary person is represented as speaking. In apostrophe, the speaker addresses the absent party; in prosopopoeia, the absent party is given a voice. The two figures frequently occur together: a poet may first address Death and then allow Death to reply. The invocation of the Muse at the opening of epic poems — "Sing to me of the man of twists and turns" in the Odyssey — is a conventional form of apostrophe to a divine addressee.

Use in modern writing

Although less central to modern prose, apostrophe survives in speeches, lyrics, and literary fiction, where direct address to abstractions ("O Liberty!"), the dead, or distant places lends emotional emphasis. Rhetoricians note that the device collapses distance: by speaking to rather than about, the writer creates the impression of presence and urgency.
