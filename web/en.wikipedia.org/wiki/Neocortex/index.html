The neocortex, also called the neopallium and isocortex, is the part of the mammalian brain involved in higher-order brain functions such as sensory perception, cognition, generation of motor commands, spatial reasoning and language. The neocortex is the largest part of the cerebral cortex, the outer layer of the cerebrum; in humans it constitutes roughly 90% of the cerebral cortex.

Structure

The neocortex is characteristically organized into six horizontal layers, numbered I (outermost) to VI (innermost), each with distinct cell types and connectivity. This laminar structure distinguishes it from the three-layered allocortex found in older regions of the brain. The primary cellular elements are excitatory pyramidal neurons, which make up about 80% of neocortical neurons, and inhibitory interneurons. Cortical columns — vertical assemblies of neurons — are often considered the functional units of the neocortex.

Function

Different neocortical regions specialize: the primary visual cortex (V1) in the occipital lobe processes vision; the auditory cortex lies in the temporal lobe; the somatosensory and motor cortices straddle the central sulcus. Association areas integrate information across modalities and underlie planning, language and conscious thought. In humans the sheet of neocortex is about 2,000–2,500 cm² when unfolded and folds into gyri and sulci to fit within the skull.

Evolution

The neocortex is unique to mammals. Its expansion, especially in primates, correlates with increased cognitive capacity. The neocortex develops from the embryonic dorsal telencephalon under control of signalling centres, and neurogenesis proceeds in an inside-out fashion, with deeper layers formed first.
