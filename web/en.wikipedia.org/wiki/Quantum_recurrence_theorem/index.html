Quantum recurrence theorem

The quantum recurrence theorem states that a quantum system with a discrete energy spectrum, evolving unitarily in time, will return arbitrarily close to its initial state after a sufficiently long (but finite) time. It is the quantum-mechanical analogue of the classical Poincaré recurrence theorem, established in this form by Bocchieri and Loinger in 1957.

The survival probability of an initial state eventually returns arbitrarily close to 1.

Statement and proof idea

For a system with Hamiltonian eigenvalues E₁, E₂, …, the time-evolved state is a superposition whose coefficients acquire phases exp(−iEₙt/ħ). The evolution is thus an almost periodic function of time: the state traces a path on a (possibly infinite-dimensional) torus of phases. By a Diophantine approximation argument — the simultaneous approximation of the finitely many relevant frequencies — there exist times at which all phases realign to within any chosen tolerance, so the state returns as close to its start as desired. The result requires a discrete spectrum; systems with continuous spectra, such as a free particle on an infinite line, can disperse and need not recur.

Discussion

Recurrence times typically grow extremely rapidly — often doubly exponentially — with the number of degrees of freedom, so for macroscopic systems the theorem has no practical observational consequence, mirroring the classical resolution of the recurrence paradox in statistical mechanics raised by Zermelo against Boltzmann. The theorem nonetheless bears on discussions of irreversibility, equilibration and the foundations of statistical mechanics in isolated quantum systems, and on the long-time behaviour of autocorrelation functions, which are almost periodic for confined systems.
