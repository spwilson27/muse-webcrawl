Aedes albopictus, commonly known as the Asian tiger mosquito, is a mosquito native to the tropical and subtropical areas of Southeast Asia that has become one of the world's most invasive insect species. It is easily recognised by the white bands on its legs and the single longitudinal white stripe down the centre of its back (scutum). It is an important vector of dengue, chikungunya, Zika, and other arboviruses.

Spread

During the twentieth century the species spread across the Pacific and Indian Ocean islands, and from the 1980s established itself in the Americas, Europe, and Africa, largely through the international trade in used tyres, which harbour water and desiccation-resistant eggs, and through shipments of plants such as lucky bamboo. It was first detected in the continental United States in Houston, Texas, in 1985 and in Italy in 1990, and it has since become established across much of southern and central Europe.

Biology

A. albopictus breeds in small water-filled containers, natural and artificial, and females bite aggressively during the daytime. Its eggs survive drying for months and, in temperate-adapted populations, enter a winter diapause, allowing the species to colonise cooler climates than its relative Aedes aegypti. In many regions it outcompetes resident mosquito species, although it is generally a less efficient dengue vector than A. aegypti where the two coexist closely with humans.

Public health

The species was responsible for the first locally acquired chikungunya outbreak in Europe (Italy, 2007) and for autochthonous dengue cases in France, Croatia, and elsewhere. Control relies on source reduction, larvicides, and experimental approaches such as Wolbachia infection and sterile-male releases.
