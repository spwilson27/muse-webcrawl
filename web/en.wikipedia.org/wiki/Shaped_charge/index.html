Shaped charge

A shaped charge is an explosive charge shaped to focus the effect of the explosive's energy on a small area. When detonated, the charge collapses a metal liner — usually a copper cone — into an extremely fast, thin jet capable of penetrating armour steel to depths many times the charge's diameter.

Principle

The effect rests on the Munroe (or Neumann) effect: a hollow cavity in an explosive block greatly increases local penetration. Lining the cavity with metal focuses the detonation pressure so that the liner material is extruded into a jet whose tip may travel at 7–9 km/s, faster than the speed of sound in the target material. Under such pressures both jet and target behave hydrodynamically, as fluids, so penetration depends largely on the relative densities and lengths of jet and target rather than on hardness.

Applications

Shaped charges are the warheads of most anti-tank guided missiles and rocket-propelled grenades such as the RPG-7, and are used in tandem-warhead designs to defeat explosive reactive armour. Related "self-forging fragment" or explosively formed penetrator designs use a shallow dish liner to form a compact slug. Outside warfare, shaped charges are used in oil-well perforating guns, demolition, and linear cutting charges that slice steel structures in controlled demolitions.

History

Charles E. Munroe described the cavity effect in 1888, and Egon Neumann independently discovered it in Germany in 1910. Practical weapon designs followed in the 1930s, and the principle was widely exploited in the Second World War in weapons such as the bazooka and the Panzerfaust.
