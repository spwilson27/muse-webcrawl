Lotka's law

Lotka's law, named after Alfred J. Lotka, is a bibliometric regularity describing the frequency with which authors publish in a given field. Formulated by Lotka in 1926 from counts of chemistry and physics authors, it states that the number of authors who publish n papers is approximately proportional to 1/n² of the number who publish just one.

Statement

In its general form, the fraction of authors publishing n papers is C/n^a, with the exponent a usually near 2. The law implies a highly skewed distribution: roughly 60 percent of authors in a field publish only a single paper, while a small productive core accounts for a large share of the literature.

Significance

Lotka's law is one of the foundational empirical laws of scientometrics, alongside Bradford's law and Zipf's law, and it is closely related to Price's law about the concentration of scientific productivity. It has been tested — with varying exponents — across many disciplines, and is used in evaluating research output and understanding careers in science.
