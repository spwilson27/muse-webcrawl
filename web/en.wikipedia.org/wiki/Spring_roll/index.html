Spring roll

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Fresh Vietnamese spring rolls with shrimp visible through translucent rice paper]

A spring roll is a rolled appetiser of fillings wrapped in a thin pastry or rice paper — fried crisp in the Chinese tradition, or fresh and translucent in the Vietnamese. Chinese chūn juǎn ("spring rolls") began as seasonal food for the spring festival season, thin pancakes wrapped around the first spring vegetables; fried versions travelled with Chinese diasporas worldwide. Vietnamese gỏi cuốn are made with softened rice paper (bánh tráng) around shrimp, pork, rice vermicelli and herbs, dipped in peanut-hoisin sauce or nước chấm — light, cool and emphatically unfried, unlike their fried cousins chả giò.

Regional forms

The Hokkien world gives popiah (Malaysia, Singapore, Taiwan) — a soft wheat crepe wrapped at the table around jicama, egg and sauces — and its fried descendant, Filipino lumpia, which crossed to Indonesia (lumpia Semarang is a city signature) and to the Netherlands as the loempia. Thai por pia, Korean variations, and the egg roll of American-Chinese restaurants — thicker-skinned, blistered, built for the deep fryer — complete the family. In China, the fried roll is also a Lunar New Year food, its golden cylinders said to resemble bars of gold.

Making

Fried rolls demand dry fillings and tight rolling: a wet filling bursts the wrapper. Rice paper for fresh rolls softens in seconds in warm water and turns tacky-gentle; overfill and it tears, underfill and it slumps. Both forms are assembly food — a table full of components, everyone rolling their own — which is half their pleasure and all of their staying power at parties.
