Scottish Gaelic phonology

The phonology of Scottish Gaelic is characterised by a large inventory of consonants, including a distinctive contrast between "broad" (velarised) and "slender" (palatalised) consonants, preaspiration of stops in many dialects, and a system of vowel length contrasts. Gaelic phonology also includes processes such as nasalisation and the unusual pattern of initial consonant mutations shared with the other Celtic languages.

Consonants

Gaelic consonants come in broad/slender pairs: broad consonants are velarised ([ˠ]) and slender consonants palatalised ([ʲ]). The distinction is phonemic and is signalled in the orthography by the choice of adjacent vowel letters, summarised by the spelling rule caol ri caol is leathann ri leathann ("slender with slender, broad with broad").

The stop system contrasts voiceless unaspirated with voiceless aspirated sounds rather than voiced with voiceless: written b, d, g are unaspirated [p, t, k] and p, t, c are aspirated [pʰ, tʰ, kʰ]. In medial and final positions, historically voiced stops are typically preaspirated: for example cat "cat" may be realised [kʰaʰt]. The sonorants include the strong trills and laterals of the traditional dialect system, though the inventory is simplifying among younger speakers under the influence of English.

Vowels

Scottish Gaelic has a system of around nine or ten monophthongs, contrasting long and short, plus a number of diphthongs. Unstressed vowels are reduced to schwa [ə] in final syllables, and many unstressed syllables are elided in connected speech. Long vowels in certain environments show "svarabhakti" — the insertion of an echo vowel after strong sonorants — as in Alba "Scotland", pronounced [ˈal̪ˠəpə]. Nasalised vowels occur adjacent to nasal consonants and are prominent in some dialects, such as that of Barra.

Stress and intonation

Stress almost always falls on the first syllable of a word. Gaelic dialects differ considerably: the dialects of Lewis, Sutherland, Skye, the Outer Hebrides and the mainland have been extensively described, and some (such as East Sutherland Gaelic) have been documented as they became moribund.
