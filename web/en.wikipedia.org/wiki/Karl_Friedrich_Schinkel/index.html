Karl Friedrich Schinkel (13 March 1781 – 9 October 1841) was a Prussian architect, city planner and painter who more than any other figure shaped the appearance of early 19th-century Berlin. Working mainly in Neoclassical and Gothic Revival styles, he designed many of the city's landmark buildings and exerted lasting influence on German architecture.

Life

Schinkel was born in Neuruppin, the son of a church superintendent. After his father's death the family moved to Berlin, where he trained under the architect Friedrich Gilly and studied at the Bauakademie. He first made his name as a painter and stage designer — his designs for Mozart's The Magic Flute (1815) are still famous — before turning mainly to architecture.

As head of the Prussian state building department he supervised public construction across the kingdom. His Berlin works include the Neue Wache (1816–18), the Schauspielhaus concert hall on the Gendarmenmarkt (1818–21), the Altes Museum (1823–30), the Friedrichswerder Church, and the brick Bauakademie (1832–36), an early landmark of rational construction. He also designed the Charlottenhof Palace in Potsdam and the Orianda Palace project for the Crimea.

Legacy

Schinkel's pattern books and his Sammlung architektonischer Entwürfe spread his influence across Germany and beyond. His Greek Revival classicism gave Prussia a monumental civic style, while his industrial and brick buildings anticipated later functionalism. The Schinkelplatz in Berlin and numerous schools bear his name.
