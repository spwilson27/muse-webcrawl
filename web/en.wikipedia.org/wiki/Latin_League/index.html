The Latin League was an ancient confederation of about thirty villages and tribes in the region of Latium in central Italy, organised for mutual defence and bound together by shared Latin language, cult, and legal customs. It existed from roughly the seventh century BC until Rome dissolved it in 338 BC at the end of the Latin War.

Origins

The League grew out of a religious association centred on the sanctuary of Jupiter Latiaris on Monte Cavo in the Alban Hills, where member cities shared the annual Latin Festival (Feriae Latinae). Alba Longa was traditionally its head; after Alba's legendary destruction by Rome, the Romans inherited a leading role. Members shared rights of intermarriage (conubium) and commerce (commercium) — the basis of the later "Latin rights" (ius Latii).

Conflict and alliance with Rome

After the fall of the Roman monarchy, the Latin cities fought Rome at the Battle of Lake Regillus (c. 496 BC) and were then bound to her by the Foedus Cassianum (493 BC), a treaty of perpetual alliance on nominally equal terms. For a century and a half the League's manpower underwrote Rome's wars against the Aequi, Volsci, and Gauls. When the Latins demanded full political equality in 340 BC, Rome refused, and the ensuing Latin War ended in a decisive Roman victory.

Dissolution and settlement of 338 BC

Rome dissolved the League and settled each city individually: some were incorporated with full citizenship, others received civitas sine suffragio (citizenship without the vote), and the remaining Latin colonies retained Latin rights. The settlement became a template for Rome's later organisation of Italy and the graduated statuses of Roman law.
