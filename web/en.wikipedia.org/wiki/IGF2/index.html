IGF-2 is a peptide hormone structurally related to insulin and IGF-1.

Insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF-2) is one of three protein hormones that share structural similarity to insulin. In humans it is encoded by the IGF2 gene on chromosome 11p15.5. IGF-2 is a major growth factor in fetal development, produced largely by the liver and acting through the IGF-1 receptor to promote cell growth and proliferation. It is best known in genetics as a textbook example of genomic imprinting.

Genomic imprinting

In most tissues, only the paternally inherited copy of IGF2 is expressed, while the maternal copy is silenced by imprinting. The neighbouring H19 gene shows the opposite pattern (maternally expressed, paternally imprinted), and the two genes share enhancers whose access is controlled by the methylation state of an imprinting control region and the insulator protein CTCF. Loss of imprinting — biallelic expression of IGF2 — is one of the most common epigenetic abnormalities in human cancer.

Clinical significance

Disruption of the 11p15 imprinted region causes growth disorders in both directions. Loss of paternal IGF2 expression is associated with Silver–Russell syndrome, characterised by growth restriction before and after birth; overexpression is associated with Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome, characterised by overgrowth, macroglossia, and predisposition to embryonal tumours such as Wilms tumour. Overproduction of incompletely processed "big" IGF-2 by some tumours can cause non-islet cell tumour hypoglycaemia (Doege–Potter syndrome) by mimicking insulin.

Function in adults

IGF-2 circulates in adults at higher concentrations than IGF-1, bound to IGF-binding proteins, though its postnatal physiological role is less well defined. It is expressed in the brain, including the choroid plexus and hippocampus, and has been implicated in memory consolidation in experimental studies.
