Conspicuous consumption

Conspicuous consumption is the purchase and display of goods and services primarily to signal wealth, status and prestige rather than for their intrinsic utility. Economist and sociologist Thorstein Veblen coined the term in The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899), alongside "conspicuous leisure" and "pecuniary emulation".

Conspicuous consumption
Coined by: Thorstein Veblen, 1899
Field: Economics, sociology
Related: Veblen goods, status symbol, conspicuous leisure

Theory

Veblen argued the leisure class demonstrates status through wasteful expenditure; others emulate them, driving demand for Veblen goods whose desirability rises with price. Later writers extended the idea to branding, luxury fashion, cars, and social media display, and contrasted it with inconspicuous consumption — spending on education, health and experiences valued by the aspirational class.

Criticism and research

Empirical studies link visible consumption to inequality and relative income concerns. Critics note not all luxury spending is purely positional, and cultural meanings vary. The concept remains central in institutional economics and consumer research.
