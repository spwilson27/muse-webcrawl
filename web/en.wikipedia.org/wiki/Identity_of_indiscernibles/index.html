Identity of indiscernibles

The identity of indiscernibles is an ontological principle that states that there cannot be separate objects or entities that have all their properties in common. That is, entities x and y are identical if every predicate possessed by x is also possessed by y and vice versa; to suppose two things indiscernible from each other is to suppose them to be the same thing. The principle is associated with Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, and is sometimes called Leibniz's law. It is one of the principles at stake in debates about identity and individuation in metaphysics.

Formulation

In second-order logic, the principle can be expressed as: for any objects x and y, if for every property F, x has F if and only if y has F, then x is identical to y. The converse principle — if x and y are identical, then they share all their properties — is the indiscernibility of identicals, and is generally considered uncontroversial. Together the two principles are sometimes called Leibniz's law.

Discussion

A central question is which properties count. If purely qualitative properties only are admitted, the principle is substantive and controversial; if properties such as "being identical to x" or "being at location L at time t" are admitted, it risks becoming trivial. The philosopher Max Black famously challenged the principle with a thought experiment describing a possible universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres, alike in every qualitative respect — including their spatial relations to one another — yet numerically two. Defenders reply that such a scenario is impossible, or that the spheres differ in some property (such as being the sphere that is a certain distance from itself in a particular direction), or that primitive thisness (haecceity) individuates them.

Applications

The principle plays a role in discussions of the philosophy of space and time, where it motivates relationalist accounts of space; in the philosophy of mind; and in quantum mechanics, where elementary particles of the same kind appear to violate it, lacking the individuality classical particles possess. Leibniz himself used the principle as an argument against absolute space in his correspondence with Samuel Clarke.
