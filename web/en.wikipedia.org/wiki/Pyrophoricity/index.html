Pyrophoricity

A substance is pyrophoric (from Greek πυροφόρος, pyrophoros, "fire-bearing") if it ignites spontaneously in air at or below 54 °C (129 °F), or within five minutes of coming into contact with air. Pyrophoric materials react vigorously with oxygen and often with water, and must be handled under an inert atmosphere.

Examples

Examples of pyrophoric substances include white phosphorus, finely divided metals such as iron and uranium powder, metal hydrides, organometallic compounds such as triethylaluminium and tert-butyllithium, and gases such as silane and phosphine. Plutonium is famously pyrophoric when finely divided, forming plutonium hydride on its surface.

Handling

Pyrophoric materials are usually handled using gloveboxes or Schlenk-line techniques under argon or nitrogen, and stored in sealed containers. Small spills may be smothered with dry sand or Class D fire-extinguishing agents; water is generally unsuitable.

Uses

The sparking property of pyrophoric alloys such as ferrocerium is exploited in lighter flints. Pyrophoric reagents are widely used in chemical synthesis and in the semiconductor industry.
