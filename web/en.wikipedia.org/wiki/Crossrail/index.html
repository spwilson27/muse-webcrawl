Crossrail is a railway construction project in London and the South East of England that delivered a new east–west line across the capital, operated as the Elizabeth line by Transport for London. It links Reading and Heathrow Airport in the west with Shenfield in Essex and Abbey Wood in south-east London, through 21 km of new twin tunnels beneath central London with ten new stations. One of Europe's largest infrastructure projects, it added about 10 per cent to London's rail capacity and was forecast to be used by around 200 million passengers a year.

History

An east–west cross-London rail link had been proposed since the 1970s, and a "Crossrail" scheme was studied jointly by London Regional Transport and British Rail in the late 1980s. A private bill failed in 1994, but the project was revived after 2000; the hybrid Crossrail Bill received Royal Assent as the Crossrail Act 2008, with funding from central government, the Greater London Authority, business contributions and TfL. Main construction began at Canary Wharf in May 2009; eight tunnel boring machines drove the central tunnels between 2012 and 2015, excavating some 7 million tonnes of spoil, much of it shipped to create the Wallasea Island nature reserve in Essex.

Delays and opening

The line was originally due to open in December 2018. In August 2018 Crossrail Limited announced a delay, and escalating problems integrating signalling systems, completing stations and testing trains pushed the opening back repeatedly while costs rose from a £14.8 billion budget to about £18.8 billion. The central section between Paddington and Abbey Wood finally opened on 24 May 2022, days before the Platinum Jubilee, and was named the Elizabeth line in honour of Queen Elizabeth II. Through services across the full route commenced in stages, with the final timetable introduced in May 2023.

The line and services

Nine-car Class 345 trains, 200 metres long, run up to 24 trains per hour in the central section at peak. New stations feature platform screen doors, step-free access and distinctive architecture — including the cavernous Bond Street and Farringdon stations — and the route interchanges with almost every London Underground line. Early ridership exceeded forecasts, and the line won the RIBA Stirling Prize for architecture in 2024 for its central stations.
