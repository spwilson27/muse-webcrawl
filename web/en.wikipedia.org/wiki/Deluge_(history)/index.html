Deluge (history)

Swedish forces in Poland during the Deluge, 1655–1660

The Deluge (Polish: Potop) was a series of mid-17th-century campaigns in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, comprising the Swedish invasion of Poland of 1655–1660 (the Second Northern War) together with simultaneous invasions by Russia, Brandenburg, Transylvania and Cossack forces. The period is remembered in Poland as one of the most destructive in its history.

Course of events

Following the Khmelnytsky Uprising and the Russo-Polish War that began in 1654, Charles X Gustav of Sweden invaded the Commonwealth in July 1655. Much of the Polish nobility initially surrendered, and Warsaw and Kraków fell quickly. Resistance coalesced around King John II Casimir, and the successful defense of the Jasna Góra Monastery in late 1655 became a symbol of national resistance. Guerrilla warfare, a Polish–Tatar alliance, and foreign intervention gradually turned the tide.

Consequences

The wars ended with the Treaty of Oliva (1660) and the Truce of Andrusovo (1667). Estimates suggest the Commonwealth lost roughly a third of its population to war, plague and famine, along with immense material and cultural losses, including art and libraries looted by Swedish forces. The Deluge marked the beginning of the Commonwealth's long decline as a great power.
