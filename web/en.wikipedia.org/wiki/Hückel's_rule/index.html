Hückel's rule

In organic chemistry, Hückel's rule predicts that a planar ring molecule will have aromatic properties if it has 4n + 2 π electrons, where n is a non-negative integer. The rule was formulated by the German physicist and chemist Erich Hückel in 1931 as part of his molecular orbital treatment of aromatic compounds, known as Hückel molecular orbital theory.

Number of π electrons = 4n + 2 (n = 0, 1, 2, ...)

Application

The rule applies to monocyclic, planar, fully conjugated systems. Benzene, with 6 π electrons (n = 1), is the prototypical example. The cyclopentadienyl anion and the tropylium cation also have 6 π electrons and are aromatic, whereas cyclooctatetraene, with 8 π electrons (4n, n = 2), is non-aromatic and adopts a non-planar tub shape to avoid antiaromatic destabilisation. Larger annulenes such as [18]annulene (n = 4) can also satisfy the rule if the ring is sufficiently planar.

Criteria for aromaticity

For Hückel's rule to apply, a compound must be cyclic, planar, fully conjugated (every ring atom contributing a p orbital), and possess 4n + 2 π electrons. Systems with 4n π electrons that are planar and conjugated are described as antiaromatic and are typically highly unstable. The rule was later extended by concepts such as Möbius aromaticity, in which twisted topologies reverse the electron-count requirement.

History

Hückel derived the rule from quantum-mechanical calculations on cyclic π systems published in the early 1930s. Although initially slow to be adopted by organic chemists, it gained wide acceptance after experimental confirmations accumulated, including the discovery of the aromatic cyclopentadienyl anion in ferrocene and the tropylium ion.
