Fennel (Foeniculum vulgare) is a flowering plant species in the carrot family Apiaceae. It is a hardy, perennial herb with yellow flowers and feathery leaves, native to the shores of the Mediterranean but now widely naturalized in many parts of the world, especially on dry soils near the sea and on riverbanks. It is cultivated for its edible, strongly flavored leaves, seeds, and—in the variety Florence fennel—its swollen bulb-like stem base.

Description

Fennel grows up to 2.5 m tall with hollow stems and finely dissected, thread-like leaves up to 40 cm long. The flowers are produced in terminal compound umbels 5–15 cm wide, each with 20–50 tiny yellow flowers. The fruit is a dry seed 4–10 mm long. All parts of the plant are aromatic, with a flavor dominated by the compound anethole, similar to anise and liquorice.

Cultivation and uses

Two main types are grown: bitter (common) fennel, cultivated chiefly for seed used as a spice and in herbal medicine, and sweet or Florence fennel (F. vulgare var. azoricum), grown as a vegetable for its bulb. Fennel seed is a staple spice in Indian panch phoron and Chinese five-spice mixtures, flavors Italian sausages and Scandinavian aquavit, and is chewed as a breath freshener and digestive in India (often as mukhwas). Fennel was historically used in herbal medicine as a carminative and is one of the plants traditionally said to ward off evil; it was also one of the herbs in the original recipe background of absinthe.

Ecology

Fennel is a larval host plant for the anise swallowtail butterfly. In some regions, such as parts of California and Australia, it is considered an invasive weed.
