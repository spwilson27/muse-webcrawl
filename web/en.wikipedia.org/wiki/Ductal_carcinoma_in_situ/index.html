Ductal carcinoma in situ

Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is a pre-cancerous or non-invasive lesion of the breast in which abnormal cells line the milk ducts but have not spread into surrounding breast tissue. DCIS is considered the earliest form of breast cancer and is often referred to as stage 0. Because it is non-invasive, it does not metastasize; however, some cases progress to invasive breast cancer if untreated.

Detection and diagnosis

DCIS is most commonly detected by screening mammography, frequently as microcalcifications. Diagnosis is confirmed by biopsy, and lesions are graded by nuclear features and classified by architectural pattern, including comedo, cribriform, micropapillary, papillary and solid types. The presence of comedonecrosis is associated with higher-grade disease.

Treatment

Standard management includes breast-conserving surgery (lumpectomy), often followed by radiation therapy, or mastectomy for extensive disease. Hormonal therapy such as tamoxifen may be offered for hormone-receptor-positive DCIS to reduce recurrence risk. Because many DCIS lesions would never progress, active-surveillance trials have investigated whether some low-risk cases can be safely monitored instead of immediately treated, and the extent of treatment remains an area of active debate regarding possible overtreatment.

Epidemiology

Rates of DCIS rose sharply with the widespread adoption of mammographic screening from the 1980s onward; it now accounts for roughly a fifth to a quarter of newly diagnosed breast "cancers" in screened populations, though its natural history without treatment is incompletely understood.
