Phenomenon

A phenomenon (plural phenomena) is any observable event, fact, or occurrence. The word comes from the Greek phainomenon, "that which appears," from the verb phainesthai, "to show oneself." In common usage the term often carries the sense of something remarkable or extraordinary — a "phenomenal" success — but its technical uses in philosophy and science are broader and more precise.

In science

In scientific usage, a phenomenon is any event that can be observed, measured, and studied, whether it occurs naturally (such as lightning, tides, or magnetism) or is produced in an experiment. Scientific inquiry typically proceeds by describing phenomena, classifying them, and explaining them through laws and theories; the data of observation themselves — as distinct from the inferred underlying entities — are the phenomena a theory is expected to "save" or account for.

In philosophy

The term acquired a central technical role with Immanuel Kant, who distinguished phenomena — things as they appear to us, structured by the forms of intuition (space and time) and the categories of the understanding — from noumena, "things in themselves," which lie beyond possible experience. The contrast goes back in part to Plato's distinction between the world of appearances and the realm of Forms.

In the twentieth century, phenomenology, founded by Edmund Husserl, took its name from the study of phenomena understood as the structures of conscious experience as experienced from the first-person point of view. In contemporary analytic philosophy, "phenomenal" character refers to the subjective, qualitative "what it is like" aspect of experience (qualia).

Usage

By extension, "phenomenon" is applied to notable people or events ("a pop phenomenon") and, in popular culture, to reported occurrences of disputed reality ("paranormal phenomena"), a usage scientists generally avoid because it suggests acceptance of the reports.
