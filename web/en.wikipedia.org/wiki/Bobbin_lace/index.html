Bobbin lace
TypeLace made with bobbins and a pillow
OriginsEarly 16th century, Italy and Flanders
Notable typesHoniton, Brussels, Chantilly, Valenciennes, Bedfordshire, Torchon

Bobbin lace is a lace textile made by braiding and twisting lengths of thread, wound on bobbins, around pins stuck in a pattern (a "pricking") pinned to a firm pillow. As the work progresses the pins are moved, and the woven sections are held in place until the lace is finished. Bobbin lace is one of the two major categories of handmade lace, the other being needle lace.

History

Bobbin lace developed in the early sixteenth century, with early centers in Genoa and other Italian cities and in Flanders; exactly where it began is debated, with some evidence pointing to its evolution from passementerie and braided trimmings. It spread rapidly as a luxury craft: lace edged the ruffs, collars, and cuffs of wealthy dress across Europe. Major regional styles emerged — Milanese, Valenciennes, Mechlin, Brussels point, Honiton (Devon), and Bedfordshire lace in England — each with characteristic grounds, motifs, and techniques. Production was often a cottage industry employing women and children; lacemaking schools trained girls from a young age.

The craft declined with the invention of machine lace — beginning with John Heathcoat's bobbinet machine (1808–1809) — which could imitate handmade grounds cheaply, though fine handmade bobbin lace continued to be made and is now practiced largely as an art and heritage craft.

Technique

The lacemaker works with pairs of bobbins, each wound with thread. The fundamental stitches — cross and twist — combine to form cloth stitch, half stitch, and whole stitch, varied to produce solid areas (clothwork), open grounds (such as the hexagonal réseau), picots, tallies, and decorative "spiders." Patterns range from simple geometric Torchon ("beggar's lace") to the extraordinarily fine floral work of Brussels and Honiton, which used dozens or hundreds of bobbins at once.

Regional varieties

English Honiton lace is known for finely worked sprigs of flowers joined by brides; Chantilly lace, originally from France, is prized for its shaded floral designs on a fine net ground; Idrija lace from Slovenia and the lace of Bruges are among surviving European traditions. Bobbin lace is recognized in several countries' intangible cultural heritage listings.
