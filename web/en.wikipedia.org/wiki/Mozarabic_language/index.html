Mozarabic (also called Andalusi Romance) was a group of closely related Romance varieties spoken in the Muslim-ruled parts of the Iberian Peninsula, known as Al-Andalus, during the Middle Ages. It was the everyday Romance speech of the Mozarabs — Christians living under Islamic rule — as well as of many Muslims and Jews. Unlike the Romance languages of the Christian north (Galician-Portuguese, Castilian, Catalan and Aragonese), Mozarabic developed in relative isolation under strong Arabic influence and eventually disappeared as the Christian kingdoms expanded southward.

History

After the Umayyad conquest of Hispania in 711, the local Romance dialects continued to be spoken by much of the population even as Arabic became the language of administration, scholarship and prestige. Over the centuries a large proportion of the population became bilingual, and Arabic vocabulary flowed into the Romance vernacular. The earliest substantial written evidence of Mozarabic consists of the kharjas (Spanish: jarchas), short lyric refrains in Romance — or mixed Arabic and Romance — appended to learned Arabic and Hebrew poems (muwashshahat) from the 11th and 12th centuries. These brief texts, often love songs in a woman's voice, are among the earliest lyric poetry in any Romance language.

As the Reconquista advanced, Mozarabic-speaking communities in cities such as Toledo (reconquered in 1085), Valencia and Granada were gradually absorbed into the linguistic areas of Castilian, Catalan and Portuguese. The language declined through the later Middle Ages and is generally considered extinct by the end of the medieval period, though its legacy survives in place names and vocabulary.

Features and influence

Mozarabic was in many respects conservative, preserving phonological features lost in the northern dialects — for example, it retained Latin word-initial groups such as pl-, cl- and fl- where Castilian palatalised them to ll-. At the same time it was deeply marked by Arabic: a large stock of loanwords, Arabic-derived suffixes, and the occasional use of the Arabic article. Written records survive chiefly in Arabic script, a practice known as aljamiado.

The language's best-known legacy is the rich stratum of Arabic and Mozarabic vocabulary in Spanish and Portuguese — words for plants, foods, irrigation and administration — and a large number of Iberian toponyms ending in forms such as -ín or preserving Arabic-Romance hybrids.
