P5 (microarchitecture)

P5
Launched: March 22, 1993
Manufacturer: Intel
Products: Original Pentium, Pentium MMX
Predecessor: i486 (P4)
Successor: P6 (Pentium Pro)

The P5 is a fifth-generation x86 microarchitecture designed by Intel, introduced with the original Pentium processor on March 22, 1993. It was the first superscalar x86 design, able to issue two instructions per clock cycle through its dual U and V integer pipelines.

Features

The P5 introduced a 64-bit external data bus, separate 8 KB instruction and data caches, a branch target buffer for branch prediction, and a much faster pipelined floating-point unit compared with the i486. Initial models ran at 60 and 66 MHz using a 5-volt, 0.8 µm process; later versions (P54C, P54CS) reached 200 MHz, and the P55C added MMX instructions and larger 16 KB caches.

FDIV bug

In 1994 a flaw in the P5 floating-point divider—the Pentium FDIV bug—was discovered by professor Thomas Nicely. After public pressure, Intel offered to replace all affected chips, taking a $475 million charge.

Legacy

The P5 design, licensed or cloned by competitors, underpinned the Pentium brand for years, and the low-power descendants of the architecture influenced later in-order designs. The microarchitecture was succeeded by the out-of-order P6 family, beginning with the Pentium Pro in 1995.
