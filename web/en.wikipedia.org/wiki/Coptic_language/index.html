Coptic (Bohairic Coptic: ϯⲙⲉⲧⲣⲉⲙⲛ̀ⲭⲏⲙⲓ, ti.met.rem.ən.khēmi) is a group of closely related Egyptian dialects, representing the most recent stage of the Egyptian language. It was spoken in Egypt from roughly the 2nd century AD until at least the 17th century, and survives today as the liturgical language of the Coptic Orthodox Church of Alexandria.

Coptic is written with the Coptic alphabet, based on Greek uncial letters supplemented by six or seven characters derived from Demotic Egyptian for sounds not present in Greek. It absorbed a large Greek vocabulary, especially in religious and administrative texts.

Dialects

Several literary dialects are attested, of which the most important are Sahidic, once the dominant dialect of Upper Egypt and the vehicle of a rich monastic literature, and Bohairic, the dialect of the Nile Delta, which became the liturgical standard of the Coptic Church. Others include Akhmimic, Lycopolitan, Fayyumic and Oxyrhynchite.

History

Coptic emerged as Egyptian Christians translated the Bible and wrote original theological, monastic and magical texts. After the Arab conquest of Egypt in the 7th century, Arabic gradually displaced Coptic as the language of administration and daily life; by the 17th century Coptic had ceased to be a mother tongue, though attempts at revival have been made since the 19th century. Knowledge of Coptic was crucial to Jean-François Champollion's decipherment of the Egyptian hieroglyphs in the 1820s, since Coptic preserved the vocabulary and grammar of the ancient language.
