In diplomacy, protocol is the body of rules, conventions and ceremonies that governs how states and their representatives treat one another: the order of precedence among officials, the forms of address and correspondence, the presentation of credentials by ambassadors, the conduct of state visits, the display of flags and national symbols, seating at official functions, and matters of dress and gift-giving. Its purpose is practical as much as ceremonial: by fixing in advance how sovereign equals are to be treated, protocol removes rank and honour from day-to-day negotiation and allows governments that may disagree on everything else to meet without disputes over status. The word derives from the Greek prōtokollon, the first sheet glued to a papyrus roll identifying its contents, and passed through Byzantine and French chancellery usage into the modern sense of the rules of official etiquette.

History

Questions of precedence were once matters of war and peace: rival ambassadors in early modern Europe literally fought with coaches and retinues in the streets of London (1661) and elsewhere over whose carriage should precede. The Congress of Vienna (1815) ended much of this by classifying diplomatic agents into ranks—ambassadors, envoys and chargés d'affaires—and fixing precedence within each rank by date of accreditation, rules adjusted at Aix-la-Chapelle (1818) and largely codified today in the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations of 1961. Within the diplomatic corps of a capital, the longest-serving ambassador becomes doyen (dean) and speaks for the corps on ceremonial occasions; in many Catholic countries the papal nuncio holds the deanship by custom. Protocol for international organisations was regularised in the twentieth century; at the United Nations, seating in the General Assembly is determined partly by lot each session, and heads of state rank by the date they took office for many ceremonial purposes.

Elements of protocol

Precedence

Every state maintains an official order of precedence ranking its own dignitaries—head of state, head of government, ministers, judges, legislators—and visiting foreigners are slotted into it by rank and seniority. Orders of precedence determine who is announced first, who sits where at a state banquet, and the order of signatures, toasts and photographs at summits.

Credentials and accreditation

An ambassador formally begins work by presenting letters of credence from their head of state to the receiving head of state, usually at a short ceremony; until then, protocol restricts their official dealings. Ambassadors who are withdrawn for consultations or recalled signal displeasure by gradations protocol makes legible to all.

State and official visits

Visits are graded—state visit, official visit, working visit, private visit—with descending ceremonial: state visits typically include a formal welcome with guard of honour and national anthems, a state banquet, and an exchange of decorations and gifts. Every detail, from the number of guns in a salute (21 for a head of state) to motorcade order, is specified.

Flags and symbols

Flag protocol prescribes the order of flags (host's flag in the position of honour, others generally in alphabetical order in the host language), their equal size and height, and their use on cars, desks and conference tables. At international organisations the flags of member states fly in a fixed order, and the organisation's flag takes precedence within its own headquarters.

Forms of address and correspondence

Protocol governs styles such as "Excellency" for ambassadors and heads of government, "Majesty" and "Royal Highness" for royalty, diplomatic notes' formulae of courtesy, and the language and seals of treaties, including the alternat by which each party's copy of a treaty lists its own plenipotentiary first.

Chiefs of protocol

Most foreign ministries and presidential administrations employ a chief of protocol—in the United States an official with the rank of ambassador—whose office plans visits, maintains the order of precedence, advises on etiquette, and accredits diplomats. In monarchies the corresponding functions lie with court officials such as the lord chamberlain or, in the United Kingdom's diplomatic corps, His Majesty's Marshal of the Diplomatic Corps. Breaches of protocol, from seating errors to mis-ordered flags, are usually accidental but are treated seriously because counterparts read them as signals; a celebrated example is the careful choreography of the 1972 Nixon visit to China, where protocol details carried the message of rapprochement.
