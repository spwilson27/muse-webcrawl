Welfare state

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A welfare state is a form of government in which the state plays a key role in protecting and promoting the economic and social well-being of its citizens, through social insurance, public services and redistribution: pensions, unemployment and sickness benefits, public healthcare and education, housing support and income floors. The principle — opportunity and security underwritten collectively, funded by taxation — distinguishes it both from the minimal "night-watchman" state and from state ownership of the whole economy; most rich democracies are welfare states of some variety.

Origins and models

The first modern system was Bismarck's Germany: social insurance laws of 1883 (health), 1884 (accidents) and 1889 (old age) — enacted by a conservative chancellor partly to undercut the socialists. Britain's Liberal reforms (1906–14: pensions, National Insurance) preceded the landmark Beveridge Report of 1942, which named the "five giants" (Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor, Idleness) and blueprinted the post-1945 settlement: the National Health Service (1948), family allowances and full-employment policy — Attlee's welfare state as the domestic face of victory in the Second World War. The political scientist Gøsta Esping-Andersen's famous typology (1990) sorted welfare states into three "worlds": social-democratic (Nordic countries — universal, generous, high decommodification), conservative-corporatist (Germany, France — status-linked insurance), and liberal (United States, UK — means-tested, residual), a map still used to compare everything from childcare to pension reform. Perennial debates — affordability under ageing populations, incentives and "dependency", immigration and solidarity, privatisation — surged after the 1970s fiscal crises and the 2008 financial crash, yet welfare states proved resilient: spending shares stabilised rather than collapsed, and the COVID-19 furlough schemes showed even liberal states reaching for the welfare toolkit at speed.
