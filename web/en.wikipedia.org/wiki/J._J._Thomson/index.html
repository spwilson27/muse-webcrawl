Sir Joseph John Thomson (1856–1940), commonly known as J. J. Thomson, was a British physicist credited with the discovery of the electron, the first subatomic particle. As Cavendish Professor at Cambridge and head of the Cavendish Laboratory, he built a school of experimental physics whose members (including Ernest Rutherford) reshaped the understanding of the atom; he received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1906.

Discovery of the electron

Investigating cathode rays, Thomson showed in 1897 that they were deflected by electric fields (earlier experimenters, using poorer vacua, had failed to observe this) and that they consisted of negatively charged "corpuscles" with a charge-to-mass ratio about 1,800 times that of a hydrogen ion and independent of the gas or electrode material used. He concluded the corpuscles — soon universally called electrons — were universal constituents of atoms, demolishing the idea of the atom as indivisible. In 1904 he proposed the "plum pudding model", in which electrons were embedded in a diffuse sphere of positive charge; the model was superseded after Rutherford's gold-foil experiments (1909–11).

Other work

Thomson's studies of positive rays with his "parabola spectrograph" amounted to the first mass spectrometer; they led him and his assistant Francis Aston to discover stable isotopes, finding in 1913 that neon consisted of atoms of mass 20 and 22. He also contributed to the theory of electromagnetic mass and the conduction of electricity through gases, the subject of his Nobel citation. His son, George Paget Thomson, won the 1937 Nobel Prize for electron diffraction — demonstrating the wave nature of the particle his father had discovered as a corpuscle.
