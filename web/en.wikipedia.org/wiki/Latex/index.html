Latex is an emulsion of polymer microparticles in water. Latices are found in nature as milky fluid exuded by many flowering plants — about 10 percent of angiosperms — but the term most often refers to the latex of the Pará rubber tree (Hevea brasiliensis), the source of natural rubber, and to synthetic polymer emulsions used in paints and adhesives.

Biology

In plants, latex circulates in specialised cells or vessels called laticifers. It is a complex mixture that may contain proteins, alkaloids, sugars, oils, tannins, resins, and the polyisoprene that becomes rubber. Its defensive function is to seal wounds and deter herbivores; in some plants it is toxic or hallucinogenic — the opium poppy's latex yields opium.

Natural rubber latex

Tappers harvest latex by cutting grooves in the bark of rubber trees and collecting the fluid in cups. Fresh latex is about 30–40 percent rubber particles; it is concentrated, preserved with ammonia, and processed into products such as medical gloves, condoms, balloons, and foam. Coagulated latex forms the raw material for tyre and solid-rubber manufacture.

Synthetic latex

Synthetic latices are produced by emulsion polymerisation of monomers such as styrene and butadiene or acrylates. They form the basis of water-based ("latex") paints, paper coatings, carpet backing, and adhesives.

Latex allergy

Proteins in natural rubber latex can cause Type I allergic reactions in sensitised individuals, a significant concern for healthcare workers and patients; this has driven substitution of synthetic alternatives such as nitrile in gloves.
