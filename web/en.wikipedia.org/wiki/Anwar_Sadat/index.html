Muhammad Anwar el-Sadat (25 December 1918 – 6 October 1981) was an Egyptian politician and military officer who served as the third president of Egypt from 1970 until his assassination in 1981. A member of the Free Officers who overthrew the monarchy in 1952, he succeeded Gamal Abdel Nasser and transformed Egypt's domestic and foreign policy.

Rise to power

Sadat graduated from the Royal Military Academy in Cairo in 1938 and took part in nationalist conspiracies, spending time in prison during the Second World War for contacts with the Axis. As a Free Officer he announced the 1952 revolution on the radio, and under Nasser he held posts including Speaker of the National Assembly and Vice President. On Nasser's death in September 1970 he became president and consolidated power in the "Corrective Revolution" of May 1971, purging pro-Soviet rivals.

Presidency

In 1972 Sadat expelled Soviet military advisers. In October 1973, together with Syria, he launched the Yom Kippur War against Israel to recover the Sinai Peninsula, lost in 1967; the initial crossing of the Suez Canal restored Egyptian military prestige even though the war ended inconclusively. Domestically, his infitah ("open door") policy liberalized the economy and encouraged Western investment, while political liberalization remained limited.

In November 1977 Sadat made a dramatic visit to Jerusalem, addressing the Knesset — the first Arab leader to recognize Israel publicly. The Camp David Accords (1978) with Menachem Begin and U.S. President Jimmy Carter followed, for which Sadat and Begin shared the Nobel Peace Prize, and the Egypt–Israel peace treaty of 1979 returned the Sinai to Egypt. The treaty isolated Egypt in the Arab world and angered Islamists at home.

Assassination

After a crackdown on dissent in September 1981, Sadat was assassinated on 6 October 1981 by members of Egyptian Islamic Jihad during the annual victory parade in Cairo. He was succeeded by his vice president, Hosni Mubarak.
