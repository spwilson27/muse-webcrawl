Stone wall

A stone wall is a kind of masonry construction that has been used for thousands of years. The first stone walls were field walls built by early farmers to mark boundaries, enclose livestock and clear stones from their land. In many regions stone walls are an enduring and characteristic part of the rural landscape.

Construction

Stone walls may be built with mortar or without. A wall built without mortar is a dry stone wall: its strength comes from the careful placement of stones so that they interlock, from slightly battered (inward-sloping) faces, from through-stones tying the two faces together, and from a capping of upright cope stones. Mortared walls rely on the binding action of lime or cement mortar. Retaining walls hold back soil on slopes and are built thicker at the base.

History

Stone walling dates to the Neolithic period; the walls of Çatalhöyük, Skara Brae and ancient field systems in Ireland and Britain (such as the Céide Fields) survive from prehistory. Later ages produced monumental defensive walls — the Great Wall of China, Hadrian's Wall and city walls across Europe and Asia. In New England, thousands of miles of fieldstone walls were built by farmers between the 17th and 19th centuries; poet Robert Frost's "Mending Wall" (1914) made the annual repair of such walls a byword. In Britain, dry stone walls define the field patterns of regions such as the Yorkshire Dales and Cumbria, and the craft of dry stone walling has been recognised by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage.

Ecology

Old stone walls provide habitat for lichens, mosses, ferns, insects, reptiles and small mammals, and act as wildlife corridors across farmland. Their crevices shelter hibernating animals and nesting birds.
