Francisco Goya

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Self-Portrait with Dr Arrieta (1820): the aged, deaf Goya supported by his doctor, one of his last paintings]

Francisco José de Goya y Lucientes (30 March 1746 – 16 April 1828) was a Spanish romantic painter and printmaker, regarded both as the last of the Old Masters and the first of the moderns. He was court painter to the Spanish Crown, a portraitist of the aristocracy and royalty, and in his uncommissioned prints and late 'Black Paintings' a pitiless chronicler of war, superstition and human folly.

His most famous works include the paired paintings The Second of May 1808 and The Third of May 1808 (1814), the etching series Los Caprichos (1799) and The Disasters of War (c. 1810–20), and the paired portraits La Maja Desnuda and La Maja Vestida.

Career at court

Born in Fuendetodos, Aragón, Goya trained in Zaragoza and Madrid, travelled to Italy, and won royal notice through tapestry cartoons for the Royal Tapestry Factory. He became painter to Charles III in 1786 and first court painter to Charles IV in 1799, painting the celebrated group portrait Charles IV of Spain and His Family (1800). A grave illness in 1792–93 left him permanently deaf and marked the turn towards the darker, more subjective art of his later years.

War and the Black Paintings

The Peninsular War and its atrocities under French occupation produced The Disasters of War, etchings of famine, torture and execution that were not published until 35 years after his death. In old age, living in retirement at the Quinta del Sordo on the Manzanares, Goya painted directly onto the walls the fourteen works now known as the Black Paintings — among them Saturn Devouring His Son — which were transferred to canvas in the 1870s and are now in the Prado. He died in exile in Bordeaux in 1828.
