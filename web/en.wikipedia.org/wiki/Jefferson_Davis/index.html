Jefferson Davis

Photograph of Jefferson Davis, President of the Confederate States

Jefferson F. Davis (June 3, 1808 – December 6, 1889) was an American politician who served as the first and only president of the Confederate States of America during the Civil War (1861–1865). Before that he was a U.S. senator from Mississippi and Secretary of War under President Franklin Pierce.

Early life and career

Born in Kentucky and raised in Mississippi, Davis graduated from West Point in 1828 and served on the frontier. He resigned to become a planter; his first wife, Sarah Knox Taylor — daughter of future president Zachary Taylor — died of malaria three months after their wedding in 1835. Davis rebuilt his fortunes as a cotton planter dependent on enslaved labor, entered Congress in 1845, and won distinction commanding a Mississippi regiment in the Mexican–American War, notably at the Battle of Buena Vista.

Senator and Secretary of War

As senator and then as Pierce's Secretary of War (1853–57), Davis modernized the army, oversaw the Gadsden Purchase, and defended slavery's expansion as a constitutional right. Returning to the Senate, he opposed secession in principle but held that states had the right to leave the Union; when Mississippi seceded in January 1861 he resigned and, in February, was chosen provisional — then elected — president of the Confederacy.

Confederate presidency

Davis struggled with quarrelsome generals and governors, an economy strangled by blockade and inflation, and the Confederacy's ideological resistance to centralized power. He appointed and feuded with commanders, clashed with his vice president Alexander Stephens, and authorized the first conscription in American history. After Richmond fell in April 1865, he fled south and was captured in Georgia on May 10, 1865.

Imprisonment and later life

Imprisoned at Fort Monroe for two years and indicted for treason, Davis was released on bail in 1867; the charges were dropped in 1869, and his citizenship rights were restored posthumously in 1978. He wrote The Rise and Fall of the Confederate Government (1881), defending secession and the Confederate cause, and spent his last years at Beauvoir estate in Mississippi, remaining an unrepentant symbol of the "Lost Cause" until his death in New Orleans in 1889.
