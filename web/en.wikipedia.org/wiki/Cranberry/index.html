Cranberry

Cranberries are a group of evergreen dwarf shrubs or trailing vines in the subgenus Oxycoccus of the genus Vaccinium, and the sour red berries they produce. The principal commercial species is the American cranberry, Vaccinium macrocarpon, native to North America.

Cultivation

Cranberries grow in acidic wetlands called bogs. Commercial beds are flooded in autumn so that harvesting machines can beat the berries loose; the buoyant fruit floats to the surface and is corralled for collection — the familiar image of a sea of red berries — although a smaller share is dry-harvested fresh. Major producing regions include Wisconsin (the largest producer), Massachusetts, New Jersey, Oregon and Washington in the United States, and Quebec and British Columbia in Canada.

Uses

Because fresh cranberries are extremely tart, most are processed into juice drinks, the sweetened dried cranberries used in baking, and cranberry sauce, the traditional accompaniment to roast turkey at Thanksgiving and Christmas. The fruit is rich in vitamin C and polyphenols; cranberry products are popularly believed to help prevent urinary tract infections, for which the evidence is mixed, with proanthocyanidins thought to discourage bacteria from adhering to the bladder wall.

History

Native American peoples used cranberries in food such as pemmican, as medicine and as dye. Commercial cultivation began in the early nineteenth century on Cape Cod, and the growers' cooperative Ocean Spray, founded in 1930, still markets much of the North American crop.
