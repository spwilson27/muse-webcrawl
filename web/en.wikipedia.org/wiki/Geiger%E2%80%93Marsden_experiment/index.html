Geiger–Marsden experiment

The Geiger–Marsden experiment (also called the gold foil experiment or the Rutherford scattering experiment) was a landmark experiment performed in 1909 by Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden under the direction of Ernest Rutherford at the University of Manchester. By firing alpha particles at a thin gold foil and observing their scattering, the experiment revealed that atoms have a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus.

Method and results

A beam of alpha particles from a radioactive source struck a gold foil only a few hundred atoms thick; a zinc sulfide screen and microscope were used to count scintillations at various angles. Most particles passed straight through, but about 1 in 8,000 was deflected through more than 90°, some almost directly backward — a result Rutherford compared to firing a shell at tissue paper and having it rebound.

Interpretation

The large-angle scattering could not be explained by J. J. Thomson's plum pudding model, in which charge is spread through the atom. In 1911 Rutherford concluded that the atom's positive charge and most of its mass are concentrated in a nucleus of radius about 10⁻¹⁴ metres, with electrons orbiting in mostly empty space — the Rutherford model of the atom and the foundation of nuclear physics.
