Helix

A helix (plural: helices) is a shape like a corkscrew or spiral staircase — a smooth space curve lying on the surface of a cylinder or cone, with a constant angle between its tangent and the axis. The helix is a fundamental form in geometry, engineering (screw threads, springs, drill bits), and biology (the DNA double helix and protein alpha helices).

A right-handed helix

Mathematical description

A helix on a circular cylinder of radius a with pitch 2πb can be parametrised as

x(t) = a cos t,   y(t) = a sin t,   z(t) = b t.

The constant ratio of curvature to torsion distinguishes the helix among space curves (Lancret's theorem): a curve is a general helix if and only if its curvature and torsion are proportional. A helix with b > 0 is right-handed; with b < 0 it is left-handed. If the radius shrinks linearly the curve is a conical helix, which lies on a cone.

Examples in nature and technology

Helical structures occur in the tendrils of climbing plants, the horns of some antelopes, the shells of gastropods, bacterial flagella and spiral galaxies. Artificial helices include coil springs, screw threads, helicopters' namesake blades (from Greek helix + pteron), spiral staircases and the Archimedes screw. In molecular biology the double helix of DNA, described by Watson and Crick in 1953, consists of two antiparallel strands wound around a common axis, and the alpha helix is a common secondary structure of proteins.
