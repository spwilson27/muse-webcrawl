Great Siege of Gibraltar

The Great Siege of Gibraltar was an unsuccessful attempt by Spain and France to capture Gibraltar from the British during the American Revolutionary War. It lasted from 24 June 1779 to 7 February 1783—three years, seven months, and twelve days—and was the longest siege endured by the British Army. Gibraltar's position at the entrance to the Mediterranean made it of great strategic importance, and Spain entered the war against Britain in 1779 largely in hopes of recovering it.

Course of the siege

The garrison, about 5,000–7,000 men under General George Augustus Eliott, was blockaded by land and sea and subjected to heavy bombardment. The garrison's survival depended on three relief operations by the Royal Navy: by Admiral George Rodney in January 1780 (after his victory at the Battle of Cape St. Vincent, the "Moonlight Battle"), by Admiral George Darby in April 1781, and by Admiral Lord Howe in October 1782. Civilian inhabitants were largely evacuated to Britain; those who remained endured disease, including scurvy, until fresh provisions arrived with the convoys.

The Grand Assault

On 13 September 1782, the besiegers launched their grand assault with ten specially built floating batteries, supported by land batteries and the combined fleets—some 60,000 Franco-Spanish troops were arrayed against the Rock. The garrison's use of red-hot shot set the floating batteries afire, and the assault collapsed with heavy losses. The last major bombardment followed in the autumn, and the siege was lifted in February 1783 after preliminary peace terms were agreed.

Legacy

The defense became one of the most celebrated episodes in British military history. The siege produced innovations including the depressed gun carriages devised by Lieutenant George Koehler and the tunneling of galleries into the north face of the Rock, the origin of Gibraltar's famous siege tunnels. The siege is commemorated in the battle honour "Gibraltar 1779–83" borne by several British regiments.
