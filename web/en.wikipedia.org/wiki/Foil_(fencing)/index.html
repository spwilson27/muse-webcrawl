The foil is a light thrusting weapon used in the sport of fencing. It is one of the three weapons used in the sport, alongside the épée and the sabre. The foil is descended from the 18th-century small sword training weapon; its name derives from the French fleuret.

The modern foil averages a total length of 110 cm (43 in) with a standardized, tapered, rectangular blade of 90 cm (35 in) and a weight under 500 grams (18 oz). Because the foil is a thrusting weapon only, hits may be scored only with the point of the blade. Foil fencers wear a conductive lamé jacket to register hits on the valid target area, and electric scoring apparatus uses a body cord connected to a reel. The foil blade has a button at the tip enclosing a spring-loaded switch that must be depressed with a force of at least 500 grams to register a touch.

Rules

The valid target in foil is the torso, including the groin and the lower part of the bib of the mask, but excluding the arms, legs, and most of the head. Foil is governed by the convention of right of way (priority): when both fencers land hits simultaneously, the touch is awarded to the fencer who initiated a correctly executed attack, or who successfully parried and riposted. A fencer who hits off-target halts the phrase. Bouts are typically fenced to 15 touches (in direct elimination) or 5 touches (in pools), within time limits.

Technique

Foil is often the first weapon taught to beginners, as it instils point control, distance, and the conventions of priority that also apply to sabre. Characteristic actions include the straight thrust, the disengage, the balestra (a hopping advance), the flèche (a running attack), and compound attacks with one or more feints; defensive actions centre on parries — quarte, sixte, octave, and septime — followed by the riposte. Flicks, in which the blade is whipped so its point lands on the back, became prominent with modern flexible blades and scoring timing.

History

The foil developed in 18th-century France as a practice weapon for the small sword, with a blunted ("flowered," fleuret) tip for safety. Foil fencing featured at the first modern Olympic Games in 1896, women's foil was added in 1924, and electric scoring was adopted for foil at the 1955 World Championships and the 1956 Olympics. Italy and France have historically dominated foil, with Hungary and, more recently, the United States, Russia, and China also producing champions. Famous foilists include Edoardo Mangiarotti, Valentina Vezzali — a three-time individual Olympic champion — and Miles Chamley-Watson.
