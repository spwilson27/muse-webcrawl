Varzea forest

A várzea forest is a seasonal floodplain forest inundated by whitewater rivers that flows alongside them in the Amazon basin of South America. Whitewater rivers such as the Amazon, Madeira and Japurá carry nutrient-rich sediments from the Andes, so várzea soils are fertile — in contrast to the nutrient-poor blackwater floodplains known as igapó and the never-flooded upland terra firme forest.

Flooding regime

Várzea forests are flooded for several months each year, with water levels rising 10–15 metres during the high-water season. Trees here are adapted to prolonged submersion: many disperse seeds during the flood, when fish enter the forest and feed on fallen fruit, acting as seed dispersers. Species composition changes with elevation and flood duration, from shrubby pioneer communities on new sandbars to tall late-successional forest on older levees.

Ecology

The flood pulse drives a highly productive ecosystem supporting fish, river dolphins, caimans, capybaras and rich birdlife. Characteristic trees include kapok (Ceiba), Calycophyllum, rubber trees and numerous palms. Várzea covers a small fraction of the Amazon basin but supports a disproportionate share of its aquatic productivity.

Human use

Riverine communities (ribeirinhos) farm várzea soils during the dry season, growing manioc, bananas and vegetables, and fish the flooded forest at high water. Because of its fertility and accessibility the várzea has been settled and altered more than terra firme, and faces pressure from logging, cattle ranching on cleared levees and oil exploration. Protected areas such as the Mamirauá Sustainable Development Reserve conserve large tracts of várzea.
