The Prix de Rome (French for "Rome Prize") was a French scholarship for arts students, initially for painters and sculptors, that was established in 1663 during the reign of Louis XIV. Winners were awarded a bursary that let them stay in Rome for several years at the expense of the state, studying the masterpieces of classical antiquity and the Renaissance. The prize was extended to architecture in 1720, to music in 1803 and to engraving in 1804.

History

The prize was administered by the Académie royale de peinture et de sculpture and later by the Académie des Beaux-Arts. Candidates — French nationals under thirty — competed in gruelling elimination stages; finalists in painting were shut away in studios (loges) for up to 72 days to execute a work on a set subject drawn from the Bible, ancient history or mythology.

Winning the premier grand prix virtually guaranteed a successful career: holders became professors, members of the Academy and recipients of state commissions. In Rome the scholars (pensionnaires) lived at the Palazzo Mancini and, from 1803, at the Villa Medici, directed for a time by Ingres.

Notable winners and controversies

Laureates included the painters Nicolas Poussin's successors such as David, Ingres and Géricault; the composer Hector Berlioz (1830, after several attempts he described witheringly in his memoirs); Georges Bizet (1857); Claude Debussy (1884); and the architect Tony Garnier. Maurice Ravel's repeated failure to win — culminating in the scandal of 1905, when he was eliminated in the preliminary round despite an international reputation — provoked public outrage and reforms at the Conservatoire.

Women were admitted only belatedly: Hélène Bertaux campaigned for their eligibility, and the first woman to win in sculpture was Lucienne Heuvelmans (1911); the first in music was Lili Boulanger (1913).

Abolition and legacy

The Prix de Rome was abolished in 1968 by the minister of culture André Malraux, amid the upheavals of May 1968. The Villa Medici continues to host artists in residence under the French Academy in Rome, without the competitive prize. Similar national prizes — the American Prix de Rome of the American Academy in Rome and Belgium's and the Netherlands' Prix de Rome — were modelled on the French original.
