The Killing Time is a term used in Scottish history for the period of persecution of Presbyterian Covenanters by supporters of the episcopalian government of Charles II and James VII, roughly from 1680 to the Glorious Revolution of 1688. The name derives from the summary executions of Covenanters by government troops and dragoons in the fields and moors of south-west Scotland.

Background

After the restoration of the monarchy in 1660, episcopacy was reimposed on the Church of Scotland, and ministers who refused to accept it were ejected from their parishes. Their followers met in illegal open-air conventicles. Defeats of Covenanter risings at Rullion Green (1666), Drumclog and Bothwell Bridge (1679), and the Sanquhar Declaration of 1680 — in which the radical Cameronians renounced allegiance to the king — brought harsher repression.

Persecution

Under figures such as John Graham of Claverhouse and the Earl of Lauderdale's successors, soldiers were empowered to demand the "abjuration oath" renouncing the Cameronian declarations; refusal could mean death on the spot. Tradition holds that around a hundred people were executed without trial during the worst years, 1684–85. Among the most remembered victims are the "Wigtown Martyrs", two women drowned at the stake in Wigtown Bay in 1685, and the executed leaders James Renwick, hanged in 1688, and Richard Cameron, killed at Airds Moss in 1680.

Legacy

The Killing Time ended with the Revolution Settlement of 1689 and the re-establishment of Presbyterian church government in 1690. Martyrs' monuments across Galloway, Ayrshire and Lanarkshire commemorate the period, and the episodes were popularised in the nineteenth century through collections such as A Cloud of Witnesses and the novels of Walter Scott and Robert Louis Stevenson.
