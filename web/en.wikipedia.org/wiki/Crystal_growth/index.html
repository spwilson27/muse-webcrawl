Crystal growth

Crystal growth is the process by which a pre-existing crystal becomes larger as more molecules or ions are added in an organised way to its structure. It is the second stage of crystallisation, following nucleation: once a stable nucleus (seed) exists, crystal growth proceeds by the attachment of growth units at the crystal surface, increasing the size of the crystal while it retains its ordered lattice. Crystal growth from solution, melt or vapour is central to geology, the chemical and pharmaceutical industries, and the manufacture of semiconductors.

Synthetic quartz crystals are grown hydrothermally for use in oscillators and optics.

Mechanisms

Growth occurs because atoms arriving at a surface are more strongly bound at steps and kinks than on flat terraces. Molecularly smooth faces can grow by the spreading of two-dimensional nuclei across the surface or, as Frank proposed, by a spiral mechanism around the emergent point of a screw dislocation, which provides a permanent step. At high supersaturation, surfaces become rough at the atomic scale and grow continuously. The relative growth rates of different faces determine the final habit of the crystal: the slowest-growing faces dominate the shape that is observed. Impurities adsorbed on particular faces can strongly modify habit — a fact exploited industrially to shape product crystals.

Growth from the melt

Large single crystals for electronics and optics are grown from the melt. In the Czochralski process, a seed crystal is dipped into molten material — most importantly silicon — and slowly withdrawn while rotating, pulling a cylindrical single-crystal ingot (boule) that is later sliced into wafers. In the Bridgman–Stockbarger method, the melt is progressively frozen by moving it through a temperature gradient. Floating-zone refining, flame fusion (the Verneuil process, used for ruby and sapphire) and the Kyropoulos method are other established melt techniques.

Growth from solution and vapour

Crystals grow from solution when the solution is supersaturated, achieved by cooling, evaporation or chemical reaction; hydrothermal growth, carried out in autoclaves at elevated temperature and pressure, produces most synthetic quartz. Gel growth is used for delicate crystals such as those needed for protein crystallography, where X-ray-quality protein crystals are grown by slow diffusion in solutions or gels. Vapour-phase methods — physical vapour transport and chemical vapour deposition — grow thin films and bulk crystals of materials such as silicon carbide and gallium nitride.

Control of growth

Industrial crystallisation aims to control crystal size distribution, purity and shape, because these govern filtration, flow, dissolution and bioavailability of products from fertilisers to pharmaceuticals. Seeding, programmed cooling, control of supersaturation and the use of anti-solvents and tailor-made additives are standard tools; unwanted growth (scaling) on plant surfaces is a persistent engineering problem, and repeated dissolution and regrowth (Ostwald ripening) coarsens suspensions over time.
