Josef von Sternberg (born Jonas Sternberg; 29 May 1894 – 22 December 1969) was an Austrian-born American film director, whose career spanned the silent and early sound eras. Celebrated for his sumptuous visual style — dense compositions of light, shadow, smoke and draped fabric — he is best known for the seven films he made with Marlene Dietrich, whom he discovered and moulded into an international star.

Early life and career

Sternberg was born in Vienna and emigrated with his family to New York as a child. He returned to Vienna for his education, then settled in the United States, working as a film repairer, cutter and assistant director. After service in the U.S. Army Signal Corps during the First World War, he directed his first feature, The Salvation Hunters (1925), which impressed Charlie Chaplin and Douglas Fairbanks. The underworld melodramas Underworld (1927), which won Ben Hecht the first Academy Award for original story, and The Docks of New York (1928) established him as one of the most distinctive stylists of late silent cinema.

The Dietrich films

Invited to Berlin by UFA, Sternberg directed The Blue Angel (1930), the first German sound feature, casting the little-known Dietrich as the cabaret singer Lola Lola opposite Emil Jannings. Its success took both to Paramount in Hollywood, where they made six more films: Morocco (1930), Dishonored (1931), Shanghai Express (1932), Blonde Venus (1932), The Scarlet Empress (1934) and The Devil Is a Woman (1935). The cycle built Dietrich's persona of enigmatic, self-possessed glamour and reached a peak of baroque visual design in The Scarlet Empress.

Later work

After the partnership with Dietrich ended, Sternberg's career faltered. His adaptation of I, Claudius (1937) for Alexander Korda was abandoned after an accident to star Merle Oberon. Later films included The Shanghai Gesture (1941), Macao (1952), and Jet Pilot (completed 1950, released 1957) for Howard Hughes. His last film, Anatahan (1953), made independently in Japan, was a personal favourite and has been reassessed as a major work. He taught film at UCLA and published an autobiography, Fun in a Chinese Laundry (1965).

Style and legacy

Sternberg treated the frame as a painterly surface, subordinating narrative to atmosphere, costume and choreographed light. His films influenced film noir, the work of directors from Max Ophüls onward, and the whole idea of the director as visual author. The Dietrich cycle in particular remains a cornerstone of classical Hollywood style.
