Installation art

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A turbine hall filled with a glowing artificial sun and mist, in the manner of Olafur Eliasson's The Weather Project (2003) at Tate Modern]

Installation art is an artistic genre of three-dimensional works, often site-specific, designed to transform a space and the viewer's experience of it. Emerging as a named practice in the 1970s from environments, happenings and Minimalist 'theatricality', it may fill a room with objects, light, sound, video or scent, and expects the viewer to enter and complete the work.

Major installations include Kurt Schwitters's Merzbau (1923–37, proto-installation), Yayoi Kusama's Infinity Mirror Rooms, James Turrell's Skyspaces, Olafur Eliasson's The Weather Project (2003) at Tate Modern — a vast artificial sun that drew two million visitors — and the site works of land art by Robert Smithson and Walter De Maria.

Practice and institutions

Installations are often temporary, documented in photographs, and commissioned for biennials, kunsthalles and turbine-hall-style museum spaces; their scale and cost made them the signature spectacle of the contemporary museum. Artists from Ilya Kabakov (the 'total installation') to Ai Weiwei (100 million porcelain sunflower seeds at Tate, 2010) used the form for social and political statement.

Criticism

The form has been criticised for spectacle and for its dependence on institutional commissioning, but its core proposition — that art is an environment to inhabit rather than an object to own — is now central to artistic practice and museum architecture alike.
