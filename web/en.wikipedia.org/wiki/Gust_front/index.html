Gust front

A gust front, also known as a storm's outflow boundary, is a boundary separating cool air from a thunderstorm downdraft from the surrounding warm environment. They are similar in nature to cold fronts, but pass over an area much faster — often within minutes — and are followed by an abrupt wind shift, a sudden temperature drop, and a rise in pressure.

Formation

Rain-cooled air within a thunderstorm descends and spreads outward at the surface like a miniature cold pool. The leading edge of this outflow is the gust front, which can travel tens of kilometres ahead of the parent storm and may persist after the storm has dissipated. At the surface it is often marked by a shelf cloud or roll cloud, lines of dust, and a sudden increase in wind speed.

Effects

Gust fronts can trigger the development of new thunderstorms where they collide with other boundaries. They are also hazardous: the strong, shifting winds and associated low-level wind shear endanger aviation, and intense outflow winds (downbursts and microbursts) can cause damage comparable to weak tornadoes. In arid regions, powerful outflows raise dust storms such as the haboob. On weather radar, gust fronts appear as thin lines of weak reflectivity and convergence, sometimes called "fine lines."
