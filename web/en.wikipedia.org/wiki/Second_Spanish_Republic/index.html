The Second Spanish Republic (Spanish: Segunda República Española), officially the Republic of Spain (República Española), was the democratic government of Spain from 1931 to 1939. The Republic was proclaimed on 14 April 1931, after the abdication of King Alfonso XIII in the wake of municipal elections widely interpreted as a plebiscite on the monarchy, and it was dissolved on 1 April 1939 after surrendering in the Spanish Civil War to the Nationalists led by General Francisco Franco.

Proclamation and the Constitution of 1931

After municipal elections on 12 April 1931 returned republican majorities in most major cities, Alfonso XIII left the country and a provisional government under Niceto Alcalá-Zamora proclaimed the Republic two days later. Elections to a Constituent Cortes in June 1931 produced a republican-socialist majority, which drafted the Constitution of 1931. The constitution established Spain as a democratic republic of workers of all classes, proclaimed universal suffrage (including, after a celebrated debate, votes for women), guaranteed civil liberties, separated church and state, and provided for regional autonomy, under which Catalonia received a Statute of Autonomy in 1932.

The Reformist Biennium (1931–1933)

The first government, headed by Manuel Azaña, pursued an ambitious programme of reform: military reform, agrarian reform, educational expansion, labour legislation, and the curtailment of the privileges of the Catholic Church. These reforms antagonised landowners, much of the officer corps, and the Church hierarchy, while the pace of land reform disappointed landless labourers. An attempted military coup by General José Sanjurjo in August 1932 failed. In the elections of November 1933, the right-wing CEDA emerged as the largest party.

The "Black Biennium" and the Asturias Rising (1933–1936)

Governments of the Radical Party, supported by the CEDA, rolled back many reforms. When CEDA ministers entered the cabinet in October 1934, the left responded with a general strike and insurrection, most seriously in Asturias, where miners established a revolutionary commune that was suppressed by troops under Franco's direction with considerable loss of life. A general strike and a rising in Catalonia were also defeated, and thousands were imprisoned.

The Popular Front and the Civil War (1936–1939)

The Popular Front coalition won the elections of February 1936, and Azaña became president. Social polarisation deepened amid strikes, church burnings, and political violence. On 17–18 July 1936, a military rising against the Republic began in Spanish Morocco and spread to the mainland, opening the Spanish Civil War. The Republic retained control of Madrid, Catalonia, and much of the east and south, and organised resistance through a mixture of loyal army units, militias, and later a rebuilt People's Army, aided by the Soviet Union and the International Brigades. The capital of the government moved to Valencia and then Barcelona. Despite initial successes, the Republic steadily lost territory; Barcelona fell in January 1939, and a coup within the Republican zone by Colonel Segismundo Casado in March 1939 failed to secure a negotiated peace. Franco declared victory on 1 April 1939, ending the Republic. Hundreds of thousands of Republicans went into exile, and the Republican government-in-exile persisted abroad for decades.
