Hermes Trismegistus

Hermes Trismegistus (from Greek Ἑρμῆς ὁ Τρισμέγιστος, "Hermes the Thrice-Greatest") is the purported author of the Hermetica, a series of sacred texts foundational to Hermeticism. He is a syncretic combination of the Greek god Hermes and the Egyptian god Thoth, and was regarded in the Hellenistic and Roman worlds as a legendary sage, priest, and king of ancient Egypt.

Origins

In Hellenistic Egypt, the Greek god Hermes, associated with writing, interpretation, and mediation between gods and mortals, was identified with Thoth, the Egyptian god of wisdom and writing. The resulting figure accumulated immense prestige as the inventor of writing, astrology, alchemy, and the occult sciences.

The Hermetica

The texts attributed to Hermes divide into "philosophical" Hermetica — dialogues such as the Corpus Hermeticum and the Asclepius, dealing with the nature of God, the cosmos, and the soul — and "technical" Hermetica concerning astrology, alchemy, and magic. The short alchemical text known as the Emerald Tablet, with its famous maxim "as above, so below," is perhaps the most influential single work attributed to him.

Reception

Renaissance scholars such as Marsilio Ficino, who translated the Corpus Hermeticum into Latin in 1463, believed Hermes to be a contemporary of Moses and a source of ancient theology (prisca theologia). In 1614 the scholar Isaac Casaubon demonstrated on linguistic grounds that the Hermetica were in fact composed in the early centuries AD, undermining their claim to primeval antiquity, though their influence on Western esotericism continued unabated.
