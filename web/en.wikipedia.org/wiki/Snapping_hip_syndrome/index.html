Snapping hip syndrome

Snapping hip syndrome, also known as coxa saltans (or dancer's hip), is a medical condition characterized by a snapping sensation felt when the hip is flexed and extended. This may be accompanied by a snapping or popping noise and pain or discomfort. Pain often decreases with rest and diminished activity.

Types

Snapping hip syndrome is classified by the location and structure involved:

  • External — the iliotibial band, tensor fasciae latae, or gluteus medius tendon slides over the greater trochanter of the femur; the most common type.
  • Internal — the iliopsoas tendon slides over structures at the front of the hip, such as the iliopectineal eminence or the femoral head.
  • Intra-articular — causes within the joint itself, such as labral tears, loose bodies, or cartilage damage.

Causes and demographics

The condition is common among athletes and dancers aged 15 to 40 whose activities require repetitive hip flexion, and it may be related to muscle imbalance, tightness of the involved tendons, or anatomical variation. In external snapping, a thickened posterior iliotibial band snaps over the trochanter as the hip moves.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually clinical: the patient can often reproduce the snap, which the examiner can feel or hear; dynamic ultrasound can visualize the tendon snapping in real time, and imaging may be used to exclude intra-articular causes. Treatment is usually conservative — rest, activity modification, stretching of the involved structures, strengthening, and anti-inflammatory medication. Surgery (such as lengthening the tight tendon) is reserved for persistent, painful cases.
