Edict of Expulsion

The Edict of Expulsion was a royal decree issued by King Edward I of England on 18 July 1290 expelling all Jews from the Kingdom of England. The edict required the entire Jewish population — roughly 2,000 to 3,000 people, with some estimates higher — to leave the country by 1 November 1290 (All Saints' Day), on pain of death. It remained in force for the rest of the Middle Ages, and Jews were not formally readmitted to England until 1656, under Oliver Cromwell.

Background

The expulsion was the culmination of more than a century of increasing persecution. Blood libel accusations had arisen in England from the 1140s, and massacres accompanied the coronation of Richard I in 1189 and the Barons' Wars. In 1275 Edward's Statute of the Jewry forbade usury and tried to force Jews into other trades. Jewish financiers were heavily taxed, and Edward had previously expelled Jews from his duchy of Gascony in 1288. In 1290, in exchange for the expulsion, Parliament granted the king a substantial tax, a connection contemporary chroniclers noted.

Aftermath

The Jews of England departed mainly for France, Flanders and Germany; the Crown seized their remaining property and outstanding debts. England was among the first European kingdoms to expel its entire Jewish population permanently, anticipating the French expulsion of 1306 and the Spanish Alhambra Decree of 1492. Small numbers of converts lived in the Domus Conversorum in London, but no openly Jewish community existed in England for over 360 years, until informal resettlement in the 1650s.
