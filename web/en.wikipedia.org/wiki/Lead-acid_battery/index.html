Lead–acid battery

Specific energy35–40 Wh/kg
Nominal cell voltage2.1 V
InventorGaston Planté (1859)
Common usesAutomotive starting, backup power, traction

The lead–acid battery is a rechargeable battery invented in 1859 by French physicist Gaston Planté. It is the oldest type of rechargeable battery and, despite its low energy-to-weight ratio, remains the most widely used secondary battery chemistry in the world because of its low cost, robustness and ability to deliver high surge currents.

Chemistry

Each cell consists of a negative electrode of spongy lead and a positive electrode of lead dioxide, immersed in an electrolyte of dilute sulfuric acid. On discharge, both electrodes convert to lead sulfate and the acid is consumed, producing water; charging reverses the reaction. A single cell delivers about 2.1 volts, so a car battery of six cells provides a nominal 12 volts.

Applications and types

Lead–acid batteries are used for starter motors in internal-combustion vehicles, uninterruptible power supplies, standby and alarm systems, forklifts and other traction applications, and off-grid energy storage. Designs include flooded (wet) cells, valve-regulated lead–acid (VRLA) batteries — both gel and absorbed glass mat (AGM) types — and deep-cycle variants. Over 99% of lead–acid batteries are recycled in many countries, making the chemistry one of the most successfully recycled products, though lead exposure in informal recycling remains a serious health hazard.
