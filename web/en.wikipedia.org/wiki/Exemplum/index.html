Exemplum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An exemplum (plural exempla) is a moral anecdote — a short story, historical incident, fable or saint's life episode — told to illustrate a point, typically the moral lesson of a sermon. The term derives from the Latin word for a sample or example, and in classical rhetoric the exemplum (Greek paradeigma) was a standard form of argument from precedent: Aristotle and Quintilian treated illustrative examples from history and myth as a means of persuasion.

Medieval use

Exempla flourished in the preaching of the 13th and 14th centuries, especially after the rise of the mendicant orders, whose friars needed engaging material for popular sermons. Preachers mined collections compiled for the purpose, such as the Disciplina Clericalis of Petrus Alphonsi, the sermons of Jacques de Vitry, Stephen of Bourbon's treatise on preaching, the Dialogus Miraculorum of Caesarius of Heisterbach, and the anonymous Gesta Romanorum, whose tales of emperors and merchants were each supplied with a moral allegory. Dominican and Franciscan exempla collections circulated throughout Europe and were translated into the vernaculars.

Content and influence

Exempla range from miracles of the Virgin and punishments of blasphemers, usurers and Sabbath-breakers to animal fables, ghost stories and witty tales of trickery. Though framed as true, many are migratory folktales found across cultures. Scholars value them as evidence of medieval popular belief and storytelling: motifs from the exempla tradition fed into the works of Boccaccio and Chaucer, and the tale collections stand behind much later European short fiction. The exemplum declined with humanist preaching fashions and the Reformation's suspicion of unauthorised legend, though the device — an illustrative anecdote carrying a moral — remains a staple of sermons, speeches and moral philosophy, where "thought experiments" such as the trolley problem serve a recognisably similar function.
