Henry Ward Beecher (June 24, 1813 – March 8, 1887) was an American Congregationalist clergyman, social reformer, and speaker, known for his support of the abolition of slavery, his emphasis on God's love, and his 1875 adultery trial. He was one of the most famous preachers of the nineteenth century; his speaking fees and packed church made him a celebrity of the lecture circuit, and President Abraham Lincoln was among those who came to hear him.

Henry Ward Beecher was the son of Lyman Beecher, a Calvinist minister who became one of the best-known evangelists of his age, and brother of Harriet Beecher Stowe, author of Uncle Tom's Cabin, and of the educator Catharine Beecher. He graduated from Amherst College in 1834 and studied theology at Lane Seminary in Cincinnati under his father.

Ministry

After pastorates in Indiana, Beecher became in 1847 the first minister of the new Plymouth Church in Brooklyn, New York, where he remained for forty years. His sermons — emotional, anecdotal, and theologically liberal — drew thousands each Sunday and were reported nationwide in his journal The Independent and later The Christian Union. Against the sterner Calvinism of his upbringing, he preached a "gospel of love", emphasising God's benevolence and Jesus's compassion rather than fire and damnation. He supported women's suffrage, temperance, and Darwin's theory of evolution, which he held compatible with Christianity.

Abolitionism and the Civil War

Beecher was a fiery abolitionist. From his pulpit he staged mock slave auctions to buy the freedom of enslaved people, including the celebrated case of the Edmonson sisters. During the Kansas conflicts of the 1850s, he raised money to send Sharps rifles to anti-slavery settlers; newspapers dubbed the crates "Beecher's Bibles". During the Civil War, Plymouth Church raised and equipped a regiment, and in 1863 Beecher toured England, speaking to huge and often hostile crowds to argue the Union cause and deter British recognition of the Confederacy. At the war's end he was chosen to speak at the ceremony raising the American flag again over Fort Sumter.

The Beecher–Tilton scandal

In 1872, the free-love advocate Victoria Woodhull published allegations that Beecher had committed adultery with Elizabeth Tilton, a parishioner and the wife of his friend, the editor Theodore Tilton. The ensuing scandal consumed the press; Plymouth Church exonerated its pastor, and Tilton's civil suit against Beecher in 1875 — one of the most widely reported American trials of the century — ended with a hung jury. Beecher retained his pulpit and much of his popularity, though the episode permanently clouded his reputation.

Beecher died of a cerebral hemorrhage in Brooklyn on 8 March 1887. His funeral drew enormous crowds, and tributes came from across the nation he had helped to shape.
