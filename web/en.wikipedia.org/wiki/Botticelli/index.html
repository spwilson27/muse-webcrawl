Alessandro di Mariano di Vanni Filipepi (c. 1445 – 17 May 1510), known as Sandro Botticelli, was an Italian painter of the Early Renaissance. Working in Florence under Medici patronage, he is celebrated for the graceful linearity of works such as Primavera (c. 1482) and The Birth of Venus (c. 1484–86) — the first full-length female nude in Western painting since antiquity, its Venus modelled on classical types descending from Praxiteles (see Venus de Milo).

Career

Botticelli was apprenticed to Fra Filippo Lippi and influenced by the sculptural clarity of Verrocchio's workshop. In 1481–82 he was summoned to Rome to paint frescoes in the Sistine Chapel. His mythological paintings for the Medici circle, rich in Neoplatonic allegory, include the dancing Graces of the Primavera. In the 1490s Florence fell under the sway of the preacher Savonarola; Botticelli's late work — the Mystic Nativity (1500) among it — turns austere and visionary, and Vasari reports (probably exaggeratedly) that he burned some of his own paintings in the Bonfire of the Vanities.

Reputation

After his death Botticelli's fame faded for three centuries, eclipsed by High Renaissance masters. He was rediscovered by the Pre-Raphaelites and 19th-century critics such as John Ruskin and Walter Pater; today the Uffizi holds the world's largest collection of his works, and his Venus is among the most recognisable images in Western art. Late in life he also produced a celebrated series of drawings illustrating Dante's Divine Comedy.
