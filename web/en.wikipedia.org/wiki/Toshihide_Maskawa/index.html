Toshihide Maskawa

Toshihide Maskawa

Died23 July 2021 (aged 81), Kyoto, Japan
Known forCKM matrix; CP violation with Makoto Kobayashi
AwardsNobel Prize in Physics (2008)
[Image: Photograph of Toshihide Maskawa speaking at a lectern]
Maskawa in 2008.

Toshihide Maskawa (7 February 1940 – 23 July 2021) was a Japanese theoretical physicist known for the Kobayashi–Maskawa theory of CP violation (1973), written with his student Makoto Kobayashi while both were at Nagoya and Kyoto. By adding a third generation of quarks to the mixing matrix, they provided the Standard Model's explanation of the symmetry violation discovered in kaon decays in 1964 — and predicted six quarks in total when only three were known.

Maskawa shared the 2008 Nobel Prize in Physics (with Kobayashi and Yoichiro Nambu). Remembered for his dry wit — he remarked that he first heard of his Nobel from the television news and, famously, did not drive or hold a passport — he directed the Yukawa Institute for Theoretical Physics at Kyoto and championed fundamental theory in Japan for five decades.

The 1973 paper

Maskawa and Kobayashi asked the minimal question: what does it take for the weak interaction to violate CP within a renormalisable theory? With two quark generations the mixing matrix can be made real — no violation — so they added a third, finding a natural irreducible phase. The bet was spectacularly right: charm (1974) completed the second generation as Kobayashi–Maskawa was being absorbed, bottom (1977) and top (1995) supplied the third, and B-factory experiments measured the predicted asymmetries precisely.
