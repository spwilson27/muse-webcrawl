Capability approach

Amartya Sen, originator of the capability approach. (Image placeholder)

The capability approach (also referred to as the capabilities approach) is a normative approach to human welfare that concentrates on the actual capability of persons to achieve lives they value, rather than solely on income, resources or subjective happiness. It was conceived in the 1980s by the Indian economist and philosopher Amartya Sen as an alternative approach to welfare economics, and was subsequently developed further by the philosopher Martha Nussbaum and others. It has become influential in development studies and underlies the United Nations' Human Development Index (HDI).

Core concepts

The approach rests on two central notions:

  • Functionings — the various things a person may value doing or being, such as being well nourished, being literate, appearing in public without shame, or taking part in the life of the community.
  • Capabilities — the real freedoms or opportunities a person has to achieve those functionings. A capability set represents the combinations of functionings a person can feasibly attain.

The approach stresses conversion factors: personal, social and environmental circumstances that determine how far a given bundle of resources can be converted into actual functionings. Two people with identical incomes may have very different capabilities if one has a disability, lives under discriminatory norms, or faces a harsher climate.

Sen and Nussbaum

Sen deliberately declined to specify a single canonical list of capabilities, arguing that the weights and content of valuable capabilities should be settled through public reasoning and democratic deliberation. Nussbaum, by contrast, proposed a list of ten central human capabilities — including life, bodily health, bodily integrity, practical reason, affiliation and control over one's environment — as a threshold for social justice and constitutional guarantees.

Applications

The capability approach has been operationalised in the UNDP's Human Development Reports and the HDI, which combines measures of health (life expectancy), education and income. It has also influenced work on poverty measurement, gender and development, disability, education policy and the multidimensional poverty indices used by many governments.

Criticism

Critics have questioned how the approach can be measured in practice, whether it is too individualistic, and how trade-offs between capabilities should be resolved. Supporters respond that these open questions are deliberate: the framework is intentionally incomplete, leaving space for democratic choice rather than technocratic prescription.
