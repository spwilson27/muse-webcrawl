Gripping beast

The gripping beast is a motif in Viking art, first appearing in the early 9th century, in which an animal's paws grip the borders of the object it decorates, its own body parts, or another beast. It is the defining feature of the Gripping Beast style, the earliest of the named Viking Age art styles, exemplified by objects from the Oseberg ship burial.

Description

The beast is typically rendered as a chunky, ribbon-like creature with a rounded or triangular head, spiral hips, and paws that clasp whatever lies beside them. In the Oseberg material the motif appears in profusion on carved wooden objects — sledges, chair, and bedposts — where ranks of small beasts grasp the frames around them.

Chronology

Art historians, following the sequencing of David Wilson and others, place the Gripping Beast style before the Borre style (into which it developed in the later 9th century). The gripping-beast device persisted, in more stylized forms, through the Borre, Jelling, and Mammen styles. Some scholars connect the motif to much older Germanic animal ornament of the Migration Period, while others see it as a fresh creation of the early Viking Age.
