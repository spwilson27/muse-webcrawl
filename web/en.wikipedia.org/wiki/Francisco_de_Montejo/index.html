Francisco de Montejo (c. 1479 – c. 1553) was a Spanish conquistador best known for leading the protracted conquest of the Yucatán Peninsula and its Maya peoples. The campaign, carried out with his son and nephew — both also named Francisco de Montejo — lasted from 1527 to 1546 and was among the most difficult of the Spanish conquests in the Americas.

A conquistador of Montejo's generation, in the steel morion and cuirass typical of the period.

Early career

Montejo sailed to the Indies in 1514 and took part in the expeditions of Juan de Grijalva (1518) and Hernán Cortés (1519) along the Mexican coast. He was one of the envoys Cortés sent to Spain to report on the conquest of the Aztec Empire. In 1526 Charles V granted him the title of adelantado and captain general of Yucatán, with licence to conquer and colonise the peninsula at his own expense.

The conquest of Yucatán

Montejo's first expedition (1527–1528) landed on the island of Cozumel and the east coast of the peninsula but was driven back by Maya resistance, disease and lack of supplies. A second campaign (1531–1535), based at Campeche and Champotón, also failed, and the Spanish were temporarily expelled altogether. The conquest was finally achieved in a third campaign led largely by his son, Francisco de Montejo "el Mozo" (the Younger), who founded Mérida in 1542 on the site of the Maya city of Tihó (Ichcaanzihó). The last major Maya resistance was broken in 1546, though the Itza of Petén remained independent until 1697.

Later life

Montejo also served as governor of Honduras and Chiapas at various points, quarrelling with rival conquistadors and royal officials. Accused of misgovernment, he was stripped of his governorship of Yucatán in 1550 and returned to Spain, where he died a few years later, still petitioning the Crown over his claims.
