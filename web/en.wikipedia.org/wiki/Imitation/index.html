Imitation

Imitation is a fundamental mechanism of human learning

Imitation (from Latin imitatio, "a copying, imitation") is a behavior whereby an individual observes and replicates another's behavior. Imitation is also a form of learning that leads to the "development of traditions, and ultimately our culture. It allows for the transfer of information (behaviours, customs, etc.) between individuals and down generations without the need for genetic inheritance."

The word imitation can be applied in many contexts, ranging from animal training to politics. The term generally relates to conscious behavior; subconscious imitation is termed mirroring.

Imitation in animals

Imitation in animals is a study within ethology and cognitive psychology of the ability of an animal to copy behaviors or sounds of others, either of the same or different species. True imitation, as distinguished from emulation and mimicry, requires that the observer copy the specific motor patterns of the model. Young animals of many species, particularly birds and primates, learn important survival skills through imitation of adults.

Child development

The psychologist Jean Piaget noted that children in the sensorimotor stage (the first two years of life) are incapable of deferred imitation but gradually develop the ability. Developmental psychologist Andrew Meltzoff proposed that infants as young as 42 minutes old can imitate facial movements. Recent studies, however, have questioned some of these findings, suggesting that neonatal imitation may be more limited than originally claimed.

Mimesis and art

In aesthetics, imitation (mimesis) is the classical theory that art imitates nature or life. Plato treated imitation with suspicion, while Aristotle in the Poetics regarded imitation as natural to human beings and a source of learning and pleasure. The theory of mimesis shaped Western art criticism for centuries.
