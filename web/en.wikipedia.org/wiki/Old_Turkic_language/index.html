Old Turkic (also called Old Uyghur in its later stage, and Göktürk in Turkish scholarship) is the earliest attested form of a Turkic language, documented from the 7th to the 13th century in the Orkhon inscriptions of the Göktürk and Uyghur khaganates in Mongolia and in manuscripts from the Turfan oasis in Xinjiang. It is the oldest Turkic language preserved in written form and a key source for the comparative study of the Turkic family.

Stages and sources

Scholars divide Old Turkic into the Orkhon (or runiform) stage, represented by the great memorial stelae erected for the princes Kül Tigin (732) and Bilge Khagan (735) and their counsellor Tonyukuk — deciphered in 1893 by the Danish linguist Vilhelm Thomsen — and the later Old Uyghur stage, written after the Uyghur Khaganate's fall (840) in the oasis states of Turfan and Gansu. Old Uyghur texts, many of them Buddhist, Manichaean, or Nestorian Christian translations, were written in several scripts, including the cursive Uyghur alphabet derived from Sogdian, which later gave rise to the Mongolian script.

Features

Old Turkic was agglutinative with vowel harmony, no grammatical gender, and a fairly free word order favouring verb-final clauses. It is distinguished from the later Karluk and Oghuz languages by features such as the retention of long vowels and of distinctions later lost (e.g. adak "foot", later ayak in most languages). Because Old Uyghur manuscripts are abundant and precisely localized, Old Turkic occupies a central place in Turkology; the decipherment of the Orkhon inscriptions is considered one of the triumphs of 19th-century philology.
