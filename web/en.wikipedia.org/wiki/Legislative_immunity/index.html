Legislative immunity

Legislative immunity, also called parliamentary immunity or parliamentary privilege in some systems, is a legal protection granted to members of a legislature that shields them from civil or criminal liability for acts performed in the course of their legislative duties. Its central purpose is to preserve the independence of the legislature from pressure by the executive, the judiciary, or private litigants.

Forms

Two broad forms are commonly distinguished:

  • Non-liability (absolute privilege): members cannot be sued or prosecuted for anything said or any vote cast in the legislature. In the United States this is provided by the Speech or Debate Clause; in the United Kingdom by Article 9 of the Bill of Rights 1689.
  • Inviolability: members cannot be arrested or prosecuted for acts outside the legislature without the permission of the house, a protection common in continental European constitutions such as those of France and Germany.

Rationale and criticism

Immunity protects free debate and prevents the harassment of legislators through the courts. Critics argue that broad immunity can shield members from legitimate accountability for corruption, and several countries have narrowed its scope or created procedures for lifting it in serious cases.

Lifting immunity

Many constitutions allow the legislature itself to waive a member's inviolability, typically by vote of the chamber. Requests are commonly considered by a privileges or rules committee, which recommends whether the prosecution appears politically motivated.
