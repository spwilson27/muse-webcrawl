Kalinago

The Kalinago, also known historically as the Island Caribs, are an Indigenous people of the Lesser Antilles in the Caribbean. They descend in large part from Arawak-speaking peoples who migrated from the Orinoco region of South America and intermingled with earlier inhabitants. Today the largest Kalinago community is the Kalinago Territory on the island of Dominica, home to around 3,000 people.

History

Early European accounts contrasted the "peaceful" Arawaks of the Greater Antilles with the supposedly warlike "Caribs" of the Windward Islands — a distinction now regarded as heavily exaggerated, since Kalinago culture was substantially Arawakan in language and material culture. Kalinago men spoke a Carib-influenced pidgin vocabulary alongside the Arawakan language spoken by women, a famous case of gendered speech registers recorded by 17th-century missionaries.

Kalinago resistance slowed European colonisation of the Windward Islands for two centuries. On Saint Vincent, Kalinago intermarried with shipwrecked and escaped Africans, giving rise to the Garifuna ("Black Caribs"), most of whom the British deported in 1797 to Roatán, seeding today's Garifuna communities of Central America. In 1903 the British administration in Dominica formally set aside the Kalinago Territory (historically the "Carib Reserve"), about 15 km² on the east coast.

Culture

The Kalinago are renowned for woven baskets of larouma reed, dugout canoes (gommier), and cassava processing. The Territory is governed by an elected Kalinago Chief and Council. The Kalinago Barana Auté cultural village presents traditional life to visitors, and language revival efforts draw on the Island Carib language, extinct as a community vernacular since the early 20th century but related to the still-spoken Garifuna language.
