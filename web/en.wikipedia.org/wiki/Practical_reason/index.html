Practical reason

Practical reason or practical rationality is the use of reason to decide how to act. It contrasts with theoretical reason, which aims at knowledge of what is the case; practical reason concerns what ought to be done, deliberating about ends and the means to achieve them.

In classical philosophy

Aristotle treated phronesis (practical wisdom) as the intellectual virtue by which agents deliberate well about what conduces to living well as a whole. His practical syllogism modelled action as arising from a general desire or principle together with a perception of the particular situation.

Hume and Kant

David Hume argued that reason alone does not motivate action — it is "the slave of the passions", informing us about means and facts while ends are set by desire. Immanuel Kant opposed this view: in the Groundwork and the Critique of Practical Reason (1788), he argued that pure reason can itself be practical, giving the moral law through the categorical imperative, which commands agents to act only on maxims they can consistently will as universal law.

Contemporary debates

Modern philosophy debates whether practical reasoning is purely instrumental (selecting means to given ends, the "Humean" model) or can also assess ends themselves, the nature of reasons for action, and the relation between practical rationality, morality and prudence.
