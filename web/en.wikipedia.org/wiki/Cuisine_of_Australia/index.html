Australian cuisine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Australian cuisine is the food and cooking practices of Australia and its inhabitants. It has been shaped by the country's Indigenous peoples, who developed a hunter-gatherer diet of native plants and animals ("bush tucker") over tens of thousands of years, and by successive waves of immigration beginning with British and Irish settlement in 1788 and continuing with European and Asian migration after the Second World War.

Indigenous and colonial foundations

Aboriginal Australians cooked kangaroo, emu, witchetty grubs, quandong, wattleseed and hundreds of other native species. British settlers brought a diet centred on meat, bread and tea; mutton and later beef became staples, and dishes such as the meat pie, the roast dinner and damper (soda bread cooked in campfire ashes) became emblematic. Vegemite, a yeast-extract spread invented in 1922, is an enduring national icon, as is the barbecue.

Modern Australian cuisine

Post-war migration from Italy, Greece, Lebanon, Vietnam, China and many other countries transformed Australian eating, bringing espresso coffee culture, pasta, souvlaki, phở and laksa into the mainstream. From the 1990s chefs promoted "Modern Australian" (or "Mod Oz") cooking — a fusion style drawing on Asian, Mediterranean and native ingredients such as lemon myrtle, finger lime and kangaroo. Melbourne and Sydney developed strong café cultures; the flat white coffee is claimed by both Australia and New Zealand. Iconic sweets include the lamington, the pavlova (also disputed with New Zealand), Anzac biscuits and Tim Tams.
