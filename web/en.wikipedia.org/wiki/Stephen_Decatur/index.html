Stephen Decatur Jr. (January 5, 1779 – March 22, 1820) was an American naval officer and commodore. He was born on the eastern shore of Maryland in the village of Sinepuxent. His father, Stephen Decatur Sr., was a commodore in the Continental Navy. Decatur became the youngest officer to reach the rank of captain in the history of the United States Navy, and he won fame in the Barbary Wars and the War of 1812.

Early career and the Philadelphia raid

Decatur entered the Navy as a midshipman in 1798 and served in the Quasi-War with France. During the First Barbary War, the frigate USS Philadelphia ran aground and was captured at Tripoli in 1803. On the night of February 16, 1804, Decatur led a party of volunteers aboard the captured ship in the harbor, overwhelmed its Tripolitan crew, and burned the frigate to prevent its use by the enemy. Admiral Horatio Nelson is said to have called it "the most bold and daring act of the age."

War of 1812

Commanding USS United States, Decatur captured the British frigate HMS Macedonian on October 25, 1812, in one of the most celebrated single-ship victories of the war; Macedonian was taken into the U.S. Navy. Later blockaded at New London and then, commanding USS President, he was captured by a British squadron in January 1815 after a fight in which President defeated HMS Endymion but was overwhelmed by following ships.

Second Barbary War

In 1815 Decatur commanded a squadron to the Mediterranean and, in the Second Barbary War, captured the Algerine flagship Mashuda and compelled the Dey of Algiers to sign a treaty ending American tribute payments. He then obtained similar agreements from Tunis and Tripoli. On returning home he uttered his famous toast: "Our country! In her intercourse with foreign nations may she always be in the right; but our country, right or wrong."

Death

Decatur served afterward on the Board of Navy Commissioners and became a prominent figure in Washington society; his house on Lafayette Square, designed by Benjamin Latrobe, was the first private residence on the square. In 1820 he was mortally wounded in a duel with Commodore James Barron, whom he had criticized over the Chesapeake–Leopard affair, and died on March 22, 1820. Numerous towns, counties, schools, and five U.S. Navy ships have been named for him.
