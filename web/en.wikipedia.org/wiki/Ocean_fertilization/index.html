Ocean fertilization

Ocean fertilization or ocean nourishment is a proposed form of climate engineering in which nutrients — most often iron — are added to the upper ocean to stimulate phytoplankton growth, with the aim of increasing the biological uptake of carbon dioxide and its export to the deep ocean. It is the best-known ocean-based method of carbon dioxide removal, and one of the most controversial.

Rationale

Vast regions of the ocean (the high-nutrient, low-chlorophyll zones of the Southern Ocean, equatorial Pacific, and subarctic North Pacific) contain abundant nitrogen and phosphorus but little plankton growth. Oceanographer John Martin showed in the 1980s that the missing ingredient is dissolved iron, and quipped: "Give me a half tanker of iron, and I will give you an ice age" — reflecting the hypothesis that dust-borne iron fertilized glacial oceans and drew down atmospheric CO2. Because photosynthesizing plankton sink when they die, fertilizing could in principle lock carbon away in deep water and sediments for centuries.

Experiments and findings

More than a dozen open-ocean iron addition experiments (IronEx, SOIREE, EIFEX, LOHAFEX and others) between 1993 and 2009 consistently produced phytoplankton blooms visible from satellites. However, the critical question — how much carbon is exported to depth — gave mixed results: some experiments (EIFEX, 2004) found substantial sinking export, while others found little, owing to grazing by zooplankton and differing plankton communities. In 2012 a controversial rogue experiment off British Columbia, conducted by the Haida Salmon Restoration Corporation with businessman Russ George, dumped about 100 tonnes of iron sulfate in the Pacific, attracting international criticism and investigation.

Governance and risks

Concerns include unintended ecosystem changes, harmful algal blooms, oxygen depletion in mid-waters, production of other greenhouse gases such as nitrous oxide, and disputes over who may claim carbon credits. The Convention on Biological Diversity called in 2008 for a moratorium on large-scale fertilization except for small coastal research studies, and the London Convention/Protocol adopted a 2013 amendment to regulate marine geoengineering. Scientific assessments generally regard ocean fertilization as inefficient at climatically relevant scale relative to its risks, though research continues.
