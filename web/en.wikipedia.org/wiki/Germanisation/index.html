Germanisation

Germanisation (German: Germanisierung) is the spread of the German language, people and culture beyond Germany, both by communities relocating and by the assimilation of non-German populations into German identity. The term is used in several historical contexts, ranging from the medieval eastward settlement movement to the coercive nationality policies of the Prussian, Austrian and Nazi states.

Medieval period

From the twelfth century the Ostsiedlung ("east settling") drew German-speaking peasants, merchants and townspeople into Slavic- and Baltic-speaking lands east of the Elbe, including Mecklenburg, Pomerania, Silesia, Bohemia and the territory of the Teutonic Order in Prussia. German town law (such as Magdeburg law) and German speech spread widely; many Slavic populations were gradually assimilated, although groups such as the Sorbs of Lusatia retained their language.

Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

In the German Empire, governments pursued Germanisation of the Polish-speaking population of the eastern provinces through measures such as German-only schooling, restrictions on Polish in public life, and the Prussian Settlement Commission of 1886, which bought land to settle Germans in Posen and West Prussia. Polish resistance — including the famous school strikes of Września and the self-help of cooperative banks and land societies — limited these policies' success. In Austria-Hungary, Germanisation was more a matter of the prestige of German in administration and education than of systematic state coercion, but it fuelled the empire's nationality conflicts.

Nazi period

Under Nazi Germany, Germanisation took a radical and criminal form in occupied Eastern Europe. The Generalplan Ost envisaged the expulsion, enslavement or murder of tens of millions of Slavs and the settlement of German colonists in their place. The Deutsche Volksliste classified inhabitants of annexed Polish territories by supposed German ancestry; children deemed racially suitable were kidnapped for Germanisation in German families, and ethnic Germans from across Europe were resettled "home into the Reich" (Heim ins Reich). These policies formed part of the crimes prosecuted after the war, and they were reversed by the postwar expulsion of Germans from Eastern Europe.
