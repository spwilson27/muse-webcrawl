Southern right whale

The southern right whale (Eubalaena australis) is a baleen whale, one of three species classified as right whales belonging to the genus Eubalaena. Southern right whales inhabit oceans south of the Equator, between about 20° and 60° south latitude. In 1998, a global population estimate put the species at about 7,500 individuals; populations have since been recovering from whaling and are now estimated in the low tens of thousands.

Description

Southern right whales are rotund, mostly black whales lacking a dorsal fin, growing to about 15–18 metres in length and weighing up to 80 tonnes or more. They are distinguished by rough patches of skin called callosities on the head, which are infested with pale cyamid crustaceans and form patterns unique to each individual, enabling researchers to identify whales photographically. They feed mainly on copepods and krill by skim-feeding with long, fine baleen.

Behaviour and distribution

In winter, the whales gather in sheltered coastal calving grounds off southern Australia, New Zealand (especially the Auckland Islands), Argentina's Valdés Peninsula, South Africa, Brazil, and Chile. They are famously active at the surface, breaching, tail-slapping, and "sailing" with their flukes held above the water. Mothers and calves stay close to shore, making the species a mainstay of land-based whale watching in places such as Hermanus, South Africa.

Whaling and recovery

Right whales were named the "right" whales to hunt: slow, approachable, rich in oil and baleen, and buoyant when dead. Southern right whales were hunted intensively from the late eighteenth century through the early twentieth, reducing them to perhaps a few hundred animals. Protected internationally since 1935 (though illegal Soviet whaling continued into the 1960s), most populations are now growing at roughly 7% per year, though the small Chile–Peru population remains critically endangered. Modern threats include ship strikes, entanglement in fishing gear, and episodic calf die-offs at Valdés Peninsula.
