Warring States period

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Bronze crossbow trigger mechanism and iron swords of the Warring States era]

The Warring States period (475–221 BC) was the final era of China's Zhou dynasty, when the nominal Zhou kings reigned powerlessly while seven major states — Qin, Chu, Qi, Yan, Han, Zhao and Wei — fought for supremacy, until Qin conquered all and unified China in 221 BC. Named for the Stratagems of the Warring States, it was an age of total war (mass conscript infantry armies, iron weapons, crossbows, cavalry), bureaucratic and military innovation, and dazzling intellectual competition among the Hundred Schools of Thought — Legalists, Confucians, Daoists, Mohists, logicians and strategists (Sun Tzu's Art of War tradition belongs to this milieu).

The seven powers

By 475 BC the Spring and Autumn patchwork had consolidated into seven great states plus minor survivors. Wars grew from aristocratic chariot duels into campaigns of hundreds of thousands: Zhao lost perhaps 400,000 men buried alive after Changping (260 BC, per Sima Qian — a figure surely inflated but indicative of scale). States responded with reforms — centralised counties, written law codes, taxation in grain and coin, meritocratic promotion: Shang Yang's transformation of Qin (from 356 BC) was the most radical and, in the end, decisive.

Unification

Qin's advantages compounded: the Sichuan basin's annexation (316 BC), irrigation works (Dujiangyan, the Zhengguo Canal), defensible geography, and a ruthless succession of able kings and ministers (Fan Ju, then Li Si). Between 230 and 221 BC Qin extinguished its rivals in sequence; in 221 BC King Zheng proclaimed himself Qin Shi Huang, First Emperor. The Warring States' legacy — the unified bureaucratic empire, Legalist statecraft tempered by Confucian ideology — structured China for two millennia.
