Salsa (food)

Salsa (Spanish for "sauce") is a range of sauces used in Mexican cuisine as condiments, typically tomato-based, spicy, and served with tortilla chips or as an accompaniment to tacos and other dishes. In the United States salsa surpassed ketchup in dollar sales in the early 1990s.

Types

Salsa roja (red) is made from cooked or raw tomatoes, chiles, onion, garlic and cilantro; pico de gallo is a fresh, chunky uncooked version. Salsa verde uses tomatillos and green chiles. Other types include salsa negra, salsa macha (oil and dried chiles), guacamole, and fruit salsas with mango or pineapple. Heat comes from jalapeño, serrano, habanero or chipotle peppers.

History

Combinations of tomato, chile and ground seeds date to Aztec, Maya and Inca cuisines; Bernal Díaz del Castillo described tomato-chile sauces sold in Tenochtitlan's markets. The word salsa entered English in the 18th–19th centuries via Spanish. Commercial production in the US began with companies in Texas and Louisiana; Pace Picante (1947) popularised jarred salsa nationally.

Use

Salsa is eaten as a dip, a table condiment, and a cooking ingredient in dishes such as huevos rancheros and chilaquiles. Fresh salsas are perishable, while cooked jarred salsas are shelf-stable until opened.
