Tension headache

SpecialtyNeurology
SymptomsMild-to-moderate, non-pulsating pain on both sides of the head; pressing or tightening quality
Duration30 minutes to 7 days per episode
TypesInfrequent episodic, frequent episodic, chronic

A tension headache, or tension-type headache (TTH), is the most common type of primary headache. The pain is typically described as a constant, dull pressure or tightness — like a band around the head — affecting both sides, of mild to moderate intensity, and not aggravated by routine physical activity. Unlike migraine, it is usually not accompanied by nausea, vomiting, or sensitivity severe enough to be disabling, though mild sensitivity to light or sound may occur.

Classification

The International Classification of Headache Disorders divides tension-type headache by frequency: infrequent episodic (fewer than 12 headache days per year), frequent episodic (1–14 days per month), and chronic (15 or more days per month for more than three months). Lifetime prevalence in the general population has been estimated at between about 30 and 78 percent in different studies; chronic tension-type headache affects roughly 2–3 percent of people and is considerably more disabling.

Causes and mechanisms

The name reflects an older belief that the pain came from sustained contraction of the muscles of the neck and scalp. Current understanding implicates increased tenderness of the pericranial muscles and fascia together with altered central pain processing: in chronic cases, the central nervous system becomes sensitised, so that normally innocuous stimuli are perceived as painful. Stress, poor posture, fatigue, and lack of sleep are common triggers. Nitric oxide has been proposed as a mediator in the development of chronic tension-type headache.

Treatment

Episodic attacks usually respond to simple analgesics such as paracetamol (acetaminophen), ibuprofen, or aspirin, though frequent use risks medication-overuse headache. For chronic tension-type headache, the tricyclic antidepressant amitriptyline is the best-established preventive treatment; other options include mirtazapine and venlafaxine. Non-drug measures with evidence of benefit include relaxation training, biofeedback, cognitive behavioural therapy, physical therapy, and acupuncture.
