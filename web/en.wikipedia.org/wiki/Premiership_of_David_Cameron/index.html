Premiership of David Cameron

David Cameron's tenure as Prime Minister of the United Kingdom began on 11 May 2010, when he accepted Queen Elizabeth II's invitation to form a government following the resignation of Gordon Brown after the inconclusive 2010 general election, and ended on 13 July 2016, when he resigned following the referendum vote to leave the European Union.

Coalition government (2010–2015)

Cameron formed the first peacetime coalition government since the 1930s with Nick Clegg's Liberal Democrats. Dominated by the aftermath of the financial crisis, Chancellor George Osborne's programme of spending restraint — the "austerity" agenda — sought to eliminate the budget deficit. The government raised university tuition fees, restructured the National Health Service in England, introduced the "bedroom tax" and Universal Credit welfare reforms, held a referendum on the voting system (2011, which retained first-past-the-post), and legislated for same-sex marriage in 2013. In foreign affairs it intervened in the Libyan civil war (2011), and in 2014 the Scottish independence referendum returned a vote to remain in the United Kingdom.

Majority government (2015–2016)

Against most predictions, the Conservatives won an outright majority at the 2015 general election. Cameron's second term was consumed by the renegotiation of Britain's EU membership and the promised in/out referendum. On 23 June 2016 voters chose Leave by 52 to 48 percent; Cameron announced his resignation the next morning, and Theresa May succeeded him on 13 July 2016.

Legacy

Assessments of Cameron's premiership commonly credit the coalition with restoring fiscal credibility and achieving deficit reduction, while criticising austerity's social effects; his decision to call the EU referendum, and the result, dominate retrospective judgements.
