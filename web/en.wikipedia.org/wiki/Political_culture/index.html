Political culture

Political culture is the set of attitudes, beliefs, and sentiments that give order and meaning to a political process and that provide the underlying assumptions and rules that govern behavior in the political system. It encompasses both the political ideals and the operating norms of a polity. Political culture is thus the manifestation in aggregate form of the psychological and subjective dimensions of politics.

Definition

The term was introduced by the political scientist Gabriel Almond, who distinguished political culture from political structure. In the classic 1963 study The Civic Culture, Almond and Sidney Verba compared the political cultures of the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, Italy, and Mexico, and identified three general types:

  • Parochial political culture — citizens have little awareness of or orientation toward the political system, and expectations of government are minimal.
  • Subject political culture — citizens are oriented toward the outputs of the system but remain largely passive participants.
  • Participant political culture — citizens are actively engaged in both input and output sides of politics, with a strong sense of political efficacy.

Almond and Verba argued that a stable democracy was best supported by a mixed "civic culture" that combined participant orientations with lingering parochial and subject elements, balancing citizen activity with trust in authority.

Components

Political culture typically includes orientations toward:

  • The political system as a whole (system affect)
  • The input processes, such as elections and interest articulation
  • The output processes, such as public policy and administration
  • The self as an active participant in politics

These orientations involve knowledge of politics, feelings about political actors and institutions, and evaluations of performance. Political science researchers measure them through surveys of public opinion, values, and norms.

Criticism and development

Critics have objected that the concept can be tautological — inferring culture from behavior and then using it to explain that same behavior — and that early formulations were too national in scope, ignoring subcultures divided by class, religion, region, and ethnicity. Later scholars such as Ronald Inglehart linked political culture to broader value change, arguing that rising prosperity produces a shift from materialist to post-materialist values, including greater support for participation, self-expression, and tolerance. Studies of political geography and subnational political cultures, such as Daniel Elazar's classification of American states, emphasize that a single nation may contain several distinct political cultures.
