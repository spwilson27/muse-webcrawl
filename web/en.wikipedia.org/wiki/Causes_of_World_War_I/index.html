The causes of World War I included a complex web of long-term factors — the alliance systems of the European great powers, imperial and colonial competition, militarism and arms races, and nationalist movements — together with the short-term trigger of the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary by the Bosnian Serb nationalist Gavrilo Princip on 28 June 1914. Historians continue to debate the relative weight of these factors and the question of responsibility, which was politically charged by the "war guilt" clause of the Treaty of Versailles.

The July Crisis

Following the assassination in Sarajevo, Austria-Hungary, backed by a German promise of support known as the "blank cheque", issued Serbia an ultimatum designed to be rejected. When Serbia accepted most but not all demands, Austria-Hungary declared war on 28 July 1914. Russia mobilised in Serbia's support; Germany, following the logic of the Schlieffen Plan, declared war on Russia (1 August) and France (3 August) and invaded Belgium, prompting Britain to declare war on Germany on 4 August 1914.

Long-term factors

Alliances

By 1914 Europe was divided into two armed camps: the Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy, and the Triple Entente of France, Russia and Britain. Although the alliances were defensive in form, they created expectations of support that emboldened governments during crises and risked dragging all the powers into a local conflict.

Imperialism and colonial rivalry

Competition for colonies and influence in Africa, Asia and the Ottoman Empire generated repeated crises — notably the Moroccan crises of 1905 and 1911 between France and Germany — and deepened mutual suspicion among the powers.

Militarism and the arms race

The Anglo-German naval race, symbolised by the launch of HMS Dreadnought in 1906, and the growth of mass conscript armies with detailed mobilisation timetables placed enormous pressure on decision-makers in a crisis, since mobilisation schedules were difficult to halt once begun.

Nationalism

Nationalist aspirations destabilised the multinational Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires. Serbian nationalism, embodied in organisations such as the Black Hand, sought the union of all Serbs, including those under Habsburg rule in Bosnia; within Germany, France and Russia, popular nationalism and a cult of the offensive made compromise politically costly.

Historiography

The Fischer thesis, advanced by Fritz Fischer in the 1960s, argued that Germany bore principal responsibility, having aimed at European hegemony and grasped at war in 1914. Other historians, such as Christopher Clark in The Sleepwalkers (2012), distribute responsibility more broadly, portraying the crisis as a multipolar tragedy in which statesmen across Europe misjudged the consequences of their decisions. The debate over war guilt remains one of the most contested topics in modern historiography.
