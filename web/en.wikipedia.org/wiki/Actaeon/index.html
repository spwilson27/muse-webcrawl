Actaeon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In Greek mythology, Actaeon was a famed Theban hunter, grandson of Cadmus and son of Aristaeus and Autonoë. Trained by the centaur Chiron, he is remembered for his tragic death: he was transformed into a stag and torn to pieces by his own hunting dogs.

The myth

The best-known account is in Ovid's Metamorphoses. While hunting on Mount Cithaeron, Actaeon accidentally stumbled upon the goddess Artemis bathing naked in a secluded pool. Enraged, the goddess splashed water on him and turned him into a stag, robbing him of speech so that he could not reveal what he had seen. His own fifty hounds, failing to recognise their master, chased and devoured him; afterwards they searched for him in vain until Chiron made an image of Actaeon to comfort them. Other ancient versions differ: in some, Actaeon's offence was boasting that he surpassed Artemis in hunting, or aspiring to marry his aunt Semele.

In art and literature

The story was a popular subject in ancient vase painting and sculpture, and again in Renaissance and Baroque art — Titian's The Death of Actaeon (National Gallery, London) is among the most famous treatments. The myth has been read as a parable of forbidden sight, of the hunter becoming the hunted, and of destructive desire.
