Rates in the United Kingdom

A local authority rates bill.

Rates are a tax on property in the United Kingdom used to fund local government, historically levied since the Poor Relief Act 1601. Traditionally, rates were charged on both domestic and non-domestic (business) property, based on the property's assessed rental value, and collected by local authorities.

From 1990 (1989 in Scotland), domestic rates were replaced by the Community Charge ("poll tax"), which in turn was replaced by Council Tax in 1993, a banded tax based on property values. Non-domestic rates — now universally called business rates — survived these reforms: since 1990 they have been set nationally (as a uniform business rate multiplier in England) but collected locally, with proceeds redistributed among authorities. In Northern Ireland, the rating system was retained for domestic properties as well.

Business rates today

Business rates are calculated from a property's rateable value — its estimated open market rental value — multiplied by the national multiplier. Periodic revaluations adjust rateable values to reflect market rents. Reliefs exist for small businesses, charities, and certain sectors, and the burden of business rates is a recurring topic of debate about town-centre retail competitiveness versus online commerce.
