Hobbits are a fictional race in the writings of J. R. R. Tolkien. They are small people, about half the height of humans, living in the Shire in north-western Middle-earth. Noted for their simple pleasures — food, gardens, pipe-weed and parties — hobbits nevertheless play central roles in The Hobbit (1937) and The Lord of the Rings (1954–55), where Bilbo and Frodo Baggins carry the story.

Hobbits live in "hobbit-holes" — comfortable burrow-like homes, or smials, dug into hillsides.

Description

Tolkien described hobbits as two to four feet tall (averaging about 3 ft 6 in), with slightly pointed ears, large hairy feet with leathery soles (they rarely wear shoes), and curly brown hair. They dress in bright colours, especially green and yellow, and are fond of six meals a day when they can get them. Hobbits have keen senses and a gift for hiding, can live to about 100 years, and their lifespan is somewhat longer than that of ordinary Men. They speak a dialect of the Common Speech (Westron).

Origins in Tolkien's legendarium

In the fiction, hobbits are related to Men and first appear in the historical record when they crossed the Misty Mountains into Eriador in the Third Age (around T.A. 1050). They migrated westwards, eventually settling the Shire, granted to them by the King of Arthedain in 1601. Tolkien divides them into three strains: the Harfoots, Stoors and Fallohides. The Shire's quiet history is guarded by the Rangers of the North.

Role in the stories

Bilbo Baggins, recruited by the wizard Gandalf and Thorin's company of Dwarves, finds the One Ring in Gollum's cave. In The Lord of the Rings, his heir Frodo undertakes the quest to destroy the Ring, accompanied by Sam, Merry and Pippin. Their resistance to the Ring's corruption — rooted in their modest desires — proves decisive, and the hobbits return to liberate the Shire in the "Scouring of the Shire".

Origin of the word

Tolkien claimed to have invented the word "hobbit" spontaneously, writing the opening line of The Hobbit — "In a hole in the ground there lived a hobbit" — on a blank exam paper around 1930. Within the legendarium he derived it from Old English holbytla, "hole-builder". The word is now in general use, and in 2004 the small fossil hominin Homo floresiensis was nicknamed "the Hobbit" in reference to Tolkien's race.
