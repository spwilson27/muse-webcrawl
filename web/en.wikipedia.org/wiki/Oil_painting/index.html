Oil painting

Oil painting
MediumPigments suspended in drying oils (linseed, walnut, poppyseed)
SupportsCanvas, wood panel, board, copper
Early useExamples from at least the 7th century CE (Afghanistan); widespread in Europe by the 15th century
Key propertiesSlow drying, blending, glazing, impasto, reworkability

Oil painting is the process of painting with pigments bound in a medium of drying oil, principally linseed oil. Oil paint's slow drying time, capacity for translucent glazing and opaque impasto, and subtle blending made it the dominant medium of Western painting from the sixteenth century until the rise of acrylic paint in the twentieth.

History

Oil-based paints were used far earlier than once thought — surviving Buddhist murals in Bamyan, Afghanistan, date to the seventh century CE — and medieval European treatises such as Theophilus Presbyter's describe oil media. But oil painting came into its own in fifteenth-century Northern Europe, in the work of Early Netherlandish masters such as Jan van Eyck and his brother Hubert van Eyck, and Robert Campin, who exploited oil glazes to render light, texture, and minute detail with unprecedented realism. The technique spread to Italy — carried, legend says, by Antonello da Messina — and by the High Renaissance had displaced tempera and fresco as the prestige medium, supremely suited to the work of Titian, Rubens, Rembrandt, and Velázquez.

Technique

Artists build an oil painting in layers governed by the "fat over lean" principle: each successive layer must contain more oil (be "fatter") than the one below to dry without cracking. Methods include direct painting (alla prima, wet-on-wet, favored by the Impressionists), underpainting in monochrome, and glazing — transparent layers of pigment over dry paint that produce luminous depth. Supports are typically primed canvas or panel. Nineteenth-century innovations — the collapsible metal paint tube (1841) and the portable easel — freed painters to work outdoors, enabling Impressionism.

Materials

Pigments range from earth colors and lead white to modern synthetics; linseed oil is the commonest binder, with walnut and poppy oils used for pale colors. Solvents such as turpentine and mineral spirits thin paint and clean brushes, while resins (damar, mastic) form varnishes. Concerns about toxicity have renewed interest in water-mixable oils.
