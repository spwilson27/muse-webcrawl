Tetradrachm

Athenian silver tetradrachm showing Athena and her owl (placeholder)

The tetradrachm (Greek: τετράδραχμον, "four drachmae") was a large silver coin of the ancient Greek world, worth four drachmae. Struck on the Attic standard it weighed about 17.2 grams. The tetradrachm was one of the most widely used and trusted coins of antiquity, functioning as a kind of international trade currency from the 5th century BC through the Roman period.

The Athenian "owl"

The most famous tetradrachm was minted by Athens from about 510 BC. Its obverse bore the head of Athena wearing a crested helmet; the reverse bore her owl, an olive sprig, a crescent, and the letters ΑΘΕ ("of the Athenians"). Athenian "owls" were struck from the rich silver mines at Laurion and circulated throughout the Mediterranean and Near East, financing Athens' navy and empire. Their consistent weight and purity made them acceptable everywhere, and imitations were produced as far away as Egypt and Arabia.

Hellenistic and later issues

After Alexander the Great, tetradrachms bearing his image (as Herakles) and Zeus enthroned were struck in enormous quantities across his empire and by successor kingdoms — the Ptolemies in Egypt, the Seleucids in Asia, and the Antigonids in Macedon. Portrait tetradrachms of Hellenistic kings became masterworks of ancient die engraving. In Judaea, the Tyrian tetradrachm (shekel) was the coin specified for the Temple tax in Jerusalem, and such coins are candidates for the "thirty pieces of silver" of the Gospels. Under Rome, provincial tetradrachms continued in Syria and Egypt for centuries, albeit increasingly debased, until the monetary reforms of the late 3rd century AD.
