Degenerate art (German: Entartete Kunst) was a term adopted in the 1920s by the Nazi Party in Germany to describe modern art. During the dictatorship of Adolf Hitler, German modernist art — including works by renowned artists — was purged from state museums and banned, being held to be an insult to German feeling, Jewish or Communist in nature, and a threat to public morality.

The 1937 exhibition

In 1937 Nazi officials confiscated more than 16,000 works from German museums. A selection of about 650 paintings, sculptures, and prints by 112 artists was displayed in Munich as the Degenerate Art exhibition, hung deliberately chaotically, accompanied by mocking labels and slogans, and juxtaposed with works by people with mental illnesses. It drew over two million visitors in Munich alone — far more than the nearby Great German Art Exhibition of approved art — before touring Germany and Austria.

Consequences

Artists branded degenerate were dismissed from teaching posts, forbidden to exhibit or even to paint, and many emigrated; some, such as Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, died by suicide, and others perished in the Holocaust. Confiscated works were sold abroad for hard currency — notably at the 1939 Fischer auction in Lucerne — or destroyed: thousands of works on paper were burned in Berlin in 1939. Jazz and atonal music were attacked as "degenerate music" in a parallel 1938 exhibition in Düsseldorf.

Legacy

The episode remains the paradigmatic case of state suppression of modernism. Provenance researchers still trace looted "degenerate" works — the 2012 discovery of the Gurlitt hoard in Munich, containing many confiscated pieces, showed how long the campaign's shadow extends over museum collections.
