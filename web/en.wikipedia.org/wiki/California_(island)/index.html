Island of California

The Island of California was a long-standing European misconception, dating from the 16th century, that the Baja California Peninsula was not part of mainland North America but rather a large island separated from the continent by a strait, sometimes identified as the mythical Strait of Anián.

History of the error

The name California itself derives from the fictional island of California in Garci Rodríguez de Montalvo's 1510 romance Las Sergas de Esplandián, populated by Black women warriors ruled by Queen Calafia. Early explorers such as Francisco de Ulloa correctly reported Baja California as a peninsula, but a Carmelite friar, Antonio de la Ascensión, who accompanied Sebastián Vizcaíno's expedition of 1602–1603, promoted the island idea. The misconception was popularized by a Dutch map published by Joan Vinckeboons around 1650 and appeared on many maps through the early 18th century, including those of Herman Moll.

Correction

The Jesuit missionary and cartographer Eusebio Francisco Kino made overland expeditions between 1698 and 1701 demonstrating that Baja California was a peninsula, and his map was published in 1701. Nevertheless the island depiction persisted on some maps for decades. In 1747 Ferdinand VI of Spain issued a decree stating that California was not an island, and the detailed maps of Ferdinand VI and later cartographers finally dispelled the error. The maps showing California as an island are now highly collectible.
