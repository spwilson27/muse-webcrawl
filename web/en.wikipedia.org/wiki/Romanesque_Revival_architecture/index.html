Romanesque Revival architecture

Romanesque Revival (also called Neo-Romanesque) is a style of building employed from the mid-19th century inspired by the 11th- and 12th-century Romanesque architecture. In contrast to the historically later Gothic Revival, Romanesque Revival buildings are simpler in form, characterised by round arches, massive walls, sturdy piers, groin vaults, large towers and decorative arcading, with a frequent absence of pinnacles, spires and pointed ornament.

Development

The style emerged first in Germany, where it was known as the Rundbogenstil ("round-arched style"), promoted by architects such as Heinrich Hübsch, who argued in his 1828 essay In welchem Style sollen wir bauen? for a modern architecture derived from early Christian, Byzantine and Romanesque precedents. In Britain the style was used extensively for Nonconformist chapels, warehouses and civic buildings; Alfred Waterhouse's Natural History Museum in London (1873–1881) is a celebrated example in buff and blue terracotta.

Richardsonian Romanesque

In the United States the style reached its most distinctive form in the work of Henry Hobson Richardson, whose Trinity Church, Boston (1872–1877), popularised a personal idiom of rock-faced stone, broad round arches, deep window reveals and picturesque massing. "Richardsonian Romanesque" was widely adopted for courthouses, railway stations, libraries, universities and city halls across North America in the 1880s and 1890s before giving way to Beaux-Arts classicism and steel-frame construction.

Features

  • Semicircular arches for windows, doors and arcades
  • Massive, rusticated stone walls with contrasting dressings
  • Asymmetrical towers of differing heights, often with pyramidal or conical roofs
  • Polychromatic masonry and carved capitals with stylised foliage
  • Round turrets and corbel tables derived from medieval precedents
