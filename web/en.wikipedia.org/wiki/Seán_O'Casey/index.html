Seán O'Casey (Irish: Seán Ó Cathasaigh; born John Casey; 30 March 1880 – 18 September 1964) was an Irish dramatist and memoirist. A committed socialist and nationalist, he was the first Irish playwright of note to write about the Dublin working classes, and his plays — combining tragedy and comedy in rich Dublin dialect — are among the most performed in the Irish repertoire. His Dublin trilogy depicts the impact of the revolutionary decade (1916–1923) on the tenement poor.

Life

Born into a Protestant working-class family, O'Casey suffered chronic eye disease (trachoma) and left school at fourteen, working as a labourer, railwayman and builder's labourer. He was active in the Irish Citizen Army during the 1913 Dublin Lock-out and briefly its secretary, resigning over its growing nationalism-first orientation. After years of rejected manuscripts, the Abbey Theatre produced The Shadow of a Gunman in 1923, when he was 43.

The Dublin trilogy

  • The Shadow of a Gunman (1923): set during the War of Independence, about a poet mistaken for an IRA gunman on the run.
  • Juno and the Paycock (1924): set during the Civil War; the Boyle family's expected inheritance evaporates while "Captain" Jack Boyle idles — one of the most famous Irish plays, including the line "the whole worl's in a terrible state o' chassis".
  • The Plough and the Stars (1926): set during the Easter Rising; its unsentimental treatment provoked riots at the Abbey, as had Synge's Playboy a generation earlier.

Exile and later work

When the Abbey rejected The Silver Tassie (1928), an experimental anti-war play, O'Casey settled permanently in England. Later works include Within the Gates (1934), The Star Turns Red (1940), Red Roses for Me (1942) and Cock-a-Doodle Dandy (1949), as well as six volumes of acclaimed autobiography beginning with I Knock at the Door (1939). He died in Torquay in 1964.
