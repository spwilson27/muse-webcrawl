Earnings management

Earnings management is the use of accounting techniques to produce financial reports that present an overly positive view of a company's business activities and financial position. Taking advantage of how accounting rules are applied, management may deliberately misrepresent financial information, either within the flexibility of generally accepted accounting principles (accrual-based earnings management) or by timing real business decisions such as cutting research spending (real earnings management).

Motives

Common motives documented in the accounting literature include meeting analysts' earnings forecasts, smoothing income to appear less volatile, avoiding debt-covenant violations, increasing executive compensation tied to earnings, and influencing stock prices around events such as seasoned equity offerings. Arthur Levitt, former chairman of the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, criticised widespread earnings management in his 1998 "Numbers Game" speech.

Techniques and detection

Techniques include "cookie jar" reserves (over-provisioning in good years to draw on in bad), big-bath write-offs in a single bad period, and channel stuffing to pull revenue forward. Researchers detect likely earnings management statistically, for example with the Jones model of discretionary accruals and by studying bunching of reported earnings just above zero or just above prior-year figures. Aggressive earnings management sits on a continuum with outright accounting fraud, as seen in scandals such as Enron and WorldCom, which prompted reforms including the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002.
