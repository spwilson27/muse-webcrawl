Eastern Orthodox theology is the theology of the Eastern Orthodox Church. It is grounded in the Bible as interpreted within Holy Tradition — the living continuity of the Church's faith expressed in its liturgy, the writings of the Church Fathers, and the decrees of the seven ecumenical councils held between the fourth and eighth centuries. Orthodox theology holds that correct doctrine is inseparable from correct worship and the lived experience of God.

The Holy Trinity

Orthodox theology confesses one God in three persons (hypostases): the Father, the Son (Jesus Christ), and the Holy Spirit, who are consubstantial (homoousios) and undivided. The Father is the sole source or cause within the Trinity; the Son is eternally begotten of the Father and the Spirit eternally proceeds from the Father. Orthodoxy rejects the Western addition of the Filioque ("and the Son") to the Nicene Creed, both on theological grounds and because the creed was altered without an ecumenical council.

Theosis and salvation

The purpose of the Christian life is theosis (deification): by grace, human beings are called to become "partakers of the divine nature," sharing in God's life without becoming God in essence. Salvation is understood less as a legal transaction than as healing and transformation of fallen human nature, accomplished through Christ's incarnation, death, and resurrection, and appropriated through the sacraments (called "holy mysteries"), prayer, fasting, and repentance within the Church.

Essence and energies

Following St Gregory Palamas and the hesychast councils of the fourteenth century, Orthodox theology distinguishes between God's essence, which is utterly unknowable and imparticipable, and God's uncreated energies, through which God is truly known and experienced — as in the uncreated light of the Transfiguration. This distinction safeguards both God's transcendence and the real possibility of communion with him.

Apophatic theology

Orthodox theology emphasises the apophatic (negative) way: God is beyond all names and concepts, and statements about God deny creaturely limitations more truly than they describe him. This approach, rooted in writers such as Pseudo-Dionysius and St Maximus the Confessor, contrasts with more systematic, scholastic methods in the West, though cataphatic (affirmative) theology is also used.

Other themes

The Orthodox Church venerates Mary as Theotokos ("Birth-giver of God") and honours the saints and icons, affirming the decrees of the Seventh Ecumenical Council (Nicaea II, 787). It teaches the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist, infant baptism, and the resurrection of the dead and the life of the age to come. Ecclesiology is conciliar: authority rests in the whole body of the Church guided by the Holy Spirit, with bishops in apostolic succession.
