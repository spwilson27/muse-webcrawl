Black–Scholes model

The Black–Scholes model, also known as the Black–Scholes–Merton model, is a mathematical model of a financial market containing derivative investment instruments, principally options. From the parabolic partial differential equation in the model, known as the Black–Scholes equation, one can deduce the Black–Scholes formula, which gives a theoretical estimate of the price of European-style options and shows that the option has a unique price regardless of the risk of the security and its expected return — by hedging the option correctly, the investor eliminates financial risk.

History

The model was introduced in a 1973 paper, "The Pricing of Options and Corporate Liabilities", by Fischer Black and Myron Scholes; Robert C. Merton provided a rigorous derivation and generalized the formula, and the model is often named for all three. In 1997 Scholes and Merton were awarded the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for the work (Black had died in 1995 and was thus ineligible).

Assumptions

The idealized model assumes that the underlying asset price follows geometric Brownian motion with constant volatility and drift; markets are frictionless, with no transaction costs or taxes; trading is continuous; the risk-free interest rate is constant and known; and the option is European, exercisable only at expiration. Under these assumptions, a portfolio combining the option with a dynamically adjusted position in the underlying can be made riskless, and by no-arbitrage its return must equal the risk-free rate, yielding the Black–Scholes equation.

The formula

For a non-dividend-paying stock, the price of a call option is C = S·N(d1) − K·e−rT·N(d2), where S is the spot price, K the strike price, T the time to expiry, r the risk-free rate, and N(·) the cumulative standard normal distribution, with d1 and d2 depending on the volatility σ of the underlying. The quantities describing the sensitivities of the option price to the inputs — delta, gamma, vega, theta, and rho — are collectively known as "the Greeks".

Use and limitations

The formula is universally used in options markets, typically inverted to quote an implied volatility. Real markets violate the assumptions: volatility varies with strike and maturity (the "volatility smile"), prices jump, and liquidity is imperfect. Extensions and alternatives, such as local and stochastic volatility models and the Heston model, address these shortcomings, but Black–Scholes remains the benchmark language of options pricing.
