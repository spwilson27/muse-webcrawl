Malmesbury Abbey, at Malmesbury in Wiltshire, England, was a Benedictine abbey founded in the seventh century. At its height it was one of the great monastic houses of medieval England, famed for its library and for the scholarship of its first abbot, Aldhelm, and of the twelfth-century historian William of Malmesbury. The abbey was dissolved in 1539, but a portion of its church survives in use as the town's parish church.

History

A hermitage was established on the site in the later seventh century by the Irish scholar Maildulph. Around 676 the community became a Benedictine monastery under Aldhelm, nephew of King Ine of Wessex, who built its first stone church. King Æthelstan, the first king of all England, was buried at Malmesbury in 939, and the abbey grew wealthy on royal patronage.

The present church was begun in the twelfth century and consecrated in 1180. Its nave was once among the largest in England, with a spire taller than that of Salisbury Cathedral; the spire collapsed around 1500, bringing down much of the building, and the west tower fell later in the century. After the Dissolution the parishioners bought part of the church for worship, and roughly a third of the abbey church — the nave — survives today.

Eilmer of Malmesbury

The abbey is associated with Eilmer of Malmesbury, an eleventh-century monk who, according to William of Malmesbury, attempted to fly from the abbey tower using artificial wings, gliding some distance before crashing and breaking both legs.

Architecture

The surviving fabric is a notable example of the transition from Norman to Early English Gothic architecture, with richly carved Romanesque sculpture around the south porch depicting scenes from the Bible in its archivolts.
