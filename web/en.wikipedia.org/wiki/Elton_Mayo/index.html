George Elton Mayo (26 December 1880 – 7 September 1949) was an Australian psychologist, industrial researcher, and organizational theorist. He is best known as the leader of the research team behind the Hawthorne studies at the Western Electric Hawthorne Works in Chicago (1927–1932), and as a founder of the Human Relations Movement in management thought.

Career

Mayo studied philosophy and psychology in Adelaide and Edinburgh, worked with shell-shocked soldiers during the First World War, and became professor of philosophy and psychology at the University of Queensland. In 1923 he moved to the United States, to the University of Pennsylvania and then, in 1926, to Harvard Business School, where he remained until his retirement in 1947.

The Hawthorne studies

Mayo and his associates, including Fritz Roethlisberger and William J. Dickson, studied workers at the Hawthorne Works in a series of experiments that began with illumination studies and continued through the Relay Assembly Test Room and the Bank Wiring Observation Room. The researchers concluded that productivity rose not primarily because of physical changes — lighting, rest breaks, hours — but because workers responded to being observed, consulted, and formed into cohesive social groups. From this emerged the concept later named the "Hawthorne effect": subjects modifying their behaviour because they know they are being studied.

The studies' findings were popularized in Mayo's The Human Problems of an Industrial Civilization (1933) and in Roethlisberger and Dickson's Management and the Worker (1939). Mayo argued that industrial unrest stemmed from social disorganization and a lack of belonging, and that management should attend to informal group norms and communication rather than treating workers as purely economic agents ("rabble hypothesis").

Criticism

Later scholars have criticized the Hawthorne research for methodological weaknesses, small samples, and the team's tendency to interpret results toward collaboration rather than conflict; reanalyses of the original data have questioned the strength and shape of the reported effects. Nonetheless, the studies remain a landmark in organizational behaviour and research-methods teaching.
