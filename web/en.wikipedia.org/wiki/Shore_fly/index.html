The shore flies (family Ephydridae) are a large family of small flies in the order Diptera, commonly found near the shores of oceans, lakes, ponds, and streams. There are roughly 2,000 described species in about 160 genera worldwide. The common name also refers to a few flies in other families, such as Canacidae (beach flies) and Scatella species, that share shoreline habitats.

Shore flies are typically 0.8 to 7 mm long, dark in colour, and often appear dull or pruinose. The antennae bear an arista that is bare or pubescent, and the face is often strongly convex. The larvae of many species feed on algae and decaying organic matter in wet habitats; others are leaf miners, predators, or parasitoids.

Notable species

Ephydra hians, the alkali fly of Mono Lake in California, famously dives underwater encased in a bubble of air to feed and lay eggs, and its pupae were traditionally harvested as food (kutsavi) by the Kutzadika'a people. The petroleum fly, Helaeomyia petrolei, develops in natural pools of crude oil in California, the only known insect to do so. Members of the genus Scatella are common greenhouse pests, feeding on algae in damp potting media.

Ecology and importance

Shore flies form a major food source for shorebirds and other predators around wetlands and salt lakes. Flocks of alkali flies at Mono Lake sustain migrating eared grebes and phalaropes. Some species are agricultural or horticultural pests, while others are used as biological control agents of aquatic weeds. Shore flies should not be confused with fungus gnats or drain flies, which they superficially resemble in greenhouses.
