Reaction mechanism

In chemistry, a reaction mechanism is the step-by-step sequence of elementary reactions by which a chemical change occurs. A mechanism describes which bonds are broken and formed, in what order, the intermediates and transition states involved, and the movement of electrons in each step. The sum of the elementary steps gives the overall reaction, but the mechanism contains far more information than the overall stoichiometry.

Elementary steps

Each elementary step is characterised by its molecularity: unimolecular (a single molecule reacting, as in a rearrangement or decomposition), bimolecular (two molecules colliding), or, rarely, termolecular. Elementary steps cannot be broken down further; intermediates formed in one step are consumed in a later step and so do not appear in the overall equation.

Determining mechanisms

Mechanisms are hypotheses supported by evidence rather than directly observed facts. Chemists infer them from reaction kinetics (the rate law and its dependence on concentrations), the stereochemistry of products, isotopic labelling experiments, trapping or spectroscopic detection of intermediates, and computational chemistry. Well-known examples include the SN1 and SN2 mechanisms of nucleophilic substitution, free-radical chain reactions, and electrophilic aromatic substitution.

Representation

Mechanisms are conventionally drawn with curved ("curly") arrows showing the flow of electron pairs from nucleophiles to electrophiles, or single-headed "fishhook" arrows for the movement of single electrons in radical reactions.
