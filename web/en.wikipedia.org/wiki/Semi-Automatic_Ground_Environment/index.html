Semi-Automatic Ground Environment

The Semi-Automatic Ground Environment (SAGE) was a system of large computers and associated networking equipment that coordinated data from many radars in the United States and Canada to produce a single unified image of the airspace over North America, directing interceptor aircraft and missiles against potential Soviet bomber attacks during the Cold War.

Development

SAGE grew out of MIT Lincoln Laboratory research and the Whirlwind computer project. Each Direction Center housed an AN/FSQ-7 computer—the largest computer ever built, with some 60,000 vacuum tubes, weighing about 275 tons, and using a duplex configuration for reliability. IBM was the principal computer contractor, with Burroughs, Western Electric and others contributing communications and radar interfaces.

Operation

Operational from the late 1950s, SAGE sectors received radar tracks over telephone lines from gap-filler and long-range radar stations, automatically tracked aircraft, and computed intercept courses transmitted digitally to fighters and to BOMARC surface-to-air missiles. Consoles with light guns allowed operators to query tracks interactively—an early large-scale instance of real-time computing, computer networking, and interactive displays. The system remained in use, progressively modernised, until 1984.

Legacy

SAGE influenced commercial computing: it spurred advances in magnetic core memory, modems, and real-time operating systems, and IBM's experience fed into the Sabre airline reservation system.
