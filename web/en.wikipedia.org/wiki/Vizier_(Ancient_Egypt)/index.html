Vizier (Ancient Egypt)

The vizier (Egyptian: ṯꜣty, tjaty) was the highest official in ancient Egypt to serve the pharaoh during the Old, Middle, and New Kingdoms. Often described as the equivalent of a prime minister or chief justice combined, the vizier stood second only to the king, supervising the entire civil administration: the judiciary, treasury, agriculture, royal works, archives, and correspondence, and receiving foreign envoys. Viziers were typically appointed by the pharaoh, frequently from powerful aristocratic families, and in the Old Kingdom often were royal relatives.

Duties

The vizier's responsibilities are laid out in the New Kingdom text known as the Installation of the Vizier and the Duties of the Vizier, preserved in the Theban tomb of the vizier Rekhmire (TT100) under Thutmose III. He reported daily to the king on the state of the country, sat in judgment as head of the courts, heard petitions from any citizen, oversaw tax collection, granaries, labour levies, and expeditions, and controlled access to the palace. The Duties stress impartiality: the vizier must not take bribes, favour kin, or lose his temper with petitioners. His distinctive costume was a long, high-waisted skirt and, on ceremonial occasions, an image of the goddess Maat worn on the chest, symbolising justice.

History

The office emerged in the early dynastic period and matured in the Old Kingdom, when viziers such as Imhotep (also architect and later deified) and Ptahhotep — to whom the wisdom text The Maxims of Ptahhotep is attributed — were buried in great mastabas near the king's pyramid. In the New Kingdom the office was divided between a vizier of the North (resident at Memphis) and a vizier of the South (at Thebes). Famous New Kingdom viziers include Rekhmire, Ramose, and Paser. The office declined with the fragmentation of the Third Intermediate Period.
