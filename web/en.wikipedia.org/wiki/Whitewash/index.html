Whitewash

Whitewash
Composition:Slaked lime (calcium hydroxide) and water, often with chalk, salt, glue or casein
Uses:Coating walls, barns, fences, tree trunks; dairy and stable hygiene
Properties:Mildly antimicrobial and insecticidal; bright white, temporary

Whitewash, also called lime wash or calcimine, is a low-cost type of paint made from slaked lime (calcium hydroxide) mixed with water and other ingredients such as chalk or whiting. It has been used for centuries to coat the walls of houses, barns and churches, and lent its name to the metaphorical sense of covering up faults.

Composition and application

Whitewash is made by slaking quicklime and diluting it to a milky consistency; additives such as salt (to aid curing), alum, tallow or linseed oil improve adhesion and durability. Applied thinly with a broad brush, it dries as the lime carbonates, reacting with carbon dioxide in the air to form a skin of calcium carbonate. It is cheap but not long-lasting, gradually wearing away and being renewed periodically.

Uses

Because slaked lime is alkaline and mildly disinfectant, whitewash was standard in dairies, stables and byres, and was once used to sanitise interiors after infectious disease. Whitewashed tree trunks protect bark from sunscald and insects — a familiar sight in Mediterranean and Latin American streets. The bright white villages of Andalusia and the Greek islands owe their appearance to lime wash, valued for reflecting heat.

In culture

Mark Twain made whitewashing famous in The Adventures of Tom Sawyer (1876), where Tom persuades his friends that whitewashing Aunt Polly's fence is a privilege worth paying for. The literal practice generated the figurative verb "to whitewash" — to gloss over wrongdoing — and in sport, a "whitewash" is a shutout victory.
