Briggs–Rauscher reaction

The Briggs–Rauscher reaction is one of the best-known oscillating chemical reactions, and is a popular chemistry demonstration. In the reaction, a mixture of hydrogen peroxide, potassium iodate, sulfuric acid, malonic acid, manganese sulfate and starch changes colour repeatedly — cycling between clear, amber and deep blue-black — for several minutes before eventually settling to a dark blue-black mixture with the odour of chlorine.

History

The reaction was developed by two high school science teachers, Thomas S. Briggs and Warren C. Rauscher of Galileo High School in San Francisco, who published it in the Journal of Chemical Education in 1973. It builds on earlier oscillating systems, including the Bray–Liebhafsky reaction and the then newly discovered Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction, but produces particularly dramatic colour changes because of the starch indicator.

Mechanism

The overall process involves two competing reaction pathways involving iodine species:

  • A radical process that generates iodine (I2), which colours the solution amber.
  • A slower non-radical process that converts iodine to iodide and then to iodate; when iodide concentration rises, the radical process is inhibited.

When iodine concentration is high enough, iodine complexes with the starch to produce an intense blue-black colour. The manganese(II) catalyst and malonic acid (which consumes iodine and iodine radicals) help sustain the oscillations. Oxygen gas is evolved during the reaction, and bubbles can be seen in the solution.

Demonstration notes

The typical recipe combines solutions of hydrogen peroxide, potassium iodate in sulfuric acid, and a mixture of malonic acid, manganese sulfate and starch. Because chlorine-like gases and iodine vapour are released, the demonstration is best performed in a fume hood.
