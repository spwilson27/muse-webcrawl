Child abandonment

Child abandonment is the practice of relinquishing interests and claims over one's offspring in an illegal or extralegal way, with the intent of never resuming care. It includes abandoning infants in public or unsafe places, leaving children without provision for their support, and deserting a child with the intention of severing parental responsibility. Abandonment is distinct from lawful surrender under safe-haven laws and from formal adoption or state removal of children.

Causes

Historically and today, abandonment is associated with poverty, social stigma (especially towards births outside marriage), disability or illness of the child, sex-selective pressures, war and displacement, and parents' mental illness or addiction. In some eras the exposure of infants was widespread enough that foundling hospitals were established across Europe to receive them.

History

Infant exposure was practised in ancient Greece and Rome. Medieval and early modern Europe developed foundling wheels — rotating hatches in convent and hospital walls allowing anonymous deposit of babies — and large foundling hospitals such as London's Foundling Hospital (founded 1741). High mortality among foundlings was common. In the 19th and 20th centuries, "orphan trains" and baby farming scandals prompted legal reform.

Law and modern responses

Abandonment of a child is a criminal offence in most jurisdictions and grounds for termination of parental rights. Beginning with Texas in 1999, all U.S. states enacted safe-haven ("Baby Moses") laws permitting parents to surrender unharmed newborns anonymously at hospitals, fire stations and similar locations. Several countries maintain modern "baby hatches" or baby boxes. Abandoned children typically enter foster care and may become eligible for adoption; long-term psychological effects on abandoned children are a significant area of research.
