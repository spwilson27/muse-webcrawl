Spy satellite

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Artist's impression of a KH-11 reconnaissance satellite

A spy satellite, officially a reconnaissance satellite, is an Earth observation satellite or communications satellite deployed for military or intelligence applications. Reconnaissance satellites provide imagery intelligence (IMINT), signals intelligence (SIGINT), measurement and signature intelligence (MASINT), and communications relay for intelligence agencies.

Types

  • Optical imaging satellites use large telescopes to photograph targets at high resolution; examples include the American CORONA and KH-11 KENNEN series.
  • Radar imaging satellites use synthetic-aperture radar to image through clouds and at night; examples include the U.S. Lacrosse/Onyx and Germany's SAR-Lupe.
  • Signals intelligence satellites intercept radio and radar emissions.
  • Early warning satellites detect missile launches via infrared sensors, such as the U.S. Defense Support Program and SBIRS.
  • Ocean reconnaissance and electronic intelligence satellites monitor naval activity.

History

The first successful reconnaissance satellite programme was the American CORONA series, whose first successful film-return mission, Discoverer 14, recovered a capsule over the Pacific in August 1960. Early satellites returned film canisters that were snatched mid-air by aircraft; later systems electro-optically scanned imagery and transmitted it digitally. The Soviet Union operated the Zenit series and its successors. Reconnaissance satellites played an important verification role in arms-control treaties during the Cold War, and the legal regime for them was shaped by the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, which permits observation from space.

Modern systems

Today the United States, Russia, China, France, Germany, Israel, India, Japan, and other states operate reconnaissance satellites. Commercial high-resolution imagery from companies such as Maxar and Planet has blurred the line between civil and military Earth observation. Ground-sample distances of the best government optical systems are classified but widely estimated at around 10 centimetres or better.
