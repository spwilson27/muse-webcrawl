Trackway

A trackway is a set of consecutive fossil footprints made in a substrate (usually soft sediment) by an animal in the course of its locomotion — walking, running, hopping, or crawling. Fossil trackways are studied as trace fossils (ichnofossils), and the branch of palaeontology concerned with them is ichnology. Unlike body fossils, which record an organism's anatomy at death, trackways record behaviour of a living animal: its gait, speed, posture, and sometimes social behaviour.

A dinosaur trackway preserved in rock, showing a regular sequence of footprints.

Information from trackways

From the spacing and arrangement of prints, researchers can estimate an animal's stride length, hip height, and speed — for example using formulas developed by R. McNeill Alexander relating stride length and estimated leg length to velocity. Trackways of dinosaurs provided early evidence that some species moved bipedally with an upright gait and did not drag their tails, and parallel trackways of sauropods and other dinosaurs have been used to argue for herding behaviour. Trackways also preserve animals that rarely fossilise otherwise.

Famous trackway sites

  • Laetoli, Tanzania — 3.66-million-year-old hominin footprints preserved in volcanic ash, attributed to Australopithecus afarensis, showing bipedal walking.
  • Dinosaur Valley State Park, Texas — theropod and sauropod tracks in the bed of the Paluxy River.
  • Lark Quarry, Australia — thousands of small dinosaur prints interpreted as a stampede.
  • Cal Orck'o, Bolivia — a limestone wall bearing thousands of dinosaur tracks.
  • White Sands, New Mexico — human footprints dated to roughly 21,000–23,000 years ago, among the earliest evidence of humans in the Americas.

Prehistoric roads

The term "trackway" is also used in archaeology for ancient routeways — paths formed by the repeated passage of people and animals — such as the Sweet Track and other prehistoric timber trackways built across wetlands in the Somerset Levels, and ridgeway paths along chalk hills in England.
