Avalanche breakdown

Avalanche breakdown (or the avalanche effect) is a phenomenon that can occur in both insulating and semiconducting materials. It is a form of electric current multiplication that can allow very large currents within materials which are otherwise good insulators. It is a type of electron avalanche. The avalanche process occurs when carriers in a strong electric field gain enough kinetic energy between collisions to create additional electron–hole pairs by impact ionization.

Explanation

When a large reverse voltage is applied across a p–n junction, thermally generated minority carriers are accelerated by the electric field in the depletion region. If a carrier acquires enough energy, it can knock a valence electron into the conduction band, creating an additional electron–hole pair. Both new carriers are then accelerated and can generate further pairs, producing a runaway multiplication of current. Unlike Zener breakdown, which is dominated by quantum tunnelling and occurs in heavily doped junctions at low voltages, avalanche breakdown dominates at higher reverse voltages and has a positive temperature coefficient of breakdown voltage.

Applications

  • Avalanche photodiodes exploit impact ionization to amplify weak optical signals.
  • Single-photon avalanche diodes (SPADs), operated above breakdown in Geiger mode, detect individual photons.
  • Avalanche (reference) diodes provide voltage references and transient suppression.
