CPU throttling

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

CPU throttling, also called dynamic clock or thermal throttling in some contexts, is a technique in computer architecture by which the clock speed of a microprocessor is automatically adjusted in response to conditions such as temperature, power availability, or workload. Throttling reduces power consumption and heat generation, extending component life and battery runtime at the cost of performance.

Thermal throttling

Modern processors include on-die temperature sensors. When junction temperatures approach a specified maximum (often around 95–105 °C), the processor inserts idle states or lowers its clock frequency and voltage, sometimes in discrete stages, until temperatures fall. If temperatures continue to rise, protective shutdown occurs. Thermal throttling is common in laptops, smartphones, and compact devices where cooling capacity is limited, and sustained workloads can run noticeably slower than short bursts.

Power and policy throttling

Operating systems and firmware also throttle CPUs to save energy. Linux's CPUFreq governors, Windows power plans, and Intel SpeedStep / AMD Cool'n'Quiet scale frequency and voltage dynamically (dynamic voltage and frequency scaling, DVFS). Servers may be throttled to respect datacentre power caps, and mobile devices throttle when the battery is low or the device is hot.

Clock modulation

An older mechanism, used in Pentium 4-era processors, modulated the clock by gating it on and off at a programmable duty cycle — an effective speed reduction without changing the actual clock frequency.
