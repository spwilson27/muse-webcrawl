Acute kidney injury

Acute kidney injury
SpecialtyNephrology
SymptomsReduced urine output, fluid retention, fatigue, confusion
CausesLow blood flow to kidneys, direct kidney damage, urinary obstruction, sepsis, nephrotoxins
DiagnosisRise in serum creatinine, decreased urine output (KDIGO criteria)

Acute kidney injury (AKI), previously called acute renal failure, is a sudden episode of kidney failure or kidney damage that happens within a few hours or a few days. It causes a build-up of waste products in the blood and makes it hard for the kidneys to maintain fluid balance. AKI is common in hospitalised patients, particularly those critically ill.

Causes

Causes are conventionally grouped as prerenal (decreased blood flow, e.g. from dehydration, haemorrhage or heart failure), intrinsic renal (damage to the kidney itself, including acute tubular necrosis from ischaemia or nephrotoxic drugs, glomerulonephritis and interstitial nephritis) and postrenal (obstruction of urine flow, e.g. an enlarged prostate or kidney stones). Sepsis is a leading cause in intensive care.

Diagnosis and staging

The KDIGO guidelines define AKI by an increase in serum creatinine of ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, an increase to ≥1.5 times baseline within seven days, or urine output below 0.5 mL/kg/h for six hours, and stage severity from 1 to 3.

Treatment and prognosis

Management focuses on treating the underlying cause, stopping nephrotoxic medications, optimising fluids and blood pressure, and correcting electrolytes; severe cases may require kidney replacement therapy (dialysis). Many patients recover function, but AKI is associated with increased mortality and later chronic kidney disease.
