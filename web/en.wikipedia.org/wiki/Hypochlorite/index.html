Hypochlorite

Formula: ClO−
Conjugate acid: Hypochlorous acid (HOCl)
Common salt: Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl)

In chemistry, hypochlorite is an anion with the chemical formula ClO−. It combines with a variety of cations to form hypochlorite salts. Common examples include sodium hypochlorite (household bleach) and calcium hypochlorite (used in swimming pool sanitation). Hypochlorites are strong oxidising agents and are widely used as disinfectants and bleaching agents.

Chemistry

Hypochlorite is the conjugate base of hypochlorous acid (HOCl, pKa ≈ 7.5). In aqueous solution the anion is in equilibrium with HOCl, which is the more potent microbicide. Hypochlorite solutions are unstable, decomposing — especially in light, heat, or in the presence of transition-metal ions — to chloride and chlorate, or releasing oxygen. Mixing hypochlorite bleach with acids releases chlorine gas, and mixing with ammonia forms toxic chloramines.

Production and uses

Sodium hypochlorite is produced industrially by chlorination of sodium hydroxide solution, and on site by electrolysis of brine. Beyond household bleach and laundry products, hypochlorites disinfect drinking water and pools, sanitise food-processing equipment, and are used in wound care (dilute Dakin's solution) and endodontics. Calcium hypochlorite, a solid, is also used for pool "shock" treatment.

Biology

Neutrophils generate hypochlorite as part of the immune respiratory burst: the enzyme myeloperoxidase catalyses the reaction of hydrogen peroxide with chloride to form hypochlorous acid, which kills engulfed microorganisms. The same chemistry can damage host tissue in chronic inflammation.
