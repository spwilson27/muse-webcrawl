Comprehensive school

A comprehensive school is a type of secondary school that does not select its intake on the basis of academic achievement or aptitude. The term is commonly used in relation to England and Wales, where comprehensive schools were introduced as part of reforms replacing the tripartite system of grammar schools, secondary modern schools, and technical schools, which selected pupils at age eleven by the eleven-plus examination. Comprehensive systems broadly comparable to the British model exist in many countries, including the United States (the general high school), Canada, and the Nordic states, where a common school for all is the norm.

History in England and Wales

After the Education Act 1944, children were allocated to different school types according to performance in the eleven-plus. Criticism of selection — on educational and social grounds — grew through the 1950s, and some local education authorities began experimenting with comprehensive schools, among them Anglesey and London. In 1965 the Labour government issued Circular 10/65, requesting all local authorities to submit plans for comprehensive reorganisation. By the late 1970s the great majority of state secondary pupils in England and Wales attended comprehensive schools. Grammar schools survived in some areas (such as Kent and Buckinghamshire), and selection remains a recurring political debate.

Organisation

Comprehensives vary in age range (11–16, 11–18, or in some areas middle schools feeding 13–18 upper schools) and in internal organisation: pupils may be taught in mixed-ability groups, streamed by general ability, or set by ability within subjects. Most comprehensives have sixth forms or link to sixth-form and further-education colleges. Supporters argue comprehensives avoid the stigma and misallocation of selection at eleven and widen opportunity; critics argue that mixed-ability teaching can under-challenge the most able, and point to continuing grammar schools' examination results. Research findings on attainment effects remain contested.

International context

Most OECD countries educate the full ability range in common secondary schools until at least age 15 or 16; systems that track pupils into separate school types early (notably in Germany, Austria, and the Netherlands) differ from the comprehensive ideal, though the boundary has blurred with the growth of integrated Gesamtschulen and similar schools.
