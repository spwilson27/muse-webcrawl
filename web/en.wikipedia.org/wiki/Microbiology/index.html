Microbiology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Microbiology is the study of microorganisms — living things too small to be seen with the naked eye, including Bacteria, archaea, microscopic fungi, protists and algae, together with the non-living viruses. It began with Antonie van Leeuwenhoek's observations of 'animalcules' in the 1670s and matured in the 19th century with Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch, whose germ theory showed microbes cause fermentation, decay and infectious disease.

Microorganisms dominate the biosphere by number and biochemical range: they fix nitrogen, drive the carbon and sulphur cycles, produce much of the atmosphere's oxygen (marine cyanobacteria and algae), and live in and on every larger organism — the human microbiome alone roughly matches the body's own cell count.

Subfields and methods

Bacteriology, virology, mycology and parasitology study the main groups; microbial ecology and environmental microbiology study communities in soil, oceans and extreme habitats. Core methods include microscopy, pure culture on agar (Koch's innovation), staining (the Gram stain), and now DNA sequencing and metagenomics, which reveal the vast majority of microbes that have never been cultured.

Applications

Microbes make bread, beer, cheese and antibiotics, treat sewage, produce insulin and other drugs by genetic engineering, and serve as model organisms for genetics. The same discipline studies pathogens — the targets of vaccines, sanitation and public health.
