In the mathematical field of graph theory, an automorphism of a graph is a form of symmetry in which the graph is mapped onto itself while preserving all of its edge–vertex connectivity. An automorphism of a graph G is a permutation σ of the vertex set of G, written σ : V → V, such that a pair of vertices (u, v) forms an edge if and only if (σ(u), σ(v)) also forms an edge. That is, it is a graph isomorphism from G to itself.

Graph automorphisms yield a rich algebraic structure: the set of all automorphisms of a given graph, under the operation of composition of permutations, forms a group, the automorphism group of the graph. In the opposite direction, given any group, one can construct a graph whose automorphism group is isomorphic to that group, following the construction of a Frucht graph; indeed, every finite group is the automorphism group of some finite graph.

Computational complexity

Constructing the automorphism group is at least as difficult (in terms of its computational complexity) as solving the graph isomorphism problem, determining whether two given graphs correspond vertex-for-vertex and edge-for-edge. For, G and H are isomorphic if and only if the disconnected graph formed by the disjoint union of G and H has an automorphism that swaps the two components. In fact, just counting the automorphisms is polynomial-time equivalent to graph isomorphism.

The graph automorphism problem is the problem of testing whether a graph has a nontrivial automorphism. It belongs to the complexity class NP of problems solvable in nondeterministic polynomial time; it is known to be solvable in quasi-polynomial time. The similar problem of determining whether a graph has any symmetry at all is closely studied in connection with graph isomorphism and its complexity classification.

Algorithms and applications

Practical algorithms for graph automorphism and canonical labelling include nauty and Traces (developed by Brendan McKay and Adolfo Piperno), bliss, and saucy. These tools are widely used in computational chemistry, where automorphisms determine equivalent atoms in a molecule, and in combinatorics for enumerating non-isomorphic structures. Automorphisms also allow the detection of symmetries used to compress graphs and speed up isomorphism tests.

Symmetric graphs

Graphs with many automorphisms are studied under the name of symmetric graphs. A graph is vertex-transitive if its automorphism group acts transitively on its vertices, and arc-transitive if the action is transitive on ordered pairs of adjacent vertices. The Petersen graph is a famous highly symmetric example: its automorphism group is the symmetric group S5, of order 120.
