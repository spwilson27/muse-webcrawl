Disc harrow

A disc harrow is a harrow whose cutting edges are a row of concave metal discs, which may be scalloped or have notched edges. It is an agricultural implement used to till the soil where crops are to be planted, to chop up unwanted weeds or crop residue, and to prepare seedbeds.

Description

The implement consists of many carbon steel or boron steel discs mounted on one or more axles, called gangs, set at an oblique angle ("gang angle") to the direction of travel. As the harrow is pulled — usually by a tractor — the discs roll and cut into the soil, slicing residue and inverting a shallow layer of earth. Disc diameters commonly range from about 300 mm on light harrows to over 800 mm on heavy offset models used for primary tillage.

Types

  • Tandem disc harrows have four gangs arranged in an X pattern, with two front gangs throwing soil outward and two rear gangs throwing it inward, levelling the field in one pass.
  • Offset disc harrows have two gangs in a V arrangement offset to one side of the tractor and are typically heavier, used for primary tillage of orchards and new ground.
  • Single-action harrows use two gangs throwing soil in opposite directions.

Uses

Disc harrows are used for secondary tillage after ploughing, for incorporating crop stubble and green manure, for weed control, and for breaking up pasture. Compared with a plough they work shallower and faster, but repeated use can compact the soil at disc depth and increase erosion risk on sloping land.
