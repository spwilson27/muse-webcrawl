Lex talionis

[Image: The upper part of the stele of the Code of Hammurabi, the Babylonian law code containing early examples of talionic penalties]

Lex talionis is the principle of retributive justice expressed in the maxim "an eye for an eye" (Latin talio, retaliation in kind): the punishment should correspond in kind and degree to the offence.

Ancient sources

The principle appears in the Code of Hammurabi (c. 1750 BC), which prescribed like-for-like penalties — for example, that if a man destroyed the eye of another man, his own eye should be destroyed, though penalties differed by the social class of the victim. It also appears in the Hebrew Bible ("eye for eye, tooth for tooth", Exodus 21:24), where rabbinic interpretation generally read it as requiring monetary compensation, and in early Roman law under the Twelve Tables.

Function and interpretation

Historians of law commonly interpret the talion not as a licence for vengeance but as a limit on it: it fixed a ceiling on retaliation, preventing feuds from escalating by requiring that the response not exceed the injury. In ancient law it also interacted with systems of monetary composition payments, such as wergild.

Influence

The idea underlies later theories of retributivism, including Kant's ius talionis, and it persists in debates about proportionality in sentencing. The phrase "an eye for an eye" is also famously invoked — and criticised — in discussions of retaliation in international affairs.
