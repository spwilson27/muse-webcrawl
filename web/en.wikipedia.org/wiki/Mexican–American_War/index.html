Mexican–American War
Date25 April 1846 – 2 February 1848
LocationTexas, northern and central Mexico, California, New Mexico
ResultAmerican victory; Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo; Mexican Cession
CommandersZachary Taylor, Winfield Scott (U.S.); Antonio López de Santa Anna (Mexico)

The Mexican–American War, also known in the United States as the Mexican War and in Mexico as the Intervención estadounidense en México ("United States intervention in Mexico"), was an invasion of Mexico by the United States Army from 1846 to 1848. It followed the 1845 American annexation of Texas, which Mexico still considered its territory, and a disputed boundary between the Nueces River and the Rio Grande.

The storming of Chapultepec, September 1847, opened the way to Mexico City.

Course of the war

After the Thornton Affair skirmish of April 1846, President James K. Polk obtained a declaration of war. Zachary Taylor's army won at Palo Alto, Resaca de la Palma, Monterrey, and Buena Vista (1847) in the north. Meanwhile, Stephen Kearny occupied New Mexico and California (with John C. Frémont and naval forces under Stockton and Sloat). Winfield Scott landed at Veracruz in March 1847 in the first major U.S. amphibious operation, then defeated Santa Anna at Cerro Gordo, Contreras, Churubusco, Molino del Rey, and Chapultepec, occupying Mexico City in September 1847. The war was controversial at home: Whigs and abolitionists, including Congressman Abraham Lincoln, denounced it as a war to expand slave territory, and Henry David Thoreau's essay "Civil Disobedience" grew out of his protest.

Treaty and consequences

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (2 February 1848) fixed the Rio Grande as Texas's border and transferred the Mexican Cession — present-day California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona, and parts of New Mexico, Colorado, and Wyoming — to the United States in exchange for $15 million and the assumption of claims. Mexico lost roughly a third of its territory. The war trained a generation of officers (Grant, Lee, Sherman, Jackson) who later fought the American Civil War, and the new territories' status reignited the slavery debate that led to that conflict.
