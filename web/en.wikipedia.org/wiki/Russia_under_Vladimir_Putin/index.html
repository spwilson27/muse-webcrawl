Russia under Vladimir Putin covers the period from Putin's rise to power — appointed prime minister in August 1999 and acting president on 31 December 1999, when Boris Yeltsin resigned — to the present. During this era Russia's political system, commonly labelled "Putinism", has been characterised by the centralisation of power in the presidency, the subordination of independent media and regional governments, the dominant role of the security services and state-aligned business elites, and an increasingly confrontational foreign policy toward the West.

Consolidation of power

Putin rose to prominence during the Second Chechen War and won the 2000 presidential election promising stability after the turmoil of the 1990s. His first presidency (2000–2008) combined high oil prices and rapid economic growth with the taming of the oligarchs — exemplified by the prosecution of Mikhail Khodorkovsky and the dismantling of the Yukos oil company — the reassertion of federal control over the regions, and the takeover or closure of independent national television. Critics describe the resulting system as a "managed democracy" or electoral authoritarianism; supporters credit Putin with restoring state capacity, raising living standards, and paying off Russia's foreign debt.

The Medvedev interlude and return

Barred by the constitution from a third consecutive term, Putin became prime minister in 2008 under President Dmitry Medvedev. His announcement in 2011 that he would return to the presidency triggered the largest protests of his rule (2011–2013), which were followed by laws restricting demonstrations, NGOs ("foreign agents"), and online speech. Putin won re-election in 2012 and again in 2018; constitutional amendments adopted in 2020 "reset" his term count, potentially allowing him to remain president until 2036. He was re-elected in 2024 in a vote widely criticised abroad.

Foreign policy

Relations with the West deteriorated over NATO enlargement, the 2008 war with Georgia, the 2014 annexation of Crimea and war in the Donbas following Ukraine's Euromaidan revolution, military intervention in Syria from 2015, election-interference allegations, and the poisoning cases of Sergei Skripal and Alexei Navalny. In February 2022 Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine, provoking sweeping Western sanctions, Russia's partial economic reorientation toward Asia, the accession of Finland and Sweden to NATO, and the deadliest war in Europe since 1945. Domestically, the war brought intensified censorship, criminalisation of anti-war speech, and the elimination of remaining independent media.

Opposition and repression

Independent opposition has been progressively marginalised through restrictive election laws, prosecution, and violence. Opposition leader Boris Nemtsov was assassinated in 2015; Alexei Navalny, the most prominent opposition figure of the 2010s, was poisoned in 2020, imprisoned on his return to Russia, and died in an Arctic penal colony in February 2024.
