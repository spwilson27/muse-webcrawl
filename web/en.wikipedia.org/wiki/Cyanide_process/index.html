Cyanide process


Cyanide heap leaching at a gold mine (placeholder image)

The cyanide process, also called cyanidation or the MacArthur–Forrest process, is a hydrometallurgical technique for extracting gold (and silver) from low-grade ore by converting the metal into a water-soluble complex with cyanide. Patented in 1887 by John Stewart MacArthur and the brothers Robert and William Forrest, it remains the dominant method of gold extraction worldwide.

Process

Crushed ore is treated with a dilute solution of sodium or potassium cyanide in the presence of oxygen, which dissolves gold as the dicyanoaurate complex (Elsner's equation). The gold is then recovered from solution by zinc cementation (the Merrill–Crowe process) or by adsorption onto activated carbon (carbon-in-pulp / carbon-in-leach), followed by electrowinning and refining.

Environmental issues

Because cyanide is highly toxic, the process is controversial. Tailings dam failures such as the Baia Mare spill in Romania in 2000 caused major river pollution, leading some jurisdictions to ban cyanide mining. Modern operations use containment, detoxification of effluent, and monitoring, while alternative lixiviants such as thiosulfate remain niche.
