Transport network
ElementsNodes (terminals, stations, junctions) and links (roads, rails, routes)
Studied byTransport geography, network theory, transport planning
TopologiesGrid, radial, hub-and-spoke, linear

A transport network, or transportation network, is a system of routes connecting a set of locations. In graph-theoretic terms it consists of nodes — terminals, stations, ports, intersections — joined by links such as roads, railway lines, air routes or shipping lanes, over which people and goods move. Spatial networks like these are a central object of transport geography and planning.

Structure and analysis

Analysts describe networks by their topology (grid, radial, hub-and-spoke), connectivity, centrality of nodes, and capacity and flow on links. Models derived from network theory — including shortest-path algorithms such as Dijkstra's, traffic assignment models, and measures like betweenness centrality — are used to predict congestion, design transit systems, and assess resilience to failures. Real networks show striking regularities, and concepts such as Braess's paradox show that adding capacity can worsen overall performance.

Examples

Familiar transport networks range from city street systems and metro maps (such as transport in London) to national motorway and rail systems, airline route networks organised around hubs, pipeline networks and the global shipping network. Integrated regional bodies such as Transport for Greater Manchester plan such networks across modes. Digital mapping and real-time data have made network analysis a routine part of operations and of traveller information services.
