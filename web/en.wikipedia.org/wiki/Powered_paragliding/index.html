Powered paragliding

Powered paragliding, also known as paramotoring or PPG, is a form of ultralight aviation where the pilot wears a back-pack motor (a paramotor) which provides enough thrust to take off using a paraglider. It can be launched in still air, and on level ground, by the pilot alone — no assistance is required.

Description

In most countries, including the United States, paramotoring is minimally regulated and requires no licence; in the US it falls under Federal Aviation Regulation Part 103 for ultralight vehicles. The aircraft consists of three main components: a paraglider wing, a harness, and the paramotor unit comprising a lightweight two-stroke (or occasionally electric) engine, propeller in a protective hoop, fuel tank, and frame.

Flight characteristics

Paramotors typically cruise at 25–40 mph (40–65 km/h) and can reach altitudes of several thousand feet; flights of two to three hours are common on a single tank of fuel. Takeoff requires only a short run in light winds. Pilots steer by weight shift and by pulling brake toggles that deform the trailing edge of the wing; throttle controls climb and descent.

Wheeled variants

Adding a wheeled cart or trike to the motor unit creates a powered paraglider trike, allowing pilots who cannot foot-launch (or who prefer not to) to fly with the same wing. Two-seat trikes are used for tandem instruction and recreational flights where regulations permit.

Safety and training

Although formal licensing is absent in many jurisdictions, structured training through schools and associations (such as the US Powered Paragliding Association) is strongly recommended. Principal hazards include flying in turbulent midday air, line twists, engine failures (mitigated by the ability to glide and land unpowered), and contact with power lines.

Uses

Beyond recreation, paramotors are used for aerial photography, cinematography, conservation surveys, and by some military and police units for quiet, low-cost reconnaissance.
