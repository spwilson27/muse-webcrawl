Coal gas

Coal gas is a flammable gaseous fuel made from coal and supplied to the user via a piped distribution system. It is produced when coal is heated strongly in the absence of air, a process called destructive distillation or pyrolysis. Coal gas is a mixture consisting primarily of hydrogen, methane, and carbon monoxide, together with smaller amounts of other hydrocarbons and impurities. When distributed for domestic and street lighting it was commonly called town gas.

History

The Scottish engineer William Murdoch lit his house and office in Redruth, Cornwall, with coal gas in 1792, and by 1807 Pall Mall in London became the first street lit by gas. Municipal gasworks spread rapidly through the industrial cities of Britain, Europe, and North America during the nineteenth century, supplying street lamps, factories, and eventually domestic lighting, cooking, and heating.

Production

In a gasworks, coal was heated in sealed cast-iron or fireclay retorts to temperatures around 1,100 °C. The volatile products were drawn off, condensed to remove coal tar and ammonia liquor, and purified to remove hydrogen sulfide before storage in large telescoping gas holders (gasometers). The solid residue, coke, was sold as a smokeless fuel.

Toxicity

The carbon monoxide content of town gas made it highly poisonous; leaks and deliberate inhalation were common causes of death until the mid-twentieth century. In Britain, the conversion to non-toxic North Sea natural gas between 1967 and 1977 was accompanied by a marked fall in suicide by gassing.

Decline

Coal gas was progressively displaced first by electricity for lighting and later by natural gas for heating. Manufactured-gas production continued in some regions into the late twentieth century, and gasification of coal remains the basis of syngas production for the chemical industry.
