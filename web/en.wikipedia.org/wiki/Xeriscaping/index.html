Xeriscaping

Xeriscaping is the process of landscaping, or gardening, that reduces or eliminates the need for irrigation. It is promoted in regions that do not have accessible, plentiful, or reliable supplies of fresh water and has gained acceptance in other regions as access to irrigation water has become limited, though it is not limited to such climates. The term was coined in 1981 by combining the Greek prefix xero- ("dry") with -scaping as in landscaping; the Denver Water department, through its task force, popularised the concept in Colorado.

Principles

Xeriscaping is commonly taught through seven principles: planning and design; soil analysis and improvement; efficient irrigation (such as drip systems); use of mulch; limitation of high-water turf areas; selection of drought-tolerant and climate-appropriate plants grouped by water needs ("hydrozoning"); and appropriate maintenance. Contrary to the caricature of a yard of only gravel and cactus, xeriscaped gardens can be lush, relying on plants such as lavender, sage, agave, yucca, and ornamental grasses alongside ground covers and shade trees.

Benefits and adoption

Well-designed xeriscapes can cut outdoor water use dramatically (studies and water utilities cite savings of 50% or more versus conventional turf landscaping), reduce mowing and fertilising, and provide habitat for pollinators. Cities in the arid American Southwest, including Las Vegas and Phoenix, have adopted turf-removal incentive programmes, and xeriscape principles are increasingly written into water-conservation ordinances.
