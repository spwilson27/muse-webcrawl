Well-quasi-ordering


A well-quasi-order contains no bad sequences: every infinite sequence has an increasing pair.

In mathematics, specifically order theory, a well-quasi-ordering (wqo) is a quasi-ordering (a reflexive, transitive binary relation) that is well-founded and has no infinite antichain. Equivalently, a quasi-order is a well-quasi-order if every infinite sequence of elements contains an increasing pair — two indices i < j with the earlier element less than or equal to the later one. A sequence with no such pair is called a bad sequence, so a wqo is an order with no bad sequences.

Examples and theorems

The natural numbers with their usual ordering form a well-quasi-order, and by Dickson's lemma so does any finite product of them. Higman's lemma states that finite sequences over a well-quasi-ordered alphabet, ordered by embedding, form a well-quasi-order. The celebrated Robertson–Seymour theorem (the graph minor theorem) implies that finite graphs under the minor relation are well-quasi-ordered, and Kruskal's tree theorem gives the analogous result for trees under homeomorphic embedding.

Motivation

Well-quasi-orders are used to prove termination and decidability results: if the states of a system form a wqo and transitions respect the order, certain infinite behaviours are excluded. Applications include the theory of well-structured transition systems in computer science, combinatorics, and logic, where wqos characterise the absence of infinite antichains in ideals and upward-closed sets.
