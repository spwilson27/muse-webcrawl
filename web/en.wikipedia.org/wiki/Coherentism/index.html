Coherentism is a family of theories in epistemology according to which beliefs are justified by their coherence with a system of other beliefs, rather than by resting on self-evident foundations. In its epistemic form it answers the regress problem of justification by denying that justification has a linear or hierarchical structure: a belief is justified when it belongs to a coherent system, and the system as a whole carries the justification.

[Image: web of belief diagram]

The coherence theory of justification

Coherentists hold that justification is a matter of mutual support among beliefs — how well they fit together, explain one another, and form a comprehensive, consistent whole. Otto Neurath captured the picture with the image of sailors rebuilding their ship at sea, plank by plank, with no dry dock of indubitable foundations. W. V. O. Quine developed a related holism in which our knowledge forms a "web of belief" that faces experience only as a corporate body.

The coherence theory of truth

A distinct, stronger doctrine defines truth itself as coherence within a specified system of propositions. Versions were defended by idealist philosophers such as F. H. Bradley and Brand Blanshard, for whom reality is a single systematic whole and truth comes in degrees. Most contemporary coherentists are coherentists about justification only, and remain realists about truth.

Objections

The best-known objection is the isolation or input problem: a belief system could be perfectly coherent yet completely detached from the world, as a consistent fairy tale is coherent without being true. Critics also raise the problem of alternative, mutually incompatible but internally coherent systems, and ask whether coherence alone can privilege one. Coherentists reply by emphasizing explanatory integration, conservatism, and the role of spontaneous perceptual beliefs that a coherent system must accommodate.
