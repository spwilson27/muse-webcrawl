Étienne Bonnot de Condillac (30 September 1714 – 2 August 1780) was a French philosopher, priest, and economist who developed an empiricist theory of knowledge derived from John Locke and known as sensationalism: the doctrine that all knowledge and all mental faculties arise from the senses.

Life

Condillac was born in Grenoble and studied for the priesthood in Paris, where he frequented the salons of the Enlightenment and became a friend of Denis Diderot and Jean-Jacques Rousseau. He was elected to the Académie française in 1768. From 1758 to 1767 he served in Italy as tutor to Prince Ferdinand of Parma, grandson of Louis XV, for whom he wrote his extensive Cours d'études. He spent his final years in retirement at his estate at Flux, near Beaugency, where he died.

Philosophy

His Essai sur l'origine des connaissances humaines (1746) extended Locke's empiricism, while the Traité des sensations (1754) presented his famous thought experiment of a statue endowed successively with the senses: beginning with smell alone, Condillac argued that attention, memory, comparison, judgment, and even the sense of self could all be generated from sensation without innate ideas. He gave language a central role in thought, holding that the use of signs is what transforms sensation into controlled reflection — a view developed in his writings on grammar and logic, including La Logique (1780). His work strongly influenced the French idéologues and was widely read in Europe.

Economics

In Le Commerce et le gouvernement (1776), published the same year as Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations, Condillac analysed value in terms of utility and scarcity, anticipating elements of later subjective value theory.
