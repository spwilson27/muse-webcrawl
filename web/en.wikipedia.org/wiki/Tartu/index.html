Tartu is the second largest city in Estonia after Tallinn, the country's capital. Situated on the Emajõgi River, which connects Lake Võrtsjärv and Lake Peipus, Tartu is widely considered Estonia's intellectual capital, home to the University of Tartu, the nation's oldest and most renowned university, founded in 1632.

History

The settlement is first mentioned in 1030, when Yaroslav the Wise of Kievan Rus' founded a fort named Yuryev on the site of the ancient Estonian stronghold of Tarbatu. Tartu joined the Hanseatic League in the 1280s under the German name Dorpat and became an important trading post between Western Europe and Russia. The city changed hands repeatedly during the Livonian War, the Polish–Swedish wars and the Great Northern War, when much of it was destroyed by fire and its population deported.

Rebuilt largely in classical style in the late 18th and early 19th centuries, Tartu became the centre of the Estonian national awakening in the 19th century: the first Estonian newspaper, the first song festival (1869) and the first professional Estonian theatre all originated here. Under the Treaty of Tartu, signed on 2 February 1920, Soviet Russia recognised Estonian independence.

The university city

The University of Tartu, founded by King Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden in 1632, dominates the city's cultural and economic life; students make up a large share of the population. Tartu also hosts the Estonian National Museum, the Supreme Court of Estonia, and the headquarters of several technology companies, earning it a reputation as a hub of Estonian start-up culture.

Landmarks

Notable sights include the ruins of the medieval cathedral on Toomemägi (Cathedral Hill), the Town Hall Square with its Kissing Students fountain, the leaning "Tartu Pisa" building, the botanical garden, and the AHHAA Science Centre. Tartu was a European Capital of Culture in 2024.
