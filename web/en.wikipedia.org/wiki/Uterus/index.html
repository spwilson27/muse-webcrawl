Uterus

The uterus (from Latin uterus, plural uteri) or womb is the organ in the reproductive system of most female mammals, including humans, that accommodates the embryonic and fetal development of one or more embryos until birth. The uterus is a hormone-responsive sex organ that contains glands in its lining that secrete uterine milk for embryonic nourishment.

Structure

The human uterus is pear-shaped and about 7.6 cm (3 in) long, 4.5 cm broad, and 3.0 cm thick in a non-pregnant adult. It is divided into the fundus (the rounded top), the body, and the cervix, which projects into the vagina. The fallopian tubes open into its upper corners.

Layers

The uterine wall has three layers: the endometrium, the inner glandular lining that thickens and sheds during the menstrual cycle; the myometrium, a thick layer of smooth muscle whose contractions expel the fetus during labour; and the perimetrium, the outer serous coat.

Function

After ovulation, a fertilized egg implants in the endometrium. The uterus then protects and nourishes the developing embryo and fetus, its wall forming part of the placenta's maternal side, and expands enormously during pregnancy from about 70 g to over 1 kg.

Clinical significance

Conditions of the uterus include uterine fibroids (leiomyomas), adenomyosis, endometrial cancer, prolapse, and congenital malformations such as a bicornuate uterus. Surgical removal of the uterus is a hysterectomy.
