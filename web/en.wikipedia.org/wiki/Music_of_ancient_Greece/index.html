Music of ancient Greece

Ancient Greek music was a central part of Greek culture, present in religious ritual, theatre, education, athletics, and daily life. The very word music derives from the Muses, the daughters of Zeus who presided over the arts. Greek theory of consonance, modes, and notation profoundly influenced later Western music.

Depiction of an aulos player on ancient Greek pottery.

Instruments

The principal stringed instruments were the lyre, played by amateurs and associated with Apollo and education, and the larger, more virtuosic kithara, played by professionals. The main wind instrument was the aulos, a double-reed pipe usually played in pairs by a single performer and associated with Dionysian ecstasy. Percussion included hand drums, cymbals (kymbala), and clappers (krotala).

Music in society

Greek drama — tragedy and comedy — was sung and danced by the chorus, and music accompanied epic recitation, sacrifices, weddings, funerals, and athletic games, which included musical contests. Plato, in the Republic, argued that musical modes affect character and should be regulated by the state; Aristotle treated music as a means of education, leisure, and emotional purgation (catharsis).

Theory

The Pythagoreans discovered that consonant intervals correspond to simple numerical ratios — the octave 2:1, the fifth 3:2, the fourth 4:3 — and linked music to the harmony of the cosmos. Theorists such as Aristoxenus systematised scales and tonoi (modes), including the Dorian, Phrygian, and Lydian, each thought to carry a distinct ethos. Greek notation used letters placed above the text, and about sixty fragments of notated music survive, including the Delphic Hymns (2nd century BCE) and the Seikilos epitaph (c. 1st–2nd century CE), the oldest surviving complete musical composition.

Legacy

Greek terminology — harmonia, melos, rhythm, chromatic, enharmonic — passed into Western music theory via Roman and medieval writers such as Boethius, shaping a millennium of musical thought.
