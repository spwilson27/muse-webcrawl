Gauss's law

FieldElectromagnetism
Formulated byCarl Friedrich Gauss (developed 1835, published 1867)
StatusOne of Maxwell's equations

Electric flux through a closed surface equals enclosed charge divided by ε₀

Gauss's law, also known as Gauss's flux theorem, is one of Maxwell's equations governing electromagnetism. It states that the total electric flux through any closed surface is proportional to the electric charge enclosed within that surface: Φ = Q/ε₀, where ε₀ is the permittivity of free space.

Formulations

In integral form, the law equates the surface integral of the electric field over a closed "Gaussian surface" with the enclosed charge divided by ε₀. Its differential form, ∇·E = ρ/ε₀, relates the divergence of the electric field at each point to the local charge density. The law is equivalent to Coulomb's law for static charges but applies generally, including to time-varying fields.

Applications

For charge distributions with planar, cylindrical, or spherical symmetry, Gauss's law gives the electric field directly and simply — for example, the field of an infinite line of charge or a uniformly charged sphere, outside of which the field is identical to that of a point charge. A magnetic analogue (Gauss's law for magnetism) states that net magnetic flux through a closed surface is zero, reflecting the absence of magnetic monopoles.
