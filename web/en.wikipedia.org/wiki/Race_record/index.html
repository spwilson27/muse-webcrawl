Race record

Race records were phonograph records recorded by and marketed to African Americans in the United States during the 1920s and 1930s. They primarily contained race music, comprising various African-American musical genres, including blues, jazz, gospel music, and comedy. These records were the first commercial recordings of African American musicians and singers, and saw the rise of artists such as Ma Rainey, Bessie Smith, Blind Lemon Jefferson, and Charley Patton.

The term was adopted by the recording industry following the commercial breakthrough of Mamie Smith's "Crazy Blues" (1920) on Okeh Records, which demonstrated a large Black record-buying audience. Labels including Okeh, Paramount, Columbia, and Vocalion maintained "race" catalogues and series. During the Great Depression, sales declined sharply, and by the 1940s the term fell out of use, replaced by "rhythm and blues" (coined at Billboard in 1949).

Significance

Race records preserved regional blues and jazz styles, created the first national Black music market, and laid the commercial and stylistic foundations for rhythm and blues and rock and roll. The marketing category also reflected the segregation of the era, with separate catalogues for Black and white ("hillbilly") artists.
