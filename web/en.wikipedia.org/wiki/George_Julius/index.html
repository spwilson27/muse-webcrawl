George Julius

Sir George Alfred Julius
Born: April 29, 1873, Norwich, England
Died: June 28, 1946, Sydney, Australia
Occupation: Engineer, inventor
Known for: Automatic totalisator

Sir George Alfred Julius (29 April 1873 – 28 June 1946) was an English-born Australian inventor and engineer best known for inventing the automatic totalisator ("the tote"), the mechanical computing machine used for pari-mutuel betting at racecourses around the world.

Life and career

Born in Norwich, Julius emigrated first to New Zealand, where he studied engineering at Canterbury University College, and then to Australia, working as chief engineer for a timber firm in Western Australia. The idea of the totalisator is said to have begun as a vote-counting machine. His first automatic totalisator was installed at Ellerslie Racecourse in Auckland in 1913; within years it was adopted at racecourses across Australia, and later in Britain, France, the United States and many other countries.

Career in Australia

Julius co-founded the Sydney engineering firm Julius, Poole & Gibson, later Automatic Totalisators Limited. During the First World War he worked on munitions and defence production. He became the first chairman of Australia's Council for Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR, now CSIRO) in 1926, serving until 1945, and was knighted in 1929 for services to industry and science. Julius also championed a uniform railway gauge for Australia and served on numerous public bodies.

Legacy

The totalisator replaced human bookmaking clerks and transformed racecourse betting by continuously computing odds from the pool of wagers. Descendants of his machines remained in service for most of the 20th century.
