Neuropathic pain
Nerve injury and abnormal pain signalling
SpecialtyNeurology
SymptomsBurning, shooting or electric-shock pain, allodynia, hyperalgesia, tingling
CausesLesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system

Neuropathic pain is pain caused by a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system, as defined by the International Association for the Study of Pain. It is distinguished from nociceptive pain, which arises from actual or threatened tissue damage activating nociceptors. Neuropathic pain affects an estimated 7–10% of the general population and is frequently described as burning, shooting or electric-shock-like, often accompanied by allodynia (pain from normally non-painful stimuli), hyperalgesia, numbness and "pins and needles."

Causes

Peripheral causes include diabetic peripheral neuropathy, postherpetic neuralgia (after shingles), chemotherapy-induced neuropathy, HIV neuropathy, trigeminal neuralgia, radiculopathy and post-surgical or traumatic nerve injury, as well as phantom limb pain. Central causes include spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis and stroke (central post-stroke pain).

Mechanisms

After nerve injury, damaged axons generate ectopic impulses; sodium channels are upregulated, central neurons in the spinal cord become sensitized and amplify incoming signals, inhibitory controls are lost, and glial cells contribute inflammatory mediators. These changes — peripheral and central sensitization — explain why pain can persist long after the initial injury heals.

Treatment

First-line pharmacotherapies recommended by guidelines (e.g., NeuPSIG) are gabapentinoids (gabapentin, pregabalin), tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline, nortriptyline) and serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors (duloxetine, venlafaxine). Topical lidocaine and high-concentration capsaicin are used for localized peripheral pain. Opioids are generally reserved because of modest efficacy and risk. Treatment of the underlying cause, physical therapy and psychological support are also important.
