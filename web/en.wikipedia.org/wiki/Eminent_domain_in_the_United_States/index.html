Eminent domain in the United States

Eminent domain in the United States refers to the power of federal, state, and local governments to take private property for public use, subject to the payment of just compensation. The power is limited by the Takings Clause of the Fifth Amendment: "nor shall private property be taken for public use, without just compensation." The clause applies to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment.

Public use

Courts have interpreted "public use" broadly. In Berman v. Parker (1954) the Supreme Court upheld urban renewal takings in Washington, D.C.; in Hawaii Housing Authority v. Midkiff (1984) it sustained a land-redistribution statute. Most controversially, in Kelo v. City of New London (2005), a 5–4 majority held that economic development qualifies as a public use, allowing the transfer of homes to a private developer. The decision provoked a broad backlash: more than 40 states enacted reforms restricting economic-development takings.

Just compensation and procedure

Just compensation is generally the fair market value of the property taken. Condemnation proceedings may be brought through formal court actions or "quick-take" procedures in which the government deposits estimated compensation and takes possession while value is litigated. Inverse condemnation suits allow owners to sue when government action effectively takes property without formal proceedings.

Uses

Eminent domain has been used for roads, schools, public buildings, utilities, pipelines, and military facilities, as well as historically for railroads and urban redevelopment projects that displaced large communities.
