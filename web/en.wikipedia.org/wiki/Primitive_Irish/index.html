Primitive Irish

Ogham stones are nearly the only surviving evidence of Primitive Irish.

Primitive Irish, also called Archaic Irish, is the oldest known form of the Goidelic languages, attested in inscriptions written in the Ogham alphabet found mainly in Ireland and western Wales, dating from roughly the 4th to the 7th or 8th centuries AD. It is the ancestor of Old Irish and, ultimately, of modern Irish, Scottish Gaelic, and Manx.

Corpus

The corpus consists of some 400 memorial inscriptions, typically formulaic: a name in the genitive case followed by terms of kinship such as MAQI (later mac, "son of") and AVI (later ua, "descendant of"). The inscriptions are extremely brief and largely onomastic, so knowledge of the language's grammar is reconstructed partly from later Old Irish and from comparison with other Celtic languages.

Features

Primitive Irish retains many archaic Indo-European features lost by Old Irish: a full system of final syllables preserving case endings (nominative, genitive, etc.), the neuter gender, and the labiovelar consonant /kʷ/ (written Q in Ogham). The drastic changes that followed — loss of final syllables (apocope), syncope of internal syllables, and lenition — transformed it into Old Irish by about the 7th century.

Examples

Inscriptions include QENUVENDAGNI MAQI... type texts and the famous bilingual stones of Wales, some accompanied by Latin versions.
