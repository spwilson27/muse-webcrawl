Armand Trousseau (14 October 1801 – 23 June 1867) was a French internist. His contributions to medicine include Trousseau's syndrome (migratory thrombophlebitis associated with visceral malignancy) and the Trousseau sign of latent tetany. He is considered a central figure in the development of clinical teaching in 19th-century Paris.

Biography

Trousseau studied medicine in Tours and Paris, receiving his doctorate in 1825. He became professor of therapeutics at the Faculty of Medicine in Paris and later professor of clinical medicine, based at the Hôtel-Dieu hospital, where his lectures — published as Clinique médicale de l'Hôtel-Dieu de Paris — were widely read and translated. He performed important work on diphtheria and popularized tracheotomy in its treatment.

Trousseau's syndrome

Trousseau described the association between recurrent migratory thrombophlebitis and occult visceral cancer, notably pancreatic cancer. Ironically, he diagnosed the same syndrome in himself in 1867 and correctly predicted his own death from gastric cancer, remarking that a new phlebitis in his left arm left him no doubt about his fate.
