Digital imaging

Digital imaging or digital image acquisition is the creation of a digital representation of the visual characteristics of an object, such as a physical scene or the interior structure of an object. The term is often assumed to imply or include the processing, compression, storage, printing and display of such images. A key advantage of a digital image, versus an analog image such as a film photograph, is the ability to digitally transfer and copy it without any degradation in quality.

History

Digital imaging was developed from the 1960s to the 1980s, largely to avoid the weaknesses of film cameras, for spy satellites, medicine, and astronomy. The first image scanner was the SEAC at the US National Bureau of Standards in 1957. In 1969 Willard Boyle and George E. Smith invented the charge-coupled device (CCD) at Bell Labs, the basis of image sensors for decades; they shared the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physics for it. Digital photography for consumers took off in the 1990s and, via camera phones, became ubiquitous in the 2000s.

Methods

Digital images are most commonly captured directly by cameras using CMOS or CCD sensors, or created by scanning photographs, documents, and film. Specialised imaging includes radiography and CT in medicine, synthetic-aperture radar and satellite remote sensing, microscopy, and photogrammetry. Once captured, images may be stored in raster formats such as JPEG, PNG and TIFF, described by metadata standards like Exif, and manipulated with image-editing software.

Applications and issues

Digital imaging underpins digital photography, cinematography, medical imaging, machine vision, and document management. Its ease of manipulation raises questions of authenticity — photo manipulation and, more recently, synthetic media — addressed in part by provenance standards such as content credentials. Mosaic and demosaicing artefacts, dynamic-range limits, and colour management are continuing technical concerns.
