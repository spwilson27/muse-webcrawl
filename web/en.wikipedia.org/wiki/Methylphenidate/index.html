Methylphenidate, sold under the brand names Ritalin and Concerta among others, is a central nervous system stimulant used to treat attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and narcolepsy. It blocks the reuptake of dopamine and norepinephrine, increasing their concentrations in the synaptic cleft.

Medical uses

Methylphenidate is a first-line treatment for ADHD in children, adolescents and adults, improving attention, impulse control and hyperactivity. Formulations range from immediate-release tablets taken two or three times daily to extended-release preparations (e.g. Concerta, using an osmotic pump) and a transdermal patch (Daytrana). It is also used for narcolepsy.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include decreased appetite, insomnia, headache, dry mouth and anxiety; growth suppression may occur in children with long-term use. It can raise heart rate and blood pressure modestly. Misuse — particularly by non-oral routes — carries dependence risk, and the drug is a controlled substance in most countries.

History

Methylphenidate was synthesised in 1944 by Leandro Panizzon of the Swiss company Ciba; he named the product Ritalin after his wife Marguerite ("Rita"), who took it before tennis matches. It was marketed from 1954 and became widely prescribed for ADHD from the 1960s onward, especially in the United States. The related compound dexmethylphenidate (Focalin) contains only the more active d-enantiomer.
