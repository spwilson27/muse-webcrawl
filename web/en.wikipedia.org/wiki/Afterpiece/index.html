An afterpiece was a short play or entertainment performed at the end of an evening's theatrical programme in the 18th and 19th centuries, following the mainpiece — the principal, usually five-act, play. Audiences paid half price to enter after the mainpiece's third act, and the afterpiece was designed as light relief: a farce, pantomime, burletta or short comic opera, typically in one or two acts.

Function and conventions

London's patent theatres competed partly through their afterpieces, which were advertised prominently on playbills. Because they were cheap to stage and could be swapped quickly, afterpieces were a testing ground for popular taste: a successful afterpiece might run for dozens of nights while mainpieces rotated nightly. Writers such as Samuel Foote, Isaac Bickerstaffe and Charles Dibdin built careers on the form, and ballad operas and pantomimes often began life as afterpieces.

Decline

The custom faded in the mid-19th century as evening programmes shortened, the half-price system declined, and the one-set drawing-room drama and long-running plays changed theatre economics. The curtain raiser — a short piece before the main play — inherited much of the afterpiece's role into the early 20th century.
