Polyketide

Polyketides include antibiotics such as erythromycin and the statin lovastatin

Polyketides are a large and structurally diverse class of natural products produced by bacteria, fungi, and plants. They are biosynthesised by enzymes called polyketide synthases, which assemble them from simple acyl building blocks—usually acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA—through repeated decarboxylative Claisen condensations, in a process closely related to fatty acid synthesis. The name refers to the poly-β-keto intermediates formed during chain assembly, which are then cyclised, reduced, and otherwise modified into the final products.

Polyketide synthases

Polyketide synthases are classified into three main types. Type I synthases are large multifunctional proteins; in their modular form, each module of the enzyme carries out one round of chain extension, as in the synthesis of the antibiotic erythromycin by 6-deoxyerythronolide B synthase. Type II synthases are complexes of separate enzymes acting iteratively and produce mainly aromatic compounds such as tetracycline and doxorubicin. Type III synthases are simpler enzymes, exemplified by chalcone synthase in plants, that act directly on CoA substrates to form flavonoids and other products.

Importance

Polyketides include many of the most valuable medicines derived from nature: the antibiotics erythromycin and tetracycline, the immunosuppressants rapamycin and tacrolimus, the cholesterol-lowering statin lovastatin, the anticancer drug doxorubicin, and the antiparasitic avermectins. Because the modular synthases follow a predictable assembly-line logic, researchers have sought to engineer them by swapping domains and modules to create novel "unnatural" natural products, a strategy known as combinatorial biosynthesis.
