Antinomianism

Antinomianism
EtymologyGreek anti (against) + nomos (law)
FieldChristian theology
Key controversiesAntinomian controversies in Lutheran Germany (1527–1540s); Antinomian Controversy in Massachusetts Bay (1636–1638)

Antinomianism (from the Greek ἀντί, "against", and νόμος, "law") is any view which rejects laws or legalism and argues against moral, religious or social norms. In Christian theology the term is generally a pejorative label applied to doctrines holding that, because salvation comes by faith and divine grace alone, Christians are freed from the obligation to obey the moral law — a charge raised by opponents who feared such teaching would licence immoral conduct. Few historical figures have described their own teaching as antinomian.

History

Elements of the debate are as old as the New Testament epistles, in which Paul insists both that believers are "not under law but under grace" and that faith does not nullify moral obligation. During the Reformation, Martin Luther's associate Johann Agricola argued that repentance should be motivated by the gospel rather than by the law, provoking the Antinomian controversies within Lutheranism (c. 1527–1540); Luther responded with the disputation Against the Antinomians (1539). In Puritan New England, the Antinomian Controversy of 1636–1638 in the Massachusetts Bay Colony centred on Anne Hutchinson and John Wheelwright, who were accused of teaching that the indwelling of the Holy Spirit freed believers from the moral law as a covenant of works; Hutchinson was tried and banished.

Related concepts

Antinomianism is contrasted with legalism, and in Reformation theology is often paired with the doctrine of justification by faith alone. The term is also used more loosely outside theology for movements that reject inherited social or moral conventions, and analogous disputes over law and grace occur in other religious traditions, including controversies over the abrogation of the Torah in early Christianity and anti-legalist currents in some forms of Sufism and Buddhism.
