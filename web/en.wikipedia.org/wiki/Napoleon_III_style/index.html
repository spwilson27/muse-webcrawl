Napoleon III style

The Napoleon III style (French: style Napoléon III), also known as the Second Empire style, was the predominant style of architecture, furniture and decoration in France during the Second French Empire (1852–1870), the reign of Napoleon III. It was a highly eclectic style, drawing simultaneously on Gothic, Renaissance, Baroque, Louis XV and Louis XVI precedents, and it was closely associated with Baron Haussmann's renovation of Paris.

Architecture

In architecture the style is exemplified by the Palais Garnier opera house (begun 1861), the completed Louvre Palace additions, and the uniform limestone apartment blocks along Haussmann's new boulevards — typically five to seven storeys with mansard roofs, continuous balconies and richly modelled stone facades. The mansard roof became a signature of the style and spread internationally.

Furniture and decoration

Furniture in the Napoleon III style favoured comfort and opulence: deeply buttoned upholstery, the low, armless crapaud armchair, the borne (circular settee) and the indiscret, a three-seat arrangement for conversation. Dark woods such as ebony and rosewood were combined with gilt bronze, mother-of-pearl inlay, porcelain plaques and boulle marquetry revivals. Interiors were densely furnished and draped, reflecting the era's taste for display and comfort.

Influence

The style spread across Europe and the Americas, where "Second Empire" became a popular mode for public buildings and mansions in the United States. In France it was criticised by later modernists as pastiche, but it was reassessed in the 20th century as an exuberant expression of industrial-age prosperity.
