Conscription Crisis of 1944

The Conscription Crisis of 1944 was a political and military crisis in Canada during the Second World War. It arose from disagreements within the federal government over the deployment of conscripts — men mobilized for home defence under the National Resources Mobilization Act (NRMA) — for overseas service. Prime Minister William Lyon Mackenzie King, who had promised in the 1942 plebiscite to seek release from his earlier pledge against overseas conscription, resisted imposing it until late in the war, when infantry reinforcement shortages in Europe forced his hand.

Background

In the Conscription Crisis of 1917, the imposition of compulsory overseas service had split the country along linguistic lines, with French Canadians strongly opposed. Determined to avoid a repeat, Mackenzie King adopted the policy of "conscription if necessary, but not necessarily conscription." The National Resources Mobilization Act of 1940 allowed conscription for the defence of Canada only. In the plebiscite of 27 April 1942, a majority of Canadians voted to release the government from its anti-conscription pledge, although a majority of French Canadians voted against.

The reinforcement crisis

By the autumn of 1944, heavy casualties in the Italian campaign and the Battle of Normandy had depleted the pool of volunteer infantry reinforcements. The Minister of National Defence, Colonel J. L. Ralston, who had inspected the front, concluded that NRMA conscripts had to be sent overseas. King, fearing Ralston was manoeuvring to replace him, dismissed him on 1 November 1944 and appointed General A. G. L. McNaughton, who believed volunteers could be induced to come forward. When McNaughton's recruiting efforts failed, King reversed course: on 22 November 1944 he announced that 16,000 NRMA men would be sent overseas.

Reactions and unrest

The decision provoked protests in Quebec and, in a handful of cases, disturbances at camps in British Columbia, including the Terrace mutiny of November 1944, in which NRMA soldiers protested their overseas posting. Because few of the conscripts reached operational units before the war ended in May 1945, the military effect of the decision was limited: of the 16,000 men ordered overseas, only a few thousand arrived at the front, and 69 were killed.

Legacy

King's manoeuvring preserved national unity and kept his government in power through the 1945 federal election, though the Liberals lost seats in Quebec. The crisis remains the classic case study of Canada's "limited liability" approach to wartime manpower and of King's political style, summarized in his diary concern to maintain "conscription if necessary, but not necessarily conscription."
