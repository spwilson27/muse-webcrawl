Prosthetic makeup

Prosthetic makeup, also known as special makeup effects or FX prosthetics, is the process of using prosthetic sculpting, moulding and casting techniques combined with cosmetics to create advanced cosmetic effects for film, television, theatre, cosplay and medical simulation. Prosthetics allow performers to be transformed into creatures, aged characters, or people with injuries and deformities far beyond what ordinary makeup can achieve.

Process

The traditional workflow begins with a lifecast of the actor's face or body part, from which a positive core is made. The artist sculpts the desired forms in clay over the core; the sculpture is then moulded, and prosthetic appliances are cast in the mould in materials such as foam latex, gelatin, or silicone (often "platinum silicone" encapsulated in a thin plastic film for blending). The finished appliances are glued to the actor's skin with medical or spirit-gum adhesives, their edges blended away, and the whole is painted with alcohol- or grease-based colours to match skin or the creature design. Complex makeups may require hours in the makeup chair each day of filming; Gary Oldman's transformation into Winston Churchill for Darkest Hour (2017) is a celebrated modern example.

History

Early film makeup relied on the actor-artist's own ingenuity: Lon Chaney, "the Man of a Thousand Faces", built his own prosthetic transformations for The Hunchback of Notre Dame (1923) and The Phantom of the Opera (1925). Jack Pierce created the classic Universal Monsters makeups (Frankenstein's monster, the Mummy) in the 1930s. The craft was revolutionised by foam latex (pioneered by John Chambers, honoured for Planet of the Apes, 1968), by Dick Smith's modular old-age makeups (Little Big Man, The Exorcist), and by the animatronic and creature work of Rick Baker (Academy Award winner for An American Werewolf in London), Stan Winston and Rob Bottin. Since the 2000s, silicone gel-filled appliances have largely supplanted foam latex for realism, while digital scanning and 3D printing are increasingly used for lifecasting and mould-making; CGI now complements or replaces prosthetics for many effects, though directors and performers often prefer the tactility of practical makeup.

Recognition

The Academy Award for Best Makeup and Hairstyling (instituted 1981, with Rick Baker its first competitive winner) honours prosthetic achievements; television and stage have parallel awards. The field supports a specialised industry of sculptors, mould makers, painters and applicators.
