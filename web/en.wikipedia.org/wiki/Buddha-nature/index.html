Buddha-nature

Buddha-nature
Sanskritbuddhadhātu; tathāgatagarbha ("Buddha-womb/embryo")
TraditionMahayana Buddhism
Key textsTathāgatagarbha Sūtra, Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra, Ratnagotravibhāga

In Mahayana Buddhism, the term Buddha-nature (Sanskrit: buddhadhātu, "Buddha-element"; Chinese: 佛性 fóxìng; Japanese: 仏性 busshō) refers to the inherent potential of all sentient beings to attain Buddhahood. Closely related is the concept of tathāgatagarbha — the "womb" or "embryo" of the Tathāgata (Buddha) present within every being, taught in a family of scriptures (the tathāgatagarbha sūtras) from about the 3rd century CE onward. The Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra declares that all beings possess the Buddha-nature, while famously debating the case of the icchantika, the spiritually deluded.

Interpretations

Interpretations vary widely. Some schools treat Buddha-nature as a positive, luminous essence temporarily obscured by adventitious defilements — like the sun behind clouds — which grounded the influential East Asian doctrine that enlightenment is original and innate. Others, especially in the Madhyamaka tradition, interpret it as a skilful teaching device (upāya) equivalent to emptiness (śūnyatā) and the potential for awakening, not a substantial self — since positing an eternal essence risks conflict with the doctrine of anattā (no-self). The Tibetan gzhan stong ("other-emptiness") school, by contrast, affirmed it as the truly existent ultimate nature of mind.

Influence

Buddha-nature thought profoundly shaped Chinese, Korean, Japanese and Tibetan Buddhism, underpinning the Chan/Zen claim that one can "see one's nature and become a Buddha", and the modern "Critical Buddhism" controversy in Japan over whether tathāgatagarbha thought is compatible with core Buddhist doctrine.
