Scythia (Greek: Σκυθική, Skythikē) is the name given in Greek and Latin sources to the region of the Eurasian steppe north of the Black Sea and the Caucasus, inhabited from antiquity by the nomadic Iranian peoples collectively called the Scythians. At its greatest extent, Scythian culture stretched from the Danube to the borders of China; in the narrower sense, Scythia proper lay in what is now Ukraine and southern Russia, between the Danube and the Don.

History

The Scythians emerged from Central Asia in the early 1st millennium BC, displacing the Cimmerians. In the 7th century BC Scythian bands campaigned as far as the Near East, and Herodotus reports that they dominated parts of Media for a generation. The Royal Scythians resisted the invasion of Darius I of Persia (c. 513 BC) by scorched-earth retreat across the steppe. Scythian power centred on the rich agricultural and trading zone along the Dnieper and Bug rivers, with Greek colonies such as Olbia mediating trade in grain, slaves and gold. From the 3rd century BC the related Sarmatians pressed in from the east and largely displaced the Scythians, though a late Scythian kingdom survived in Crimea with its capital at Scythian Neapolis until the Gothic invasions of the 3rd century AD.

Culture

Herodotus's fourth book of the Histories is the principal literary source: it describes Scythian burial customs (great kurgans with sacrificed attendants and horses), warfare by mounted archers, scalping of enemies, cannabis vapour baths, and worship of deities including Tabiti and Papaios. Archaeology confirms the spectacular gold work of the kurgans — the "Scythian triad" of weapons, horse gear and animal-style art — most famously in the gold pectoral from Tovsta Mohyla.

Legacy

The Scythian languages belonged to the Eastern Iranian family; their only modern descendant is Ossetian, spoken in the Caucasus. "Scythian" became a generic classical term for steppe nomads, and Scythia a shifting geographical label well into the medieval period.
