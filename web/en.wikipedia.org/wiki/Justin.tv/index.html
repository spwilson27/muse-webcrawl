Justin.tv was a website created by Justin Kan, Emmett Shear, Michael Seibel, and Kyle Vogt in 2007 that allowed anyone to broadcast live video online. It began as a single channel in which Kan wore a camera on his cap and streamed his life around the clock — an early example of "lifecasting" — and grew into a general-purpose platform hosting thousands of live channels across categories such as sports, entertainment, gaming, and news. Its gaming section was spun off in 2011 as Twitch, which rapidly outgrew the parent site; Justin.tv was shut down on 5 August 2014 as the company renamed itself Twitch Interactive and focused entirely on gaming. History

Justin Kan and Emmett Shear, friends from Yale, initially worked on a calendar application before pivoting, with encouragement from Y Combinator's Paul Graham, to a live video experiment. On 19 March 2007 Kan began transmitting his entire daily life via a webcam mounted on a baseball cap, a mobile broadcasting rig, and a laptop in a backpack. The stunt attracted wide media attention and imitators, and in October 2007 the site opened broadcasting to the public.

The founders also launched the Justin.tv API and mobile applications, incubated what became the clip-captioning service Socialcam, and in June 2011 launched TwitchTV for video game streaming and esports. By 2013 Twitch accounted for the bulk of the company's traffic and revenue growth. In February 2014 Justin.tv, Inc. renamed itself Twitch Interactive, and the original general-interest site closed that August, days before Amazon acquired Twitch for approximately US$970 million.

Technology and growth

Justin.tv pioneered low-latency Flash-based video streaming at mass scale and handled more than a thousand simultaneous live channels at its peak, reporting tens of millions of monthly visitors by the end of the decade. Live chat accompanied each channel, a design carried over wholesale to Twitch.

Controversies

The site struggled with unauthorised streams of copyrighted sports and television content, attracting lawsuits and congressional scrutiny, and with occasionally dangerous or disturbing lifecast content broadcast live. Its moderation and rights-enforcement tools developed in response became part of the foundation of Twitch's later systems.

Closure and legacy

When Justin.tv closed in August 2014, users were directed to Twitch and to successor services for non-gaming content. The project is generally credited with popularising the concept of live online broadcasting and demonstrating the audience for always-on personal video a decade before livestreaming became a mainstream format on social platforms.
