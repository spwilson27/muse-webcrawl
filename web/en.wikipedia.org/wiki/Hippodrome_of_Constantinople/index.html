Hippodrome of Constantinople

The Hippodrome of Constantinople (Greek: Ἱππόδρομος τῆς Κωνσταντινουπόλεως) was a circus that served as the sporting and social centre of Constantinople, capital of the Byzantine Empire. Begun under Septimius Severus and completed and enlarged by Constantine the Great in the 4th century AD, it stood beside the Great Palace and could hold tens of thousands of spectators for chariot races.

Chariot racing and the factions

Races were run by factions identified by colour — principally the Blues and the Greens — whose supporters' rivalries spilled into politics and religion. The emperor presided from the imperial box (kathisma), connected to the palace, and the Hippodrome was the setting for imperial ceremonies, acclamations and popular uprisings, most notoriously the Nika riots of 532, in which much of the city was destroyed and tens of thousands died.

Monuments

The central barrier (spina) was decorated with monuments, several of which survive in what is now Sultanahmet Square in Istanbul: the Egyptian Obelisk of Thutmose III (re-erected by Theodosius I in 390), the bronze Serpent Column from Delphi, and the Walled Obelisk. The four bronze horses of the quadriga on the imperial box were looted during the Fourth Crusade in 1204 and taken to St Mark's Basilica in Venice. The Hippodrome declined after 1204 and was progressively built over in the Ottoman period.
