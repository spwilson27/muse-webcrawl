In meteorology, a cyclone is a large air mass that rotates around a strong centre of low atmospheric pressure. Cyclones are characterized by inward-spiraling winds that rotate counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere, a consequence of the Coriolis effect. Large low-pressure systems include polar vortices, extratropical cyclones, and the smaller tropical cyclones.

Types

  • Tropical cyclones form over warm ocean waters and derive energy from latent heat released by condensing moist air; when they reach sustained winds of 119 km/h they are called hurricanes, typhoons, or severe tropical cyclones depending on the basin.
  • Extratropical cyclones form along weather fronts in mid-latitudes and drive much of the day-to-day weather there.
  • Mesocyclones are rotating updrafts within severe thunderstorms that can spawn tornadoes.
  • Polar lows and polar vortices affect high-latitude regions.

Formation and effects

Cyclogenesis — the development of a cyclone — occurs where there are strong temperature gradients, upper-level divergence, and favourable wind shear. Cyclones bring clouds, precipitation, and often destructive winds, storm surge, and flooding; tropical cyclones such as Cyclone Yasi rank among the most damaging natural disasters. The opposite of a cyclone is an anticyclone, a high-pressure system with outward-spiraling winds.
