Hydrodynamic drive

A hydrodynamic drive or hydraulic drive is a system that uses fluid under moving pressure to transmit mechanical power between a prime mover and a driven machine. Unlike a hydrostatic drive, which relies on static pressure and positive-displacement pumps and motors, a hydrodynamic drive transmits power through the kinetic energy (momentum change) of a working fluid, typically oil. The two fundamental devices are the fluid coupling, which transmits torque at nearly equal speed, and the torque converter, which multiplies torque at low output speeds.

Principle

A pump impeller driven by the input shaft flings oil outward against the blades of an output turbine. The oil's change in angular momentum produces torque on the turbine. In a fluid coupling, output torque equals input torque and efficiency peaks when slip is small. Adding a stationary stator between turbine and pump, as in the torque converter, redirects the returning oil so that torque is multiplied — by a factor of two or more at stall — which is why torque converters suit automatic transmissions in automobiles.

Applications

Hydrodynamic drives are used in automotive automatic gearboxes, diesel locomotives (hydraulic transmissions by firms such as Voith), marine propulsion, industrial machinery drives, and start-up couplings for conveyors, fans, and crushers, where they provide smooth starting, overload protection, and torsional vibration damping.
