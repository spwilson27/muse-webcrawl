Owain Glyndŵr (pronounced [ˈoʊain ɡlɨ̞nˈduːr]; c. 1359 – c. 1415), also known as Owain ap Gruffydd, was a Welsh leader and the last native Welshman to hold the title Prince of Wales (Tywysog Cymru). He instigated a fierce and long-running yet ultimately unsuccessful revolt against English rule of Wales, which began in 1400.

Early life

Glyndŵr was a member of the Anglo-Welsh gentry, descended from the princes of Powys and Deheubarth. He studied law at the Inns of Court in London and may have served in the English military in Scotland and France before returning to Wales and marrying Margaret Hanmer.

The revolt

A land dispute with his neighbour, Baron Grey de Ruthyn, and the English government's dismissive response pushed Glyndŵr to revolt. On 16 September 1400 he was proclaimed Prince of Wales by his supporters at Glyndyfrdwy. His forces won victories at Hyddgen (1401) and Pilleth (1402), temporarily captured castles including Harlech and Aberystwyth, and held Welsh parliaments at Machynlleth and Harlech. He formed the Tripartite Indenture of 1405 with Edmund Mortimer and Henry Percy, Earl of Northumberland, proposing to divide England and Wales between them.

Decline and legacy

English counter-offensives under Henry IV and the future Henry V gradually retook Wales; Harlech fell in 1409. Glyndŵr refused pardons and was never captured; his fate after about 1412 is unknown, and several burial places are claimed. He remains a folk hero and an icon of Welsh nationalism.
