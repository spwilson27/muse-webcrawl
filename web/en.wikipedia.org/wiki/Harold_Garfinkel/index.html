Harold Garfinkel

Harold Garfinkel (October 29, 1917 – April 21, 2011) was an American sociologist and ethnomethodologist who taught at the University of California, Los Angeles. Having developed and established ethnomethodology as a field of inquiry in sociology, he is probably best known for his classic book Studies in Ethnomethodology (1967).

Born: October 29, 1917, Newark, New Jersey, U.S.

Died: April 21, 2011 (aged 93), Los Angeles, California, U.S.

Education: Harvard University (PhD, 1952)

Known for: Ethnomethodology, breaching experiments

Institutions: UCLA

Early life and education

Garfinkel was born in Newark, New Jersey, and studied business administration at the University of Newark (now Rutgers University–Newark). He earned a master's degree in sociology at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, where his thesis examined interracial homicide, and completed his doctorate at Harvard in 1952 under the influence of Talcott Parsons, whose theory of action Garfinkel both absorbed and reacted against.

Ethnomethodology

Garfinkel coined the term ethnomethodology for the study of the methods people use to produce and recognise the ordinary orderliness of everyday life. He argued that social order is an ongoing, practical accomplishment: members of society use shared but largely tacit "background expectancies" to make sense of one another's actions, and accounts of action are reflexively part of the settings they describe.

His "breaching experiments" — in which students deliberately violated taken-for-granted norms, for example by behaving as lodgers in their own homes or insisting on clarifying trivial remarks — revealed the strenuous moral work people perform to restore a sense of normal order. Other classic studies examined jury deliberations, the work of coroners classifying suicides, and the case of "Agnes", a transsexual person, as an investigation into how gender is practically achieved.

Influence

Ethnomethodology strongly influenced conversation analysis, founded by Garfinkel's student Harvey Sacks, as well as science and technology studies and studies of work. Later books include Ethnomethodology's Program: Working Out Durkheim's Aphorism (2002). Garfinkel received the Cooley-Mead Award of the American Sociological Association in 2007.
