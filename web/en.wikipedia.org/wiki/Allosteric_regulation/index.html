Allosteric regulation

In biochemistry, allosteric regulation (or allosteric control) is the regulation of an enzyme by binding an effector molecule at a site other than the enzyme's active site — an allosteric site (from the Greek allos, "other", and stereos, "solid/shape"). The binding changes the protein's conformation and thereby increases or decreases its activity, allowing cells to tune metabolic pathways in response to signals.

Mechanism

Allosteric effectors may be activators, which stabilise the active (R, "relaxed") state, or inhibitors, which stabilise the inactive (T, "tense") state. Many allosteric enzymes are oligomers with several subunits, and binding at one subunit can affect the others — the basis of cooperativity, famously seen in hemoglobin, whose sigmoid oxygen-binding curve arises because binding of one O2 molecule raises the affinity of the remaining sites. Two classic models describe the transition: the concerted Monod–Wyman–Changeux (MWC) model and the sequential Koshland–Némethy–Filmer (KNF) model.

Examples

  • Aspartate transcarbamoylase (ATCase) in pyrimidine synthesis is inhibited by the end product CTP and activated by ATP — a textbook case of feedback inhibition.
  • Phosphofructokinase-1, the committed step of glycolysis, is activated by AMP and fructose 2,6-bisphosphate and inhibited by ATP and citrate.
  • Hemoglobin is allosterically regulated by 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate, protons, and CO2 (the Bohr effect).

Pharmacological importance

Allosteric sites on receptors and enzymes are attractive drug targets because modulators can fine-tune activity with greater subtype selectivity and a built-in ceiling effect; benzodiazepines, which act at an allosteric site on the GABAA receptor, are a classic example.
