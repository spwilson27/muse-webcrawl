Pappus (botany)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In botany, a pappus is the modified calyx of a floret in plants of the family Asteraceae (the daisy or sunflower family). It typically forms a tuft of hairs, bristles, awns, or scales crowning the ovary and persisting on the ripe fruit (a cypsela), where it commonly aids dispersal by wind, functioning like a small parachute. The name comes from the Ancient Greek pappos, "grandfather", in allusion to white hair.

Forms

The pappus varies widely in form among Asteraceae genera, and the differences are important in identification and classification. It may consist of:

  • Fine, soft hairs (capillary bristles), smooth or feathery (plumose) — as in dandelions (Taraxacum) and thistles (Cirsium)
  • Coarse, barbed bristles that catch in fur or clothing, aiding animal dispersal — as in Bidens
  • Stiff awns, as in Helianthus
  • Scales, or a small crown or rim of tissue
  • It may be entirely absent in some genera, such as Bellis

Dispersal

The pappus increases the surface area and drag of the fruit so that it can be carried long distances by the wind (anemochory). In the dandelion, the pappus sits at the end of a slender stalk (the beak of the fruit), forming the familiar "clock" of the seed head; studies have shown that the pappus creates a distinctive vortex ring of air above it that enhances lift and keeps the seed airborne longer. Barbed pappus bristles instead attach fruits to passing animals (epizoochory).

Terminology

Because the florets of Asteraceae have an inferior ovary and the calyx sits atop it, the pappus crowns the fruit. Individual elements are sometimes called pappus bristles, pappus hairs, or pappus scales. In some related families, similar dispersal structures occur, but the term pappus is used principally for Asteraceae.
