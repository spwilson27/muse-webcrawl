Somali famine

Somalia has suffered repeated famines driven by drought combined with armed conflict and the collapse of the state. The most severe modern episodes occurred in 1991–1992, during the civil war that followed the fall of Siad Barre, and in 2011, and the country again approached famine during the drought of 2016–2017 and the record drought of 2020–2023.

Famine of 1991–1992

As rival factions fought over Mogadishu and the fertile inter-riverine south, agriculture collapsed and food was looted or used as a weapon. An estimated 220,000–300,000 people died of starvation and related disease. Images of the famine prompted the United Nations intervention (UNOSOM I), followed by the US-led Unified Task Force (Operation Restore Hope) in December 1992, which secured relief corridors and reduced mortality, before the mission deteriorated into conflict with Somali militias in 1993.

Famine of 2011

The worst drought in the Horn of Africa in six decades, combined with the al-Shabaab insurgency's restrictions on aid in areas it controlled, led the UN to declare famine in parts of southern Somalia in July 2011 — the first formal famine declaration of the 21st century. Around 260,000 people, half of them children under five, died between October 2010 and April 2012.

Later crises

In 2017 a rapid international response and improved access are credited with averting a formally declared famine despite severe drought. From 2020, five consecutive failed rainy seasons produced Somalia's longest drought on record; although famine thresholds were narrowly not crossed at the national level in 2022, excess mortality was estimated in the tens of thousands, and food insecurity remained extreme, with millions displaced.
