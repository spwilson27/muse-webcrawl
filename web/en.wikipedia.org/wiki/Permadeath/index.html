Permadeath or permanent death is a game mechanic in which a player character that dies is permanently lost, rather than respawning or being restored from a saved game. In games with permadeath, the player must start over with a new character, losing accumulated progress, equipment and achievements associated with the deceased character.

History

Permadeath is a defining feature of the roguelike genre, beginning with Rogue (1980) and continuing through NetHack, Angband and Dungeon Crawl Stone Soup. In these games, procedural generation ensures each new run differs, softening the cost of losing a character. Early multi-user dungeons (MUDs) and massively multiplayer games debated the mechanic; Diablo II offered an optional "hardcore" mode, and MMORPGs largely abandoned strict permadeath because of the heavy time investment characters represent, though titles such as Realm of the Mad God built their whole design around it.

Design rationale and reception

Designers use permadeath to heighten tension, give decisions real consequences and make survival itself an achievement. Critics argue it can frustrate players and devalue their time. Compromise designs — often called "roguelites", as in Rogue Legacy, Dead Cells and Hades — let players retain meta-progression (unlocks, currency, story progress) between runs while each individual character's death remains permanent. Permadeath also appears as an optional challenge in strategy and simulation games, such as "Ironman" modes in Paradox grand strategy titles and the soldier loss of the XCOM series, and as a narrative device in games like Fire Emblem, where fallen units are lost for the rest of the campaign.

Other uses

The term is also used in discussions of virtual worlds and online identity, where the permanent deletion of an avatar or account is sometimes proposed as a meaningful "death" for persistent characters.
