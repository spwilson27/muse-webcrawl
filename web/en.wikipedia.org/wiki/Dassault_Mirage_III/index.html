The Dassault Mirage III is a family of single-seat, single-engine, supersonic fighter aircraft designed and manufactured in France by Dassault Aviation. It was the first Western European combat aircraft to exceed Mach 2 in level flight, and its simple tailless delta-wing configuration made it one of the most widely exported fighters of the Cold War: more than 1,400 were built and the type served with some twenty air forces, seeing extensive combat with Israel, South Africa, Pakistan, Argentina and others.

Development

The Mirage began as a lightweight interceptor study of the early 1950s (the MD 550 Mystère-Delta). After experiments with the small Mirage I and II prototypes, Dassault scaled up the design around the SNECMA Atar 9 turbojet, producing the Mirage III, which first flew on 17 November 1956 with test pilot Roland Glavany. The production interceptor, the Mirage IIIC, entered French service in 1961; the multirole Mirage IIIE followed, and the simplified, ground-attack-oriented Mirage 5 — developed at Israeli urging — removed radar avionics for extra fuel and hardpoints. The later Mirage 50 added an uprated engine.

Design

The Mirage III uses a low-mounted delta wing with no horizontal tail; pitch and roll are controlled by combined elevons on the wing trailing edge. The delta offers a large internal fuel volume, low wing loading at altitude and good supersonic drag characteristics, at the cost of high landing speeds and poor sustained turn performance at low altitude. Armament typically comprised two 30 mm DEFA cannon and air-to-air missiles (initially the Matra R.530) or bombs on wing pylons. An auxiliary SEPR rocket motor could be fitted in the rear fuselage for interception boosts, and reconnaissance (IIIR), two-seat trainer (IIIB) and export versions proliferated.

Operational history

The type's most famous operator was the Israeli Air Force, whose Mirage IIICJs won striking air-to-air victories in the Six-Day War of 1967 — where Israeli Mirages destroyed much of the Egyptian air force on the ground and in the air — and in the War of Attrition and Yom Kippur War. Israel's experience led, via the embargoed Mirage 5 and espionage of Swiss production plans, to the indigenous IAI Nesher and Kfir. South Africa's Mirage IIIs and F1s fought in the Border War over Angola; Pakistan's Mirages served in the 1971 war with India and were later upgraded under Project ROSE; Argentina's Mirage IIIs and Daggers flew against the Royal Navy in the 1982 Falklands War. Switzerland, Australia (which built the type under licence), Brazil, Spain and others also operated the family.

Variants

  • Mirage IIIC — initial interceptor with Cyrano radar.
  • Mirage IIIE — longer fuselage, improved avionics, multirole.
  • Mirage IIIR — reconnaissance version.
  • Mirage 5 — simplified attack version with extended nose and extra hardpoints.
  • Mirage 50 — Mirage 5 airframe with the Atar 9K-50 engine.
  • Licensed and derivative production included the Australian Mirage IIIO, Swiss Mirage IIIS, South African Atlas Cheetah upgrades, and Israel's Nesher/Kfir line.

Legacy

The Mirage III established Dassault as a major fighter manufacturer and fixed the tailless delta as a company hallmark, continued in the Mirage 2000 and Rafale. Most operators retired the original aircraft by the 2000s, though upgraded derivatives soldiered on — Pakistan's ROSE Mirages into the 2020s — a service life of more than six decades from the first flight.
