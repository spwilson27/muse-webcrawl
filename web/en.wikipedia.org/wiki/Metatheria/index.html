Metatheria

Metatheria is a mammalian clade that includes all mammals more closely related to marsupials than to placentals (Eutheria). First proposed by Thomas Henry Huxley in 1880, the group comprises the living marsupials and all of their extinct relatives, including the sparassodonts of South America and a wide diversity of early forms from the Cretaceous of North America and Asia.

Evolutionary history

The oldest known metatherians date to the Early Cretaceous of China, roughly 125 million years ago, represented by forms such as Sinodelphys. Metatherians were diverse and widespread in the Northern Hemisphere during the Late Cretaceous, but suffered heavily in the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event. In South America they diversified into the sparassodonts, marsupial-like predators that occupied carnivore niches until the late Pliocene, while in Australia marsupials underwent a major adaptive radiation after the continent's isolation.

Characteristics

Metatherians are distinguished from eutherians by features of the teeth and skull, and (in marsupials) by their mode of reproduction: young are born at a very early stage of development after a short gestation and typically complete development attached to a teat, often within a pouch (marsupium). The dental formula and the structure of the ankle and wrist bones are also used to diagnose fossil members of the group.

Classification

Metatheria includes the crown group Marsupialia — the opossums, dasyuromorphs (including the Tasmanian devil), peramelemorphs, diprotodonts (kangaroos, koalas, wombats), and others — together with stem groups such as the Deltatheroida and the extinct South American Sparassodonta.
