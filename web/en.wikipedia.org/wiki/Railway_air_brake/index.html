Railway air brake

A railway air brake is a railway brake power braking system with compressed air as the operating medium. Modern trains in most countries rely wholly or partly on air brakes. The system was invented by George Westinghouse in 1869, and the automatic form, patented in 1872, transformed railway safety by making braking fail-safe and allowing a driver to brake an entire train from the locomotive.

In the automatic air brake, a brake pipe runs the length of the train and is normally kept charged with compressed air (commonly about 5 bar or 90 psi). Each vehicle carries an auxiliary reservoir and a triple valve (or distributor). To apply the brakes, the driver reduces brake-pipe pressure; the valve on each vehicle responds by admitting air from its reservoir into the brake cylinder, which pushes brake blocks or pads against the wheels or discs. Restoring the pressure releases the brakes and recharges the reservoirs.

Fail-safe principle and development

Because a reduction in brake-pipe pressure applies the brakes, a train that divides or a pipe that bursts automatically stops — the key advance over earlier straight air and vacuum systems. Later developments include graduated release, load-sensitive braking, electro-pneumatic brakes, and electronically controlled pneumatic (ECP) brakes for long freight trains.
