The tiger trout (Salmo trutta × Salvelinus fontinalis) is a sterile hybrid trout, produced by cross-breeding the male brook trout with the female brown trout. The name comes from the pronounced vermiculations — wavy, maze-like markings reminiscent of a tiger's stripes — that cover its body.

Characteristics

Tiger trout have a distinctive pattern in which the spots of their parent species merge into labyrinthine worm-like trails over an olive-brown body. The cross is unusual because the parents belong to different genera, and their chromosome counts differ (brook trout 84, brown trout 80), which is why the hybrid is almost invariably sterile. Weight typically reaches about 30–45 cm in length and a few pounds in stocked waters, though larger specimens occur.

Occurrence and management

The hybrid occurs rarely in nature where the two species overlap, but it is widely produced in hatcheries — often by heat-shocking fertilised eggs to improve survival — and stocked for sport fishing and fisheries management. Because they are aggressive predators and (being sterile) cannot establish self-sustaining hybrid populations, tiger trout are sometimes used to control overabundant forage fish or unwanted species. In the United States they are stocked especially in western states such as Utah, where the tiger trout is a popular sport fish.
