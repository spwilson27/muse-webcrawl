Woking Muslim Mission

The Shah Jahan Mosque, base of the Woking Muslim Mission

The Woking Muslim Mission was the first organisation of its kind founded in the United Kingdom for the propagation of Islam, established in 1913 at the Shah Jahan Mosque in Woking, Surrey. It was founded by Khwaja Kamal-ud-Din (1870–1932), a lawyer from Lahore and a follower of the Ahmadiyya movement, who came to England in 1912, and it was supported financially by the Begum of Bhopal. The mission became the centre of organised Islamic activity in Britain during the first half of the 20th century.

History

The Shah Jahan Mosque, built in 1889 by the orientalist Gottlieb Wilhelm Leitner with funds from Sultan Shah Jahan, Begum of Bhopal, was Britain's first purpose-built mosque. After Leitner's death in 1899 the mosque fell into disuse. Khwaja Kamal-ud-Din arrived in England in 1912, established the Woking Muslim Mission in 1913, and reopened the mosque, with the financial backing of the Begum of Bhopal, who had the building repaired.

Kamal-ud-Din was joined in 1914 by Maulana Muhammad Ali, the eminent scholar of the Lahore Ahmadiyya Movement, and by Lord Headley (Rowland George Allanson-Winn, 5th Baron Headley), a British peer who had embraced Islam in 1913 and became chairman of the British Muslim Society. The mission's imams included figures such as Marmaduke Pickthall, the translator of the Quran into English, who served as acting imam during the First World War, and Abdullah Yusuf Ali in its orbit of prominent British Muslims.

Publications

The mission published The Islamic Review, founded in 1913, which was distributed in some 60 countries and for decades was the best-known English-language Muslim periodical in the West. It also published Muhammad Ali's English translation of the Quran (1917) — one of the first widely read English translations by a Muslim — and books and pamphlets presenting Islam to English readers. The mission's mosque hosted the Eid prayers for Muslims in Britain, including soldiers of the Indian Army during both world wars, and became a social as well as religious centre, registering marriages and conversions.

Later history

The mission and the mosque were administered by the Shah Jahan Mosque Trust. Prominence passed in the latter 20th century to the new central mosques of London, and the Woking mission declined in influence, though the mosque remains active today under Sunni administration. The period of the mission is nonetheless regarded by historians, such as Humayun Ansari, as the foundational era of organised Islam in Britain.
