Hip bone

The hip bone (os coxae, innominate bone, pelvic bone, or coxal bone) is a large, flat, irregularly shaped bone, constricted in the centre and expanded above and below. It consists of three parts — the ilium, ischium, and pubis — which are distinct in the young subject but fused together in the adult. The two hip bones join at the pubic symphysis and, together with the sacrum and coccyx, form the pelvis.

Components

The ilium is the broad upper part, whose curved ridge, the iliac crest, can be felt beneath the skin; it bears the anterior superior iliac spine, a landmark and muscle attachment. The ischium forms the lower and back part and ends in the ischial tuberosity — the "sitting bone". The pubis forms the front part. All three meet in the acetabulum, the deep socket that receives the head of the femur to form the hip joint. Below the acetabulum the ischium and pubis enclose the obturator foramen, the largest foramen in the skeleton, through which the obturator nerve and vessels pass.

Development

The three components are joined in childhood by a Y-shaped triradiate cartilage in the acetabulum, and fuse completely by about the age of 20–25. Sex differences in the hip bone — a wider pelvis and broader sciatic notch in females — make it the most reliable bone for sex estimation in forensic and archaeological contexts.
