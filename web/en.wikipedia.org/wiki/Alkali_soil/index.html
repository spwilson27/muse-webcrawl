Alkali soils are clay soils with a high pH (greater than 8.5), a poor, dispersed structure and a low infiltration capacity. Chemically they are characterised by an excess of exchangeable sodium relative to calcium and magnesium; the closely related terms sodic soil and solonetz describe the same family of salt-affected soils, in contrast to saline soils, which hold an excess of neutral soluble salts.

Formation

Alkali soils form mainly in arid and semi-arid climates where evaporation exceeds rainfall. As water evaporates or is used by plants, salts derived from the weathering of sodium-bearing minerals concentrate in the surface soil. Sodium carbonate ("black alkali") hydrolyses to give the high pH, and dispersed organic matter gives such soils a dark colour. Irrigation with sodium-rich waters can create alkali conditions artificially.

Properties and problems

Sodium on the clay exchange complex causes the clay to disperse rather than flocculate. The result is a dense, nearly impermeable soil that is hard and cloddy when dry and sticky when wet, with poor rooting, crusting of the surface and often direct toxicity of sodium, carbonate and boron to plants. Few crops grow well; rice and certain grasses and saltbush species are relatively tolerant.

Reclamation

Reclamation replaces exchangeable sodium with calcium, usually by applying gypsum (calcium sulfate) or, in calcareous soils, elemental sulfur or sulfuric acid, which dissolve native lime. The displaced sodium is then leached away with irrigation water, and drainage must be provided so it does not return. Green manuring and deep-rooted crops help restore structure.
