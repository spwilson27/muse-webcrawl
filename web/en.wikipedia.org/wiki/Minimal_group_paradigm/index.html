The minimal group paradigm is a methodology employed in social psychology. Although it may be partly rational, it demonstrates that a division between groups can trigger discriminatory behaviour toward an "outgroup" even when the division between the "ingroup" and "outgroup" is based on a completely trivial, minimal, or even arbitrary criterion. The method has commonly been used to investigate the minimal conditions required for discrimination to occur between groups. Experiments using this approach have revealed that even arbitrary and apparently meaningless distinctions between groups, such as preferences for certain paintings or the colour of their shirts, can trigger a tendency to favour one's own group at the expense of others, even when it means sacrificing in-group gain.

History

The minimal group paradigm was developed by the Polish-born British social psychologist Henri Tajfel and his colleagues at the University of Bristol in the late 1960s and early 1970s. Tajfel, a survivor of the Holocaust, was motivated to understand how ordinary people could come to discriminate against members of other groups. He sought to strip away all the usual ingredients of intergroup hostility — historical enmity, competition over resources, differences in values — to find the minimal conditions under which discrimination would still occur.

Method

In the classic experiments (Tajfel, Billig, Bundy & Flament, 1971), schoolboy participants were arbitrarily assigned to groups on the basis of trivial criteria, such as their supposed preference for paintings by Paul Klee or Wassily Kandinsky, or even the toss of a coin. Participants never met the other members of their group, did not know who they were, and had no history of conflict with the outgroup. Each participant then privately allocated points (later exchangeable for money) between anonymous members of the ingroup and the outgroup using a series of allocation matrices. Participants could not award points to themselves.

Findings

Despite the triviality of the group distinction, participants consistently awarded more points to members of their own group than to members of the outgroup. More strikingly, when forced to choose between strategies, many participants preferred allocations that maximised the difference between the groups in the ingroup's favour over allocations that would have maximised the absolute profit of the ingroup — suggesting that relative advantage over the outgroup, rather than material gain, drove the behaviour.

Interpretation

Tajfel interpreted the results as evidence that the mere act of categorising people into groups is sufficient to produce ingroup favouritism. This work became the empirical foundation for social identity theory, developed by Tajfel and John Turner, which proposes that people derive part of their self-concept from group membership and strive for a positive social identity, which they achieve in part by favourably comparing their group with outgroups.

Criticism and later developments

Critics have noted that participants may infer experimenter expectations (demand characteristics), that the allocation task itself invites competitive interpretations, and that positive–negative asymmetry effects mean discrimination is weaker when participants allocate negative outcomes rather than rewards. The paradigm nonetheless remains one of the most influential experimental frameworks in the study of intergroup relations, prejudice, and stereotyping.
