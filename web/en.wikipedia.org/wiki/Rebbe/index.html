A Rebbe (Yiddish: רבי) is the spiritual leader of a Hasidic community or dynasty. The word is the Yiddish form of the Hebrew rabbi ("my master"), but in Hasidic usage it denotes a distinct institution: the rebbe is not merely a scholar or communal rabbi but a tzadik (righteous person) believed to serve as a channel of divine blessing and an intermediary between his followers (Hasidim) and God. A rebbe is also called an Admor, an acronym of the Hebrew Adonainu, Morainu, V'Rabainu — "our lord, our teacher, and our rabbi".

Role and authority

Hasidim consult their rebbe on every significant matter — business, marriage, health, and spiritual life — and seek his blessing (bracha). Followers traditionally give the rebbe a kvitel (a note with names for prayer) accompanied by a pidyon, a monetary offering. The rebbe's court centers on practices such as the tish, a festive communal meal at which the rebbe delivers Torah teachings and distributes shirayim (portions of his food), and on melodies (niggunim) attributed to the dynasty.

Dynasties and succession

Leadership of a dynasty is usually hereditary, passing from father to son (or son-in-law), which has produced dynasties named for their towns of origin in Eastern Europe — Belz, Ger, Satmar, Bobov, Vizhnitz, and many others. The near-destruction of East European Hasidism in the Holocaust led surviving rebbes to rebuild their courts in Israel and the United States. A famous exception to ordinary succession is Chabad-Lubavitch, whose seventh rebbe, Menachem Mendel Schneerson (d. 1994), has not been succeeded, and whom many followers regard as the Messiah. The term is also used more loosely: students in yeshivas call their teachers "rebbe", and the leader of the non-dynastic Lithuanian yeshiva world is sometimes addressed similarly, though the Hasidic institution is distinct.
