Pilot ACE

The Pilot ACE (Automatic Computing Engine) was one of the first computers built in the United Kingdom. It was built at the National Physical Laboratory (NPL) in the early 1950s. It was a preliminary version of the full ACE, which had been designed by Alan Turing, who left NPL before the construction was completed.

History

Alan Turing produced the design of the Automatic Computing Engine while working at NPL in 1945-47. Because the full design was judged too ambitious to build immediately, a simplified test model — the Pilot ACE — was constructed under the direction of Jim Wilkinson and others. The Pilot ACE ran its first program on 10 May 1950 and was demonstrated to the press in November 1950.

Design

The Pilot ACE was built around mercury delay line memory. It had no hardware for multiplication or division; these operations were performed in software. Its clock speed of 1 MHz made it one of the fastest computers of its era. The machine used about 800 vacuum tubes (thermionic valves).

Initially there was no programming language; all programming was done in machine code. Later, a simple library of subroutines was developed. The machine's architecture embodied Turing's idea of optimal coding, in which the timing of instructions relative to the delay line contents had to be carefully arranged.

Legacy

The Pilot ACE led to the full ACE, which became operational in 1958, and influenced the design of the commercial DEUCE computer built by English Electric. The Pilot ACE is preserved at the Science Museum in London.
