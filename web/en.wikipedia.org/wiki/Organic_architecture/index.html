Organic architecture

Organic architecture is a philosophy of architecture which promotes harmony between human habitation and the natural world. This is achieved through design approaches that aim to be sympathetic and well-integrated with a site, so buildings, furnishings, and surroundings become part of a unified, interrelated composition. The term was coined by Frank Lloyd Wright, its foremost practitioner.

Frank Lloyd Wright

Wright adopted the word "organic" from his mentor Louis Sullivan, who taught that "form ever follows function," and developed it into a comprehensive design philosophy from the 1900s onward. An organic building, in Wright's view, should grow from its site as a plant grows from soil: built of materials native to the place, with spaces flowing into one another and out to the landscape, and every element — furniture, lighting, decoration — designed as part of the whole. His Prairie Houses, with low horizontal lines echoing the Midwestern plain, and later masterpieces such as Fallingwater (1935), cantilevered over a Pennsylvania waterfall, and the spiralling Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum (1959), exemplify the idea.

Other practitioners

Antoni Gaudí's nature-derived forms in Barcelona, the work of Alvar Aalto in Finland, Hugo Häring in Germany, and Jørn Utzon's Sydney Opera House are frequently classed as organic. In North America, protégés such as Bruce Goff and Bart Prince pursued sculptural, site-specific organic designs. The philosophy influenced later sustainable and biophilic architecture, which similarly seeks to connect buildings with natural systems and patterns.

Principles

Wright's stated principles included: the building and site as one; openness and continuity of space; honesty of materials ("the nature of materials"); and design generated from an internal geometric order rather than applied style. He summarized the ideal as a building that "belongs" to its place and time, as a tree belongs to its ground.
