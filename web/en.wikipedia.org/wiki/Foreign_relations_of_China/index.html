Foreign relations of China

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The foreign relations of China, officially the People's Republic of China (PRC), are conducted through the Ministry of Foreign Affairs in Beijing. China is a permanent member of the United Nations Security Council and maintains diplomatic relations with the great majority of the world's countries. Its foreign policy has traditionally been framed by the Five Principles of Peaceful Coexistence, first articulated in the 1950s.

History

After the founding of the PRC in 1949, most Western countries continued to recognize the Republic of China on Taiwan as the legitimate government of China. The PRC's international position transformed in 1971, when UN General Assembly Resolution 2758 transferred China's seat at the United Nations to Beijing, and again in 1979, when the United States established diplomatic relations with the PRC.

Policy principles

Chinese foreign policy emphasizes sovereignty and territorial integrity, non-interference in the internal affairs of other states, and the One China principle regarding Taiwan. In the 21st century, China has expanded its global engagement through initiatives such as the Belt and Road Initiative, launched in 2013, and institutions such as the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank.

Relations by region

China's most consequential bilateral relationship is with the United States, combining extensive trade interdependence with strategic competition. Relations with Russia have deepened into a "comprehensive strategic partnership." China is also the largest trading partner of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), Japan, South Korea, and Australia, and has built extensive economic ties with Africa and Latin America.
