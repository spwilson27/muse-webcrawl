TrkB (tropomyosin receptor kinase B), also known as tyrosine receptor kinase B, is a protein that in humans is encoded by the NTRK2 gene. It is the high-affinity receptor for brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and neurotrophin-4 (NT-4), and is the receptor through which BDNF exerts most of its effects on neuronal survival, growth, and synaptic plasticity.

Structure and signalling

TrkB is a single-pass membrane receptor tyrosine kinase. Binding of a BDNF dimer causes the receptor to dimerise and autophosphorylate tyrosine residues in its intracellular kinase domain, creating docking sites that activate three main signalling cascades: the Ras–MAPK/ERK pathway (promoting differentiation and neurite growth), the PI3K–Akt pathway (promoting cell survival), and PLCγ signalling (affecting synaptic plasticity and calcium dynamics). A truncated isoform, TrkB.T1, lacks the kinase domain and is abundant in glial cells, where it can act as a dominant-negative regulator and in calcium signalling.

Function

Through BDNF, TrkB signalling is essential for the development and maintenance of the nervous system, for long-term potentiation in the hippocampus, and hence for learning and memory. It also regulates appetite and energy balance in the hypothalamus: humans with loss-of-function mutations in NTRK2 develop severe obesity with developmental delay. TrkB is expressed outside the brain too, including in the vasculature and in several cancers, where inappropriate activation — sometimes through NTRK gene fusions — can drive tumour growth; such fusions are the targets of the approved TRK-inhibitor drugs larotrectinib and entrectinib.

Clinical significance

Because BDNF–TrkB signalling is reduced in depression and rapidly enhanced by fast-acting antidepressants, TrkB has become a major research focus in psychiatry: several studies have reported that conventional antidepressants and ketamine bind directly to TrkB and facilitate BDNF signalling, and the receptor is implicated in the neurobiology of schizophrenia, Alzheimer's disease, and epilepsy.
