Covenanters

[Image: signing of the National Covenant in Greyfriars Kirkyard, Edinburgh, 1638]
Period1638–1690s
Key documentsNational Covenant (1638); Solemn League and Covenant (1643)
ReligionScottish Presbyterianism

The Covenanters were members of a 17th-century Scottish religious and political movement who supported a Presbyterian Church of Scotland and opposed the interference of the Stuart kings in its affairs. They take their name from the covenants — solemn bonds, modelled on biblical covenants — that they signed, above all the National Covenant of 1638 and the Solemn League and Covenant of 1643.

Origins and the Bishops' Wars

The movement erupted in 1637–38 in resistance to Charles I's attempt to impose a new prayer book on Scotland. Thousands signed the National Covenant, first in Greyfriars Kirkyard, Edinburgh, pledging to defend the reformed religion. Covenanter armies defeated the king's forces in the Bishops' Wars (1639–40), and in 1643 the Covenanters allied with the English Parliament in the Civil War in return for the Solemn League and Covenant, which promised a Presbyterian settlement across Britain. Scottish armies fought at Marston Moor, but the alliance collapsed amid disputes over the execution of Charles I, and Cromwell conquered Scotland in 1650–51 after Dunbar.

Persecution — the "Killing Time"

After the Restoration of Charles II in 1660, episcopacy was reimposed and Covenanter ministers were ejected. Field conventicles (open-air services) were suppressed with escalating violence: the Pentland Rising (1666), the battles of Drumclog and Bothwell Bridge (1679), and the assassination of Archbishop Sharp were followed in the 1680s by the "Killing Time", in which scores of Covenanters — including the "Wigtown martyrs" — were executed or killed without trial. The suffering ended with the Glorious Revolution of 1688–89, after which Presbyterianism was re-established in the Church of Scotland in 1690.

Legacy

A strict minority, the Cameronians (Society People), rejected the 1690 settlement as insufficient; their tradition continued in the Reformed Presbyterian Church. The Covenanters remain central to Scottish Presbyterian identity, commemorated by monuments, martyrs' graves and memorials across the south-west of Scotland.
