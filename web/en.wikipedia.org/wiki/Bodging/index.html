Bodging is a traditional woodworking craft in which green (unseasoned) wood is turned on a pole lathe, historically practised by itinerant craftsmen called bodgers who lived and worked in the woods. The term is particularly associated with the beechwoods of the Chiltern Hills in Buckinghamshire, England, where bodgers produced chair legs and other components for the furniture industry of High Wycombe.

The craft

The bodger set up a pole lathe — a simple lathe powered by a treadle, with the reciprocating motion provided by a springy sapling (the "pole") anchored overhead — in a temporary shelter in the woodland. Logs were cleft into billets, roughed out with an axe, and turned between centres into chair legs, stretchers and spindles. Only the turned parts of the chair were made in the woods; seats, backs and assembly were completed in workshops in town. Finished parts were sold to factories by the gross.

History

The Chiltern bodgers flourished through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, supplying the booming chair trade of High Wycombe. The craft declined with factory mechanisation and the two world wars; one of the last full-time traditional bodgers was Samuel Rockall, who died in 1962. The craft has since been revived as part of the greenwood working movement, with pole-lathe turning demonstrated at craft fairs and taught on courses.

The word "bodge"

Contrary to popular assumption, the bodgers were highly skilled, and the modern slang "bodge" meaning a clumsy, makeshift repair probably derives from "botch" rather than from the bodgers' work — although the association is often made. A "bodged" chair part was, in trade usage, simply one made in the woods by a bodger.
