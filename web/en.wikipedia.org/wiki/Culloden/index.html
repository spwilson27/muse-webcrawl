Culloden

Culloden (Scottish Gaelic: Cùil Lodair) is a village three miles (five kilometres) east of Inverness, Scotland, and the surrounding moorland — Culloden Moor, or Drummossie Moor — which gives its name to the Battle of Culloden of 16 April 1746, the last pitched battle fought on British soil and the final, decisive defeat of the Jacobite Rising of 1745. The village stands close to the Bronze Age Clava cairns and within the district of the Culloden estate; today the moor is preserved by the National Trust for Scotland, whose visitor centre interprets the battle and its aftermath, and the name Culloden has passed into Scottish memory as a symbol of tragedy and of the end of the old Highland world.

The Battle of Culloden (16 April 1746)

The Jacobite army of Charles Edward Stuart ("Bonnie Prince Charlie") — perhaps 5,000–6,000 men, many of them Highland clan levies, hungry and exhausted after a failed night march to surprise the government camp at Nairn — faced on the open moor an army of some 8,000–9,000 men under the Duke of Cumberland, the king's son: British regular infantry, cavalry and artillery, well fed and rested, with Lowland Scots and a Hanoverian contingent among them. The choice of the flat, exposed moor — against the advice of Lord George Murray, who wanted broken boggy ground where government cavalry and guns would count for less — surrendered every advantage the Highlanders had.

After a one-sided artillery exchange, the Jacobite clans charged in their famous Highland charge against the government left. Government infantry, drilled in a new bayonet technique — each man thrusting at the exposed right side of the Highlander opposite his neighbour rather than at the shielded man before him — and supported by enfilading fire and cavalry on the flank, broke the charge in minutes. The right-hand clans (Atholl, Camerons, Stewarts, MacIntoshes) reached the bayonets and died in heaps; the left, crossing worse ground, never closed. In under an hour the Jacobite army was destroyed: between 1,500 and 2,000 Jacobites were killed or wounded, against roughly 300 government casualties. Cumberland's troops killed wounded men and prisoners on the field and in the days after — a severity that earned him the name "Butcher" in Scotland (and "Sweet William" among his admirers in England, where the flower Sweet William was named for him, as the Scottish stinkweed was said to be named for him too).

Aftermath

Charles fled the field and, after five months as a fugitive in the Highlands and islands — aided famously by Flora MacDonald, who conveyed him, disguised as her maid "Betty Burke", over the sea to Skye — escaped to France. The government followed victory with a systematic pacification of the Highlands: the Disarming Act and the Act of Proscription banned tartan and Highland dress and the carrying of arms, heritable jurisdictions were abolished, Jacobite estates were forfeited, some 120 prisoners were executed and over 3,000 transported or imprisoned, and military roads and forts — above all the great new Fort George, built on the coast a few miles from Culloden itself — garrisoned the region. Historians are careful to note that the Highland clearances and the social transformation that followed owed as much to economics and landlords' choices as to Culloden itself, but the battle ended the Jacobite cause for good — there was no rising in 1759 or thereafter — and stands as the last act of the centuries-long struggle over the British crown that began in 1688.

The battlefield today

The battlefield is cared for by the National Trust for Scotland (which acquired the core of the site from the 1930s and runs a major visitor centre, opened in 2007, near the battlefield). The moor is dotted with the memorial cairn erected in 1881 by Duncan Forbes of Culloden and the clan gravestones marking the mass graves of the clans, the Keppoch and Cumberland stones, the "Field of the English" where government dead were buried, and Leanach Cottage, which stood on the field in 1746. The site is protected as one of Scotland's inventoried battlefields; proposals for housing development on its edges have repeatedly drawn controversy, and Culloden remains among the most visited and most emotionally charged historic sites in Scotland.
