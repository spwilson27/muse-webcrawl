The Oedipus complex is a concept in psychoanalytic theory, introduced by Sigmund Freud, which holds that during the phallic stage of psychosexual development (roughly ages three to six) a child feels unconscious desire for the opposite-sex parent and rivalry and hostility toward the same-sex parent. Freud regarded the successful resolution of the complex — through identification with the same-sex parent — as central to the formation of the superego and of adult gender identity and morality. The term takes its name from the Greek myth of Oedipus.

Freud's formulation

In the classical account, a boy desires his mother and wishes to eliminate his father as a rival, while fearing retaliation in the form of castration. The anxiety is resolved when the boy represses his desire and identifies with the father, internalising paternal prohibitions as the superego. Freud argued that the complex is dissolved under the pressure of this anxiety and that residues of it persist in normal adult psychology; its failed or incomplete resolution, he claimed, underlies neuroses.

Development of the idea

Freud first voiced the idea in letters to Wilhelm Fliess in 1897 following his self-analysis, and publicly in The Interpretation of Dreams (1899), where he argued that the enduring power of Sophocles' Oedipus Rex rests on the audience's recognition of their own childhood impulses. The term "Oedipus complex" appeared in print in 1910, and Freud elaborated the theory in Three Essays on the Theory of Sexuality, The Ego and the Id (1923) and later papers.

The female case and the Electra complex

Freud's account of girls was more convoluted and repeatedly revised, involving "penis envy" and a shift of attachment from mother to father. Carl Jung proposed the term Electra complex (after the Greek figure Electra, who avenged her father Agamemnon by helping kill her mother Clytemnestra) for the female analogue, a label Freud himself rejected, preferring "feminine Oedipus attitude".

Criticism and legacy

The Oedipus complex became one of the most famous — and most contested — ideas of psychoanalysis. Critics including Karl Popper cited it as exemplifying unfalsifiability; many empirical psychologists find little evidence for its specific claims, and anthropologists such as Malinowski challenged its universality from matrilineal societies. Defenders in the psychoanalytic and humanities traditions treat it as a theory of desire, family structure and identification rather than a literal developmental timetable. Whatever its scientific standing, it profoundly influenced 20th-century literature, criticism and popular culture.
