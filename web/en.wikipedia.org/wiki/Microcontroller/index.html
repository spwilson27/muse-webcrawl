A microcontroller (often abbreviated MCU) is a compact integrated circuit designed to govern a specific operation in an embedded system. A typical microcontroller includes a processor, memory, and input/output peripherals on a single chip. Unlike the general-purpose microprocessors used in personal computers, microcontrollers emphasize self-contained operation, low cost, and low power consumption, and they are embedded by the billions in products from washing machines and microwave ovens to automobiles and medical devices.

A typical microcontroller in a surface-mount package.

Structure

A microcontroller contains one or more CPU cores alongside program memory (historically ROM or EPROM, today usually flash memory) and a small amount of RAM. On-chip peripherals commonly include:

  • General-purpose input/output (GPIO) pins
  • Timers and counters, including PWM outputs
  • Analog-to-digital and digital-to-analog converters (ADC/DAC)
  • Serial interfaces: UART, SPI, I²C, USB, and CAN bus
  • Interrupt controllers and watchdog timers

History

The first microcontrollers appeared in the 1970s. Texas Instruments developed the TMS 1000 (released 1974), and Intel followed with the 8048 (1976) and the influential 8051 (1980). Through the 1980s and 1990s, families such as Motorola's 68HC11 and Microchip's PIC became widespread. In the 2000s, 32-bit ARM Cortex-M cores came to dominate, and open RISC-V microcontrollers have since gained ground. Popular maker platforms such as Arduino (based on AVR) and the ESP32 brought microcontrollers to hobbyists and the Internet of things.

Programming

Microcontrollers are usually programmed in C or C++, sometimes in assembly language or, on maker boards, MicroPython. Firmware is typically written as an infinite loop handling events, or under a small real-time operating system (RTOS). Programs are loaded through interfaces such as JTAG, SWD, or in-circuit serial programming.

Applications

Microcontrollers control engine functions in cars (a modern vehicle may contain dozens), run home appliances, manage battery-powered sensors, drive industrial automation, and power consumer electronics such as remote controls, toys, and wearables. Their unit cost can be well under one US dollar in volume.
