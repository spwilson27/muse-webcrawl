Benedict's reagent

Benedict's reagent (often called Benedict's solution) is a chemical reagent and complex mixture of sodium carbonate, sodium citrate, and copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate. It is used in Benedict's test to detect the presence of reducing sugars, including glucose, lactose, and maltose. The reagent is named after the American chemist Stanley Rossiter Benedict, who described it in 1908 as an improvement on Fehling's solution.

The test

A small quantity of the sample is mixed with Benedict's reagent and heated in a water bath for several minutes. If a reducing sugar is present, the sugar reduces the blue copper(II) ions to copper(I), which precipitates as copper(I) oxide. The colour of the mixture changes through green, yellow, and orange to brick-red as the amount of reducing sugar increases, making the test semi-quantitative:

  • Blue — no reducing sugar
  • Green — trace amounts
  • Yellow — low amounts
  • Orange — moderate amounts
  • Brick-red — high amounts

Uses

Benedict's test was historically used to screen urine for glucose in the management of diabetes mellitus. It is still widely taught in school and undergraduate laboratories. Non-reducing sugars such as sucrose give no reaction unless first hydrolysed by boiling with dilute acid. The reagent has the practical advantage over Fehling's solution of being a single, stable solution rather than two solutions mixed before use.
