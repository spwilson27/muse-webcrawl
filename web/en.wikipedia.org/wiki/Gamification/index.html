Gamification

Gamification is the strategic attempt to enhance systems, services and activities by applying game-design elements and game principles in non-game contexts, in order to increase engagement, motivation and participation. Typical elements include points, badges, leaderboards, progress bars, levels, quests and immediate feedback. The term was coined in 2002 by British programmer Nick Pelling and gained wide currency after about 2010.

Applications

Gamification is used in education (language-learning apps such as Duolingo, classroom platforms), health and fitness (activity-tracking streaks and challenges), marketing and loyalty programmes, workplace productivity and training, crowdsourcing (Foldit, Zooniverse), and civic participation. Its methods draw on behavioural psychology — operant conditioning, goal-setting, social comparison and the endowment effect — as well as on theories of intrinsic motivation such as self-determination theory.

Criticism

Critics, notably game designer Ian Bogost, have dismissed superficial gamification as "exploitationware" — the reduction of games to points and badges ("pointsification") that manipulates rather than engages. Research findings are mixed: meta-analyses find modest positive effects on motivation and learning that depend heavily on design quality and context, and effects often decay over time. Ethical concerns include manipulation of workers and consumers, encouragement of compulsive behaviour, and the blurring of play and surveillance in data collection.

Related concepts

Gamification differs from serious games and game-based learning, which use complete games for non-entertainment purposes, and from playful design, which aims at playfulness rather than rule-bound game structures. Legal and regulatory interest has grown where gamified mechanics — such as variable-ratio rewards — overlap with gambling-like features in apps and trading platforms.
