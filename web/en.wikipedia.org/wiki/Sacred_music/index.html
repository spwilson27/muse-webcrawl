Sacred music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sacred music is music composed or performed for religious use, worship or spiritual purposes. It encompasses a vast range of traditions across the world's religions, from ancient chant to contemporary worship music, and includes both music intended for liturgy and music on religious themes performed in concert settings.

Christian sacred music

In the Christian tradition, sacred music developed from the chanting of psalms and hymns. Gregorian chant, the monophonic liturgical chant of the Roman Catholic Church, was codified in the early Middle Ages and remains foundational. During the Renaissance, composers such as Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina, Thomas Tallis and William Byrd developed rich polyphonic settings of the Mass and motets. The Baroque era produced monumental sacred works including Handel's Messiah, Bach's Passions and Mass in B minor, and Vivaldi's Gloria. Later centuries added the requiems of Mozart, Verdi and Fauré, and the oratorios of Mendelssohn and others.

Protestant traditions developed congregational hymn singing, chorales, and later gospel music. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, contemporary worship music and praise songs became widespread in evangelical and charismatic churches, while Taizé chant and other contemplative forms renewed older practices.

Other traditions

Jewish sacred music includes cantillation of the Torah, the art of the cantor (hazzan), and psalmody. In Islam, where instrumental music in worship is generally avoided, sacred sound centres on the recitation of the Qur'an (tilawa) and the call to prayer (adhan); Sufi traditions developed devotional forms such as qawwali in South Asia and the music of the Mevlevi whirling dervishes. Hindu traditions include Vedic chant, bhajan and kirtan devotional singing; Sikh worship is structured around sung hymns (shabad kirtan). Buddhist chant, Shinto gagaku, and indigenous ceremonial musics also form part of the global landscape of sacred music.

Characteristics

Sacred music is often defined by its function — accompanying ritual, prayer and communal worship — rather than by any single style. Many traditions employ texts drawn from scripture, responsive singing between leader and congregation, and instruments considered appropriate to the sacred setting, such as the church organ.
