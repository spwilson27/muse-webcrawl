Minimum wage in the United States

The minimum wage in the United States is set by federal, state and local law. The federal minimum wage, established under the Fair Labor Standards Act, has been $7.25 per hour since July 24, 2009. Where different rates apply, employees are entitled to the highest. Many states and cities set substantially higher rates; as of the mid-2020s, the highest statewide rates, in Washington, California and Connecticut, exceed $16 per hour, and some cities are higher still.

History

The first federal minimum wage, of 25 cents per hour, took effect in 1938 as part of President Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal. Congress raised the rate periodically over subsequent decades, reaching $5.15 in 1997 and, under the Fair Minimum Wage Act of 2007, $7.25 in 2009. Because the federal rate is not indexed to inflation, its real value has eroded substantially since its peak in 1968, prompting state and municipal action.

State and local variation

Some states set rates equal to or below the federal level, or have no state law, in which case the federal rate applies; others index their wage to inflation. Ballot initiatives and "Fight for $15" campaigns have driven increases in many jurisdictions. Separate rules apply to tipped employees, for whom the federal cash wage is $2.13 per hour provided tips bring total earnings to at least the full minimum, and to certain youth, student and disabled workers under special certificates.

Debate

Supporters argue increases reduce poverty and inequality and stimulate demand; opponents argue they reduce employment, particularly among teenagers and low-skilled workers, and raise prices. Economic research findings are contested, though many modern studies find modest or negligible employment effects for moderate increases.
