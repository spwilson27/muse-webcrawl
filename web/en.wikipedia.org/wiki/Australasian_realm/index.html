Australasian realm

The Australasian realm is one of the Earth's eight biogeographic realms. It includes Australia, the island of New Guinea, and the eastern part of the Indonesian archipelago, including the island of Sulawesi, the Molucca Islands (the Indonesian provinces of Maluku and North Maluku), and the islands of Lombok, Sumbawa, Sumba, Flores, and Timor, often known collectively as the Lesser Sunda Islands. The realm also includes several Pacific island groups, including the Bismarck Archipelago, Vanuatu, the Solomon Islands, and New Caledonia. New Zealand is sometimes included in the Australasian realm and sometimes classified in its own realm (or in the Oceanian realm in the WWF scheme).

Boundaries

The western boundary of the realm is defined by the Wallace Line, which runs through the deep-water straits between Bali and Lombok and between Borneo and Sulawesi, separating the Australasian fauna from the Indomalayan realm to the west. Within Indonesia, an intermediate zone of islands sometimes called Wallacea marks the transition between the two realms.

Biology

The realm's biota reflects its long isolation after the breakup of the supercontinent Gondwana. Australia and New Guinea share many characteristic groups: marsupials (such as kangaroos, koalas, wombats, and possums) dominate the mammal fauna, alongside the egg-laying monotremes (the platypus and echidnas). Placental mammals are represented naturally mainly by bats and rodents, which arrived later by dispersal.

Distinctive bird families include the flightless ratites (emu, cassowaries, and the extinct moas and elephant birds in neighbouring regions), megapodes, birds-of-paradise (centred on New Guinea), honeyeaters, and a great diversity of parrots. Characteristic plant families include the Proteaceae, Myrtaceae (including eucalypts), and the southern beeches (Nothofagus), all with Gondwanan distributions.

Ecoregions

The World Wide Fund for Nature divides the realm into terrestrial ecoregions ranging from the tropical rainforests of New Guinea—among the most species-rich on Earth—to the deserts of central Australia, the Mediterranean-climate shrublands of southwestern Australia (a global biodiversity hotspot), and the montane grasslands of New Zealand. Conservation challenges include habitat loss, climate change, and introduced species such as rabbits, foxes, cane toads, and feral cats, which have caused many extinctions of native mammals and birds.
