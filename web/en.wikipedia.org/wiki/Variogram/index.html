Variogram


Experimental variogram with fitted model

In spatial statistics, the theoretical variogram is a function describing the degree of spatial dependence of a spatial random field or stochastic process. It is defined as the variance of the difference between field values at two locations across realizations of the field. Half of the variogram is the semivariogram, a key function in geostatistics used to quantify spatial correlation and to drive interpolation methods such as kriging.

Definition

For a random field Z(x), the variogram is 2γ(h) = Var[Z(x + h) − Z(x)], where h is the separation (lag) vector. Under the assumption of intrinsic stationarity, the semivariogram can be estimated from data by averaging half the squared differences between all pairs of observations separated by approximately the lag h. A plot of these estimates against distance is the experimental (empirical) variogram.

Parameters

Fitted models—such as the spherical, exponential and Gaussian models—are characterized by three parameters: the nugget, the discontinuity at the origin caused by measurement error and micro-scale variation; the sill, the value at which the variogram levels off, representing the total variance; and the range, the distance beyond which observations are effectively uncorrelated. Directional variograms can reveal anisotropy, where correlation extends farther in one direction than another.

Applications

Variograms originated in gold mining geology, in the work of Danie Krige and Georges Matheron, and are now used in mining and petroleum reserve estimation, soil science, hydrology, meteorology, environmental monitoring and remote sensing—anywhere samples taken close together are expected to be more alike than samples far apart.
