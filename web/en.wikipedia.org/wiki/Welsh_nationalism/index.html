Welsh nationalism emphasises the distinctiveness of Welsh language, culture and history, and advocates greater self-government or independence for Wales. Although Wales was politically united with England from the 16th century, a distinct Welsh identity survived, centred especially on the Welsh language and Nonconformist religion, and a modern nationalist movement developed from the late 19th century.

Origins

Nineteenth-century cultural nationalism revived the eisteddfod and promoted Welsh history and language, while political radicals campaigned for disestablishment of the Church in Wales (achieved in 1920) and for Welsh home rule. In 1925 Saunders Lewis and others founded Plaid Genedlaethol Cymru (later Plaid Cymru), initially focused on defending the Welsh language. The 1936 burning of the RAF bombing school at Penyberth by Lewis and two colleagues became an iconic act of protest.

Language and protest

The flooding of the Tryweryn valley to supply Liverpool in 1965, against the wishes of most Welsh MPs, galvanised nationalist sentiment; Plaid Cymru won its first Westminster seat at Carmarthen in 1966 under Gwynfor Evans. The Welsh Language Society (Cymdeithas yr Iaith Gymraeg), founded in 1962, campaigned by direct action for Welsh-language rights, contributing to the Welsh Language Acts of 1967 and 1993, bilingual signage, and the creation of the Welsh-language television channel S4C in 1982 after Gwynfor Evans threatened a hunger strike.

Devolution and independence

A 1979 referendum rejected a Welsh assembly by a large margin, but in 1997 a referendum narrowly approved devolution, and the National Assembly for Wales (now the Senedd) opened in 1999, gaining primary law-making powers after the 2011 referendum. Plaid Cymru advocates full independence; support for independence has generally been lower in Wales than in Scotland but has shown growth in polls since the 2010s, and independence marches organised since 2019 have drawn large crowds.
