Indian indenture system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Indian indenture system was a system of indentured servitude, by which more than 1.6 million workers from British India were transported to labour in European colonies, as a substitute for slave labour, following the abolition of slavery in the European empires between 1834 and the system's final abolition in the early 20th century. The system expanded after the abolition of slavery in the British Empire in 1833, in France in 1848, and in the Netherlands in 1863, though indentured labour continued to be recruited by other nations until after World War I.

History

The first shipments of Indian indentured labourers went to Mauritius in 1834, where some 450,000 Indians eventually arrived. Other major destinations included British Guiana, Trinidad, Jamaica and other Caribbean colonies; Natal in South Africa; Fiji; Malaya; Ceylon; and French colonies such as Réunion, Guadeloupe and Martinique, as well as Dutch Suriname. Labourers were recruited mainly from the Bhojpuri-speaking districts of eastern Uttar Pradesh and Bihar, and later from Tamil- and Telugu-speaking regions of the Madras Presidency, embarking chiefly from Calcutta and Madras.

Conditions

Labourers signed agreements (called girmit in Fiji, from "agreement") typically binding them to five years of plantation work in return for wages, rations, housing and, in many colonies, a return passage. Recruitment abuses, deceptive practices by recruiters (arkatis), harsh discipline, penal sanctions for absence, poor housing and high mortality led critics such as Hugh Tinker to describe the system as "a new system of slavery". The experience shattered caste distinctions on the ships—fellow passengers became jahaji bhai ("ship brothers")—and produced enduring Indo-Caribbean, Indo-Fijian, Indo-Mauritian and other diaspora communities.

Abolition

Indian nationalist criticism, led by figures including Gopal Krishna Gokhale and Mohandas Gandhi (whose campaigns in South Africa began over the treatment of Indians), together with wartime shipping shortages, led the Government of India to prohibit indentured emigration in 1917; the system was formally wound up by the early 1920s. Descendants of indentured labourers form substantial populations in Mauritius, Fiji, Trinidad and Tobago, Guyana, Suriname, Jamaica, South Africa and Malaysia, and their languages, food and festivals preserve a distinct girmit heritage.
