Lviv is the largest city in western Ukraine and the sixth-largest city in the country overall, with a population of roughly 718,000. It serves as the administrative centre of Lviv Oblast and is one of Ukraine's principal cultural, educational and economic centres. Its historic city centre is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

History

Lviv was founded in the mid-13th century by Prince Daniel of Galicia and named after his son Lev (Leo). It became the capital of the Kingdom of Galicia–Volhynia. In 1340 the city passed to the Kingdom of Poland, where, as Lwów, it flourished as a multicultural trading centre inhabited by Poles, Jews, Ukrainians, Armenians and Germans. After the First Partition of Poland in 1772 it became the capital of the Austrian province of Galicia under the name Lemberg.

After the First World War the city was contested in the Polish–Ukrainian war and became part of interwar Poland. In the Second World War it was occupied first by the Soviet Union and then by Nazi Germany; its large Jewish population was almost entirely destroyed in the Holocaust, including in the Lviv pogroms of 1941. After 1945 the city's Polish population was expelled westward and Lviv became part of the Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic. Since 1991 it has been part of independent Ukraine and is regarded as a stronghold of Ukrainian national culture.

Culture

Lviv is noted for its well-preserved Renaissance, Baroque and Secession architecture, its opera house, numerous churches and museums, and its coffee-house tradition. The University of Lviv, founded in 1661, is among the oldest universities in Eastern Europe.
