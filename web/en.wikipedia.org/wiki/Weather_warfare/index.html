Weather warfare

Weather warfare is the use of weather modification techniques such as cloud seeding for military purposes. During the Vietnam War, the United States carried out Operation Popeye, a cloud-seeding programme intended to extend the monsoon season over the Ho Chi Minh Trail and soften road surfaces in order to impede North Vietnamese troop movements.

Concern about such programmes, and about the prospect of larger-scale environmental weapons, led to the adoption in 1977 of the Environmental Modification Convention (ENMOD), which prohibits the military or other hostile use of environmental modification techniques having widespread, long-lasting or severe effects.

History

Interest in weather control grew after the Second World War, when scientists such as Irving Langmuir promoted cloud seeding. In 1947, Project Cirrus attempted to modify a hurricane. British experiments by the Royal Air Force in the 1950s explored rainmaking, and during the Vietnam War the US military's Operation Popeye (1967–1972) flew thousands of sorties seeding clouds over Laos, Cambodia and Vietnam.

Legality

The ENMOD convention, signed in Geneva on 18 May 1977 and entering into force in 1978, bans hostile environmental modification but permits peaceful uses such as rain enhancement. The United States and the Soviet Union ratified the treaty, and later agreements within the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons and debates in the United Nations have kept environmental warfare under scrutiny. Large-scale weather control for destructive purposes remains beyond proven technical capability, and claims of "weather weapons" are a recurring theme in conspiracy theories.
