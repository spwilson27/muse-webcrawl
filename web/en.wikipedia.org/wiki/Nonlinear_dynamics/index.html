Nonlinear dynamics

Nonlinear dynamics is the branch of mathematics and physics that studies systems whose behaviour is not proportional to their inputs — that is, dynamical systems governed by nonlinear equations. It encompasses chaos theory, bifurcation theory, solitons, fractals and the study of complex systems. Unlike linear systems, which can be decomposed and solved by superposition, nonlinear systems can exhibit rich behaviour including multiple stable states, limit cycles, quasiperiodicity and deterministic chaos.

Overview

Most systems in nature are nonlinear. The equations describing fluid flow (the Navier–Stokes equations), chemical reactions, electrical circuits such as the van der Pol oscillator, population dynamics and the motion of planets all contain nonlinear terms. Because exact solutions are rarely available, nonlinear dynamics relies heavily on qualitative methods: phase-plane analysis, stability theory, numerical simulation and perturbation techniques.

Key concepts

  • Attractors — sets in phase space toward which trajectories evolve, including fixed points, limit cycles and strange attractors.
  • Bifurcations — qualitative changes in a system's behaviour as a parameter is varied, such as the saddle-node, Hopf and period-doubling bifurcations.
  • Chaos — aperiodic long-term behaviour in a deterministic system that exhibits sensitive dependence on initial conditions, famously illustrated by the Lorenz system derived from atmospheric convection.
  • Solitons — self-reinforcing solitary waves that maintain their shape while propagating, solutions of equations such as the Korteweg–de Vries equation.
  • Fractals — geometric structures with self-similarity across scales, often arising as invariant sets of nonlinear maps such as the logistic map.

History

The foundations were laid by Henri Poincaré in his work on the three-body problem in the 1890s, where he discovered homoclinic tangles and the possibility of extremely complicated motion. In the 1960s, Edward Lorenz's computer model of weather produced the first widely recognised example of chaos, and Mitchell Feigenbaum discovered universality in period-doubling transitions. The field expanded rapidly with the growth of computing, finding applications in engineering, biology, economics and neuroscience.

Applications

Nonlinear dynamics is applied to weather and climate modelling, the control of chaos in engineering systems, cardiac and neural rhythm modelling, laser physics, ecology and the design of secure communications based on chaotic synchronisation.
