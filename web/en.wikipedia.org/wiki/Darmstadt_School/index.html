The Darmstadt School is a name given to the group of composers associated with the Darmstadt International Summer Courses for New Music (Darmstädter Ferienkurse) in Germany in the years after the Second World War, and to the rigorously modernist style — above all integral (total) serialism — that they championed in the 1950s.

The Ferienkurse

The summer courses were founded in 1946 by the music critic Wolfgang Steinecke, partly as an act of cultural reconstruction: under the Nazis, modernist music had been suppressed as "degenerate", and German musicians had been cut off from developments abroad. Teachers and visitors in the early years included René Leibowitz, Olivier Messiaen, Ernst Krenek and Edgard Varèse.

The serialist generation

Around 1951 a younger generation — Pierre Boulez, Karlheinz Stockhausen, Luigi Nono, Bruno Maderna, Henri Pousseur and others — seized the agenda, extending twelve-tone technique so that rhythm, dynamics and timbre were serialised along with pitch. Landmark works include Stockhausen's Kreuzspiel and Boulez's Structures I. The label "Darmstadt School" was partly a journalistic invention, and the composers' styles soon diverged sharply: Stockhausen turned toward electronic music and intuition, Boulez toward controlled chance, and Nono toward politically committed music theatre.

Influence and criticism

For a generation the Darmstadt outlook dominated European new music, attracting both devoted followers and fierce criticism for dogmatism — the composer Cornelius Cardew, once Stockhausen's assistant, later attacked it in Stockhausen Serves Imperialism. The courses continue to this day as a major festival of contemporary music.
