Spurn Point

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Spurn Point, also called Spurn Head, is a narrow tidal sand spit at the mouth of the Humber Estuary in the East Riding of Yorkshire, England. It extends for about 3.5 miles (5.6 km) from the Holderness coast, curving southwest into the estuary, and in places is only about 50 yards (46 m) wide. The point forms the northern shore of the mouth of the Humber and protects the estuary's shipping channels. It is managed by the Yorkshire Wildlife Trust as a nature reserve.

Formation and erosion

Spurn is built from sand and shingle eroded from the soft boulder-clay cliffs of Holderness to the north and carried south by longshore drift. The spit grows, narrows, and is periodically breached by storms; over historical time the point has repeatedly been destroyed and rebuilt farther west, and the former port of Ravenser Odd, founded on an earlier spit in the thirteenth century, was lost to the sea in the 1360s. A major storm surge in December 2013 washed over and damaged the access road, making the tip tidal and accessible only on foot at low water.

Lighthouse and lifeboat

Lights have marked Spurn since the seventeenth century to guide vessels into the Humber. The present High Lighthouse, built in 1895, stands 39 metres tall and is now disused; a smaller light remains in service. Spurn is also home to the Humber lifeboat, one of the few RNLI stations with a full-time paid crew, whose members and families historically lived in cottages at the point — a community that included a school and, during the First World War, a military railway and gun batteries defending the estuary entrance.

Wildlife

The reserve is noted for bird migration: the narrow point funnels migrating birds, and the Spurn Bird Observatory records rarities from across the Palearctic each spring and autumn. Sand dunes, saltmarsh, and mudflats support breeding ringed plovers, overwintering waders, and grey seals hauled out at the tip.
