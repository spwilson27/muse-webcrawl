Newar (endonym: Nepāla bhāṣā, "Nepal language"), also known as Newari, is a Sino-Tibetan language spoken by the Newar people, the indigenous inhabitants of the Kathmandu Valley and surrounding regions of Nepal. Despite the name used by its speakers, it is unrelated to the Indo-Aryan Nepali language, which became the country's official language. Newar was the administrative and literary language of Nepal from the 14th to the late 18th century.

History

Newar has a written tradition extending back roughly a thousand years; the earliest dated manuscript is from 1114 AD. Classical Newar flourished under the Malla dynasty, producing extensive literature in drama, poetry, chronicles and religious texts. After the Gorkhali conquest of the valley in 1768–69, the language lost official status, and during the Rana regime (1846–1951) its public use was suppressed; writers such as Chittadhar Hridaya composed works while imprisoned for promoting the language. The movement to revive Newar, known as the Nepal Bhasa movement, has campaigned for its recognition and use in education and media.

Dialects and scripts

Major dialects include Yen-Yala (Kathmandu–Patan), Khwapa (Bhaktapur), Dolakha and others. Newar has historically been written in several indigenous scripts, including Ranjana and Pracalit, both derived from Brahmi; today Devanagari is most common, though there is a revival of interest in Ranjana calligraphy.

Status

Newar is classified as a "definitely endangered" language by UNESCO, as intergenerational transmission has weakened under the dominance of Nepali, though revitalisation efforts, community schools and media have grown since Nepal's transition to federalism. In 2024, Bagmati Province formally introduced Newar (alongside Tamang) as an additional official working language of the province.
