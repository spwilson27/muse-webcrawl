The Organized Crime Control Act of 1970 (Pub. L. 91–452; 84 Stat. 922) is a United States federal law that was enacted by the 91st United States Congress and signed into law by President Richard M. Nixon on October 15, 1970. The act was the product of two years of effort by Senator John McClellan, Robert Blakey, and others, and represented the most comprehensive federal attack on organized crime — particularly the American Mafia — up to that time.

The Act was signed into law on October 15, 1970.

Provisions

The Act contained eleven titles. Among its major provisions were:

  • Title IX, the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO), which created criminal and civil liability for persons engaged in a pattern of racketeering activity connected to an enterprise, and allowed for treble damages in civil suits.
  • Authorization of special grand juries with extended terms to investigate organized crime.
  • Provisions for witness immunity and a federal witness protection programme, the predecessor of WITSEC.
  • Recalcitrant witness statutes allowing confinement of witnesses who refuse to testify.
  • Enhanced sentences for "dangerous special offenders" and restrictions on bail in organized crime cases.
  • Measures against illegal gambling businesses and the use of explosives in crimes.

Legacy

Although the Act as a whole covered many subjects, its RICO title proved by far the most consequential. Initially rarely used, RICO became in the 1980s the principal weapon of federal prosecutors against Mafia families, culminating in cases such as the Mafia Commission Trial. Its civil provisions were later applied widely in commercial litigation, prompting ongoing debate and reform proposals.
