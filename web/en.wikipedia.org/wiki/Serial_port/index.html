A DE-9 serial port, the standard connector on PCs from the 1980s to the 2000s.

A serial port is a serial communication interface through which information transfers one bit at a time, in contrast to a parallel port. On personal computers, the term refers to the port implementing the RS-232 standard (later EIA/TIA-232), used for decades to connect modems, mice, printers, terminals, and industrial equipment.

Hardware

Classic PC serial ports used a 9-pin DE-9 connector (often mislabelled DB-9) or the older 25-pin DB-25. The core logic is provided by a UART chip, such as the 8250 and later the buffered 16550, which converts parallel data from the bus into framed serial data with start, data, parity, and stop bits, and vice versa. Signals include TxD (transmit), RxD (receive), ground, and modem control lines such as RTS/CTS (hardware flow control), DTR, and DSR.

Configuration

Serial ports are configured by parameters that both ends must share: baud rate (commonly 9,600 or 115,200 bit/s), number of data bits (usually 8), parity (none, even, or odd), and stop bits—the familiar "9600 8N1" notation. On DOS and Windows systems, ports were named COM1, COM2, and so on; on Unix-like systems they appear as device files such as /dev/ttyS0.

Uses and decline

Serial ports connected dial-up modems, serial mice, plotters, label printers, and consoles of network equipment. They remain common in industrial automation, embedded systems, point-of-sale hardware, and as console ports on routers and servers. USB displaced the serial port on consumer PCs in the 2000s, but USB-to-serial adapters (e.g. FTDI and Prolific chipsets) keep the interface ubiquitous for development and field service work.
