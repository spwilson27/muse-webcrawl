John Duns Scotus (Latin: Ioannes Duns Scotus; c. 1266 – 8 November 1308), commonly called Duns Scotus, was a Scottish Catholic priest and Franciscan friar, university professor, philosopher and theologian. One of the three most important philosopher-theologians of Western Europe in the High Middle Ages, alongside Thomas Aquinas and William of Ockham, he was nicknamed Doctor Subtilis — the "Subtle Doctor" — for the penetration and complexity of his thought. His followers, the Scotists, formed one of the great schools of late medieval scholasticism.

Life

Scotus was born near Duns in Berwickshire, Scotland (his name means "John of Duns, the Scot"). He entered the Franciscan Order at Dumfries around 1279 and was ordained priest at Northampton in 1291. He studied and lectured at Oxford and at Paris, commenting on the Sentences of Peter Lombard. In 1303 he was one of the few Paris academics who refused to sign an appeal against Pope Boniface VIII in the king's quarrel with the papacy and was briefly exiled from France. He became regent master at Paris in 1305, was transferred to the Franciscan studium at Cologne in 1307, and died there the following year.

Philosophy and theology

Scotus's great commentary on the Sentences survives in several versions, the Ordinatio chief among them, alongside the Lectura, the Reportatio and works such as De primo principio ("On the First Principle"). His most distinctive doctrines include:

  • Univocity of being — the concept of "being" applies in the same sense to God and creatures, against the Thomist doctrine of analogy.
  • Haecceity ("thisness") — the positive quality by which an individual is this particular individual, beyond its common nature.
  • The formal distinction — a distinction between aspects of one reality that is stronger than a mere distinction of reason but weaker than a real distinction between things.
  • Voluntarism — an emphasis on the divine will: much of the moral law is contingent on God's free choice, though Scotus holds the first table of the commandments to be necessary.
  • The Immaculate Conception — Scotus's subtle defence of the doctrine that Mary was conceived without original sin ("He could do it, it was fitting, therefore He did it") cleared the philosophical obstacles and shaped its eventual definition as dogma in 1854.

Influence and reputation

Scotism dominated Franciscan theology for centuries and influenced thinkers from Ockham to Leibniz and Peirce. The pejorative word "dunce" derives from later mockery of his followers (the "Duns men") by Renaissance humanists and reformers — an ironic fate for one of the subtlest minds of the Middle Ages. Pope John Paul II beatified him in 1993.
