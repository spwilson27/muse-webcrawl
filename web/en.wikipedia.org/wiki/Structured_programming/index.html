Structured programming

Structured programming is a programming paradigm aimed at improving the clarity, quality, and development time of a computer program by making extensive use of the structured control flow constructs of selection (if/then/else) and repetition (while and for), block structures, and subroutines. It emerged in the late 1950s with the appearance of the ALGOL programming language family and was developed in the 1960s, in contrast to the unrestricted jumps of earlier practice.

The structured program theorem

The theoretical basis is the structured program theorem, proved by Corrado Böhm and Giuseppe Jacopini in 1966, which states that any computable function can be implemented using only three control structures: sequence (executing subprograms in order), selection (executing one of two subprograms according to a Boolean condition), and iteration (repeatedly executing a subprogram while a condition holds).

History and advocacy

Edsger W. Dijkstra's 1968 letter "Go To Statement Considered Harmful", published in the Communications of the ACM, gave the movement its rallying cry, arguing that unrestricted goto statements made programs hard to reason about. The paradigm was expounded in the 1972 book Structured Programming by Ole-Johan Dahl, Dijkstra, and C. A. R. Hoare. Languages such as Pascal were designed to teach and enforce structured style. Debate continued over exceptions — such as early exits from loops — with Donald Knuth's 1974 paper "Structured Programming with go to Statements" advocating a pragmatic middle course.

Principles and legacy

Structured programming encourages dividing programs into functions or modules with single entry and single exit points, top-down design, and limited use of global state. Its ideas underlie later paradigms: procedural programming, modular programming, and, indirectly, object-oriented programming. Structured analysis and design methods extended the principles to systems analysis. Today, virtually all mainstream languages provide structured control constructs, and goto is rarely used in application code, though constructs like exceptions, breaks, and early returns embody controlled departures from strict single-exit style.
