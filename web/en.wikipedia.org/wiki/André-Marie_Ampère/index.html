André-Marie Ampère

Born20 January 1775, Lyon, France
Died10 June 1836, Marseille, France
FieldsPhysics, mathematics, chemistry
Known forElectrodynamics; Ampère's law; the ampere

André-Marie Ampère (20 January 1775 – 10 June 1836) was a French physicist and mathematician who was one of the founders of the science of classical electromagnetism, which he referred to as "electrodynamics". He is also the inventor of numerous applications, such as the solenoid (a term he coined) and the electrical telegraph. An autodidact, Ampère was a member of the French Academy of Sciences and a professor at the École polytechnique and the Collège de France.

Discoveries

Within weeks of Hans Christian Ørsted's 1820 discovery that an electric current deflects a compass needle, Ampère presented his own findings to the Académie des Sciences: parallel currents attract and antiparallel currents repel, and a coil of wire carrying a current behaves like a magnet. He developed a mathematical theory of the magnetic forces between current-carrying conductors, summarised in Ampère's circuital law, one of the equations later completed by Maxwell.

The SI unit of electric current, the ampere, is named in his honour, as is Ampère's law and his name's place among the 72 names inscribed on the Eiffel Tower. With Jean-Baptiste Dumas he also proposed an early form of Avogadro's hypothesis on the volume of gases.

Early life

Ampère was largely self-educated in his father's library at Poleymieux-au-Mont-d'Or near Lyon; his father was guillotined during the French Revolution. Ampère became a tutor in Lyon and in 1802 published a treatise on the mathematics of games of chance, which attracted the attention of the mathematician Lalande.
