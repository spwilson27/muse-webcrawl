Law of Germany

The law of Germany (deutsches Recht) is a civil law system grounded in Roman law and codification, with the Basic Law (Grundgesetz) of 1949 as its constitution. German law has been highly influential, serving as a model for legal systems in Japan, Korea, China, and elsewhere, and it blends Romanist and Germanic traditions refined by 19th-century legal scholarship (the Pandectist school).

Principal codes

The cornerstones of private law are the Civil Code (Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch, BGB), in force since 1900 and famous for its systematic, abstract structure, and the Commercial Code (HGB). Criminal law is codified in the Strafgesetzbuch (StGB) of 1871, as amended. Public law is dominated by the Basic Law, whose catalogue of fundamental rights and strong constitutional review by the Federal Constitutional Court (Bundesverfassungsgericht) shape all other branches of law.

Court system

Germany has five branches of jurisdiction — ordinary (civil and criminal), administrative, labour, social, and fiscal courts — each with its own federal supreme court, capped by the Federal Constitutional Court for constitutional questions. Judges are career professionals; lay assessors (Schöffen) sit with professional judges in some criminal cases. Since reunification in 1990, the law of the Federal Republic extends across all sixteen Länder, and European Union law is deeply integrated into the German legal order.
