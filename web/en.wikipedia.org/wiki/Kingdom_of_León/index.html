The Kingdom of León (Leonese: Reinu de Llión; Spanish: Reino de León) was an independent kingdom in the northwestern Iberian Peninsula, established around 910 when the Kingdom of Asturias was divided among the sons of Alfonso III. Its capital was the city of León. It became one of the principal Christian kingdoms of the Reconquista.

History

García I was the first king of an effectively separate León. During the tenth century the kingdom endured civil strife and attacks from the Caliphate of Córdoba, including the sack of León by Almanzor. Under rulers such as Ramiro II, victor at Simancas in 939, León asserted leadership among the Christian states of Iberia, and its kings at times claimed an imperial title over all Spain.

In 1037 Ferdinand I of Castile acquired León, uniting the two crowns for the first time. They separated and reunited repeatedly over the following two centuries; Alfonso VII was crowned "Emperor of all Spain" in León in 1135. In 1188 Alfonso IX summoned the Cortes of León, which admitted representatives of the cities — regarded as one of the earliest parliamentary bodies in Europe. In 1230 Ferdinand III permanently united León and Castile, though León retained its own laws and institutions within the Crown of Castile.

Culture and law

The kingdom was a major patron of Mozarabic and Romanesque art and architecture, exemplified by the Basilica of San Isidoro in León. The Leonese language developed in its chancery and territory, and the kingdom's legal traditions, including the Fuero de León of 1017, influenced Iberian municipal law.
