Matrilineality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
In matrilineal societies, kinship and inheritance pass through the mother's line.

Matrilineality is the tracing of kinship through the female line. In a matrilineal society, individuals belong to the kin group, clan or lineage of their mother, and property, names, titles and status are inherited through women. Matrilineality is contrasted with patrilineality, in which descent is traced through the father. It should not be confused with matriarchy: in most matrilineal societies, formal political authority is still often exercised by men — typically the mother's brother rather than the husband — a relationship anthropologists call the avunculate.

Distribution

Matrilineal systems are found in many parts of the world. Well-known examples include the Minangkabau of West Sumatra, Indonesia — the world's largest matrilineal society — the Akan peoples of Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire, the Khasi and Garo of north-eastern India, the Mosuo of south-western China, the Hopi, Zuni and other Pueblo peoples of North America, and the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois), among whom clan mothers nominate chiefs. In Judaism, religious identity is traditionally transmitted matrilineally.

Features

Matrilineal descent is frequently combined with matrilocal residence, in which a husband moves into his wife's household or village. Inheritance of land and houses typically passes from mother to daughter, while men's authority roles pass from a man to his sister's sons. Because paternity is less socially central, matrilineal societies often show different patterns of marriage stability and childcare, with the maternal uncle playing a key disciplinary and economic role in children's lives.

Theory

Nineteenth-century evolutionist writers such as Johann Jakob Bachofen and Lewis Henry Morgan speculated that matrilineal ("mother-right") systems were a universal early stage of human society, an idea later largely abandoned. Twentieth-century anthropologists, notably Audrey Richards, who coined the term "matrilineal puzzle" for the tension between maternal descent and male authority, studied how such systems adapt to economic change and colonial pressure.
