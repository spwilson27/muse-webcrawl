Portmanteau

In linguistics, a portmanteau (also called a blend) is a word formed by blending the sounds and combining the meanings of two others, for example smog from smoke and fog, brunch from breakfast and lunch, and motel from motor and hotel. The word "portmanteau" itself originally denoted a suitcase with two compartments; its use for blended words was coined by Lewis Carroll.

Origin of the term

Lewis Carroll introduced the term in Through the Looking-Glass (1871), where Humpty Dumpty explains the coinages in the poem "Jabberwocky": "You see it's like a portmanteau — there are two meanings packed up into one word." Carroll's own examples include slithy (slimy + lithe) and mimsy (flimsy + miserable). The older word portmanteau comes from French porte-manteau, "coat carrier".

Formation

Blends are usually formed in one of four ways: combining the beginning of one word with the end of another (smog, motel); joining the beginnings of two words (cyborg, from cybernetic + organism); overlapping words that share sounds (slanguage); or combining fragments with substitution. Unlike compounds, which join whole words, blends typically clip at least one source word. Unlike abbreviations, blends are pronounced as ordinary words.

Examples

  • Brunch — breakfast + lunch (coined in the 1890s)
  • Smog — smoke + fog (1905)
  • Motel — motor + hotel (1925)
  • Brexit — Britain/British + exit (2012), which spawned many imitators
  • Hangry — hungry + angry
  • Infomercial — information + commercial
  • Spork — spoon + fork
  • Chunnel — Channel + tunnel
  • Emoticon — emotion + icon
  • Blog — web + log (via "weblog")

Portmanteaus are very common in branding (Microsoft = microcomputer + software; Pinterest = pin + interest), in entertainment journalism for celebrity couples ("Brangelina"), and in Pokémon and other fictional naming.

Related concepts

In morphology, the term "portmanteau morpheme" is used for a single morpheme that realises two or more grammatical categories at once, as with French au (à + le). Linguists distinguish blends from compounds, clippings, acronyms and folk etymologies, though boundaries can be fuzzy.
