Experimental music

Experimental music is a general label for music that pushes the boundaries of conventional practice — in sound materials, compositional method, performance situation, or the very definition of music. Composer Michael Nyman's book Experimental Music: Cage and Beyond (1974) distinguished post-1950 experimentalism — indeterminate notations, process, and new performance practices — from the earlier European avant-garde.

History

Precursors include Luigi Russolo's Futurist The Art of Noises (1913), Charles Ives's polytonal experiments, Henry Cowell's tone clusters, and Edgard Varèse's "organized sound." The pivotal figure is John Cage, whose 4′33″ (1952) frames ambient sound as music, and whose chance operations and prepared piano redefined composition. Around him developed the New York School (Earle Brown, Morton Feldman, Christian Wolff), the Fluxus artists, and later the minimalists (La Monte Young, Terry Riley, Steve Reich), whose process-based works grew out of experimental milieux. Tape studios, musique concrète, and the synthesizer opened further territory, and the term now commonly stretches across genres, from free improvisation and noise to experimental rock and electronic music.

Characteristics

Recurring traits include indeterminacy or aleatoric procedures, unconventional notation (graphic scores), extended instrumental techniques, just intonation and microtonality, repetition and long durations, and work that foregrounds the act of listening itself.
