African American English

African American English (AAE) is the set of varieties of English spoken principally by working- and middle-class African Americans. Its best-known vernacular form, African American Vernacular English (AAVE), is a systematic, rule-governed dialect with distinctive phonology, grammar, and vocabulary; the umbrella term also includes more standard varieties spoken by African Americans.

Features

Characteristic grammatical features include habitual be ("He be working" = he works habitually), copula absence where Standard English permits contraction, negative concord ("ain't nobody"), and remote-time BIN. Phonological features include consonant cluster reduction, th-stopping, and distinctive stress and intonation patterns.

Origins and status

Scholars debate the relative contributions of English dialect input and creole origins to AAE's formation; the consensus view treats it as rooted in the English brought to colonial America, reshaped by the conditions of slavery and segregation. AAE became a national controversy in the 1996 Oakland "Ebonics" debate. Linguists emphasize that AAE is not "broken" English but a fully systematic variety, while its speakers often face dialect discrimination in education, courts, and employment.
