River otter

River otter is a common name applied to several species of semiaquatic otters that live primarily in rivers and other freshwater habitats. The term most often refers to the North American river otter (Lontra canadensis), but it is also used for the Eurasian otter (Lutra lutra), the neotropical otter (Lontra longicaudis), the southern river otter (Lontra provocax), and the giant otter (Pteronura brasiliensis) of South America, the largest of the group.

Description

River otters have long, streamlined bodies, short legs with webbed feet, dense waterproof fur, muscular tails, and small rounded ears that close underwater. Their fur is typically brown, paler on the throat and belly. North American river otters measure roughly 66 to 107 centimetres in head-and-body length, with tails of 30 to 50 centimetres, and weigh between 5 and 14 kilograms. The giant otter is much larger, reaching up to 1.8 metres in total length.

Behaviour and diet

River otters are agile swimmers that propel themselves with undulations of the body and tail, steering with their webbed feet. They can stay submerged for several minutes and swim at speeds approaching 11 kilometres per hour. They are playful animals, well known for sliding down muddy or snowy banks into the water.

Their diet consists mainly of fish, supplemented by crayfish, crabs, amphibians, molluscs, aquatic insects, small mammals, and birds. Sensitive whiskers (vibrissae) help them detect prey in murky water. Otters hunt mostly by sight in clear water and by touch in turbid conditions.

Habitat and distribution

River otters inhabit rivers, streams, lakes, marshes, and coastal estuaries across their respective ranges. They require clean water with abundant prey and vegetated banks providing cover. Dens, called holts, are dug in banks or taken over from other animals, with entrances often below the waterline.

Reproduction

Females give birth to litters of one to six pups, which are born blind and helpless and remain in the den for about two months. Pups learn to swim at around two months of age and stay with their mother for roughly a year. Many river otters exhibit delayed implantation, so the interval between mating and birth can approach a year even though active gestation lasts only about two months.

Conservation

Historically, river otters declined across much of their range because of fur trapping, water pollution, and habitat loss. Reintroduction programmes in North America during the late twentieth century restored the North American river otter to many states from which it had vanished, and it is now listed as a species of least concern. Other river otter species remain more threatened; the southern river otter and giant otter are endangered, threatened by habitat destruction, hunting, and conflict with fisheries.
