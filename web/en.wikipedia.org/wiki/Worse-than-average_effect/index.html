Worse-than-average effect

Worse-than-average effect
FieldSocial psychology
Key studyKruger (1999)
Related conceptsBetter-than-average effect; self-serving bias; focalism

The worse-than-average effect is a cognitive bias in which people underestimate their own ability, performance or skill relative to others when judging difficult tasks. It is the counterpart of the better-than-average effect, which occurs for easy or common tasks.

Research

In a series of studies, Justin Kruger (1999) found that participants rated themselves below average on abilities perceived as rare or difficult — such as juggling, computer programming, unicycling or playing chess — while rating themselves above average on common, easy abilities such as driving or using a mouse. Research attributes the effect in part to focalism (concentrating on one's own struggles and overlooking that others face the same difficulties) and to the egocentric weighting of one's own experience when estimating the performance of an abstract "average person".

Explanations

Proposed mechanisms include regression to the mean in the construction of the comparison target, differential information about the self versus others, and the perceived diagnosticity of failures on hard tasks. The effect matters for self-assessment, education and clinical psychology, where underestimation of competence may discourage people from attempting challenging activities.
