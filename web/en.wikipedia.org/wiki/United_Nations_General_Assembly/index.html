United Nations General Assembly

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The United Nations General Assembly (UNGA) is one of the six principal organs of the United Nations, serving as its main deliberative, policymaking and representative organ. All 193 UN member states have equal representation and one vote each. The Assembly meets in regular annual sessions — opening each September with a general debate attended by heads of state and government — and in special and emergency sessions as required.

Functions

The Assembly may discuss and make recommendations on any matter within the UN Charter's scope (except disputes under active Security Council consideration, a limit loosened by the 1950 "Uniting for Peace" resolution). Its resolutions are generally recommendations rather than binding law, but carry political and moral weight and evidence of customary law; some decisions are internally binding, including approval of the UN budget, admission of new members (on Security Council recommendation), and election of the non-permanent Security Council members, ICJ judges and (on the Council's recommendation) the Secretary-General. The Assembly acts through six main committees and has created bodies such as the Human Rights Council and programmes including UNDP and UNICEF.

Historic acts

Landmark Assembly actions include the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), the partition plan for Palestine (1947), the Declaration on Decolonisation (1960), the creation of the state of Israel's admission framework, and the adoption of the Sustainable Development Goals (2015) and the Paris Agreement's political groundwork. Voting blocs — historically the Non-Aligned Movement and the Group of 77 — shape its politics.
