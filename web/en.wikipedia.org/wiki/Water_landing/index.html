Water landing

A floatplane making a routine water landing.

In aviation, a water landing is, in the broadest sense, an aircraft landing on a body of water. Seaplanes, such as floatplanes and flying boats, land on water as a normal operation. Ditching is a controlled emergency landing on the water surface, such as might occur when an airliner suffers engine failure or other malfunction over water; it is an infrequent and dangerous maneuver, distinct from a crash into water.

Ditching

Airliners are designed and certified with ditching in mind: certification rules require demonstration that the aircraft will float long enough for evacuation and that emergency exits remain usable, and safety briefings describe life vests, rafts and slides. Successful ditchings are rare because water behaves like concrete at high speed, waves can break up the airframe, and one wing dipping first can cartwheel the aircraft. The most famous successful ditching was US Airways Flight 1549, which lost both engines to a bird strike after departing New York in January 2009; Captain Chesley "Sully" Sullenberger ditched the Airbus A320 in the Hudson River, and all 155 people aboard survived — an event dubbed the "Miracle on the Hudson". Other survivable ditchings include Pan Am Flight 6 in 1956 and the controlled water landing of a Japan Airlines DC-8 in San Francisco Bay in 1968.

Routine water landings

For seaplanes, water landings are routine and require attention to wind direction, wave state, floating debris and boat traffic rather than runway conditions. Amphibious aircraft can land on either water or a prepared runway. Space capsules, notably those of Mercury, Gemini and Apollo, also made water landings ("splashdowns") as their normal recovery method.
