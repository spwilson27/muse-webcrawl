Sappho

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Sappho with her lyre on an Attic red-figure vase painting: the poetess of Lesbos reading a scroll, c. 5th century BC]

Sappho was an Archaic Greek poet from the island of Lesbos, born around 630 BC and writing in the Aeolic dialect around 600 BC. Alexandria's scholars ranked her among the Nine Lyric Poets and edited nine books of her work; almost all of it was lost in the Middle Ages, and what survives — one complete poem, the 'Ode to Aphrodite', and hundreds of fragments, some recovered from Egyptian papyrus in the last century — is among the most admired poetry in Greek.

Her poems, sung to the lyre, speak with unmatched directness of love, desire, jealousy and the gods: the fragment conventionally numbered 31, describing the symptoms of watching a beloved talk with another, was imitated by Catullus and remains a touchstone of lyric intensity.

Life and circle

Sappho was born into an aristocratic family on Lesbos, was exiled to Sicily around 600 BC amid the island's factional strife, and returned to lead a circle of young women — companions, pupils or cultic associates, scholars still argue — in Mytilene. The gossip of later comedy and biography (her supposed leap from the Leucadian rock for love of the ferryman Phaon) is mostly legend.

Survival and fame

Her 'Sapphic stanza' gave its name to a metre used by Horace and by poets into the modern era. New fragments continue to surface from papyrus — the 'Brothers Poem' published in 2014 is the latest major find — and translations by poets such as Anne Carson (If Not, Winter, 2002) have made Sappho a living presence in contemporary poetry, her name and island bequeathing the words 'sapphic' and 'lesbian'.
