Edward Donnall "Don" Thomas (March 15, 1920 – October 20, 2012) was an American physician and professor emeritus at the University of Washington and director emeritus of the clinical research division at the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center. In 1990 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Joseph E. Murray for their discoveries concerning "organ and cell transplantation in the treatment of human disease." This work demonstrated that severe leukemia and other hematologic diseases could be cured by transplanting bone marrow.

Early life and education

Thomas was born in Mart, Texas, and studied chemistry and chemical engineering at the University of Texas at Austin, earning bachelor's and master's degrees. He entered Harvard Medical School in 1943 and received his M.D. in 1946. His wife, Dorothy "Dottie" Thomas, trained as a laboratory technician and became a central collaborator in his research, sometimes called the "mother of bone marrow transplantation."

Career

At the Mary Imogene Bassett Hospital in Cooperstown, New York, beginning in the mid-1950s, Thomas began developing bone marrow transplantation as a treatment for leukemia. He moved to Seattle in 1963, joining the University of Washington School of Medicine, and later became the founding director of the division of medical oncology at what became the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center, where his team performed the first successful bone marrow transplants from matched unrelated donors and progressively improved survival for patients with leukemia and other blood disorders.

Key obstacles his team overcame included graft-versus-host disease, immune rejection of transplanted marrow, and developing conditioning regimens combining chemotherapy and total body irradiation. His demonstrations that graft-versus-host reactions could be controlled with drugs such as methotrexate, and that donor registries could supply matched marrow for unrelated patients, turned an experimental procedure into a standard, often curative therapy performed tens of thousands of times each year worldwide.

Honors

In addition to the Nobel Prize, Thomas received the National Medal of Science in 1990 and was elected to the National Academy of Sciences. He died in Seattle on October 20, 2012, at the age of 92.
