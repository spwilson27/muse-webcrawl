Forbidden graph characterization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In graph theory, a forbidden graph characterization is a description of a family of graphs as exactly those graphs that do not contain some specified graphs as subgraphs, induced subgraphs, or minors. The excluded graphs are called forbidden subgraphs (or forbidden minors, or forbidden induced subgraphs, depending on the relation). Characterisations of this kind turn a structural property into a membership test: a graph belongs to the family if and only if it avoids the forbidden configurations.

Famous examples

The archetype is Kuratowski's theorem (1930): a finite graph is planar if and only if it contains no subdivision of the complete graph K5 or the utility graph K3,3 — equivalently, by Wagner's theorem, no K5 or K3,3 minor. Forests are exactly the graphs with no cycle as a subgraph; bipartite graphs are those with no odd cycle; chordal graphs are characterised by forbidden induced subgraphs in another style, as graphs with no induced cycle of length four or more.

Minor-closed families

The celebrated Robertson–Seymour theorem (proved over a series of papers from 1983 to 2004) states that every minor-closed family of graphs can be characterised by a finite set of forbidden minors, because graphs are well-quasi-ordered under the minor relation. The theorem guarantees finiteness but does not by itself provide the list, and for many families the explicit forbidden sets remain unknown or enormous.

Uses

Forbidden characterisations matter algorithmically: if membership in a family is defined by finitely many forbidden minors, then for any fixed family there is a polynomial (indeed cubic) algorithm to test membership, via the machinery developed around the graph minors project.
