Jesse Gelsinger

Jesse Gelsinger (18 June 1981 – 17 September 1999) was an American teenager from Tucson, Arizona, who died while participating in a gene therapy clinical trial at the University of Pennsylvania. His death was the first publicly identified fatality directly attributed to gene therapy, and it had a lasting effect on the oversight of human genetic research in the United States.

Background

Gelsinger was born with a partial deficiency of ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC), a rare X-linked disorder of the urea cycle that prevents the liver from properly breaking down ammonia. The severe form is usually fatal in newborns, but Gelsinger's partial deficiency was managed with a low-protein diet and a regimen of dozens of pills a day; even so, he had suffered episodes of dangerously high ammonia, including a coma in his teens.

The trial and his death

In 1999 Gelsinger enrolled in a Phase I dose-escalation safety trial led by Dr. James M. Wilson at the Institute for Human Gene Therapy at the University of Pennsylvania. The trial was not expected to benefit the volunteers directly; it tested the safety of delivering a corrected OTC gene into the liver using an adenovirus vector. Gelsinger, the eighteenth and last patient treated and the first in the highest-dose cohort, received an infusion of some 3.8 trillion vector particles into his hepatic artery on 13 September 1999.

Within hours he developed a severe immune reaction — a massive inflammatory cascade that led to blood-clotting abnormalities, organ failure and brain death. He died on 17 September 1999, four days after the infusion.

Aftermath

Investigations by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and the National Institutes of Health found serious problems with the trial: adverse events in earlier patients and in animal studies had not been reported as required, some volunteers did not meet eligibility criteria, and the consent process had not adequately disclosed risks, including the deaths of monkeys given a similar vector. The university and Wilson also had financial interests in the technology through the company Genovo. Gelsinger's father, Paul Gelsinger, sued; the case was settled, with the university and other parties paying an undisclosed amount and issuing a public apology.

The FDA shut down Wilson's human trials, gene therapy research nationwide slowed for years, and the case became a standard study in research ethics, prompting tighter reporting rules for adverse events and closer scrutiny of investigators' conflicts of interest.
