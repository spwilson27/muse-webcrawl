Sovereign credit rating

Map of countries by sovereign credit rating

A sovereign credit rating is the credit rating of a sovereign entity, such as a national government. The sovereign credit rating indicates the risk level of the investing environment of a country and is used by investors when looking to invest in particular jurisdictions. It takes political risk into account.

Methodology

Sovereign credit ratings are assigned by credit rating agencies, principally the "Big Three": Moody's Ratings, S&P Global Ratings, and Fitch Ratings. Analysts weigh factors including:

  • Per-capita income and economic growth prospects
  • Fiscal performance — government revenue, expenditure, deficit and debt burdens
  • External debt and foreign-exchange reserves
  • Monetary flexibility and inflation history
  • Political stability and institutional effectiveness
  • The country's default history

Long-term ratings are expressed on alphabetic scales, from AAA (highest quality, minimal risk) down through investment-grade categories (BBB- and above) to speculative or "junk" grades, and ultimately to D or equivalent for default.

Importance

The rating strongly influences the interest rate a government must pay on its bonds: lower-rated sovereigns pay higher yields to compensate investors for risk. Ratings also act as a ceiling for the ratings of companies and banks domiciled in the country, and many institutional investors are required by mandate or regulation to hold only investment-grade debt. A downgrade can therefore trigger forced selling, capital flight, and currency depreciation — a dynamic that attracted heavy criticism during the European debt crisis, when downgrades of Greece, Portugal, and other eurozone states were accused of worsening the crisis they described.

Criticism

Agencies have been criticised for conflicts of interest (issuers pay for their own ratings), for reacting after markets rather than anticipating crises, and for the outsized macroeconomic consequences of downgrades. Reforms since the 2008 financial crisis have sought to reduce regulatory reliance on ratings, with mixed success.
