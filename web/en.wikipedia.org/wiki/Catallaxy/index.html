Catallaxy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Catallaxy (from the Greek katallasso, meaning "to exchange" and also "to admit into the community" or "to change from enemy into friend") is a term used by the Austrian economist Friedrich Hayek to describe the order brought about by the mutual adjustment of many individual economies in a market. Hayek introduced the term in his 1976 book Law, Legislation and Liberty, in part to avoid the word "economy," which he felt misleadingly suggested conscious management of scarce resources.

Concept

Hayek drew a contrast between a taxis, a deliberately made order (such as an organisation), and a cosmos, a spontaneous or grown order. A catallaxy is the paradigm case of a cosmos in human affairs: it arises not from anyone's design but from individuals pursuing their own purposes while following abstract rules of just conduct, particularly rules protecting property and honouring contracts. The price system, in Hayek's view, is the principal mechanism by which the dispersed knowledge of millions of participants is coordinated within a catallaxy.

The word was earlier used by the English economist Ludwig von Mises in a slightly different sense: Mises used "catallactics" as the name for the branch of economics concerned with market exchange and price formation, a usage he adopted in Human Action (1949).

Catallactics

In the Misesian tradition, catallactics is the theory of how market prices are formed through voluntary exchange. It is treated as a part of praxeology, the broader logic of human action. Under this usage, catallactics studies exchange ratios, money prices, and the coordination problems solved by entrepreneurial activity.
