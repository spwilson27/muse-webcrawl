Weasel word

A weasel word, or anonymous authority, is a word or phrase aimed at creating an impression that something specific and meaningful has been said, when in fact only a vague or ambiguous claim has been communicated. Examples include phrases such as "studies show," "some people say," "experts agree," and "it is often said." The use of weasel words gives the impression of authority without providing verifiable support.

Origin

The expression derives from the weasel's reputed ability to suck the contents from an egg while leaving the shell intact, so that an apparently whole egg is in fact empty. The term appeared in print in the early 20th century; Stewart Chaplin used it in a 1900 short story, and William Allen White popularized it in editorials. Theodore Roosevelt complained in 1916 of the "weasel word" in Woodrow Wilson's statements, helping the phrase enter general usage.

Forms

Common devices include unattributed authorities ("researchers have found…"), vague quantification ("up to 50% more"), passive constructions that omit the actor ("mistakes were made"), and adverbs such as "clearly" or "interestingly" that assert rather than demonstrate. Advertising is a frequent habitat: claims that a product "helps fight" a condition or "virtually eliminates" a problem are hedged to remain technically defensible.

On Wikipedia

Wikipedia's style guidelines advise editors to avoid weasel words in encyclopedic writing, attributing claims to identifiable sources instead of constructions like "some critics argue." The guideline page "Words to watch" catalogues these and similar expressions, including peacock terms and editorializing.
