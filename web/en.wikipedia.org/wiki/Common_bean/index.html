Common bean

Phaseolus vulgaris, the common bean, is a herbaceous annual plant grown worldwide for its edible dry seeds or green, unripe pods. Its leaf is also occasionally used as a vegetable and the straw as fodder. Its botanical classification, along with other Phaseolus species, is as a member of the legume family Fabaceae. Like most members of this family, common beans acquire the nitrogen they require through an association with rhizobia, which are nitrogen-fixing bacteria.

History

The common bean is one of the longest-cultivated plants in the Americas. Genetic evidence indicates it was domesticated independently in two regions: Mesoamerica and the southern Andes. Along with squash and maize, beans form the "Three Sisters" of traditional companion planting in the Americas. The plant was introduced to Europe following the Columbian exchange and from there spread to Africa and Asia.

Varieties

Thousands of cultivars exist, varying in seed size, colour and pattern. Well-known dry bean types include the kidney bean, pinto bean, black bean, navy bean and cannellini bean, while green beans (French beans) are harvested immature and eaten in their pods. Popular varieties include 'Blue Lake', 'Borlotto', 'Bush Romano' and 'Scarlet Runner' (a related species).

Cultivation

Common beans grow as either bushy, determinate plants or climbing, indeterminate vines requiring support. They are frost-sensitive and thrive in warm weather with well-drained soil. Beans are typically sown after the last frost and harvested either green or after the pods have dried on the plant.

Nutrition and toxins

Dry beans are a rich source of protein, complex carbohydrates, folate, iron and dietary fibre. Raw or undercooked beans, especially red kidney beans, contain the lectin phytohaemagglutinin, which is toxic; soaking and boiling beans thoroughly destroys the toxin.
