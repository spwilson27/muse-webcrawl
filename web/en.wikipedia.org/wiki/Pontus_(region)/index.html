Pontus (region)

Pontus (Greek: Πόντος, romanized: Pontos, "sea") is a historical region on the southern coast of the Black Sea, located in modern-day eastern Turkey. The name derives from the Greek word for the sea itself, the Pontus Euxinus ("Hospitable Sea"). The region is bounded by the Pontic Mountains to the south and includes the ancient cities of Trapezus (Trabzon), Amisus (Samsun), and Sinope. It was home to the Kingdom of Pontus, ruled by the Mithridatic dynasty, and remained predominantly Greek-speaking until the population exchange of 1923.

History

Greek colonists from Miletus founded Sinope and Trapezus in the eighth century BCE, and the coastal cities prospered on trade in grain, fish, timber, and slaves. Inland, the region was inhabited by peoples such as the Chalybes, famous as iron-workers, and was ruled in turn by the Achaemenid Persians as part of their satrapies. After Alexander the Great's conquests, Mithridates I Ktistes founded the Kingdom of Pontus around 281 BCE. His descendant Mithridates VI Eupator (r. 120–63 BCE) expanded the kingdom around the Black Sea and into Greece, fighting three wars against Rome before his defeat by Pompey.

Under Roman and then Byzantine rule, Pontus was divided among provinces including Pontus Polemoniacus and Helenopontus; the city of Trebizond grew wealthy on Silk Road trade. After the Fourth Crusade in 1204, the Empire of Trebizond, ruled by the Komnenos dynasty, made the region an independent Greek state until its conquest by the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II in 1461. Pontic Greek communities — merchants, miners, and farmers — survived under Ottoman rule for nearly five centuries, retaining a distinctive dialect (Pontic Greek, or Romeyka).

Twentieth century

During and after the First World War, the Pontic Greeks suffered massacres, death marches, and deportations at the hands of the Ottoman authorities and Turkish nationalist forces — events widely recognised by scholars as part of the Greek genocide. The 1923 Treaty of Lausanne's population exchange transferred some 350,000 surviving Pontic Greeks to Greece, ending over two and a half millennia of Greek presence in the region. In Greece, Pontic refugees preserved their music (the Pontic lyra, or kemenche), dances, and dialect; 19 May is commemorated as Pontic Greek Genocide Remembrance Day.

Geography

Pontus is a narrow coastal strip between the Black Sea and the Pontic (North Anatolian) Mountains, watered by rivers such as the Halys (Kızılırmak), Iris (Yeşilırmak), and Harşit. Its humid climate supports hazelnuts, tea, and tobacco, and the region today corresponds roughly to Turkey's eastern Black Sea provinces, including Trabzon, Samsun, Ordu, Giresun, Rize, and Amasya inland.
