The mediastinum (from Medieval Latin mediastinus, "midway") is the central compartment of the thoracic cavity. Surrounded by loose connective tissue, it is the region between the two pleural cavities (lungs) and contains all the principal tissues and organs of the chest except the lungs themselves, including the heart and its great vessels, the oesophagus, trachea, phrenic and cardiac nerves, the thoracic duct, the thymus and the lymph nodes of the central chest.

Divisions

For anatomical description the mediastinum is divided at the level of the sternal angle (T4/T5) into a superior mediastinum above and an inferior mediastinum below. The inferior part is subdivided by the pericardium into the anterior mediastinum (between sternum and pericardium, containing fat, lymph nodes and, in children, the thymus), the middle mediastinum (the heart within the pericardium, the roots of the great vessels, the tracheal bifurcation and main bronchi), and the posterior mediastinum (behind the pericardium, containing the oesophagus, descending aorta, azygos system of veins, thoracic duct and vagus nerves).

Clinical significance

The mediastinum is a common site of tumours (mediastinal masses), whose likely identity is taught by compartment: the anterior compartment classically hosts the "4 Ts" — thymoma, teratoma (and other germ-cell tumours), thyroid masses and "terrible" lymphoma. Widening of the mediastinum on a chest radiograph can indicate aortic dissection or rupture; air in the mediastinum (pneumomediastinum) may arise from oesophageal perforation (Boerhaave syndrome) or barotrauma; infection of the compartment (mediastinitis) is a surgical emergency. The region is accessed surgically by median sternotomy or by mediastinoscopy for lymph-node staging of lung cancer.
