Optimal tax

Optimal tax theory is the study of designing and implementing a tax that maximises a social welfare function subject to economic constraints, or equivalently minimises the total distortion and efficiency cost of raising a given amount of revenue. The field, founded largely by Frank Ramsey (1927) and James Mirrlees (1971), underlies modern thinking about income and commodity taxation.

Optimal commodity taxation: the Ramsey rule

Frank Ramsey asked how commodity taxes should be set to raise fixed revenue with minimum distortion. The Ramsey rule implies that tax rates should be higher on goods with inelastic demand and lower on goods with elastic demand — the "inverse elasticity rule" — spreading the distortion where it changes behaviour least. Because necessities tend to have inelastic demand, pure Ramsey taxation can be regressive, motivating later work combining efficiency with equity.

Optimal income taxation: Mirrlees

James Mirrlees modelled income taxation when the government cannot observe individuals' ability, only their earnings. Because a tax on earnings distorts labour supply, the optimal schedule balances revenue and redistribution against incentive effects. Mirrlees's results — for which he shared the 1996 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics — suggested surprisingly low marginal rates at the top of the distribution in the original model, though later work with different assumptions about earnings distributions supported higher top rates.

Later developments

The Atkinson–Stiglitz theorem (1976) showed that with an optimal nonlinear income tax and weak separability of preferences, differential commodity taxes are unnecessary. Diamond and Saez have developed "sufficient statistics" approaches that express optimal rates in terms of measurable elasticities and distributional parameters.
