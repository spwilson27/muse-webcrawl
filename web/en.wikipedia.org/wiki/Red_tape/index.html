Red tape

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Red tape is a term for excessive regulation or rigid conformity to formal rules that is considered redundant or bureaucratic and hinders or prevents action or decision-making. The phrase derives from the practice, attested from the sixteenth century in Spain and later in Britain, of binding official documents with red cloth tape; Charles Dickens and Thomas Carlyle popularised its pejorative use for officialdom's delays.

Public administration scholarship distinguishes red tape properly so called — rules whose costs exceed their benefits or which no longer serve their purpose — from necessary due process (procurement controls, environmental assessment, anti-corruption checks) that critics may lump with it. Reform cycles recur: deregulation drives, “one-in, one-out” rules for new regulation, sunset clauses, digital one-stop government, and regulatory budgets. Empirical studies find perceived red tape concentrated at organisational interfaces (grants, permits, compliance reporting) and correlated with lower public-service satisfaction — while also finding that some despised “tape” protects equity, legality and the audit trail that makes administration answerable.
