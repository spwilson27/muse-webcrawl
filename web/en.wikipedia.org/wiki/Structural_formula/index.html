Structural formula

Full (left) and skeletal (right) structural formulas of ethanol-like molecules show atom connectivity and bond orders.

The structural formula of a chemical compound is a graphic representation of the molecular structure, showing how atoms are arranged and bonded together in the three-dimensional space. A structural formula provides more information than a molecular formula (which counts atoms only) or an empirical formula: it indicates connectivity and often bond orders and approximate geometry. Structural formulas are the language of organic chemistry and underlie the concept of structural isomers — compounds with the same molecular formula but different structures.

Types

  • Full (expanded) structural formula — every atom and every bond is drawn explicitly; hydrogen atoms are shown.
  • Condensed structural formula — written in line, grouping atoms, e.g. ethanol as CH3CH2OH; branches are put in parentheses.
  • Skeletal (line-angle) formula — carbon atoms sit at vertices and line ends, hydrogens on carbons are implicit, and heteroatoms are labelled. The standard depiction in organic chemistry.
  • Lewis structure — shows valence electrons as dots and shared pairs, revealing formal charges and lone pairs.
  • Wedge–dash notation — solid wedges (coming out) and hashed wedges (going back) indicate stereochemistry at stereocentres.
  • Newman and Fischer projections — two-dimensional projections capturing conformation or configuration.
  • Kekulé structures — alternating double bonds for aromatic rings (e.g., benzene), versus circle notation.

History

Structural theory was founded in 1857–1861 by August Kekulé and Archibald Scott Couper (carbon's tetravalence and self-linking) and Alexander Butlerov, who coined the term "chemical structure" in 1861. Jacobus van 't Hoff and Joseph Le Bel added three-dimensional stereochemistry in 1874. The conventions were standardized through the 20th century and are now encoded digitally in line notations such as SMILES and InChI.

Relation to other formulas

A structural formula differs from a molecular formula (e.g. C2H6O) because the same molecular formula corresponds to distinct structural formulas — ethanol and dimethyl ether — while condensed and skeletal formulas compress the full structure to different degrees without losing connectivity.
