The Battle of the Allia was a catastrophic defeat of the Roman Republic by the Senones, a Gallic tribe under their chief Brennus, fought on 18 July in 390 BC (or 387 BC) at the Allia River, a small tributary of the Tiber about eleven miles north of Rome. The defeat left Rome open to the famous Gallic sack of the city.

Background

The Senones had crossed the Apennines and besieged the Etruscan city of Clusium, whose envoys appealed to Rome. According to Livy, Roman ambassadors violated the law of nations by fighting against the Gauls, and the Senate's refusal to surrender the offenders provoked Brennus to march on Rome itself.

The battle

The Romans deployed in a thin line stretched to avoid being outflanked, weakening their centre and placing reserves on high ground to the right. Brennus attacked those reserves first, fearing a trick; their collapse panicked the whole Roman army, which fled across the Tiber to Veii rather than back to Rome. Many soldiers drowned in the river.

Aftermath

With no army left to defend it, Rome was occupied and sacked by the Gauls; only the Capitoline Hill held out. The anniversary of the battle, the dies Alliensis (18 July), was thereafter marked in the Roman calendar as a day of ill omen. The disaster prompted military reforms traditionally credited to Marcus Furius Camillus, honoured as the "second founder of Rome".
