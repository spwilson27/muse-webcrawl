The Free Speech Movement (FSM) was a massive, lengthy student protest that took place during the 1964–65 academic year on the campus of the University of California, Berkeley. Led by Mario Savio, Jack Weinberg and others — many of them veterans of civil rights work in the South — the movement demanded that the university administration lift its ban on on-campus political activities and acknowledge students' rights to free speech and academic freedom.


Students rallying at Sproul Plaza, 1964

Origins

In September 1964 the administration prohibited political tables and advocacy at the traditional Bancroft strip area, citing rules against campus fundraising and recruiting for off-campus political action. Students who had just returned from Freedom Summer in Mississippi saw the ban as an attempt to stifle civil rights organising.

Escalation

On October 1, 1964, Jack Weinberg was arrested for manning a CORE table; students surrounded the police car, trapping it for 32 hours while speakers, including Mario Savio, addressed the crowd from its roof. In December students occupied Sproul Hall, the administration building; nearly 800 were arrested in one of the largest mass arrests in California history. Savio's "bodies upon the gears" speech, delivered on the Sproul Hall steps, became a classic of protest rhetoric.

Outcome and legacy

In January 1965 the Berkeley faculty and the new acting chancellor conceded most demands, and campus speech rules were liberalised. The FSM is widely regarded as the first major movement of the 1960s student revolt, a model and inspiration for protests against the Vietnam War and for university reform around the world.
