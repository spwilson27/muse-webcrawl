Palpation

Palpation is the process of using one's hands to check the body, especially while perceiving or diagnosing a disease or illness. Usually performed by a health care practitioner, it is the process of feeling an object in or on the body to determine its size, shape, firmness, or location — for example, a veterinarian can feel the stomach of a pregnant animal, or a midwife can feel the position of a fetus.

Palpation is an important part of the physical examination; the sense of touch is just as important in this examination as the sense of sight. Physicians develop great skill in palpating problems below the surface of the body, becoming able to detect things that untrained persons would not. Mastery of anatomy and practice are required to achieve a high level of skill. The concept of palpation also extends to animals and even to engineers detecting faults in structures by touch.

Uses

Palpation is used by physicians, nurses, paramedics, physical therapists, chiropractors, osteopaths and veterinarians for many purposes, including:

  • Assessing the texture, temperature, moisture and mobility of the skin and underlying tissues.
  • Locating and characterising swellings, lumps and tumours — their size, consistency, mobility and tenderness.
  • Examining the abdomen for organ enlargement (such as liver and spleen), masses, tenderness, guarding and rigidity.
  • Feeling pulses to assess rate, rhythm and character of blood flow.
  • Assessing the chest for tactile fremitus and the respiratory excursion.
  • Evaluating joints and muscles for swelling, crepitus and range of motion.
  • Obstetric palpation to determine fetal position, presentation and engagement.

Technique

Palpation may be light or deep. Light palpation, using the fingertips with gentle pressure, detects surface characteristics and tenderness. Deep palpation uses firmer pressure, sometimes with both hands (bimanual palpation), to feel internal organs and deep masses. Ballottement — pushing an organ or fluid-containing structure and feeling it rebound against the fingers — is a specialised technique used for floating structures such as a fetus or an enlarged kidney. Palpation generally begins in non-painful areas and moves toward painful ones, and should be performed with warm hands and with the patient relaxed.

Clinical significance

Findings from palpation guide further investigation: a palpable abdominal mass, an enlarged liver edge, absent pulses or localised tenderness each narrow the differential diagnosis before any imaging or laboratory tests are performed. Although modern imaging has supplemented manual examination, palpation remains a rapid, inexpensive and information-rich component of clinical assessment.
