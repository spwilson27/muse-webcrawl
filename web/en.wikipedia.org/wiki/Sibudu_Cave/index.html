Sibudu Cave

TypeRock shelter

Sibudu Cave is a rock shelter in a sandstone cliff above the uThongathi River, about 40 km north of Durban in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa. It is one of the most important Middle Stone Age sites in the world, with a long, well-preserved sequence of occupation layers dating from roughly 77,000 to 38,000 years ago, spanning the Still Bay, Howiesons Poort, and later Middle Stone Age industries.

Findings

Excavations directed by Lyn Wadley (from 1998) and subsequently Nicholas Conard's team have produced a wealth of evidence for early modern human behaviour. Finds include bifacial points of the Still Bay industry; the small backed segments and bone tools of the Howiesons Poort; some of the earliest known bone arrow points and a bone needle; compound adhesives made from red ochre and plant gum used to haft stone tools; a probable early pressure-flaked point; and evidence interpreted as snares and traps. Plant remains are exceptionally well preserved: bedding layers of sedges and leaves, some topped with insect-repellent aromatic leaves (such as river wild-quince, Cryptocarya woodii) dated to about 77,000 years ago — among the earliest known examples of plant bedding — and traces of cooked starchy rhizomes from about 70,000 years ago.

Significance

Along with sites such as Blombos Cave and Diepkloof, Sibudu informs debates about the origin of behavioural modernity: its occupants demonstrated hafting technology, long-distance planning, pyrotechnology, hunting of dangerous game such as buffalo and bushpig in the surrounding forest, and flexible adaptation to changing environments at the end of the Pleistocene.
