Vacuum

A vacuum (plural vacuums or vacua) is a space entirely devoid of matter. The word stems from the Latin adjective vacuus for "vacant" or "void". An approximation to such a vacuum is a region with a gaseous pressure much less than atmospheric pressure: physicists often discuss ideal test results that would occur in a perfect vacuum, while engineers use the term "partial vacuum" for the imperfect vacuums achievable in practice.

History

Whether nature "abhors a vacuum" was debated from Aristotle through the Middle Ages. In 1643 Evangelista Torricelli produced the first sustained laboratory vacuum at the top of his mercury barometer. Otto von Guericke invented the vacuum pump (c. 1650) and dramatized atmospheric pressure with the Magdeburg hemispheres in 1654.

Measurement and uses

Vacuum is measured in pascals (SI) or torr, using gauges from simple manometers to ionization gauges for ultra-high vacuum. Vacuum technology underpins vacuum tubes, particle accelerators, semiconductor fabrication, freeze-drying, mass spectrometry and electron microscopy.

Quantum vacuum

Even a "perfect" vacuum is not empty in quantum field theory: it contains fluctuating electromagnetic waves and virtual particle–antiparticle pairs, producing measurable effects such as the Casimir effect and contributing the vacuum (zero-point) energy. Interstellar space, with only a few hydrogen atoms per cubic metre, is a better vacuum than any produced on Earth.
