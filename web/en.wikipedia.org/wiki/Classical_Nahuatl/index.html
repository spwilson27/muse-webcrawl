Classical Nahuatl

Classical Nahuatl (Nahuatl: Nāhuatlahtōlli) is the variety of the Nahuatl language that was spoken as a lingua franca in the Valley of Mexico and central Mexico at the time of the 16th-century Spanish conquest, and which became the basis of a large written literature produced during the colonial period. It was the prestige language of the Aztec (Mexica) empire and was used in administration and oratory across Mesoamerica.

Corpus

After the conquest, Nahua scribes and Franciscan friars adapted the Latin alphabet to Nahuatl, producing an enormous documentary record: annals, wills, petitions, religious texts, songs (cantares), and the great ethnographic encyclopaedia compiled under Bernardino de Sahagún, the Florentine Codex (Historia general de las cosas de Nueva España). Carochi's grammar of 1645 is considered the finest colonial description of the language.

Features

Classical Nahuatl is a polysynthetic, agglutinative language with complex verb morphology marking subject, object, applicative, causative, and honorific forms. It has a small phoneme inventory including the distinctive lateral affricate /tɬ/ (written tl), long and short vowels, and a saltillo (glottal stop). Difrasismo — paired metaphors such as in xōchitl in cuīcatl ("the flower, the song", meaning poetry) — characterises its formal rhetoric. Modern Nahuatl varieties descend from related dialects; many Spanish and English words (tomato, chocolate, coyote, avocado) derive from Nahuatl.
