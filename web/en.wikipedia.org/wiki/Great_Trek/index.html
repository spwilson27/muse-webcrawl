Great Trek

The Great Trek (Afrikaans: Die Groot Trek) was a migration of Dutch-speaking colonists, known as Boers, from the British-ruled Cape Colony in southern Africa into the interior of the continent during the 1830s and 1840s. The emigrants, called Voortrekkers, sought to escape British administration and to found independent republics beyond the colonial frontier.

Causes

Among the grievances driving the emigration were the anglicisation of administration and law under British rule, frontier insecurity and disputes over colonial defence policy, restrictions on the expansion of pastoral farming, and the abolition of slavery in 1834, whose compensation arrangements embittered slave-owning farmers. A manifesto issued by Piet Retief in 1837 set out the emigrants' case.

Course of the trek

Between 1835 and the mid-1840s some 12,000 to 14,000 Boers left the Cape in organised wagon parties. Crossing the Orange River and the Drakensberg, they fought the Ndebele of Mzilikazi, whom they drove north beyond the Limpopo, and the Zulu kingdom. After Retief and his party were killed by Dingane in February 1838, Zulu attacks on trekker camps followed; the trekkers under Andries Pretorius won a decisive victory at the Battle of Blood River on 16 December 1838.

Legacy

The trekker republics — Natalia, the Orange Free State, and the South African Republic (Transvaal), among smaller states — shaped the political map of southern Africa for the rest of the 19th century. The Great Trek became a central event in Afrikaner national mythology, commemorated in the Day of the Vow and the Voortrekker Monument in Pretoria, inaugurated at the 1949 centenary celebrations.
