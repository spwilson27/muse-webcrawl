Gravimetric analysis

Gravimetric analysis
FieldAnalytical chemistry
PrincipleDetermination of an analyte from the mass of a related pure substance
Main typesPrecipitation, volatilisation, electrogravimetry, thermogravimetry

Gravimetric analysis describes a set of methods used in analytical chemistry for the quantitative determination of an analyte based on its mass. The analyte is converted, physically or chemically, into a substance of known composition that can be isolated and weighed; from the mass of that product and the stoichiometry of the conversion, the amount of the original analyte is calculated.

Precipitation gravimetry

In precipitation gravimetry, the analyte is converted to a sparingly soluble precipitate by addition of a precipitating reagent. The precipitate is filtered, washed free of impurities, dried or ignited to a compound of known composition, and weighed. Classic examples include the determination of chloride as silver chloride, sulfate as barium sulfate, and nickel with dimethylglyoxime. A related quantity, the gravimetric factor, converts the mass of the weighed form into the mass of the analyte sought.

Good precipitates should be highly insoluble, easily filterable, pure, and of known and stable composition. Slow precipitation from hot, dilute solution and a digestion (ageing) step are used to obtain larger, purer crystals, minimising errors from co-precipitation and adsorption of impurities.

Volatilisation gravimetry

In volatilisation methods the analyte, or a decomposition product of it, is vaporised at a suitable temperature and either collected and weighed, or the loss of mass of the sample is measured. Examples include determining the water content of a hydrate by heating, and determining carbon dioxide in a carbonate by treatment with acid. Thermogravimetric analysis extends this idea by recording mass continuously as temperature is raised.

Electrogravimetry and other forms

In electrogravimetry the analyte is deposited electrolytically onto a weighed electrode, commonly used for metals such as copper. Particulate gravimetry determines analytes already present as suspended particles, such as suspended solids in water samples.

Advantages and limitations

Gravimetric methods are among the most accurate in analytical chemistry, with relative errors often below 0.1%, and they require only simple equipment — principally an analytical balance. They are therefore used as reference methods. Their disadvantages are that they are slow, labour-intensive, usually applicable to a single analyte at a time, and require the analyte to be present in relatively large amounts.
