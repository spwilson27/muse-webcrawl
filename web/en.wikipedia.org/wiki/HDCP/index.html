HDCP

High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP) is a form of digital copy protection developed by Intel Corporation to prevent copying of digital audio and video content as it travels across connections such as HDMI, DVI and DisplayPort. The specification is proprietary and is licensed by Digital Content Protection LLC.

How it works

HDCP encrypts the signal between a source device (such as a Blu-ray player or streaming box) and a display. Before transmitting content, the source authenticates the receiving device using a set of secret device keys administered by the licensing authority; receivers found on a revocation list can be blocked. The content itself is encrypted during transmission so that unauthorised devices cannot record the stream in usable form.

Versions

HDCP 1.x is used with DVI and HDMI connections up to HD resolutions. HDCP 2.2, based on different cryptography, is required for protected 4K Ultra HD content over HDMI 2.0 and is mandated by most streaming services for UHD playback. HDCP 2.3 followed with minor updates.

Criticism and circumvention

The security of HDCP 1.x was undermined in 2010 when a master key, apparently derived from a leaked or reverse-engineered source, was published, allowing construction of unauthorised decryption devices. Critics also note that HDCP can cause compatibility problems between otherwise functional devices, and security researchers have questioned restrictions on studying the system under anti-circumvention law.
