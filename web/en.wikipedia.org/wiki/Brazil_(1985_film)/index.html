Brazil (1985 film)

Brazil is a 1985 dystopian science fiction film directed by Terry Gilliam and written by Gilliam, Charles McKeown, and Tom Stoppard. It stars Jonathan Pryce as Sam Lowry, a low-level bureaucrat in a totalitarian, paperwork-choked future society who daydreams of heroic escape and becomes entangled with the woman from his dreams and a renegade repairman. The cast includes Robert De Niro, Kim Greist, Michael Palin, Katherine Helmond, Bob Hoskins, and Ian Holm. The title refers to the 1939 Ary Barroso song "Aquarela do Brasil", used as a recurring musical motif.

Plot

In a society dominated by the Ministry of Information, a clerical error — a fly falling into a printer, changing "Tuttle" to "Buttle" — causes the arrest and death of an innocent man, Archibald Buttle. Sam Lowry, who must correct the resulting wrongful charge, becomes fascinated by Buttle's upstairs neighbour Jill Layton, the woman who appears in his dreams. Meanwhile the authorities hunt Archibald Tuttle (Robert De Niro), a freelance heating engineer who repairs systems without authorisation. Sam's attempts to help Jill draw the attention of the state; captured, he is strapped into a torture chair by his friend Jack Lint (Michael Palin) and escapes only into a permanent fantasy, a twist ending in which his apparent rescue is revealed as delusion.

Production and release dispute

Gilliam's visual style blends 1930s–40s technology with futuristic elements, shot largely in England and France. The film is famous for the battle over its American release: Universal Pictures, deeming Gilliam's ending too bleak, prepared a shortened 94-minute version with a happy ending, the so-called "Love Conquers All" cut. Gilliam campaigned publicly against the studio, and the Los Angeles Film Critics Association awarded the unreleased director's version its Best Picture prize, pressuring Universal into releasing a Gilliam-approved 132-minute version in the United States.

Reception and legacy

Brazil was nominated for two Academy Awards (Best Original Screenplay and Best Art Direction) and won two BAFTAs. Initially a modest commercial performer, it has become a cult classic and is routinely ranked among the greatest science fiction films, praised for its production design, satire of bureaucracy and surveillance, and bleakly comic tone. It is often read alongside Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four and as part of Gilliam's informal "Trilogy of Imagination" with Time Bandits (1981) and The Adventures of Baron Munchausen (1988).
