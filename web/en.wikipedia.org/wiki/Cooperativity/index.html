Cooperativity

Cooperativity is a phenomenon displayed by systems involving identical or near-identical elements that act in a non-independent manner, so that the binding or activity at one site affects the others. In biochemistry, it most famously describes ligand binding to a macromolecule with multiple binding sites: if binding of the first ligand increases the affinity of the remaining sites, the cooperativity is positive; if it decreases affinity, it is negative; if affinity is unchanged, binding is non-cooperative.

Haemoglobin

The classic example is oxygen binding to haemoglobin. Christian Bohr described the sigmoid shape of its dissociation curve in 1904, and Archibald Hill's 1910 analysis yielded the Hill equation and coefficient:

Y = [L]ⁿ / (K + [L]ⁿ)

where n (the Hill coefficient) is about 2.8 for haemoglobin, between 1 (no cooperativity) and the maximum of 4 (its number of subunits). Structurally, oxygen binding shifts haemoglobin from the tense (T) to the relaxed (R) state, increasing the affinity of unoccupied subunits — an allosteric mechanism modelled by the concerted model of Monod, Wyman and Changeux (1965) and the sequential model of Koshland, Némethy and Filmer (1966).

Other forms

Cooperativity also appears in enzyme kinetics (allosteric enzymes with sigmoidal rate curves), in DNA binding by transcription factors, in supramolecular chemistry (chelate and self-assembly effects), and in phenomena such as cooperative spin transitions and cooperative motion in glassy materials. Homotropic cooperativity concerns identical ligands; heterotropic effects involve different molecules, as when 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate lowers haemoglobin's oxygen affinity.

Significance

Positive cooperativity sharpens responses: over a narrow range of ligand concentration, occupancy switches from mostly empty to mostly full, which suits physiological delivery (loading O₂ in the lungs, unloading in tissues) and switch-like regulation in signalling and gene expression.
