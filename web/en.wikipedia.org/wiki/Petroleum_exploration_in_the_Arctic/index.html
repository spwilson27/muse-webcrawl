Petroleum exploration in the Arctic is the search for oil and natural gas deposits in the region north of the Arctic Circle, both onshore and in the offshore waters of the Arctic Ocean. A 2008 appraisal by the United States Geological Survey estimated that the Arctic contains about 90 billion barrels of undiscovered oil and some 1,670 trillion cubic feet of natural gas — roughly 13 percent of the world's undiscovered oil and 30 percent of its undiscovered gas — most of it offshore.

History

Onshore Arctic production began in the mid-twentieth century, notably at Prudhoe Bay in Alaska (discovered 1968) and in Russia's West Siberian basin. Offshore exploration expanded into the Beaufort Sea, the Barents Sea and the Canadian Arctic islands, but extreme cold, sea ice, remote locations and long supply lines make Arctic operations among the most expensive and technically difficult in the industry. Major projects include Norway's Snøhvit and Goliat fields in the Barents Sea.

Environmental and political issues

Exploration has been controversial. Environmental groups warn that an oil spill in ice-covered waters would be exceptionally hard to clean up, and climate advocates argue that Arctic oil is incompatible with emissions targets. Shell's troubled Chukchi Sea campaign, abandoned in 2015 after billions in spending, illustrated commercial and operational risk. Meanwhile, Arctic coastal states have advanced overlapping claims to extended continental shelves under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, partly motivated by resource potential.
