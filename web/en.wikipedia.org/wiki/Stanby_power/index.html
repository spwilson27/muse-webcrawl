Standby power

Standby power, also known as vampire power, vampire draw, phantom load, ghost load or leaking electricity, refers to the electric power consumed by electronic and electrical appliances while they are switched off (but still plugged in) or in standby mode. Appliances draw this power to maintain remote-control readiness, display clocks, keep network connections alive, or power chargers and external power supplies.

Scale

Individual loads are small — commonly 0.5 to 10 watts — but they operate continuously, and the typical home contains dozens of such devices. Studies by the International Energy Agency and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory have estimated that standby consumption accounts for roughly 5–10% of residential electricity use in developed countries, costing consumers billions of dollars and requiring the output of many power stations worldwide. Televisions, set-top boxes (notably older DVRs, which could draw nearly full power in standby), game consoles, computers and chargers left plugged in are major contributors.

Reduction and regulation

The International Energy Agency's "One Watt Initiative" (1999) set a target of reducing standby power to one watt or less per device by 2010. The European Union's Ecodesign Regulation (EC) No 1275/2008 capped standby consumption at 1 W, later 0.5 W for many products, and similar limits appear in standards in California, South Korea, Australia and elsewhere. Practical countermeasures include unplugging devices, using switchable power strips, smart plugs and timers, and choosing products with low standby ratings. Modern regulations and better power-supply design have sharply reduced per-device standby draw, though the growing number of always-connected devices offsets some gains.
