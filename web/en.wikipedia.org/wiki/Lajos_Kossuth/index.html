Lajos Kossuth de Udvard et Kossuthfalva (19 September 1802 – 20 March 1894) was a Hungarian nobleman, lawyer, journalist, politician, statesman and governor-president of the Kingdom of Hungary during the revolution and war of independence of 1848–1849. He is widely regarded as the greatest Hungarian statesman of the 19th century and a symbol of Hungarian national identity.

Early life

Kossuth was born in Monok, in Zemplén County, into a landless noble family of Slovak origin. He studied law at the University of Pest and began his career as a lawyer. His reputation grew after he began reporting on the Diet of Hungary; his handwritten parliamentary reports, circulated despite Habsburg censorship, made him famous and led to his imprisonment from 1837 to 1840 on charges of high treason.

Reform era

After his release, Kossuth became editor of the newspaper Pesti Hírlap in 1841, which under his direction became the leading voice of the liberal reform opposition. He advocateded the abolition of serfdom, taxation of the nobility, and the transformation of Hungary into a modern constitutional state, while his rivals, notably István Széchenyi, feared his nationalism would provoke conflict with Vienna.

Revolution of 1848

In March 1848, inspired by revolution in Paris and Vienna, Kossuth's speech of 3 March demanding a constitution for the Habsburg lands helped ignite the Hungarian Revolution. He became minister of finance in the first responsible Hungarian government of Lajos Batthyány, organised the national defence, raised an army, and issued paper money. When Habsburg forces invaded, the Diet elected him head of the Committee of National Defence, making him effective ruler of Hungary.

Governor-President and exile

On 14 April 1849 the Diet at Debrecen proclaimed the dethronement of the Habsburgs and Hungarian independence, electing Kossuth governor-president. The revolution was crushed after Russian intervention on Austria's behalf; Kossuth resigned in August 1849 and fled into exile. He toured Britain and the United States in 1851–1852, where he was received with extraordinary public enthusiasm, though he failed to win foreign intervention for Hungary. He spent his final decades in Turin, Italy, refusing amnesty because it required acknowledging Habsburg rule. He died in 1894; his remains were returned to Budapest and buried amid national mourning.

Legacy

Kossuth's name remains central to Hungarian national symbolism: the Kossuth Lajos Square in front of the Hungarian Parliament, statues across the country, and the folk song "Kossuth Lajos azt üzente" all testify to his enduring place in Hungarian memory. His celebrated oratory also influenced democratic movements abroad; a statue of Kossuth stands in the United States Capitol.
