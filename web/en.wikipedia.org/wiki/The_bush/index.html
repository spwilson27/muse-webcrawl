The bush

"The bush" is a term mostly used in Australia and New Zealand to describe natural, undeveloped land, typically covered with native vegetation, as opposed to settled or cultivated areas. In Australia it has become a central concept of national identity, celebrated in the "bush ballads" and literature of writers such as Banjo Paterson and Henry Lawson.

Usage in Australia

In Australian English, "the bush" denotes any rural or wilderness area away from cities, whether forest, scrub or desert fringe, and by extension the people who live there — "bush people". Derived expressions include bushfire, bushwalking (hiking), bush tucker (native foods), bush telegraph (the informal spread of news by word of mouth) and going bush (retreating into the wild or disappearing). The phrase "the bush capital" is a nickname for Canberra, set amid native woodland.

Usage in New Zealand

In New Zealand, "the bush" refers especially to dense native forest — podocarp and beech forest — and the associated logging and tramping culture. Clearing the bush for pasture was a major theme of nineteenth-century settlement.

Other uses

The term is also used in Canada (particularly for the forested north), South Africa (as bos, or in "bushveld"), Alaska ("the Bush" for remote communities off the road network) and parts of the southern United States. In military and African contexts, a "bush war" is a conflict fought in remote terrain.

Cultural significance

The Australian bush legend — of resourceful, egalitarian mateship in a harsh landscape — was shaped by the Bulletin school of the 1890s and remains a defining element of Australian culture, even though most Australians now live in cities.
