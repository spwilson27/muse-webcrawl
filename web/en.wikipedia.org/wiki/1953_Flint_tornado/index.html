The 1953 Flint tornado, also called the Flint–Beecher tornado, struck the northern suburbs of Flint, Michigan, on the evening of Monday, 8 June 1953. The massive F5 tornado killed 116 people, injured 844, and destroyed or damaged hundreds of homes in the Beecher district, making it the deadliest tornado in Michigan history and, at the time, the tenth deadliest in United States history. It remains the last U.S. tornado to kill more than 100 people until the Joplin tornado of 2011.

The storm

The tornado touched down near Linden Road west of Flint around 8:30 p.m. and tore east-northeast through the unincorporated community of Beecher along Coldwater Road, a densely built residential corridor. Witnesses described a huge, dark wedge more than half a mile wide. Entire rows of homes were swept from their foundations; vehicles were thrown and mangled, and debris was driven into trees and the ground. The tornado continued into open country before dissipating near Lapeer County after a path of roughly 27 miles.

Outbreak context

The Flint tornado was the capstone of the Flint–Worcester tornado outbreak sequence of 7–9 June 1953, which also produced deadly tornadoes in Nebraska, Iowa, and Ohio, and the following day's F4 tornado in Worcester, Massachusetts, that killed 94. The same weather system thus produced two of the most lethal tornadoes in U.S. history on consecutive days in very different regions.

Aftermath and significance

Because no tornado warning was issued — forecasters feared public panic, and a severe thunderstorm warning reached the area only minutes before the strike — the disaster became a pivotal case in the expansion of the U.S. tornado warning programme, the Skywarn spotter networks, and weather radar deployment. The death toll of 116 would not be exceeded by a single American tornado for 58 years, until Joplin, Missouri, in 2011 killed 158.
