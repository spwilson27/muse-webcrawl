Field ion microscope

Field ion microscope
Inventor: Erwin Wilhelm Müller
Introduced: 1951
Resolution: Atomic (~0.25 nm)
Imaging gas: Helium or neon

The field ion microscope (FIM) is an analytical instrument invented by Erwin Wilhelm Müller in 1951. It was the first instrument with which individual atoms could be directly observed: a field ion microscope image of a sharp metal needle shows a pattern of bright spots, each corresponding to a single atom on the specimen's surface.

Principle

The specimen is a needle etched to a tip radius of tens of nanometres, held at a high positive potential (several kilovolts) and cooled to cryogenic temperatures inside an ultra-high-vacuum chamber containing a trace of imaging gas, usually helium or neon. The electric field at the tip reaches tens of volts per nanometre — strong enough to ionise gas atoms that approach the most protruding surface atoms. The resulting positive ions are accelerated radially to a phosphor screen or detector, producing a greatly magnified projection (about a million times) of the atomic arrangement of the tip surface.

History and development

Müller had earlier invented the field emission microscope (1936), which imaged surfaces at nanometre but not atomic resolution. The field ion microscope, reported in 1951, resolved individual atoms for the first time — notably in images of tungsten tips made with his student Kanwar Bahadur in 1956. FIM became a powerful tool for studying crystal defects, vacancies, dislocations, and surface diffusion at the atomic level. Müller went on to combine FIM with a time-of-flight mass spectrometer to create the atom probe (1967), which identifies individual atoms chemically; modern atom probe tomography remains a direct descendant.

Limitations

FIM is restricted to refractory metals and alloys that can be formed into sharp tips and withstand enormous electrostatic stress, and imaging damages or alters delicate specimens. The technique demands ultra-high vacuum and cryogenic cooling.
