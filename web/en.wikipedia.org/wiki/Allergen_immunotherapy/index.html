Allergen immunotherapy

Allergen immunotherapy, also known as desensitisation or hypo-sensitisation, is a medical treatment for environmental allergies, such as allergic rhinitis, allergic asthma, conjunctivitis and venom hypersensitivity. It involves exposing people to gradually larger quantities of the allergen that provokes their symptoms, with the aim of inducing long-term tolerance. It is the only treatment for allergic disease that modifies the underlying immune response rather than merely controlling symptoms.

Methods

In subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT, "allergy shots"), increasing doses of allergen extract are injected under the skin, typically weekly during a build-up phase and then monthly for three to five years. Sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT) delivers the allergen as tablets or drops under the tongue, taken daily at home after a supervised first dose; standardised tablets exist for grass pollen, ragweed, dust mite and certain tree pollens. Oral immunotherapy for food allergy, notably peanut, follows a related principle and is licensed in some countries.

Mechanism

Successful immunotherapy shifts the immune response away from the allergic pattern: allergen-specific IgE falls over time while "blocking" IgG4 antibodies rise, regulatory T cells are induced to produce IL-10 and TGF-β, and the recruitment of mast cells, basophils and eosinophils to target tissues declines. These changes persist for years after treatment stops.

Effectiveness and safety

Systematic reviews find strong evidence of benefit in allergic rhinitis and conjunctivitis, useful benefit in allergic asthma, and very high effectiveness for preventing systemic reactions to wasp and bee venom. Local reactions — redness and swelling at the injection site, or oral itching with SLIT — are common. Systemic reactions, including rare anaphylaxis, occur mainly with SCIT, which is why injections are given in settings equipped to treat them and patients are observed afterwards.

History

The method began with Leonard Noon and John Freeman's grass-pollen inoculations at St Mary's Hospital, London, in 1911. It remains a standard part of allergy practice worldwide, guided by bodies such as the World Health Organization, which endorsed its disease-modifying role in a 1998 position paper.
