Minimal medium

A minimal medium (also called a defined medium or minimal salts medium) is a culture medium for growing microorganisms that contains only the minimum nutrients possible for colony growth, generally in the absence of amino acids. It is composed of a carbon source such as glucose or succinate, inorganic salts providing essential elements such as nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur, and trace metals, and water. Minimal media are widely used in microbiology and molecular genetics to select for organisms with particular genotypes and to study biosynthetic pathways.

Principles

Wild-type (prototrophic) microorganisms can synthesise all the compounds they need from the simple ingredients of a minimal medium. Mutants that have lost the ability to synthesise a particular nutrient — auxotrophs — cannot grow on unsupplemented minimal medium, but will grow if the missing nutrient is added. This property underlies classic experiments such as replica plating and the identification of biosynthetic genes.

Composition

A typical minimal medium for the bacterium Escherichia coli, such as M9 medium, contains:

ComponentRole
GlucoseCarbon and energy source
Na2HPO4 / KH2PO4Buffer and phosphorus source
NH4ClNitrogen source
MgSO4Magnesium and sulfur source
CaCl2Calcium source
Trace elements and (optionally) vitaminsCofactors

Uses

Minimal media are used to select for recombinant organisms carrying plasmids or mutations that confer prototrophy, to carry out nutritional and metabolic studies, to measure growth rates under defined conditions, and in the production of specific metabolites. Media that contain all possible growth requirements are called rich or complex media; supplementing a minimal medium with a single nutrient produces a supplemented (selective or differential) medium.
