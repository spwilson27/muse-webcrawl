Euler line

In geometry, the Euler line, named after Leonhard Euler, is a line determined from any triangle that is not equilateral. It is a central line of the triangle, passing through several important points: the orthocenter, the circumcenter, the centroid, and the center of the nine-point circle, among others.

Euler showed in 1765 that in any triangle, the orthocenter, circumcenter and centroid are collinear. In an equilateral triangle all these points coincide, so no unique line is defined. In a right triangle, the Euler line passes through the right-angle vertex (the orthocenter) and the midpoint of the hypotenuse (the circumcenter).

Properties

The centroid divides the segment joining the orthocenter and circumcenter in the ratio 2:1; that is, the distance from the centroid to the orthocenter is twice the distance from the centroid to the circumcenter. The center of the nine-point circle lies at the midpoint of the segment between the orthocenter and the circumcenter. In vector terms, if O is the circumcenter and H the orthocenter, the centroid G satisfies the relation that the vector OH equals the sum of the vectors from O to the three vertices.

Generalizations

The concept of an Euler line extends to other polygons and to higher-dimensional simplexes. For a tetrahedron, an analogous line passes through its centroid, circumcenter and Monge point. Points on the Euler line include the de Longchamps point and the Schiffler point, and its direction is related to the triangle's shape: the Euler line of a triangle is perpendicular to its sides in limiting symmetrical cases, and its slope in an affine coordinate system can be expressed in terms of the slopes of the sides.

For an isosceles triangle, the Euler line coincides with the axis of symmetry. For a scalene triangle, the line is well defined and none of the special points coincide.
