Evagoras I

Evagoras I (Greek: Εὐαγόρας; died 374 BC) was king of Salamis in Cyprus, reigning from about 411 BC until his assassination in 374 BC. A descendant of the legendary Teucer, founder of Salamis, Evagoras recovered his ancestral throne from Phoenician usurpers and went on to make Salamis the most powerful city-state on Cyprus, briefly extending his authority over much of the island and challenging the might of the Persian Empire. He is the subject of a famous encomium by the Athenian orator Isocrates, one of the earliest works of Greek prose biography.

Rise to power

According to tradition, Evagoras traced his descent from Teucer, the Greek hero of the Trojan War who founded Salamis. The ruling family had been displaced by a Phoenician regime backed by Persia, and Evagoras grew up in exile. Around 411 BC he returned to Cyprus with a small band of followers — Isocrates says only about fifty men — seized Salamis, and deposed the Phoenician ruler Abdemon, restoring the Greek dynasty.

Reign

Evagoras cultivated close ties with Athens, whose general Conon took refuge at his court after the Peloponnesian War and worked with him to rebuild Athenian naval power. With Athenian support, Evagoras equipped a fleet and expanded his rule over much of Cyprus, reducing cities that resisted, including Citium, Amathus and Soli. He also intervened in Cilicia and maintained relations with the rebel Egyptian pharaoh Hakor.

War with Persia

His growing power alarmed the Persian king Artaxerxes II, who had originally tolerated him as a tributary. Open war broke out around 391 BC and lasted about ten years. Evagoras won a significant naval success off Citium early in the conflict but was defeated in a great sea battle near Citium around 381 BC, after which Persian forces besieged Salamis. In 376 BC he negotiated a peace that preserved his throne: he retained the title of king of Salamis but acknowledged Persian suzerainty and agreed to pay tribute.

Death and legacy

In 374 BC Evagoras was assassinated by a eunuch, reportedly over a private grudge; his son Nicocles succeeded him. Evagoras was celebrated for Hellenising Cyprus — promoting Greek language, culture and immigration — and for making Salamis a flourishing commercial and cultural centre. Isocrates' Evagoras, addressed to Nicocles, praised him as the ideal ruler and is regarded as a landmark in the development of Greek rhetorical prose.
