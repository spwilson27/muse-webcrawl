Black locust

Robinia pseudoacacia
Family: Fabaceae
Native to: eastern North America

Black locust (Robinia pseudoacacia), also known in its native territory as false acacia, is a medium-sized hardwood deciduous tree in the tribe Robinieae of the legume family Fabaceae. It is endemic to a few small areas of the United States, but it has been widely planted and naturalized elsewhere in temperate North America, Europe, Southern Africa and Asia, and is considered an invasive species in some areas.

Description

The black locust reaches heights of 12–30 m (40–100 ft) with a diameter of 0.6–1.2 m (2–4 ft). The bark is dark and deeply furrowed. The leaves are pinnately compound, 20–35 cm long, with 9–19 oval leaflets. In late spring the tree produces fragrant white flowers in pendulous racemes; the flowers are an important source of nectar for honey bees. The fruit is a legume pod 5–10 cm long containing 4–10 seeds.

Distribution and ecology

The native range is centered on the Appalachian Mountains and the Ozark Plateau. Like other legumes, black locust hosts nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules, enriching poor soils. It spreads readily by root suckers, forming clonal colonies, which contributes to its invasiveness outside its native range.

Uses

The wood is extremely hard, rot-resistant, and durable, traditionally used for fence posts, firewood, shipbuilding, and furniture. Flavonoids in the heartwood allow the wood to last for decades in contact with soil. Black locust is also planted for erosion control and land reclamation, and its flowers yield a light, prized honey sold as "acacia honey" in Europe.

Toxicity

The bark, leaves, and seeds contain toxic proteins, including robin, and are poisonous to livestock, especially horses, though the flowers are considered edible.
