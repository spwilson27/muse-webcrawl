Kaiseki (懐石) or kaiseki-ryōri is a traditional multi-course Japanese dinner, considered the pinnacle of Japanese haute cuisine, in which a succession of small, meticulously prepared dishes showcase the chef's mastery of technique, seasonality, and presentation. The term also refers to the meal's artful composition as a whole: balance of taste, texture, colour, and appearance, served on carefully chosen tableware that complements the food and the season. Origins

Kaiseki developed from several sources: the simple meal served before the tea ceremony (cha-kaiseki), formalised in the 16th century in the circle of Sen no Rikyū; the banquet cuisine of the imperial court and nobility (yūsoku-ryōri); samurai ceremonial dining (honzen-ryōri); and the vegetarian temple cooking of Zen monasteries (shōjin-ryōri). The word itself, written "bosom stone", alludes to the practice of Zen monks pressing a warm stone to the belly to stave off hunger — implying a modest meal, though restaurant kaiseki can be lavish.

Course structure

A formal kaiseki meal unfolds in a conventional sequence, each course prepared by a different technique:

  • Sakizuke — an appetiser, akin to an amuse-bouche
  • Hassun — a seasonal tableau setting the theme, including small morsels from mountain and sea
  • Mukōzuke — sashimi of the freshest seasonal fish
  • Takiawase — vegetables simmered separately and combined, often with meat, fish, or tofu
  • Futamono — a lidded soup course, classically a clear suimono
  • Yakimono — a grilled dish, usually fish
  • Su-zakana, shiizakana — palate cleanser and a more substantial course at the chef's discretion
  • Gohan — rice, accompanied by miso soup and pickles (tsukemono)
  • Mizumono — a seasonal dessert, often fruit

Philosophy

The governing idea is shun: ingredients used at the fleeting peak of their season, sometimes at the very beginning (hashiri) or end (nagori) thereof. Classical rules call for five tastes, five colours, and five methods of preparation across the meal. Chefs leave much of the food's character unadorned, aiming to express the ingredient itself, and the choice of dishes, bowls, and garnishes is coordinated to suggest the season to all five senses.

Modern kaiseki

Kyoto, the old imperial capital, remains the centre of kaiseki, and celebrated ryōtei restaurants such as Kikunoi and Hyotei have carried its techniques into the era of the Michelin Guide, which awards several kaiseki restaurants three stars. Kaiseki's course architecture — tasting menus of many small, seasonal plates — has influenced modern tasting-menu cuisine worldwide.
