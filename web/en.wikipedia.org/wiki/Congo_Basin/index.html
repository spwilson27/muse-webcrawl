Congo Basin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Congo Basin is the sedimentary basin of the Congo River, a vast lowland region of central Africa covering roughly 3.7 million square kilometres across Cameroon, the Central African Republic, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the Republic of the Congo, Equatorial Guinea, and Gabon. It contains the world's second-largest tropical rainforest after the Amazon, and the Congo River system is the second-largest river by discharge volume in the world.

Geography and ecology

The basin is a shallow bowl ringed by plateaus and mountain ranges, including the Rwenzori Mountains to the east. Its equatorial climate produces rainfall of 1,500–2,000 mm or more per year in the central depression, the Cuvette Centrale, which holds the world's largest tropical peatland complex — an enormous carbon store estimated to hold some 29 billion tonnes of carbon. The rainforest is exceptionally biodiverse, home to forest elephants, lowland gorillas, chimpanzees, bonobos (found only south of the Congo River), okapis, and thousands of plant species, many endemic. The region is also home to Indigenous forest peoples, including various groups known collectively as Pygmies.

People and threats

Tens of millions of people depend on the basin's forests for food, medicine, and livelihoods. The forests face pressure from commercial and artisanal logging, mining for minerals such as cobalt and coltan, expansion of agriculture and oil-palm plantations, charcoal production, and bushmeat hunting. Conservation efforts include large protected areas such as Salonga and Virunga national parks, and international initiatives to value the basin's carbon sequestration and rainfall generation, which influences weather as far away as the Sahel and East Africa.
