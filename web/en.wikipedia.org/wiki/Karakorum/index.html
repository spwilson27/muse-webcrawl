Karakorum (Mongolian: Хархорин) was the capital of the Mongol Empire between 1235 and 1260 and of the Northern Yuan dynasty in the 14th–15th centuries. Its ruins lie in present-day Övörkhangai Province of Mongolia, near the modern town of Kharkhorin and the Orkhon River. For a generation it was the political centre of the largest contiguous land empire in history.

History

Genghis Khan established his headquarters at the site around 1220, choosing the Orkhon valley, long sacred to the steppe peoples and home to earlier Turkic imperial monuments. His son and successor Ögedei Khan enclosed the camp with walls in 1235 and built the Tumen Amgalan (Ten Thousand Peace) palace. Under Ögedei and Möngke, Karakorum became a cosmopolitan court city of Mongol, Chinese, Muslim and European craftsmen, merchants and envoys. The Franciscan friar William of Rubruck, who visited in 1253–54, left a famous description of the palace, its silver tree fountain designed by the Parisian goldsmith Guillaume Boucher, and the city's temples, mosques and churches.

In 1260 Kublai Khan moved the imperial capital south to Shangdu and then to Khanbaliq (modern Beijing), and Karakorum declined into a provincial centre. After the fall of the Yuan in 1368 it briefly regained importance under the Northern Yuan, but was devastated in wars with the Ming and the Oirats. In 1585 the Erdene Zuu monastery — the oldest surviving Buddhist monastery in Mongolia — was built largely from the city's stones just outside its walls.

Archaeology

Excavations, including Mongolian-German expeditions since the 1990s, have uncovered the palace precinct, craft quarters and kiln sites. A giant stone tortoise, one of two remaining, still marks the city site. The Orkhon Valley Cultural Landscape, including Karakorum and Erdene Zuu, is inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
