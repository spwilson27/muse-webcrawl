Yōshoku

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Yōshoku (洋食, "Western food") refers to a style of Western-influenced cooking which originated during the Meiji Restoration in Japan. The term is used to describe Japanese dishes inspired by European and American cuisine but adapted to Japanese tastes and ingredients, distinct from washoku, traditional Japanese food.

History and dishes

After Japan opened to the West in the 1850s–1860s, the Meiji government promoted Western food as part of modernization, and the Emperor's public consumption of meat lifted the long-standing taboo on eating it. Restaurants in Tokyo's Ginza district began serving Japanized Western dishes from the 1870s–1890s.

Classic yōshoku dishes include curry rice (karē raisu), tonkatsu (breaded pork cutlet), omurice (omelette over fried rice), hayashi rice (hashed beef in demi-glace), hamburg steak (hanbāgu), korokke (croquettes), ebi fry (fried shrimp), Napolitan spaghetti, and doria (rice gratin). These are typically served with shredded cabbage, rice, and miso soup in family restaurants and long-established yōshoku-ya. Many yōshoku items are written in katakana and are considered comfort food staples of modern Japanese home cooking.
