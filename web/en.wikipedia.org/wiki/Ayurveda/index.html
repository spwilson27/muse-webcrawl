Ayurveda (Sanskrit: आयुर्वेद, "knowledge of life") is an alternative medicine system with historical roots in the Indian subcontinent. It is heavily practised throughout India and Nepal, where as much as 80% of the population report using it. The theory and practice of Ayurveda is pseudoscientific, though it remains one of the world's oldest holistic healing systems, and many of its dietary and lifestyle recommendations overlap with generally accepted health advice.

History

Ayurvedic texts describe mythological origins in which the knowledge of medicine was transmitted from the gods to sages and then to human physicians. The earliest literature emerged during the Vedic period (c. 1500–500 BCE). The foundational works are the Charaka Samhita, attributed to Charaka and focused on internal medicine, and the Sushruta Samhita, traditionally dated between the 6th century BCE and the early centuries CE, which describes surgical procedures including rhinoplasty and cataract surgery. Together with the later Ashtanga Hridayam of Vagbhata, these form the "great triad" (Brihat-trayi) of Ayurvedic literature.

Principles

Ayurvedic theory holds that the body is governed by three regulatory principles called doshas: vata (air and space), pitta (fire and water), and kapha (water and earth). Each person is said to have a unique constitution (prakriti) determined by the proportions of the doshas, and illness is attributed to their imbalance. The body is also composed of seven tissues (dhatus), metabolic waste products (malas), and a digestive "fire" (agni). Diagnosis traditionally involves examination of the pulse, urine, tongue and other signs, and treatment emphasises diet, herbal preparations, massage with oils, yoga, meditation, and purification procedures (panchakarma).

Practice and regulation

In India, Ayurveda is regulated by the Ministry of AYUSH (established in 2014), and practitioners complete a Bachelor of Ayurvedic Medicine and Surgery (BAMS) degree of about five and a half years. Outside South Asia, Ayurveda is generally classed as complementary or alternative medicine, and practitioners may not be licensed as medical doctors.

Research and safety

Clinical evidence for the efficacy of Ayurvedic preparations is limited; rigorous trials are few and often small. Safety concerns include reports that some Ayurvedic products, particularly rasa shastra preparations made according to traditional methods, contain toxic levels of lead, mercury or arsenic. Toxicologists and regulators in several countries have warned against such products, and some are banned or restricted. The World Health Organization recognises Ayurveda as a traditional medicine system while urging member states to regulate its practice and products.
