Island arc

An island arc is a curved chain of volcanic islands formed where one oceanic tectonic plate subducts beneath another, generating magma that rises to build volcanoes parallel to an oceanic trench.

Formation

As the descending slab reaches depth, water released from it lowers the mantle melting point, producing arc magma. The islands sit on the overriding plate, typically 100–200 km from the trench, with a forearc and back-arc basin behind them.

Examples

Examples include the Aleutian Islands, Japan, the Philippines, Indonesia's Sunda and Banda arcs — including volcanoes such as Mount Merapi on Java — the Lesser Antilles in the Caribbean, the Mariana Islands and Tonga. Island arcs are sites of intense earthquakes and volcanism along the Pacific Ring of Fire.

Significance

Over geological time arcs can accrete to continents, contributing new continental crust; ancient arc terranes are recognised in many mountain belts.
