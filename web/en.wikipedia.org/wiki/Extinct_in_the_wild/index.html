Extinct in the wild

Extinct in the wild (EW) is a conservation status given to species known only to survive in captivity, as a naturalised population outside their historic range, or otherwise removed from their natural habitat. It is one of the categories of the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species, ranking between "critically endangered" and "extinct".

The scimitar oryx survived only in captivity for decades before reintroduction to Chad.

Definition

Under the IUCN criteria, a taxon is extinct in the wild when exhaustive surveys in its known or expected habitat, at appropriate times and throughout its historic range, have failed to record an individual in the wild. Species in this category persist only as captive, cultivated or naturalised populations well outside their past range.

Examples

Well-known EW species have included the scimitar oryx (reintroduced to Chad from 2016 and later downlisted), Père David's deer (surviving in herds descended from a handful of animals in European collections), the Socorro dove, the Wyoming toad, the Kihansi spray toad, the Alagoas curassow, and Spix's macaw — declared extinct in the wild in 2019 and the subject of a major reintroduction programme in Brazil's caatinga from 2022. Among plants, the Franklin tree (Franklinia alatamaha) has been extinct in the wild since the early 19th century and all living specimens descend from seeds collected by the Bartrams.

Reintroduction

EW status is not necessarily permanent: successful captive breeding followed by reintroduction can return a species to the wild, as with the Arabian oryx (the first species to move from EW back to vulnerable status) and Przewalski's horse. However, reintroduction requires that the original causes of decline have been removed, and captive populations may suffer from inbreeding and adaptation to captivity.
