Latium

Latium (Latin: Lātium) is the region of west central Italy in which the city of Rome was founded and grew to be the capital city of the Roman Empire. It corresponds broadly to the modern Italian region of Lazio. In ancient times Latium was the homeland of the Latins, an Italic people whose language, Latin, spread with Roman power to become the ancestor of the Romance languages and a lingua franca of scholarship for two millennia.

Geography

Ancient Latium vetus (Old Latium), the original Latin heartland, was a small territory bounded by the Tiber to the north, the Apennines to the east, the Alban Hills and the Pomptine Marshes to the south, and the Tyrrhenian Sea to the west. The fertile volcanic soil of the Alban Hills and the coastal plain supported agriculture, while the Tiber provided transport and a defensible crossing point at Rome. As Rome expanded, Latium adiectum ("added Latium") extended the name southeastward toward Campania, incorporating the lands of the Volsci, Aurunci, and Hernici.

History

Latium was settled by Italic-speaking peoples by the early first millennium BC. Its independent cities — among them Alba Longa, Tusculum, Praeneste, Tibur, Lavinium, and Ardea — formed the Latin League, a religious and defensive confederation centred on the sanctuary of Jupiter Latiaris on the Alban Mount, where the Feriae Latinae festival was held. Rome, traditionally founded in 753 BC, gradually dominated the league, which was dissolved after the Latin War of 340–338 BC; the settlement of 338 BC gave many Latins Roman citizenship and defined the "Latin right" (ius Latii) later extended across the empire. Under Augustus, Latium was joined with Campania as Regio I of Roman Italy.

Language and legacy

The region's name survives in "Latin", "Lazio", and in the root of words such as "Latino". Latin literature and law, Roman roads such as the Via Appia and Via Latina that crossed Latium, and the villas of the Roman aristocracy in the Alban Hills all testify to the region's central place in Roman civilisation.
