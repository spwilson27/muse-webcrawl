The Cantabrian Mountains (Spanish: Cordillera Cantábrica) are a mountain range running about 300 km across northern Spain, parallel to the Bay of Biscay, from the Pyrenees westward through the Basque Country, Cantabria, Asturias and into Galicia. They form the spine of "Green Spain", separating the wet Atlantic coast from the dry central plateau (Meseta).

Geography and nature

The highest and most rugged section is the Picos de Europa, a limestone massif whose Torre de Cerredo reaches 2,650 m; the Picos were protected as Spain's first national park (1918). The range's deep gorges — such as the Cares Gorge — and high pastures support Atlantic deciduous forest, Cantabrian brown bears, wolves and the Cantabrian chamois. Rivers are short and fast-flowing to the north, while longer tributaries of the Ebro and Duero drain southward.

History and economy

Named for the ancient Cantabri, the mountains sheltered resistance to Rome and later to the Muslim conquest; the Asturian kingdom that grew here began the Reconquista at Covadonga in 722. Coal mining, hydroelectric power, livestock rearing and, increasingly, rural tourism underpin the regional economy.
