Edmond Rostand

Edmond Eugène Alexis Rostand (1 April 1868 – 2 December 1918) was a French poet and dramatist. He is best known as the author of the play Cyrano de Bergerac (1897), whose title character — a brilliant, eloquent swordsman-poet cursed with an enormous nose — became one of the most famous figures of the French theatre.

Life

Rostand was born in Marseille into a wealthy Provençal family; his father Eugène Rostand was an economist and poet, and his great-uncle Alexis Rostand a banker and composer. He studied law in Paris but soon devoted himself to literature. In 1890 he published a volume of verse, Les Musardises, and married the poet Rosemonde Gérard, granddaughter of the marshal Gérard de Nerval's circle and herself a writer of note.

His early comedy Les Romanesques (1894) — later adapted as the musical The Fantasticks — brought him recognition. The verse drama La Samaritaine (1897), written for Sarah Bernhardt, confirmed his talent for the romantic theatre at a moment when naturalism dominated the stage.

Cyrano de Bergerac

Cyrano de Bergerac, a heroic comedy in five acts written in rhyming alexandrines, premiered on 28 December 1897 at the Théâtre de la Porte Saint-Martin with Constant Coquelin in the title role. It was an immediate triumph: the audience applauded for an hour after the final curtain. The play tells of Cyrano, a cadet of Gascony whose wit, swordsmanship and poetic genius are undermined by his conviction that his huge nose makes him unlovable; he woos the beautiful Roxane by proxy, writing and speaking love letters for the handsome but tongue-tied Christian, and reveals the truth only as he dies. Rostand borrowed the name and some traits of the historical Savinien de Cyrano de Bergerac, a 17th-century writer.

Later works

L'Aiglon (1900), about the life of Napoleon II, the Duke of Reichstadt, was another great success, with Bernhardt playing the male lead. In 1901 Rostand became the youngest writer elected to the Académie française. The verse fable Chantecler (1910), whose hero is a rooster who believes his crowing makes the sun rise, was a costly and only partly successful production. Ill health plagued Rostand's later years; he died in Paris of Spanish influenza in December 1918.
