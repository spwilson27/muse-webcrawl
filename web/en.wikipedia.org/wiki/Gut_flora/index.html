Gut flora

Gut flora, or gut microbiota, is the complex community of microorganisms that live in the digestive tracts of humans and other animals, including bacteria, archaea, viruses, and fungi. The human gut microbiota contains tens of trillions of microorganisms and over a thousand different species of bacteria, with the greatest density and diversity found in the colon.

Escherichia coli, one of many species present in the human gut

Composition

The gut microbiota is dominated by two bacterial phyla, Bacillota (formerly Firmicutes) and Bacteroidota (formerly Bacteroidetes), which together make up more than 90 percent of the known species. Other phyla include Actinomycetota (including Bifidobacterium), Pseudomonadota (including Escherichia), Verrucomicrobiota, and Fusobacteriota. Each individual's microbiota is shaped by birth mode, diet, environment, antibiotic use, and genetics, and it changes throughout life.

Functions

Gut microbes ferment dietary fibre and other undigested carbohydrates, producing short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate, propionate, and acetate that nourish colon cells and regulate metabolism. They synthesise vitamins B and K, metabolise bile acids and drugs, train and modulate the immune system, and protect against pathogens through colonisation resistance. The gut–brain axis links the microbiota with the central nervous system, and microbial signals influence mood and behaviour in experimental studies.

Health and disease

Disturbances of the microbiota, known as dysbiosis, have been associated with inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, type 2 diabetes, allergies, colorectal cancer, and other conditions, though causation is often difficult to establish. Antibiotics can profoundly disrupt the community; in severe cases, loss of diversity enables infection by Clostridioides difficile. Fecal microbiota transplantation has proved highly effective for recurrent C. difficile infection.
