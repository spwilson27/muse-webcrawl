Hyperacusis

Hyperacusis is an uncommon hearing disorder characterized by an abnormally decreased tolerance to environmental sounds, in which sounds of ordinary intensity are perceived as uncomfortably or painfully loud. It is distinct from phonophobia (fear of sound), misophonia (strong emotional reactions to specific sounds), and recruitment associated with hearing loss. Hyperacusis affects an estimated 0.2–3 percent of the population, with estimates varying by methodology and population studied.

Causes

The most common cause is exposure to acoustic trauma, such as a sudden loud noise. Hyperacusis is also associated with tinnitus — a large majority of hyperacusis patients report tinnitus — and with conditions including Bell's palsy and stapedectomy (in which the stapedius reflex that damps loud sound is impaired), Lyme disease, Ménière's disease, head injury, migraine, and certain neurodevelopmental and genetic conditions such as Williams syndrome. The underlying mechanism is thought to involve increased central auditory gain, in which the brain amplifies sound following reduced input or damage.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Affected people describe everyday sounds — dishes clattering, doors closing, running water — as piercing, painful, or overwhelming, leading to avoidance of social situations and, in severe cases, near-total isolation. Diagnosis relies on history and loudness discomfort level testing during audiological evaluation; there is no single objective test.

Treatment

There is no cure, but treatment typically centers on sound therapy — gradually introducing low-level broadband noise, often with wearable sound generators — to retrain tolerance (desensitization). Cognitive behavioral therapy helps address the anxiety and avoidance that maintain the condition. Clinicians generally discourage habitual earplug use outside genuinely dangerous noise, since overprotection can worsen sensitivity. Treatment of underlying causes, where identifiable, is also important.
