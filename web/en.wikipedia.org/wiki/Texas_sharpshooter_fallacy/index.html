Texas sharpshooter fallacy

Drawing the target around the bullet holes after shooting (placeholder)

The Texas sharpshooter fallacy is an informal fallacy committed when differences in data are ignored while similarities are overemphasised, leading to a false conclusion. It is named after a joke about a Texan who fires shots at the side of a barn, then paints a target centred on the tightest cluster of holes and claims to be a sharpshooter.

Structure of the fallacy

The fallacy has two stages: first, an investigator gathers a large collection of data; second, a pattern or cluster is selected after the fact and treated as if it had been specified in advance. The sharpshooter has (1) a large space (the barn wall) in which to find apparent hits and (2) a target chosen after seeing where the shots landed. Likewise, the fallacy arises when a hypothesis is derived from the same data used to test it, so random clusters are mistaken for meaningful effects. It is closely related to the clustering illusion, apophenia, and the multiple comparisons problem in statistics: if one searches enough variables and subgroups, some will correlate by chance.

Examples and countermeasures

Classic examples include "cancer cluster" investigations where a boundary is drawn around cases after they are noticed, and cherry-picked windows of stock-market or climate data. In epidemiology, early anecdotal clusters rarely survive rigorous testing with pre-specified boundaries. Remedies include stating hypotheses before examining the data, correcting for multiple comparisons (e.g. Bonferroni), holding out a validation sample, and distinguishing exploratory from confirmatory analysis.
