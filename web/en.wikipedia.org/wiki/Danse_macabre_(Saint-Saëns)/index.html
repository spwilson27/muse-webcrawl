Danse macabre (Saint-Saëns)

Danse macabre, Op. 40, is a tone poem for orchestra, written in 1874 by French composer Camille Saint-Saëns. It is one of the composer's most famous works, based on the medieval legend and artistic genre of the Danse Macabre, in which Death summons the dead from their graves to dance at midnight.

The piece began as an art song (mélodie) for voice and piano in 1872, setting a poem by Henri Cazalis; Saint-Saëns reworked it into the orchestral tone poem two years later. It premiered in Paris on 24 January 1875 under Édouard Colonne.

Programme

According to the legend, Death appears at midnight every year on Halloween. The work opens with a harp playing twelve D notes — the twelve strokes of midnight — followed by the solo violin, representing Death tuning his fiddle: the top string is deliberately tuned down a semitone (scordatura) to create the tritone, the "diabolus in musica". Skeletons rise and dance a waltz to Death's tune, their bones rattling in the xylophone — an instrument Saint-Saëns helped introduce to the orchestra. A gentler oboe theme (the cockcrow, heralding dawn) interrupts: the skeletons return to their graves as day breaks.

Music

The work is in G minor, in 3/4 time, and marked Allegro non troppo. It quotes the Dies irae chant, transformed into the waltz rhythm, and features prominent solos for violin and xylophone. Franz Liszt made a celebrated piano transcription (S. 146).

Reception and legacy

Early audiences found the piece grotesque, but it quickly became a staple of the orchestral repertoire and a fixture of Halloween concerts. It has been used widely in popular culture — in films, cartoons, television themes and ice-skating programmes — and its xylophone "rattling bones" remain among the most recognisable effects in classical music.
