Northern European short-tailed sheep

The Northern European short-tailed sheep are a group of traditional sheep breeds and landraces native to northern Europe and the North Atlantic islands. Their defining characteristic is a short, tapering, "fluke-shaped" tail, quite unlike the long, woolly tails of most modern breeds, together with small size, hardiness, and an ability to thrive on poor grazing in harsh climates.

Characteristics

Breeds in the group typically have a short tail of 13 vertebrae, often hair-covered or with a bare tip, rather than the long docked tails common elsewhere. Many are double-coated, with a fine soft undercoat and a longer protective outer coat, and they occur in a wide range of natural colours. Rams of several breeds may carry four horns (polycerate), and ewes may also be horned. The sheep are generally thrifty, good mothers, and suited to extensive grazing systems.

Breeds

The group includes the Shetland and North Ronaldsay (Orkney) sheep of Scotland; the Icelandic sheep; the Faroe sheep; the Finnsheep of Finland; the Russian Romanov; the Norwegian Spælsau, Old Norwegian (Villsau), and other short-tailed landraces; the Swedish Gute, Roslag, and Dala breeds; the Åland sheep; the Estonian Ruhnu; the German Grey Heath (Heidschnucke); the Polish Wrzosówka; and remnant populations such as the Manx Loaghtan of the Isle of Man and the Soay and Boreray sheep of St Kilda.

History

Short-tailed sheep are thought to descend from the primitive sheep spread across northern Europe by early farmers and later carried by Norse settlers to Iceland, the Faroes, Shetland, and Orkney. Because the isolated island populations were little crossed with outside stock, they preserve ancient traits; the seaweed-eating North Ronaldsay, confined to the shores of its island by a drystone dyke, is among the most distinctive. Many breeds in the group are now rare and the subject of conservation breeding.
