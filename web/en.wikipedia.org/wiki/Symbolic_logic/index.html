Symbolic logic

Symbolic logic, also called mathematical logic in its modern mathematical form, is the study of reasoning using formal languages and precisely defined symbols, in which arguments are represented by formulas whose structure can be manipulated according to explicit rules. It developed in the nineteenth century from the work of George Boole, who gave logic an algebraic treatment (1847, 1854), and Gottlob Frege, whose Begriffsschrift (1879) introduced quantification and the predicate calculus.

Formal languages and systems

A symbolic logic begins with a formal language: a vocabulary of constant, predicate and function symbols plus variables, and logical connectives such as ¬ (not), ∧ (and), ∨ (or), → (implies) and the quantifiers ∀ (for all) and ∃ (there exists). Rules of inference — such as modus ponens — license derivation of theorems from axioms. Propositional logic studies whole propositions combined by connectives; first-order (predicate) logic adds quantifiers over individuals; higher-order and modal logics extend the framework further.

Foundations and results

Frege, Russell and Whitehead attempted to reduce mathematics to logic (logicism), culminating in Principia Mathematica (1910–13). In the twentieth century the field produced landmark limitative results: Kurt Gödel's completeness theorem (1929) and incompleteness theorems (1931), and the work of Turing and Church on undecidability. Symbolic logic now underpins computer science (type theory, verification, databases), linguistics, and analytic philosophy.

Notation

Peano introduced much of the modern notation, later standardised by Russell and others. The symbol ⊃ was once used for implication (now →), and the Sheffer stroke and Polish (prefix) notation developed by Jan Łukasiewicz remain in use in specialised contexts.
