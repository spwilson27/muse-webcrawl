Camelid

The llama, a South American camelid.

Camelids are members of the biological family Camelidae, the only currently living family in the suborder Tylopoda of the even-toed ungulates. The family comprises the Old World camels of the genus Camelus and the South American camelids: the llama, alpaca, guanaco and vicuña. Camelids originated in North America some 45 million years ago and later spread to Asia across the Bering land bridge and to South America across the Isthmus of Panama, becoming extinct in their continent of origin only around 10,000 years ago.

Characteristics

Camelids are large, strictly herbivorous animals with slender necks and long legs. Unlike other even-toed ungulates they walk on padded, two-toed feet rather than hooves, and they lack horns or antlers. They have a three-chambered stomach and chew the cud. Their upper lip is split, their red blood cells are oval, and they produce distinctive heavy-chain-only antibodies. Male camelids fight by biting and wrestling with their necks.

Living species

  • Camelus dromedarius — dromedary camel
  • Camelus bactrianus — domestic Bactrian camel
  • Camelus ferus — wild Bactrian camel
  • Lama glama — llama
  • Lama guanicoe — guanaco
  • Vicugna pacos — alpaca
  • Vicugna vicugna — vicuña

The llama and alpaca were domesticated in the Andes several thousand years ago — the llama as a beast of burden, the alpaca for its fibre — while the vicuña is prized for exceptionally fine wool and is protected in the wild.
