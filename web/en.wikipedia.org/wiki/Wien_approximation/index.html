Wien approximation

The Wien approximation (also called Wien's law or the Wien distribution law) is a law of physics used to describe the spectrum of thermal radiation (frequently called the blackbody function). This law was first derived by Wilhelm Wien in 1896. The equation approximates the spectrum of blackbody radiation well, but only at high frequencies (short wavelengths).

The Wien approximation plotted against Planck's law.

Derivation and relation to Planck's law

Wien derived his distribution law from thermodynamic arguments combined with assumptions about the emission of radiation by molecules. The law gives the spectral radiance of a black body at temperature T as a function of frequency or wavelength, with an exponential dependence on frequency divided by temperature.

Max Planck's law, published in 1900, reduces to the Wien approximation when the energy of a quantum of radiation is much larger than the thermal energy — that is, at high frequencies or low temperatures. At low frequencies (long wavelengths) the Wien approximation fails badly, and the correct limiting behaviour is given by the Rayleigh–Jeans law. Wien's approximation thus survives as the short-wavelength limit of the full Planck spectrum, and remains useful in astrophysics and radiation thermometry where the relevant frequencies are high.

Not to be confused with

The Wien approximation should not be confused with Wien's displacement law, which Wien derived in 1893 and which relates the wavelength of peak emission of a black body to its temperature; that law is exact and follows from Planck's law.
