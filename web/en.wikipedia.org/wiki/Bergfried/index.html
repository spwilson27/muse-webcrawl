Bergfried

The bergfried was the tallest and strongest tower of a German medieval castle.

A bergfried (plural: bergfriede) is a tall defensive tower found in medieval castles of the German-speaking lands of Central Europe. Rising above every other building of the castle, the bergfried served as a watchtower, a symbol of the lord's power, and the final stronghold to which the garrison could retreat during a siege.

Characteristics

Bergfrieds were typically 20 to 30 metres high, built of massive stone with walls several metres thick at the base. Unlike the English or French keep, a bergfried was generally not designed as a permanent residence: its interior was cramped, dark and divided into a few stacked chambers, and the entrance was usually placed high above the ground — often 8 to 12 metres up — reachable only by a ladder or a removable wooden stair that could be destroyed in an attack. The ground-floor chamber, accessible only from above, sometimes served as a dungeon or store room.

Function

The tower gave defenders a commanding view over the surrounding country and a platform from which to observe and harass attackers. As the castle's last point of resistance, it could hold out after the walls and courtyard buildings had fallen. Scholars debate how far its role was practical as opposed to symbolic: as the most visible element of the castle, the bergfried advertised the status and authority of its owner across the landscape.

Distribution and survival

Bergfrieds are characteristic of castles built between the 12th and 15th centuries in Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Bohemia and neighbouring regions. Well-preserved examples survive at many castles, such as Marksburg on the Rhine, and the closely related French donjon and English keep differ chiefly in their residential function. When castles were converted into palaces in later centuries, the bergfried was often retained as a proud relic of the fortress's medieval origins.
