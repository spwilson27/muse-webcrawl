Eucharistic theology is the branch of Christian theology concerned with the Eucharist — the ritual meal of bread and wine instituted by Jesus at the Last Supper. It addresses how Christ is present in the elements, whether the Eucharist is a sacrifice, who may celebrate and receive it, and what grace it confers. Divergent answers to these questions were among the defining doctrinal disputes of the Reformation and remain significant differences between Christian traditions.

The real presence

Most historic churches affirm some doctrine of the real presence. The Catholic Church teaches transubstantiation: the whole substance of the bread and wine is converted into the body and blood of Christ, while the appearances (accidents) of bread and wine remain — a doctrine defined at the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) and reaffirmed at the Council of Trent. Eastern Orthodoxy likewise affirms a real change (metabole) while declining to define its mechanics. Lutheran theology teaches the sacramental union — Christ's body and blood are present "in, with and under" the forms of bread and wine (often popularly called consubstantiation, a term Lutherans generally reject). Reformed theology, following Calvin, teaches a real spiritual presence: believers truly feed on Christ, but by the Holy Spirit rather than by a local presence in the elements. Memorialist traditions, following Zwingli and including most Baptists, understand the Supper primarily as a commemoration and proclamation of Christ's death.

The Eucharist as sacrifice

Catholic and Orthodox theology teaches that the Eucharist re-presents (makes present) the one sacrifice of Christ on the cross and applies its benefits; Trent anathematised denial that the Mass is a propitiatory sacrifice. Classic Protestant theology rejected the "sacrifice of the Mass", insisting on the once-for-all sufficiency of the cross, while allowing the Eucharist to be a "sacrifice of praise and thanksgiving" — the literal meaning of eucharistia.

Other questions

Eucharistic theology also treats the minister of the sacrament, the matter and form required for validity, the fruits of communion, reservation and adoration of the sacrament, and the conditions of admission to communion — questions closely tied to ecclesiology and to ecumenical dialogue. Twentieth-century ecumenical texts, such as the World Council of Churches' Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry (1982) and the Anglican–Roman Catholic agreed statements, documented substantial convergence on presence and memorial while leaving differences unresolved.
