Monotype System

The Monotype system was a system for printing by hot-metal typesetting, invented by Tolbert Lanston and developed by the Lanston Monotype Machine Company, patented in its practical form in the 1890s. Unlike the competing Linotype machine, which cast whole lines of type as solid slugs, the Monotype cast individual letters and spaces, making corrections easier and permitting a wider range of typefaces and sizes.

Components

The system had two principal parts. The Monotype keyboard was operated by a compositor and punched a wide paper tape (perforated ribbon) encoding the text, along with justification codes computed from a unit-counting mechanism. The Monotype caster (composition caster) read the tape in reverse and cast type from molten metal, using compressed air to position a matrix case holding the character matrices. Because the tape was read backwards, the justification information, punched last, was encountered first.

Unit system

Monotype used a unit system in which each character's width was expressed in units of an em divided into 18 units, allowing precise calculation of line justification. Type sizes and "set" widths were coordinated through this system, which printers found flexible for book and jobbing work.

Use

The Monotype system was widely used for book composition, fine typography, and work requiring frequent corrections or complex matter such as tables. The British Monotype Corporation, under typographic adviser Stanley Morison, paired the equipment with a celebrated typeface programme (Times New Roman, Gill Sans, Perpetua, and revivals of historical faces such as Baskerville and Garamond). The system declined with the rise of phototypesetting and then digital typesetting in the later twentieth century, though equipment remained in use at some fine presses.
