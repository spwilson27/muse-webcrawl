Non-cooperation movement (1919–22)

DateAugust 1920 – February 1922
LocationBritish India
Led byMahatma Gandhi / Indian National Congress
GoalsSwaraj (self-rule); redress for Punjab and Khilafat wrongs
ResultWithdrawn by Gandhi after the Chauri Chaura incident

The non-cooperation movement was a political campaign launched by Mahatma Gandhi in 1920 to have Indians withdraw their cooperation from the British Raj in protest against the Rowlatt Acts, the Jallianwala Bagh massacre of 1919, and the treatment of the Ottoman caliph after the First World War (the Khilafat issue). It was the first nationwide satyagraha movement and transformed the Indian National Congress into a mass organisation.

Programme

Adopted by Congress at its Calcutta special session (September 1920) and confirmed at Nagpur (December 1920), the programme called for the surrender of government titles and honours; boycott of government schools, colleges, law courts, and legislatures; boycott of foreign cloth; and the promotion of swadeshi goods, especially hand-spun, hand-woven khadi, using the spinning wheel (charkha). Gandhi promised that if the programme were fully carried out, swaraj would follow within a year.

Course of the movement

Response was dramatic: prominent lawyers such as Motilal Nehru, C. R. Das, and Rajendra Prasad gave up their practices; students left government institutions for new national schools and colleges; foreign cloth was publicly burned; and imports of British textiles fell sharply. The Khilafat alliance brought large numbers of Muslims into the movement alongside Hindus. The government responded with repression — Congress and Khilafat volunteer organisations were banned and tens of thousands arrested by early 1922.

Chauri Chaura and withdrawal

On 4 February 1922, at Chauri Chaura in the United Provinces, a crowd of demonstrators clashed with police and set fire to the police station, killing 22 policemen. Horrified that the movement was turning violent, Gandhi called the whole campaign off on 12 February 1922 (the Bardoli resolution), against the wishes of many colleagues. He was arrested in March 1922 and sentenced to six years' imprisonment for sedition.

Significance

Although it did not achieve swaraj, the movement demonstrated that mass, non-violent non-cooperation could shake British rule, politicised millions of Indians, and established Gandhi's leadership of the national movement — methods later revived in the civil disobedience movement of 1930.
