History of the United States

The history of the United States spans the Indigenous peoples who inhabited North America for millennia, European colonisation from the sixteenth century, the founding of the United States in 1776, its expansion across the continent, the Civil War of 1861–65, industrialisation and mass immigration, two world wars, the Cold War, and its emergence as a global superpower. The United States today is a federal republic of 50 states with the world's largest economy.

The Declaration of Independence was adopted on 4 July 1776.

Indigenous peoples and colonisation

Humans reached the Americas by at least 15,000 years ago; diverse Native American societies — from Mississippian mound-builders to the Haudenosaunee Confederacy — developed across the continent. European contact after 1492 brought catastrophic epidemics. Spain founded St. Augustine (1565); England established Jamestown (1607) and Plymouth (1620). The Thirteen Colonies grew wealthy on agriculture, including tobacco and cotton worked by enslaved Africans, whose importation began in 1619.

Revolution and the early republic

Disputes over taxation and representation led to the Revolutionary War (1775–1783) and independence, recognised by the Treaty of Paris (1783). The Constitution of 1787 (effective 1789) created a federal republic; George Washington was its first president. The Louisiana Purchase (1803) doubled the nation's size. Westward expansion, fuelled by "Manifest Destiny", brought the Mexican–American War (1846–48) and the displacement of Native peoples, including the Trail of Tears (1830s).

Civil War and Reconstruction

The expansion of slavery into new territories fractured the Union; eleven Southern states seceded in 1860–61 to form the Confederacy. The Civil War (1861–1865) killed roughly 620,000–750,000 soldiers and ended slavery (Thirteenth Amendment, 1865) under President Abraham Lincoln, assassinated days after Lee's surrender. Reconstruction (1865–1877) briefly extended civil and political rights to freedpeople before Jim Crow segregation took hold in the South.

Industrialisation, world wars, and the Cold War

The Gilded Age brought railroads, steel, oil and mass immigration; reform followed in the Progressive Era. The United States joined the First World War in 1917. After the roaring 1920s came the Great Depression (1929) and Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal. In the Second World War (joined 1941), American industry and arms proved decisive in Europe and the Pacific. Postwar prosperity, the civil rights movement, and Cold War rivalry with the Soviet Union (1947–1991) followed; the U.S. fought in Korea and Vietnam and landed Apollo 11 on the Moon in 1969.

Contemporary era

After the Soviet Union's collapse, the United States stood as sole superpower; the September 11 attacks (2001) launched wars in Afghanistan and Iraq. The financial crisis of 2007–08, deepening political polarisation, and the COVID-19 pandemic mark recent decades, alongside continued technological and cultural influence.
