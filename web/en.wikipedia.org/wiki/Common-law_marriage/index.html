Common-law marriage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Common-law marriage, also known as non-ceremonial marriage, sui juris marriage, or informal marriage, is a legal framework in which a couple is considered legally married without having formally registered their relation or having a marriage ceremony, provided they meet the jurisdiction's requirements — typically an agreement to be married, cohabitation, and holding themselves out publicly as spouses.

Terminology

The term derives from English common law, under which marriages could be contracted by mutual consent without ecclesiastical form. Popular usage often wrongly assumes that merely living together for a fixed period (commonly said to be seven years) creates a common-law marriage; in jurisdictions that retain the doctrine, the couple's intent and public reputation as married are the essential elements, not duration alone.

United States

Only a minority of U.S. states still permit new common-law marriages, among them Colorado, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, Oklahoma, Rhode Island, Texas, Utah, and the District of Columbia; several others recognise them only if contracted before a cutoff date. Under the Full Faith and Credit Clause, a valid common-law marriage contracted in one state is generally recognised in others. Ending such a marriage requires a formal divorce.

Other jurisdictions

In England and Wales, common-law marriage was effectively abolished by the Marriage Act 1753, and the widespread belief that cohabitants acquire "common-law" rights is a myth, though Scotland recognised marriage by cohabitation with habit and repute until 2006. Comparable institutions include the de facto relationships recognised in Australia and Canada's common-law partnerships for tax and benefits purposes, which confer some but not all incidents of marriage.
