Ostsiedlung

German eastward settlement, c. 12th–14th centuries

Ostsiedlung (German pronunciation: [ˈɔstˌziːdlʊŋ], literally "East settling"), in English called the German eastward expansion, was the medieval eastward migration and settlement of Germanic peoples from the western Holy Roman Empire and other parts of Europe into less-populated regions of Central and Eastern Europe. It took place mainly between the 11th and 14th centuries, though in some regions it continued longer.

The affected area ranged roughly from Slovenia in the south, across the southern Baltic coast, to Estonia and Latvia in the north, and extended east into modern-day Poland, the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Hungary, and Romania (Transylvania). Settlers founded new towns under German municipal law (such as Magdeburg law and Lübeck law), cleared forests, established villages, and introduced new agricultural techniques.

Background and course

Population growth in the western Empire, the invitation of settlers by local Slavic and Hungarian rulers seeking to develop their lands, and military campaigns such as the Wendish Crusade (1147) and the conquests of the Teutonic Order in Prussia all contributed to the movement. The settlers (called hospites) were often granted hereditary tenures, personal freedom, and lower dues in return for developing new land.

Effects

The Ostsiedlung reshaped the ethnic, linguistic, legal, and urban geography of east-central Europe for centuries. Many cities, including Gdańsk (Danzig), Wrocław (Breslau), Szczecin (Stettin), and Tallinn, were founded or refounded under German law in this period. The process ended in most areas by the mid-14th century, slowed by the Black Death and political change. Its demographic legacy was largely reversed after the Second World War with the westward redrawing of borders and the expulsion of Germans from Eastern Europe.
