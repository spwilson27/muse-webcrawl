Composite bow

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. Not to be confused with compound bow.

A composite bow is a traditional bow made from horn, wood, and sinew laminated together. The materials exploit their complementary properties: horn resists compression on the belly (the side facing the archer), sinew stores energy in tension on the back, and a wooden core joins them. The result is a bow that stores far more energy for its size than a simple wooden self bow, making it ideal for use on horseback.

Composite bows were typically short and strongly recurved, suiting mounted archers.

Construction

Making a composite bow was slow and skilled work. Thin strips of water-buffalo or ibex horn were glued to a shaped wooden core with fish or hide glue; layers of sinew (leg tendon) were then laid on the back with glue and left to cure, sometimes for a year. Stiffening laths of bone or antler (siyahs) often reinforced the limb tips. Because animal glue weakens with moisture, such bows were carefully protected from damp.

History

Composite bows were developed on the Eurasian steppe by the second millennium BC and spread to Egypt, Assyria, Persia, Greece, and Rome. They were the signature weapon of steppe peoples — Scythians, Huns, Magyars, Turks, and Mongols — whose horse archers dominated warfare from Hungary to China. Distinct regional forms include the Turkish bow, optimised for flight shooting over extreme distances; the Mongol bow; and the Manchu bow, large and powerful for heavy arrows. The English longbow, by contrast, was a self bow of yew. Composite bows remained in military and sporting use into the age of firearms, and traditional forms are still made and shot today, notably in Korean, Turkish, and Hungarian archery revivals.
