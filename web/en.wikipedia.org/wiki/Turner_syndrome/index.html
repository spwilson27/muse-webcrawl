Turner syndrome (TS), also known as 45,X or 45,X0, is a genetic disorder in which a female is partially or completely missing an X chromosome. It was described by American endocrinologist Henry Turner in 1938. Signs and symptoms vary among those affected but commonly include short stature, a webbed neck, low hairline, heart defects such as coarctation of the aorta, and ovarian insufficiency, which usually results in infertility.

Signs and symptoms

Common features include short stature (untreated adult height averages about 20 cm below peers), swelling of the hands and feet at birth (lymphedema), a broad chest with widely spaced nipples, low posterior hairline, and a webbed neck. Cardiac anomalies — especially bicuspid aortic valve and coarctation of the aorta — occur in a substantial minority, as do kidney abnormalities, hearing loss, and increased risk of autoimmune thyroid disease and type 2 diabetes. Intelligence is usually normal, though some have difficulties with spatial and mathematical reasoning.

Cause

Turner syndrome results from a chromosomal abnormality in which one X chromosome is missing or structurally altered. In about half of cases the karyotype is 45,X (complete monosomy X); others are mosaics (45,X/46,XX) or carry a partial deletion or ring X. Unlike many aneuploidies, it is not associated with advanced maternal age; the missing chromosome is usually of paternal origin.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis may be made prenatally by ultrasound findings and genetic testing, or after birth based on physical features, confirmed by karyotype analysis. Some individuals with mosaicism are not diagnosed until adolescence when puberty fails to develop.

Treatment

There is no cure; treatment is supportive and multidisciplinary. Growth hormone therapy in childhood increases final adult height. Estrogen replacement induces puberty and maintains bone and cardiovascular health. Regular screening of the heart, kidneys, thyroid, hearing, and blood pressure is recommended throughout life. Fertility treatment using donor eggs allows some women with TS to carry pregnancies.

Prognosis and epidemiology

Turner syndrome occurs in approximately 1 in 2,000 to 2,500 live female births, though it is present in a much higher proportion of conceptions, most of which miscarry. Life expectancy is modestly reduced, largely due to cardiovascular disease.
