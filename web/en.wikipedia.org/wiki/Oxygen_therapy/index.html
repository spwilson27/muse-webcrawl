Oxygen therapy

Oxygen therapy is the administration of supplemental oxygen as a medical treatment, given when the body is not getting enough oxygen on its own (hypoxaemia). It is one of the most common interventions in hospitals and emergency care, used in conditions ranging from pneumonia and heart failure to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), shock and carbon monoxide poisoning. Oxygen is treated as a drug: it is prescribed with a target saturation range, delivered through devices ranging from nasal cannulae to non-rebreather masks and mechanical ventilators, and monitored with pulse oximetry.

Indications

Acute indications include respiratory failure from pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, asthma and COPD exacerbations; shock and major trauma; sepsis; carbon monoxide poisoning (where high-flow oxygen accelerates CO elimination); cluster headache, which often responds to high-flow oxygen; and peri-operative and post-anaesthetic care. Long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT) at home, used for at least 15 hours a day, improves survival in patients with severe COPD and chronic hypoxaemia. Ambulatory and palliative oxygen may relieve breathlessness, although evidence for benefit in patients who are not hypoxaemic is limited.

Delivery

Low-flow systems include nasal cannulae (delivering roughly 24–40% oxygen at 1–6 L/min) and simple face masks. Fixed-performance Venturi masks deliver precise oxygen concentrations, important for patients with COPD. Non-rebreather masks with a reservoir bag can deliver 60–90% oxygen in emergencies. Heated, humidified high-flow nasal oxygen can deliver up to 60 L/min and provides some positive airway pressure. In critical care, oxygen is delivered via invasive or non-invasive ventilation, and in the most severe cases of respiratory failure via extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Standard sources are wall-supplied piped oxygen, compressed-gas cylinders and oxygen concentrators, which are common for home use.

Targets and safety

Modern guidelines, such as those of the British Thoracic Society, emphasise prescribing oxygen to a target range of oxygen saturation (SpO2) rather than at a fixed dose: 94–98% for most acutely ill patients, but 88–92% for those with COPD or other conditions predisposing to hypercapnic respiratory failure, in whom excessive oxygen can worsen carbon dioxide retention. Excess oxygen is not benign: hyperoxia can cause absorption atelectasis, worsen outcomes after myocardial infarction and cardiac arrest in some studies, and, at high pressures or prolonged exposure, cause oxygen toxicity affecting the lungs and central nervous system. In premature infants, excessive oxygen causes retinopathy of prematurity. Oxygen also strongly supports combustion, so smoking near oxygen equipment is a serious fire risk.

History

Oxygen was isolated in the 1770s by Carl Wilhelm Scheele and Joseph Priestley, and its discoverers themselves speculated about medical uses; "pneumatic medicine" enjoyed a brief vogue around 1800. Systematic clinical use began in the early 20th century, notably with John Scott Haldane's work treating soldiers gassed in the First World War, and oxygen tents and masks became standard between the wars. The development of pulse oximetry in the 1970s–80s transformed monitoring, making continuous non-invasive measurement of saturation routine.
