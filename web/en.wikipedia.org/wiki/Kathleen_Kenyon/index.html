Kathleen Kenyon

Born: Kathleen Mary Kenyon, 5 January 1906, London, England
Died: 24 August 1978 (aged 72), Wrexham, Wales
Occupation: Archaeologist
Known for: Excavations at Jericho; Wheeler–Kenyon method

Dame Kathleen Mary Kenyon, DBE, FBA, FSA (5 January 1906 – 24 August 1978) was a British archaeologist of Neolithic culture in the Fertile Crescent. She led excavations at Jericho (Tell es-Sultan) in Jordan from 1952 to 1958, and has been called one of the most influential archaeologists of the 20th century. She was principal of St Hugh's College, Oxford, from 1962 to 1973.

Early life and education

Kenyon was born in London, the eldest daughter of Sir Frederic Kenyon, director of the British Museum. She read history at Somerville College, Oxford, where she became the first female president of the Oxford University Archaeological Society. Her early fieldwork included excavations at Great Zimbabwe in 1929 with Gertrude Caton-Thompson, and at the Roman city of Sabratha in Libya.

Career

From 1931 to 1934 Kenyon worked with Sir Mortimer Wheeler at Verulamium (St Albans), where she refined the stratigraphic excavation technique known as the Wheeler–Kenyon method — digging in a grid of squares separated by baulks, so that sections remain visible for recording the sequence of layers. In the 1930s she also excavated at Samaria in Palestine with John Crowfoot.

Her best-known work was the excavation of ancient Jericho in the 1950s. There she demonstrated that the Neolithic settlement at Jericho was far older than previously thought, identifying one of the earliest fortified towns in the world — including the great Neolithic tower — and showing that the famous walls associated with the biblical story of Joshua dated to a period that did not fit the traditional chronology. From 1961 to 1967 she excavated in Jerusalem, uncovering remains of the City of David.

Kenyon was created a Dame Commander of the Order of the British Empire in 1973. She died in Wrexham in 1978.
