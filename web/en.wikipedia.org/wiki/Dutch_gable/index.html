Dutch gable

A Dutch gable or Flemish gable is a gable whose sides have a shape made up of one or more curves and which has a pediment at the top. The gable may be entirely decorative or the roof behind it may be supported by the gable wall, in the manner of a parapet.

The style is associated with Dutch Renaissance architecture and with the architecture of the Dutch colonial world, including the Netherlands, Flanders, South Africa (Cape Dutch architecture), Indonesia, and New Netherland in North America.

History

During the late medieval period, stepped gables were the dominant type in the Low Countries. Curved, volute-like gables became fashionable in the late 16th and 17th centuries as part of the Northern Mannerist and Dutch Renaissance styles, visible on canal houses in Amsterdam and other Dutch cities. Merchants often decorated the gable to indicate the nature of their trade or warehouse goods.

In Cape Dutch architecture in South Africa, ornate curvilinear gables, often whitewashed and centred on the front façade, are a defining feature of farmhouses such as those at Groot Constantia.

The term is also used in Britain and Australia, where the Dutch gable roof (a combination of a hip roof and a gablet) is a related but distinct roofing form.
