Decimal

Simon Stevin's De Thiende (1585) popularised decimal fractions in Europe.

The decimal numeral system, also called the base-ten or denary system, is the standard system for denoting integer and non-integer numbers throughout the world. It extends the Hindu–Arabic numeral system by using ten digits, 0 through 9, with the value of each digit determined by its position relative to a decimal separator. Numbers smaller than one are written as decimal fractions to the right of the separator, each successive position representing a power of ten: tenths, hundredths, thousandths and so on.

Decimal fractions

A decimal fraction is a fraction whose denominator is a power of ten; in decimal notation the denominator is not written but is implied by the position of the digits. Some decimal fractions terminate, such as 1/4 = 0.25; others repeat indefinitely, such as 1/3 = 0.333… Every rational number can be expressed as a terminating or repeating decimal, and every repeating decimal represents a rational number. Irrational numbers, such as π, have decimal expansions that neither terminate nor repeat.

History

Positional decimal counting arose in India and was transmitted to Europe through Arabic mathematics. Decimal fractions themselves were developed in China and the Islamic world before Simon Stevin introduced them systematically to Europe in his 1585 pamphlet De Thiende ("The Tenth"). The modern decimal point and decimal comma conventions diverged in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries; today the point is standard in most English-speaking countries while the comma predominates in much of continental Europe and Latin America.

Computing

Computers normally store numbers in binary floating point, which cannot represent most decimal fractions exactly; standards such as IEEE 754 also define decimal floating-point formats for financial calculations where exact decimal representation is required. The Dewey Decimal Classification is an unrelated library system that shares only the name.
