Marker-assisted selection

Marker-assisted selection (MAS) is an indirect selection process in plant and animal breeding in which a trait of interest is selected not on the trait itself but on a molecular marker — such as a DNA sequence — that is linked to the trait. Because the marker can be scored early, cheaply and regardless of environment, MAS can greatly speed up breeding compared with conventional phenotypic selection.

How it works

Breeders first identify markers (for example SNPs, microsatellites or RFLPs) that co-segregate with a target gene or quantitative trait locus (QTL). Individuals carrying the favourable marker allele are retained for further breeding, while others are discarded, often at the seedling stage.

Applications

MAS is widely used to introgress disease-resistance genes in crops such as rice (for example bacterial blight and blast resistance and submergence tolerance via the SUB1 gene), to pyramid multiple resistance genes into a single variety, and to select for quality traits. In livestock it supports selection for traits that are expensive or slow to measure.

Limitations

MAS requires initial investment in marker development and validation, and works best for traits controlled by a few large-effect loci; for highly polygenic traits, genomic selection is generally preferred.
