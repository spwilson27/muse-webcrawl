Bentonite is an absorbent aluminium phyllosilicate clay consisting mostly of montmorillonite, a member of the smectite group. It is formed from the weathering of volcanic ash in seawater, which converts the volcanic glass present in the ash to clay minerals. Bentonite beds are typically white or pale blue when fresh, darkening to yellow, olive green, and eventually brown with exposure.

Types

The two main commercial types are sodium bentonite and calcium bentonite. Sodium bentonite expands dramatically when wet, absorbing many times its dry mass in water, and is prized as a sealant. Calcium bentonite swells much less but can be converted into a swelling ("sodium-exchanged") form by treatment with soda ash. Potassium bentonite, formed by potassium-rich alteration, also occurs.

The name was proposed in 1898 by Wilbur C. Knight for clay occurring near Fort Benton, Montana, in the Benton Shale. The large deposits in Wyoming are therefore sometimes called Wyoming bentonite.

Uses

  • Drilling mud: the principal use by volume; bentonite viscosifies water-based drilling fluids, seals borehole walls, and helps remove cuttings.
  • Foundry sand: a binder in green sand moulds for metal casting.
  • Sealing and civil engineering: slurry walls, landfill liners, and pond sealing, exploiting its low permeability when hydrated.
  • Iron ore pelletising and other pelletising applications.
  • Cat litter, absorbents, and desiccants (fuller’s-earth-type uses).
  • Clarification of wine, oils, and other liquids, and as a filler in paints, pharmaceuticals, and cosmetics.

Geology and occurrence

Bentonite deposits occur worldwide, usually in Cretaceous and younger sedimentary sequences derived from altered volcanic ash falls. Major producing regions include Wyoming and Montana in the United States, the island of Milos in Greece, and deposits in China, India, and Turkey.
