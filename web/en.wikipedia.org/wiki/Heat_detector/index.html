Heat detector

A heat detector is a fire alarm device designed to respond to the thermal energy of a fire. Heat detectors have two main classifications: rate-of-rise detectors, which respond to a rapid increase in temperature, and fixed-temperature detectors, which respond when the operating element reaches a predetermined temperature (commonly 57 °C / 135 °F or higher). They are used where smoke detectors would give false alarms — kitchens, garages, boiler rooms and dusty industrial spaces — and respond more slowly than smoke detectors to most fires.

Types

  • Fusible-element (eutectic alloy) detectors — a solder of a defined melting point holds contacts or a sprinkler-style element; once melted, it cannot be restored.
  • Bimetallic detectors — differential bending of a bimetal strip closes or opens contacts; these reset when cool.
  • Thermistor/electronic detectors — temperature-sensitive resistors monitored by a panel, which may combine fixed-temperature and rate-of-rise functions.
  • Line-type heat detection — heat-sensitive cable run along conveyors, cable trays or tunnels.

Applications and standards

Heat detectors are specified in fire codes and standards such as NFPA 72 (U.S.) and BS 5839 (U.K.), with spacing based on ceiling height and detector temperature rating and classification. Because life-safety warning is slower, codes generally require smoke alarms in sleeping areas, with heat detectors as a supplement in hostile environments.
