Foundationalism

Foundationalism concerns philosophical theories of knowledge resting upon the claim that knowledge and justification are structured like a building: some beliefs are basic or foundational, justified without support from other beliefs, and these support all other beliefs, which are justified by inference from the foundations. Foundationalism is a response to the regress problem in epistemology: if every justified belief requires justification by another belief, the chain either regresses infinitely, circles back on itself, or stops at beliefs that need no further support.

Basic beliefs

Foundationalists hold that there are basic beliefs that are justified but not by other beliefs. Classical foundationalism, associated with René Descartes, required basic beliefs to be infallible, indubitable, and incorrigible—for Descartes, the cogito ("I think, therefore I am") and clear and distinct perceptions served this role. Modest or fallibilist foundationalism, developed by philosophers such as Roderick Chisholm and later Alvin Plantinga, allows basic beliefs to be justified yet defeasible.

Sources of basic beliefs

Candidates for basic beliefs include those formed directly from sensory experience, introspection of one's own mental states, memory, rational intuition (such as elementary truths of logic and arithmetic), and, in Plantinga's Reformed epistemology, belief in God formed through a sensus divinitatis.

Criticism

The main rival to foundationalism is coherentism, which holds that beliefs are justified by their coherence with the whole system of beliefs. Critics such as Wilfrid Sellars attacked the "Myth of the Given", arguing that experience cannot justify beliefs without conceptual content. Donald Davidson and others have questioned whether a belief can be justified by anything other than another belief.
