Chymotrypsin

Ribbon diagram of bovine chymotrypsin.

Chymotrypsin (EC 3.4.21.1) is a digestive serine protease enzyme produced by the pancreas that is released into the small intestine, where it hydrolyses proteins during digestion. It is secreted as the inactive precursor (zymogen) chymotrypsinogen and is activated in the duodenum by trypsin, which cleaves the precursor to yield the active enzyme.

Mechanism and specificity

Chymotrypsin preferentially cleaves peptide bonds on the carboxyl side of large hydrophobic amino acid residues, especially aromatic residues such as tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine, and also leucine and methionine. Its active site contains the classic catalytic triad of aspartate, histidine, and serine (Asp102, His57, Ser195 in bovine chymotrypsin), and catalysis proceeds through a covalent acyl-enzyme intermediate, stabilised by an "oxyanion hole". Because its mechanism, kinetics, and structure (solved by David Blow and colleagues in 1967) are so well characterised, chymotrypsin is one of the most thoroughly studied enzymes in biochemistry and a standard model of enzyme catalysis.

Activation

Trypsin first cleaves chymotrypsinogen between Arg15 and Ile16 to give π-chymotrypsin, which then processes itself to the mature α-chymotrypsin: a molecule of three polypeptide chains (A, B and C chains) held together by disulfide bonds, with a molecular weight of about 25 kDa.

Uses

Chymotrypsin is used analytically in proteomics for protein digestion prior to mass spectrometry, and has been used clinically as an anti-inflammatory enzyme preparation and in cataract surgery (zonulysis). It belongs to the chymotrypsin family (clan PA) of serine peptidases; related human enzymes include chymotrypsin-like proteases encoded by genes such as CTRC and CTRL1.
