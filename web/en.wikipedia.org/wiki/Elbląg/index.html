Elbląg

Elbląg (German: Elbing) is a city in the Warmian-Masurian Voivodeship of northern Poland, located on the Elbląg River near its outflow into the Vistula Lagoon, about 55 kilometres (34 mi) south-east of Gdańsk. With a population of around 115,000, it is one of the oldest cities in the region, founded by the Teutonic Order in 1237, and was long a significant Baltic port and member of the Hanseatic League.

History

The site was near the early medieval trading settlement of Truso, visited by the traveller Wulfstan around 890. The Teutonic Knights built a castle here in 1237 and the town received Lübeck law in 1246. Elbing flourished as a Hanseatic port; in the 16th and 17th centuries it was a notable centre of English trade in the Baltic, hosting the Eastland Company. The city passed to the Polish Crown in 1466 under the Second Peace of Thorn, forming part of Royal Prussia, and was annexed by Prussia in the First Partition of Poland in 1772. The opening of the Elbląg Canal (Oberland Canal) in the 19th century, with its famous inclined planes carrying boats over land, linked the city to the Masurian lake district. Heavily destroyed in 1945 during the East Prussian offensive, Elbląg became part of Poland after the Second World War; its Old Town has been gradually rebuilt since the 1980s in a distinctive "retroversion" style that recreates historic street patterns with contemporary buildings.

Sights

Landmarks include St Nicholas Cathedral, a brick Gothic church whose 95-metre tower dominates the city, the Market Gate (Brama Targowa), the Elbląg Canal boat trips, and the Museum of Archaeology and History housed in the former Teutonic castle complex and gymnasium. The nearby Bażantarnia park provides woodland walking in the city's moraine hills.

Economy and transport

Elbląg has industries in power engineering (including a turbine works now part of GE), brewing (the Elbrewery, maker of EB beer) and furniture. The city is a rail junction, and the opening of the shipping channel through the Vistula Spit in 2022 gave its port direct access to the Baltic Sea without passing Russian waters.
