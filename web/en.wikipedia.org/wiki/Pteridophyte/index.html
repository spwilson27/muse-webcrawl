Pteridophyte

Includes: Ferns, horsetails, clubmosses, spikemosses, quillworts
Reproduction: Spores; alternation of generations
Status: Paraphyletic informal grouping

A pteridophyte is a vascular plant that reproduces by spores rather than seeds or flowers. The term traditionally covers the ferns (including horsetails) and the lycophytes (clubmosses, spikemosses, and quillworts). The grouping is paraphyletic, since seed plants evolved from within this grade, so modern classifications treat lycophytes and ferns as separate lineages; the word survives as a convenient informal label.

Life cycle

Pteridophytes show alternation of generations: the conspicuous plant is the diploid sporophyte, which produces spores by meiosis, typically in sporangia clustered as sori on fern fronds. Spores germinate into small, free-living haploid gametophytes (prothalli) that produce gametes; the swimming sperm require water to reach the egg, a legacy of their aquatic ancestry.

History

Lycophytes dominated Carboniferous forests as giant tree-sized forms whose remains form major coal deposits. Today there are roughly 12,000 fern species, making them the second most diverse group of vascular plants after the flowering plants.
