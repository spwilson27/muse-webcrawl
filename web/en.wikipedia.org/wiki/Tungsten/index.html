Tungsten (also known as wolfram) is a chemical element with the symbol W and atomic number 74. It is a rare, steel-gray transition metal famous for having the highest melting point of all metallic elements (3,422 °C) and the highest tensile strength at elevated temperatures, together with an extremely low vapour pressure. Its name comes from the Swedish tung sten, "heavy stone"; its symbol W derives from wolfram, from the mineral wolframite, one of its principal ores along with scheelite.

Occurrence and production

Tungsten is never found free in nature. It is extracted mainly from wolframite and scheelite ores, with China dominating world production and reserves. Because the metal cannot be melted and cast by ordinary means, tungsten parts are made by powder metallurgy — pressing and sintering tungsten powder. The element was isolated in 1783 by the Spanish brothers Juan José and Fausto Elhuyar.

Applications

More than half of tungsten consumption goes into cemented carbides (such as tungsten carbide, WC), among the hardest engineering materials known, used for cutting tools, drills, and wear parts. Pure tungsten's refractoriness made it the classic filament material of incandescent lamps and remains essential for TIG welding electrodes, X-ray tube targets, vacuum-tube and heating elements, and rocket nozzles. Alloyed with steel it produces high-speed and tool steels. Its high density (19.25 g/cm³, comparable to gold and uranium) is exploited in counterweights, radiation shielding, darts, and kinetic-energy penetrator ammunition. In electronics, tungsten is used for interconnects in integrated circuits.

Biological role

Though generally of low toxicity, tungsten is notable in biology as the heaviest element known to be used by living organisms: certain archaea and bacteria employ tungsten-containing enzymes (tungstoenzymes) in place of the molybdenum enzymes used by most life.
