Science as a Vocation

"Science as a Vocation" (German: Wissenschaft als Beruf) is a lecture delivered in 1917 by the German sociologist Max Weber to students at the University of Munich, and published in 1919. Together with its companion lecture "Politics as a Vocation", it is among Weber's most famous works.

Content

Weber examines the external conditions of academic life in Germany and America and the inner calling required of the scholar. He argues that modern science is a specialised, endless enterprise in which every achievement will in time be surpassed, and that the scholar must find meaning in serving that enterprise rather than in producing lasting results. Central to the lecture is the idea of the "disenchantment of the world" (Entzauberung): rationalisation and intellectualisation mean that mysterious forces no longer govern life, and science itself cannot answer the ultimate questions of meaning or tell us how to live. The teacher, Weber insists, must not use the lectern as a prophet or demagogue, because value judgements cannot be derived from empirical science; students should be given intellectual integrity and the courage to face the "demands of the day".

Influence

The lecture remains a classic statement of the ethics of scholarship and of value-freedom in social science, and its account of disenchantment is a cornerstone of Weber's theory of modernity.
