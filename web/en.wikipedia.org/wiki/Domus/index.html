Domus

Domus
TypeSingle-family town house, ancient Rome
Key featuresAtrium with impluvium, tablinum, peristyle garden, cubicula
Well-preserved examplesPompeii and Herculaneum (House of the Faun, House of the Vettii)

In ancient Rome, the domus (plural domus or domūs) was the type of town house occupied by the upper classes and, in the provinces, by wealthy families. It was inward-facing and often outwardly plain, in contrast to the insulae (apartment blocks) that housed most urban Romans and the suburban villa.

Layout

A typical domus was entered through a vestibule and fauces (corridor) from the street, leading to the atrium, a large central hall open to the sky through a sloped roof (compluvium) that channelled rainwater into a shallow pool, the impluvium. Around the atrium opened the bedrooms (cubicula), dining rooms (triclinia), and the tablinum, the master's study where he received clients at the morning salutatio. Larger houses added a peristyle — a colonnaded garden court — at the rear, around which further reception and family rooms were arranged. Shops (tabernae) were often built into the street frontage.

Decoration and daily life

Wealthy domus were richly decorated with wall paintings (the four Pompeian styles), mosaics, and sculpture; the House of the Faun in Pompeii, which covered an entire city block and contained the Alexander Mosaic, is among the most famous examples. The domus was both a private residence and a stage for its owner's public and political life.
