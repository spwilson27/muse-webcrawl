Yasujirō Ozu (小津 安二郎, 12 December 1903 – 12 December 1963) was a Japanese filmmaker, widely regarded as one of the greatest directors in the history of cinema. Beginning in the silent era, he made 54 films, developing a distinctive style marked by low "tatami-level" camera positions, elliptical storytelling and quiet domestic subjects. His masterpiece Tokyo Story (1953) is frequently ranked among the greatest films ever made.

Life and career

Born in the Fukagawa district of Tokyo, Ozu grew up partly in Matsusaka, Mie, where he became a devoted cinemagoer. He joined the Shochiku studio in 1923 as an assistant cameraman and directed his first film, Sword of Penitence (1927). His early work ranged across genres, including student comedies such as I Graduated, But… (1929) and the social drama Tokyo Chorus (1931). With I Was Born, But… (1932), a comedy of childhood with serious undertones, he found his characteristic manner.

Ozu served in the army in China from 1937 to 1939 and again made propaganda films in Singapore during the Pacific War. Post-war works including Late Spring (1949), Early Summer (1951) and Tokyo Story (1953) formed the celebrated "Noriko trilogy" with actress Setsuko Hara. He made his first colour film, Equinox Flower, in 1958, and his final film, An Autumn Afternoon, in 1962. He died of cancer on his 60th birthday. His gravestone at Engaku-ji in Kamakura bears only the character 無 (mu, "nothingness").

Style

Ozu's mature films depict family life — marriages, generational conflict, the departure of children — with minimal melodrama. He used a static camera placed about a tatami-mat's height from the floor, eschewed fades and dissolves in favour of straight cuts, employed "pillow shots" of empty rooms and landscapes, and frequently omitted key plot events, showing instead their before and after. Long-time collaborators included screenwriter Kōgo Noda and actors Chishū Ryū and Setsuko Hara.

Reputation

Long considered "too Japanese" for Western audiences, Ozu was championed abroad from the 1970s onward; in the 2012 Sight and Sound directors' poll, Tokyo Story was voted the greatest film of all time.
