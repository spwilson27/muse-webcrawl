Quantum optics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Photograph of a quantum optics laboratory: a table of mirrors, lenses and laser beams in the dark]
A quantum optics experiment on an optical table.

Quantum optics is a branch of atomic, molecular and optical physics dealing with how individual quanta of light, known as photons, interact with atoms and molecules. It includes studying the particle-like properties of photons, the quantised nature of the electromagnetic field, and light's nonclassical states — squeezed light, single photons, entangled photon pairs — that have no counterpart in classical optics.

The field was born with the quantum theory of radiation (Dirac, 1927) and came of age with the laser (1960): Roy Glauber's coherence theory (1963, Nobel 2005) gave the language; photon antibunching (1977) proved light's quantum granularity; cavity QED (Serge Haroche) and trapped ions (David Wineland) made single atoms and photons engineerable — work crowned by the 2012 Nobel. Quantum optics supplies the photons for Bell tests, Quantum key distribution, Quantum teleportation and much of Quantum computing, and its squeezed light now improves LIGO's sensitivity beyond the standard quantum limit.

Nonclassical light

A laser's light is a coherent state — the most classical quantum light. Quantum optics begins where classical description fails: a single photon (which cannot be split at a beam splitter — it goes one way, whole), squeezed states (noise redistributed between quadratures), and entangled pairs from parametric down-conversion, the workhorse source of modern quantum information experiments.
