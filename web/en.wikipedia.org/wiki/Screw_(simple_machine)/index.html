Screw (simple machine)

A screw is a simple machine that converts rotational motion into linear motion, and a torque into a linear force. It consists of a cylinder with a helical groove — the thread — cut into its surface. The screw is one of the six classical simple machines described by Renaissance scientists, and geometrically it can be understood as an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder.

History

The water screw, used for lifting irrigation water, was described by Archimedes in the 3rd century BC and bears his name. Screws were later used in presses for olive oil and wine, in the screw press adapted by Gutenberg's era for printing, and from the 18th century in precision instruments and machine tools. Henry Maudslay's screw-cutting lathe around 1800 made standardised threads practical.

Mechanical advantage

When a torque is applied to a screw — by a screwdriver, a handle, or a nut — the thread advances by one pitch per revolution. The ideal mechanical advantage is the ratio of the distance moved by the effort in one turn (2πr, where r is the handle radius) to the pitch. Fine threads give large mechanical advantage but advance slowly; friction, usually a disadvantage in machines, is what allows screws to hold fasteners tight without unwinding.

Applications

Screws serve as threaded fasteners, in vises and clamps, in jacks and micrometers, as Archimedes' screws and screw conveyors for moving fluids and granular material, and as worm gears for speed reduction.
