Lucius Junius Brutus is traditionally regarded as the founder of the Roman Republic and, by tradition, one of its first two consuls in 509 BC. He led the revolt that overthrew the Roman monarchy and expelled the last king, Lucius Tarquinius Superbus.

Legend

According to Livy and other later sources, Brutus was the nephew of King Tarquin the Proud. After Tarquin murdered his father and brother, Brutus feigned dullness — his cognomen Brutus means "dullard" — to survive at court. The outrage committed by the king's son Sextus Tarquinius against the noblewoman Lucretia, and her subsequent suicide, ignited revolt: Brutus cast off his disguise, brandished the bloody knife, and swore to expel the Tarquins. Rome abolished the kingship and instituted the consulate, with Brutus and Lucius Tarquinius Collatinus as the first consuls.

consulship and death

As consul, Brutus is said to have presided over the execution of his own sons, who had conspired to restore the Tarquins — an act held up for centuries as the supreme example of republican duty over family feeling. He died in 509 BC at the Battle of Silva Arsia, killing the king's son Arruns in single combat as both men fell. The women of Rome are said to have mourned him for a year as the avenger of Lucretia.

Legacy

Brutus became the archetypal Roman republican hero. His supposed descendant Marcus Junius Brutus invoked his example when assassinating Julius Caesar in 44 BC. The ancient bronze bust known as the Capitoline Brutus is traditionally identified with him, and his story furnished subjects for artists and dramatists, including Jacques-Louis David's The Lictors Bring to Brutus the Bodies of His Sons.
