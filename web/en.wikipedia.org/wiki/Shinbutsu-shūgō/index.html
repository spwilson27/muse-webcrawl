Shinbutsu-shūgō

Shinbutsu-shūgō (神仏習合, "syncretism of kami and buddhas"), also called Shinbutsu-konkō (神仏混淆, "contamination of kami and buddhas"), is the syncretism of Shinto and Japanese Buddhism in Japan. When Buddhism was introduced to Japan through Baekje in the 6th century, it did not displace the native kami worship; instead, the two belief systems gradually merged over the following centuries, so that for most of Japanese history shrines and temples were intertwined in combined complexes (jingū-ji) and the kami were understood within Buddhist cosmology.

Development

The process of fusion unfolded over several stages. Initially, temples were built within shrine precincts to perform Buddhist rites for the kami, who were conceived as beings themselves in need of salvation. In the Nara and Heian periods, kami were given Buddhist titles such as gongen ("provisional manifestation") and bodhisattva ranks. The doctrine of honji suijaku ("original ground and manifest traces") systematised the synthesis: the kami were the local Japanese traces or emanations of universal buddhas and bodhisattvas — for example, the sun goddess Amaterasu came to be identified with Dainichi Nyorai (Vairocana). Mountain asceticism (Shugendō) grew directly out of this fusion, and combinatory systems such as Ryōbu Shintō interpreted Shinto shrines through the two mandalas of esoteric Buddhism.

Separation

The fusion endured for over a millennium, until the Meiji Restoration. In 1868, the new government promulgated the shinbutsu bunri ("separation of kami and buddhas") edicts, forcibly disentangling the two religions: Buddhist images and implements were removed from shrines, shrine priests who were Buddhist monks were laicised, and many temples — especially those fused into shrine complexes — were destroyed or confiscated in the ensuing haibutsu kishaku anti-Buddhist movement. The separation was, however, never fully achieved at the level of folk practice, and elements of the syncretism persist in Japanese religious life today, from household altars combining kamidana and butsudan to combined ritual calendars.
