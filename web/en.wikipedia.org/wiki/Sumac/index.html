Sumac

KingdomPlantae
FamilyAnacardiaceae
GenusRhus L.
Type speciesRhus coriaria

Sumac (also spelled sumach) is a common name for shrubs and small trees of the genus Rhus in the cashew family, Anacardiaceae, together with related segregate genera. Sumacs grow in subtropical and temperate regions worldwide, and are recognised by their spirally arranged pinnate leaves and dense conical clusters of small red drupes, often called sumac bobs.

Description

Sumacs are dioecious shrubs or small trees, typically 1–10 metres tall. The leaves are usually pinnately compound, though some species have trifoliate or simple leaves, and many turn brilliant red or orange in autumn. The flowers are small, greenish or creamy white, borne in dense terminal panicles. The fruit is a drupe covered in fine hairs that give the clusters a fuzzy, deep-red appearance.

Culinary use

The dried, ground fruit of species such as Sicilian sumac (Rhus coriaria) is a staple spice in Middle Eastern, Mediterranean, and Central Asian cooking. It has a tart, lemony flavour and a deep red colour, and is sprinkled over salads, grilled meats, hummus, and rice dishes; it is also a key ingredient of the spice blend za'atar. In North America, the sour drupes of smooth and staghorn sumac were traditionally steeped in water to make a lemonade-like drink sometimes called "Indian lemonade" or sumac-ade.

Other uses

Sumac leaves and bark are rich in tannins and were historically used in leather tanning — Sicilian sumac in particular produced fine, supple leather. The plants are also grown as ornamentals for their autumn colour. Some former members of the genus, such as poison sumac (now Toxicodendron vernix), are toxic and cause contact dermatitis; edible sumacs are distinguished by their red, upright fruit clusters, whereas poison sumac bears loose clusters of white berries.

Selected species

  • Rhus glabra — smooth sumac
  • Rhus typhina — staghorn sumac
  • Rhus copallinum — winged sumac
  • Rhus aromatica — fragrant sumac
  • Rhus chinensis — Chinese sumac, source of nutgalls
