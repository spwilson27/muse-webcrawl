Lucky charm
CategoryFolklore, superstition
Related conceptsAmulet, talisman
Common examplesFour-leaf clover, horseshoe, rabbit's foot, lucky coin

A lucky charm is an object believed to bring good luck to its possessor. Lucky charms are found in cultures worldwide and range from naturally occurring curiosities to manufactured amulets and talismans. The belief in them is a form of superstition, though many people keep such objects as sentimental tokens without literal belief.

Common examples

Among the best-known lucky charms in Western tradition are the four-leaf clover, the horseshoe hung above a door, the rabbit's foot, wishbones, and "lucky" coins. In other cultures, examples include the maneki-neko "beckoning cat" of Japan, dreamcatchers of Native American origin, the Nazar (evil eye) amulet of the Mediterranean and Middle East, scarabs of ancient Egypt, and jade charms in China. Certain numbers, colours, and gemstones are also treated as lucky in different societies.

History

Amulets date to prehistory: pierced shells, animal teeth, and unusual stones were carried for protection and fortune. The word "charm" derives from the Latin carmen (a song or incantation), reflecting the spells once spoken over such objects. Religious medals and relics functioned similarly in medieval Europe.

Psychology

Psychologists note that lucky charms can improve performance through increased self-confidence and reduced anxiety: experiments have found that participants using "lucky" objects performed better on memory and motor tasks, an effect attributed to higher self-efficacy rather than any property of the object itself.
