Aldol reaction

Aldol reaction
TypeCoupling reaction (C–C bond formation)
Discovered byCharles-Adolphe Wurtz (1872)
Productβ-hydroxyaldehyde or β-hydroxyketone

The aldol reaction is a reaction in organic chemistry that combines two carbonyl compounds — aldehydes or ketones — to form a new carbon–carbon bond, producing a β-hydroxy carbonyl compound known as an aldol (from aldehyde + alcohol). It is one of the most important methods for constructing carbon skeletons in synthesis.

Mechanism

Under basic conditions, a base abstracts an α-hydrogen from one carbonyl partner to form an enolate, which attacks the carbonyl carbon of the second partner; protonation then gives the aldol product. Under acidic conditions the reaction proceeds through the enol tautomer. The initial addition product can dehydrate (often on heating) to give an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound — the aldol condensation.

Selectivity

Controlling which partner forms the enolate and which acts as the electrophile (the "crossed aldol" problem) is a central challenge. Preformed enolates, silyl enol ethers (the Mukaiyama aldol reaction), and chiral auxiliaries such as the Evans syn-propionyl oxazolidinone allow highly diastereo- and enantioselective variants. Proline and related organocatalysts catalyse asymmetric aldol reactions.

Biological significance

Aldol chemistry is central to metabolism: in glycolysis, the enzyme fructose-bisphosphate aldolase cleaves fructose 1,6-bisphosphate into two triose phosphates, and the reverse reaction operates in gluconeogenesis.

Applications

The aldol reaction is used industrially and in the laboratory synthesis of complex molecules, including sugars, statins, and many natural products.
