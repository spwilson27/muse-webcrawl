Servitude (Roman law)

In Roman law, a servitude (Latin: servitus, plural servitutes) was a real right (ius in re aliena) over another person's property, entitling the holder to use that property in a defined way, or to prevent the owner from using it in a defined way. Servitudes are the historical origin of the easements, profits, and covenants of modern property law in civil-law and common-law systems alike.

Categories

Roman jurists divided servitudes into praedial (real) servitudes, which benefited a piece of land (the dominant tenement) and burdened neighbouring land (the servient tenement), and personal servitudes, which benefited a specific person.

Praedial servitudes

Praedial servitudes were further classed as rustic or urban. Rustic servitudes included rights of way in ascending degrees of width and use: iter (passage on foot or horseback), actus (driving cattle or vehicles), and via (a full road); the aquaeductus (conveying water across another's land); and the aqua haustus (drawing water). Urban servitudes concerned buildings, such as the right to discharge rainwater onto a neighbour's roof (stillicidium), to rest beams in a neighbour's wall (tigni immittendi), to build higher or not to be built higher (altius tollendi / altius non tollendi), and rights protecting light and prospect (ne luminibus officiatur).

Personal servitudes

The principal personal servitudes were usufruct (ususfructus), the right to use a thing and take its fruits for life without impairing its substance; usus, a more limited right of use without the fruits; habitatio, the right to dwell in a house; and operae servorum, the right to the services of another's slave.

Principles

Several rules governed all servitudes: a servitude could not impose a positive duty on the servient owner (it consisted in tolerating or abstaining — in patiendo aut non faciendo); there could be no servitude over one's own property; a servitude could not exist over another servitude; and praedial servitudes had to benefit the dominant land permanently and had to be exercised reasonably. Servitudes were created by conveyance, adjudication, legacy, or long use, and were extinguished by merger of the tenements, renunciation, destruction of the property, or non-use for the prescriptive period.

Legacy

Through the medieval ius commune and the great civil codes — notably the French Code civil and the German BGB — Roman servitudes passed into modern civil law, while English law developed the closely related doctrines of easement and profit à prendre.
