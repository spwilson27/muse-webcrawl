The Wisdom of Crowds is a 2004 book by American journalist James Surowiecki arguing that the aggregation of information in groups frequently produces decisions that are better than those any single group member — even an expert — could make. The title alludes to Charles Mackay's 1841 classic Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds, whose thesis Surowiecki inverts.

Argument

Surowiecki opens with Francis Galton's 1906 observation that the median guess of nearly 800 fairgoers at the weight of an ox came within one per cent of the true weight. For a crowd to be "wise", he argues, four conditions must hold: diversity of opinion (each person brings some private information), independence (opinions are not dictated by those of others), decentralisation (people can draw on local knowledge) and aggregation (some mechanism exists for turning private judgements into a collective decision). He explores cognition, coordination and cooperation problems, and cases ranging from prediction markets and the search for the submarine USS Scorpion to traffic flow and corporate decision-making.

Reception and influence

The book became a widely cited popular treatment of collective intelligence, influencing thinking about prediction markets, crowdsourcing and deliberative democracy. Critics have noted limits: crowds fail when members influence each other too strongly, when information cascades form, or when the crowd shares systematic biases.
