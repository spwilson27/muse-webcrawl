Charles Joseph Minard

Charles Joseph Minard (27 March 1781 – 24 October 1870) was a French civil engineer celebrated for his pioneering contributions to information graphics and data visualisation. His flow maps of trade, migration and military campaigns — above all the 1869 chart of Napoleon's disastrous Russian campaign of 1812 — are considered masterpieces of statistical graphics.

Engineering career

Minard studied at the École Polytechnique and the École des Ponts et Chaussées, and spent his career in the French corps of civil engineers, working on canals, bridges and railways across Europe and rising to inspector general before retiring in 1851. He began publishing statistical maps in the 1820s while investigating transport economics, using proportional bands to show the volume of goods and passengers moving along roads, canals and railways.

Thematic cartography

In retirement Minard produced more than fifty "cartes figuratives", distributed free to statesmen and engineers to make arguments about trade and policy. His 1858 maps of French wine exports and British coal shipments, and his paired pie-chart maps comparing meat supplies to French cities, were innovations of thematic cartography. He also charted Hannibal's crossing of the Alps, juxtaposing it with Napoleon's march in a single plate.

The Russian campaign chart

Published in 1869, when Minard was 88, the Carte figurative des pertes successives en hommes de l'Armée Française dans la campagne de Russie 1812–1813 encodes six variables — army size, position, direction of movement, temperature, and time — in a single two-dimensional figure: a thick tan band, narrowing catastrophically, traces the Grande Armée's advance from 422,000 men to Moscow, and a black band, plotted against a temperature scale, shows the remnant of some 10,000 men struggling back during the winter retreat. The statistician Edward Tufte called it "probably the best statistical graphic ever drawn". Minard, a pacifist, intended the chart as an indictment of war. He died in Bordeaux in 1870.
