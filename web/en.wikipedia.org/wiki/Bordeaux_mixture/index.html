Bordeaux mixture

Bordeaux mixture (also called Bordo Mix) is a mixture of copper(II) sulfate (CuSO4) and quicklime (CaO) used as a fungicide. It is used in vineyards, fruit-farms and gardens to prevent infestations of downy mildew, powdery mildew and other fungi. It is sprayed on plants as a preventive treatment; its mode of action is ineffective after a fungus has become established.

History

The mixture was invented in the Bordeaux region of France in the late nineteenth century and is generally credited to the botanist Pierre-Marie-Alexis Millardet, who noticed in 1882 that vines sprayed with a copper-lime mixture (originally daubed to deter pilferers) escaped downy mildew (Plasmopara viticola), which was devastating European vineyards after its accidental introduction from America. Millardet published the treatment in 1885, and it is credited with saving the French wine industry.

Preparation and use

Bordeaux mixture is prepared by dissolving copper sulfate in water and mixing it with suspended hydrated lime. Proportions vary (commonly expressed as the weight of copper sulfate : lime : water, e.g. 1:1:100). Excess copper can accumulate in soils, and the mixture's use in organic farming is restricted under EU rules because of copper's environmental persistence.
