Russian art

Russian art encompasses the visual arts produced in Russia and by Russian artists from the medieval period to the present. Its history is marked by the long dominance of Orthodox religious art, a rapid westernisation after Peter the Great, a celebrated 19th-century realist school, and an explosive avant-garde in the early 20th century that reshaped world modernism, followed by the state-imposed Socialist Realism of the Soviet period.

Icons and medieval art

Art in Kievan Rus' grew from Byzantine models after Christianisation in 988. Icon painting reached its height in the 14th–15th centuries with Theophanes the Greek and Andrei Rublev, whose Trinity (c. 1411 or 1420s) is the most famous Russian icon. Frescoes, illuminated manuscripts, metalwork and carved white-stone churches of Vladimir-Suzdal characterise the period.

Imperial period

Peter the Great's reforms brought Western-style secular painting; the Imperial Academy of Arts was founded in 1757. In the 19th century the Peredvizhniki (the "Wanderers", from 1870) — including Ilya Repin, Ivan Kramskoi, Vasily Surikov and Isaac Levitan — broke with the Academy to paint realistic, socially engaged scenes of Russian life and landscape. Collectors such as Pavel Tretyakov (founder of the Tretyakov Gallery) and Sergei Shchukin patronised both Russian and modern French art.

Avant-garde and Soviet art

Russia produced foundational modernist movements: Rayonism, Kazimir Malevich's Suprematism (the Black Square, 1915), Vladimir Tatlin's Constructivism, and the work of Wassily Kandinsky and El Lissitzky. After 1934 Socialist Realism became the mandatory style in the USSR, though unofficial and conceptual art flourished underground from the 1960s. Since 1991 Russian contemporary art has reintegrated with international markets and institutions.
