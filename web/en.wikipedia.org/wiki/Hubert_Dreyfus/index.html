Hubert Lederer Dreyfus (October 15, 1929 – April 22, 2017) was an American philosopher and professor of philosophy at the University of California, Berkeley. He is best known for his exegesis of Martin Heidegger and for his influential critique of artificial intelligence (AI), arguing that human expertise and skillful coping could not be captured by the rule-based symbol manipulation assumed by classical AI research.

Education and career

Dreyfus studied at Harvard University, where he earned his bachelor's degree in 1951, a master's degree in 1952, and his PhD in 1964. He taught at Brandeis University and at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology before joining the Berkeley faculty in 1968, where he remained for the rest of his career. He also held visiting positions and delivered lectures internationally, and was a fellow of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences.

Critique of artificial intelligence

Dreyfus's critique began in the 1960s with his work at the RAND Corporation and his 1965 paper "Alchemy and Artificial Intelligence." His 1972 book What Computers Can't Do (revised as What Computers Still Can't Do, 1992) drew on Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty to argue that human intelligence depends fundamentally on embodied, background know-how — "being-in-the-world" — rather than on explicit representations and rules. The book provoked strong controversy within the AI community; some predictions proved prescient as symbolic AI struggled, while later connectionist and statistical approaches partially answered his challenge. Dreyfus also wrote Mind over Machine (1986) with his brother Stuart Dreyfus, presenting their five-stage model of skill acquisition, from novice rule-following to expert intuitive response.

Heidegger scholarship

Dreyfus's Being-in-the-World (1991), a commentary on Division I of Heidegger's Being and Time, became a standard reference and helped shape the American reception of Heidegger. His Berkeley lectures on Heidegger, Kierkegaard, and Foucault were widely followed, and recordings of his courses circulated widely online. Late in his career he co-authored All Things Shining (2011) with Sean Kelly, a reading of Western literature through the lens of the sacred and nihilism.
