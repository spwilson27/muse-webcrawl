Haggis

Haggis is a savoury pudding containing sheep's pluck (heart, liver, and lungs), minced with onion, oatmeal, suet, spices, and salt, mixed with stock, and traditionally encased in the animal's stomach. Although its description is not immediately appealing, it has an excellent nutty texture and delicious savoury flavour. Haggis is a traditional Scottish dish, celebrated as the national dish of Scotland, and is the centrepiece of a Burns supper, at which Robert Burns's poem "Address to a Haggis" (1786) is recited.

History

Puddings of offal cooked in a stomach or intestine are ancient and widespread, valued as a way of using perishable parts of a slaughtered animal. Although often claimed as uniquely Scottish — and humorously attributed to a fictional wild animal, the "wild haggis" — similar dishes are found across Britain and Scandinavia, and the word may derive from Old Norse or Middle English terms connected with chopping or haggling meat. The first known recipes appear in 15th-century verse cookbooks. Burns's 1786 address, calling the haggis "great chieftain o' the pudding-race," fixed its emblematic status.

Modern haggis

Commercial haggis is now usually made in a sausage casing rather than a stomach, and vegetarian and vegan versions replace the meat with vegetables, pulses, and mushrooms. Since 1971 the United States has prohibited the import of haggis containing sheep lung, so authentic Scottish haggis cannot legally be imported there. Haggis is traditionally served with mashed turnips (neeps) and potatoes (tatties), often with a whisky sauce, and is also found in Scottish chip shops battered and deep-fried.

Folklore

A longstanding joke presents the haggis as a small Highland animal with legs of unequal length, adapted for running around hillsides. Surveys have repeatedly found a substantial minority of American visitors to Scotland who believe the wild haggis is real.
