POLST — Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment — is an approach to end-of-life planning used in the United States that converts a seriously ill patient's treatment preferences into a set of actionable, portable medical orders. The POLST form is typically printed on distinctive bright-colored paper so that it can be found quickly, is signed by a clinician, travels with the patient between care settings, and is honored by emergency medical services and hospitals. Programs descended from the Oregon original operate in most U.S. states under names such as MOLST (Medical Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment), POST, MOST and others, coordinated by the National POLST organization.

History

POLST grew out of a 1991 conference in Oregon on medical treatment decision-making, where clinicians and ethicists concluded that patients' advance directives were too often unavailable or too vague to guide emergency care. The first POLST form was released in Oregon in 1995, and the paradigm spread state by state during the 2000s, becoming a national movement in care planning for people near the end of life.

Contents

A POLST form is completed after a conversation between the patient (or their surrogate decision-maker) and a health-care professional, and signed as a medical order. Its sections typically cover:

  • Cardiopulmonary resuscitation — attempt CPR, or do not attempt resuscitation (allow natural death) if the person has no pulse and is not breathing.
  • Medical interventions / level of treatment — full treatment; selective treatment (hospital care but no ICU/intubation); or comfort-focused treatment.
  • Artificially administered nutrition — options for long-term feeding, a trial period, or no artificial nutrition.
  • Additional orders, the basis for the decisions, and signatures.

Distinctions and evidence

Unlike a living will or advance directive — a legal document written by an individual, often while healthy, expressing values and future wishes — POLST is an immediate medical order for patients with serious, advanced illness or frailty whose clinicians would not be surprised if they died within the next year or so. Unlike a simple out-of-hospital DNR order, POLST addresses a broader range of interventions and can also direct full treatment. Studies have generally found high rates of concordance between POLST orders and the treatment patients actually receive, including most receiving CPR orders being respected in the field, though research and implementation vary by state and setting, and ethicists debate safeguards around who completes forms and whether institutional pressure can distort patient choice.
