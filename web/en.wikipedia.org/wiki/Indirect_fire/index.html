Indirect fire

Indirect fire is aiming and firing a projectile without relying on a direct line of sight between the gun and its target, as in the case of direct fire. Aiming is performed by calculating azimuth and inclination, and may include correcting aim by observing the fall of shot and calculating new angles.

An artillery battery engaging a target it cannot see directly.

Description

Indirect fire is most commonly associated with field artillery (although the concept dates to before the era of rifled artillery and has since been applied to mortars, naval gunfire against shore targets and other systems). The gun crew does not see the target; instead, aiming data is computed by a fire direction centre and transmitted to the guns, which are laid at the required elevation and bearing. A forward observer, or sensors such as radar, then report where rounds fall so corrections can be applied.

The key advantage of indirect fire is that the firing unit can remain concealed behind terrain — a hill, ridge or urban cover — making it far harder for the enemy to locate and engage it. The disadvantage is dependence on accurate survey, maps, meteorological data and communications.

History

Although indirect fire methods were theorised in the late 19th century, they became dominant during the First World War, when static trench warfare and the need for massed, predicted fire drove the development of techniques such as map shooting, registration, and the predicted-fire methods perfected by artillery staffs. By the Second World War, the American and British artillery systems, built around radio communications and centralised fire direction, were regarded as among the most effective of the conflict.

Technique

To engage a target indirectly, the firing unit must know its own position, the target's position, and the ballistic characteristics of its weapons. Firing data is then corrected for wind, air temperature and density, propellant temperature, and even the rotation of the Earth on long-range shots. Modern systems automate much of this computation.
