Limestone

Limestone is a sedimentary rock composed largely of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃), usually as the mineral calcite, formed from the accumulated shells, skeletons and precipitates of marine organisms. Chalk, travertine and (after metamorphism) marble are its relatives.

Limestone is quarried in vast tonnages for cement, concrete aggregate, steel flux and agricultural lime; it builds landscapes of cliffs, caves and karst towers, and stores much of Earth's carbon. Many famous buildings — from the pyramids' casing to Gothic cathedrals — are limestone.

[Image: A limestone cliff and a cave with stalactites]

Formation and features

Most limestones began as lime muds and shell banks in warm shallow seas; buried and compacted, they lithify. Groundwater slowly dissolves them along joints to carve caves, sinkholes and underground rivers (karst), and precipitates calcite as stalactites and stalagmites.

Acid sensitivity

Limestone fizzes with dilute acid and is attacked by acid rain — the same chemistry that makes it useful as a soil conditioner and flue-gas scrubber, and vulnerable as a building stone.
