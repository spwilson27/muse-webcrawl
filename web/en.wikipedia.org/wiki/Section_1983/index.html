Section 1983

42 U.S.C. § 1983
Section 1983 suits are heard in federal and state courts
Enacted1871 (Civil Rights Act of 1871, § 1)
Codified42 U.S.C. § 1983
PurposeCivil action for deprivation of constitutional rights under color of state law

Section 1983 is a provision of United States law, codified at 42 U.S.C. § 1983, that provides a civil cause of action for the deprivation, under color of state law, of rights secured by the United States Constitution and federal laws. Originally enacted as section 1 of the Civil Rights Act of 1871 — also called the Ku Klux Klan Act — it is the principal vehicle for constitutional tort litigation in the United States, including most federal lawsuits against police officers and other state officials for excessive force, unlawful searches, and other constitutional violations.

Text and elements

The statute provides that every person who, under color of any statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage of a state, subjects any citizen or other person within the jurisdiction to the deprivation of federally secured rights shall be liable to the injured party in an action at law, suit in equity, or other proper proceeding. A plaintiff must therefore show (1) conduct by a "person" acting under color of state law, and (2) that the conduct deprived the plaintiff of a right secured by the Constitution or laws of the United States.

History and development

Enacted during Reconstruction to combat Ku Klux Klan violence and state inaction, the provision lay largely dormant for ninety years. It was revived by the Supreme Court in Monroe v. Pape (1961), which held that Chicago police officers acted under color of law even though their conduct violated state law, and that plaintiffs need not exhaust state remedies. Monell v. Department of Social Services (1978) held that municipalities are "persons" under the statute but are liable only when the violation results from an official policy or custom, not on a theory of respondeat superior.

Qualified immunity and related doctrines

Section 1983 itself contains no immunities, but the Supreme Court has read common-law immunities into it. Most importantly, individual officials enjoy qualified immunity, shielding them from damages unless they violated "clearly established" law — a doctrine that has become a major focus of debate about police accountability. States themselves and state agencies are not "persons" under the statute (Will v. Michigan Dept. of State Police, 1989), and judges, prosecutors and legislators enjoy absolute immunity for acts within their functions. In Bivens actions, an analogous damages remedy is implied directly under the Constitution against federal officials.
