Toffee

Toffee is made by caramelizing sugar or molasses with butter.

Toffee is a confection made by caramelizing sugar or molasses (creating inverted sugar) along with butter, and occasionally flour. The mixture is heated until its temperature reaches the hard crack stage of 149 to 154 °C (300 to 310 °F). While being prepared, toffee is sometimes mixed with nuts or raisins.

History

The word was first recorded in the Oxford English Dictionary in 1825; it is possibly a variation of taffy. Toffee became particularly popular in England in the early 19th century, with regional specialities such as Everton toffee — a buttery toffee first made in the Everton district of Liverpool, associated with the mint shop of Molly Bushell and later given as a treat to supporters of Everton Football Club.

Varieties

A popular variant in the United States is "English toffee", a chewy or hard toffee often coated in chocolate and sprinkled with nuts. Honeycomb toffee (also called cinder toffee or sponge toffee) is aerated with baking soda to produce a rigid, sponge-like texture. Bonfire toffee, a dark treacle-based version, is traditionally eaten around Bonfire Night in Britain.

Toffee vs. caramel

Toffee differs from caramel mainly in temperature and ingredients: toffee is cooked to the hard-crack stage and usually contains butter, giving a brittle or chewy snap, whereas caramel is generally cooked to a lower temperature and is softer.
