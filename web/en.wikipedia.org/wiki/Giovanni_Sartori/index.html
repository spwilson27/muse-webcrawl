Giovanni Sartori (13 May 1924 – 4 April 2017) was an Italian political scientist specialised in comparative politics and democratic theory. His work on political parties, constitutional engineering and concept formation made him one of the most influential European political scientists of the twentieth century.

Career

Sartori taught at the University of Florence, where he founded Italy's first chair of political science, and later at Stanford, Yale and Columbia University in New York. In 1979 he founded the Rivista Italiana di Scienza Politica. His writings on Italy's polarised politics made him a prominent public intellectual at home.

Contributions

In Parties and Party Systems: A Framework for Analysis (1976) Sartori proposed the most widely used classification of party systems, distinguishing predominant, two-party, moderate pluralist and polarised pluralist systems — the latter illustrated by Italy, with its anti-system parties and centrifugal competition. The framework is a standard point of comparison in studies of systems as different as the two-party system and dominant parties such as Japan's LDP.

His methodological writings warned against "conceptual stretching" when comparative concepts travel across contexts, and his "ladder of generality" remains a standard teaching tool. In The Theory of Democracy Revisited (1987) he defended a procedural, electoral understanding of democracy. His critique of television's effect on public reasoning, Homo Videns (1997), reached a wide audience.
