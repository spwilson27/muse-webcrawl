Backbeat

A backbeat is a rhythmic accentuation on the "off" beats of a bar in 4/4 time — specifically beats 2 and 4 — typically played on the snare drum in popular music. It is a defining feature of rock and roll, rhythm and blues, funk, hip hop and most contemporary pop drumming, and contrasts with the downbeat emphasis (beats 1 and 3) characteristic of much European-derived music such as the march.

History

The backbeat was developed by American rhythm-and-blues drummers in the 1940s and early 1950s. Earl Palmer, who played on recordings by Fats Domino and Little Richard, is widely credited with pioneering the rock and roll backbeat — he described it as something he adapted from New Orleans second-line rhythms, played in a forceful eighth-note pattern. Chuck Berry's guitarist-driven records and the drummers of Motown and Stax carried the pattern worldwide in the 1960s.

Effect

Emphasising beats 2 and 4 creates a sense of lift and "groove" and is what audiences clap along to at concerts. Musicologist Anne Danielsen and others have studied the subtle timing variations ("laid-back" or "pushed" backbeats) that distinguish individual drummers and genres such as New Orleans funk.
