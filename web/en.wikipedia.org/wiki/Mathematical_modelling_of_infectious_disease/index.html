Mathematical modelling of infectious disease

Mathematical models are used in epidemiology to study the mechanisms of infectious disease transmission, forecast the course of outbreaks, and evaluate control strategies such as vaccination and social distancing. The field began with Daniel Bernoulli's 1766 analysis of smallpox inoculation, and was formalised in the early 20th century by Kermack and McKendrick, whose work founded the compartmental modelling tradition.

Compartmental models

Compartmental models divide the population into classes such as susceptible (S), infectious (I), and recovered (R), with differential equations governing movement between them. In the basic SIR model, infection spreads at a rate proportional to contacts between susceptible and infectious people, and infectious individuals recover at a constant rate. Extensions add compartments for exposed (E, in SEIR models), maternally protected (M), vaccinated, asymptomatic, quarantined, or deceased individuals, and can include births, deaths, waning immunity, and age structure.

Key quantities

A central parameter is the basic reproduction number, R0 — the average number of secondary cases generated by one case in a fully susceptible population. If R0 > 1, an epidemic can grow; interventions aim to push the effective reproduction number below 1. Models also estimate the herd-immunity threshold, 1 − 1/R0, and the final size of an epidemic.

Other approaches and use

Beyond deterministic compartmental models, researchers use stochastic models for small populations, network models for structured contacts, and agent-based models simulating individuals. Such models informed public-health responses to Ebola, influenza, and COVID-19, though their forecasts depend heavily on assumptions about behaviour and parameters, and are best treated as scenario tools rather than predictions.
