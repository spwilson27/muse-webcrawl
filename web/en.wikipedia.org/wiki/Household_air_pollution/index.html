Household air pollution

Household air pollution (also called indoor air pollution from solid fuels) is the pollution of indoor air caused by cooking, heating and lighting with solid fuels such as wood, crop residues, dung, charcoal and coal in open fires and inefficient stoves, typically in poorly ventilated dwellings. According to the World Health Organization, around 2.1 billion people worldwide rely on such fuels and technologies, and household air pollution was responsible for an estimated 3.2 million deaths per year in 2020, including over 237,000 deaths of children under five.

Pollutants and health effects

Smoke from solid fuels contains fine particulate matter (PM2.5), carbon monoxide, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and other toxic compounds, with kitchen concentrations during cooking often many times higher than outdoor air-quality limits. Women, who usually cook, and young children who stay near them bear the greatest exposure. The resulting disease burden includes pneumonia in children, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, stroke, ischaemic heart disease, lung cancer and cataracts. Smoke from inefficient combustion also contributes to ambient air pollution and climate change through black carbon emissions.

Mitigation

The principal remedy is a transition to clean household energy: liquefied petroleum gas, electricity (including induction cooking), biogas, ethanol and natural gas, alongside improved biomass stoves, chimneys and ventilation as interim measures. Programmes such as India's Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana LPG scheme and the work of the Clean Cooking Alliance have expanded access, though affordability of fuel refills and cultural cooking preferences remain obstacles.
