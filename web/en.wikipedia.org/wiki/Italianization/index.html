Italianization

Italianization (Italian: Italianizzazione) is the process of cultural assimilation in which non-Italian populations adopt Italian language and culture. The term is most often used for the coercive policies of Fascist Italy in the 1920s–1940s.

Fascist-era policies

After the First World War, Italy gained territories with large German-, Slovene- and Croat-speaking populations: South Tyrol, the Julian March (Venezia Giulia) and, after 1939, annexed areas of Slovenia and Dalmatia. The Fascist regime imposed Italian in schools and administration, Italianised surnames and place-names (a programme associated in South Tyrol with Ettore Tolomei's Prontuario), banned minority-language press, and encouraged Italian settlement. Similar assimilation pressure applied in the colonies and, in different forms, to Italy's own regional dialects.

Aftermath

After 1945 some measures were reversed; South Tyrol eventually received extensive autonomy (1972), and Slovene minority rights were recognised in law, though the legacy of the policies remains politically sensitive.
