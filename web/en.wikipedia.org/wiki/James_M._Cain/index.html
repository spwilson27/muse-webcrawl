James Mallahan Cain (July 1, 1892 – October 27, 1977) was an American writer, novelist and journalist. Although Cain himself vehemently opposed the label, he is commonly associated with the hardboiled school of American crime fiction, alongside Dashiell Hammett and Raymond Chandler, and is credited with helping to shape the genre of noir fiction.

Career

Cain worked as a journalist in Baltimore and New York, serving as managing editor of The New Yorker in 1931, before moving to Hollywood as a screenwriter. His first novel, The Postman Always Rings Twice (1934) — the confession of a drifter who murders his lover's husband — was an immediate success, banned in Boston for obscenity and later filmed in 1946 with John Garfield and Lana Turner. Double Indemnity, serialised in 1936, became Billy Wilder's 1944 film noir classic, scripted by Chandler. Mildred Pierce (1941), a tale of maternal ambition without a murder plot, was filmed in 1945 with Joan Crawford. Cain's taut, dialogue-heavy first-person style stripped crime fiction of ornament: his narrators typically want something, scheme to get it, and are destroyed by getting it.

Legacy

French critics in particular placed Cain at the centre of the emerging concept of the roman noir; the postwar Parisian circle around Cahiers du Cinéma and Série noire championed the film versions of his work. Cain received the Grand Master Award from the Mystery Writers of America in 1970.
