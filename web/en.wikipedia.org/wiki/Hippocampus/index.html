Hippocampus

The hippocampus (plural: hippocampi) is a major component of the brains of humans and other vertebrates. It is a curved structure embedded in the medial temporal lobe, named for its resemblance to a seahorse (Greek: hippokampos). The hippocampus belongs to the limbic system and plays central roles in the consolidation of information from short-term memory to long-term memory, and in spatial memory and navigation.

Anatomy

Each hemisphere contains one hippocampus, a folded ridge of cortex within the medial temporal lobe. Its main subdivisions are the dentate gyrus and the hippocampus proper (the cornu ammonis fields CA1–CA3), together with the subiculum. The classic trisynaptic circuit carries information from the entorhinal cortex through the dentate gyrus and CA3 to CA1.

Function

The crucial role of the hippocampus in forming new episodic memories was demonstrated by the famous case of patient H.M. (Henry Molaison), who after bilateral hippocampal removal in 1953 could no longer form new long-term declarative memories. In rodents, "place cells" in the hippocampus fire for specific locations, forming cognitive maps; John O'Keefe and May-Britt and Edvard Moser received the 2014 Nobel Prize for discovering this positioning system. The hippocampus is also involved in imagining future events and is unusually vulnerable to stress, oxygen deprivation, and the pathology of Alzheimer's disease, in which it is among the first structures damaged.

Neurogenesis

The dentate gyrus is one of the few brain regions where new neurons continue to be generated in adulthood, a process implicated in learning and mood regulation, though its extent in adult humans remains debated.
