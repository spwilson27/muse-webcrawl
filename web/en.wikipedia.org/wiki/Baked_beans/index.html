Baked beans is a dish traditionally containing white beans (usually haricot or navy beans) that are parboiled and then baked or stewed in a sauce at low temperature for a long period. Two main styles dominate: the New England style, sweetened with molasses and flavoured with salt pork or bacon, and the British style, in which beans are stewed in a tomato sauce and sold canned.

History

Indigenous peoples of North America cooked beans with maple sugar and bear fat in clay pots buried in coals, a technique adopted by colonial settlers who substituted molasses and salt pork. Boston's association with the dish earned it the nickname "Beantown". Commercial canning began in the late 19th century; the H. J. Heinz company launched baked beans in Fortnum & Mason in London in 1901 and began production in Britain in 1928, making "beans on toast" a staple of the British diet and an essential component of the full English breakfast.

Regional varieties

Boston baked beans use molasses and mustard. In Quebec, fèves au lard are baked with pork and maple syrup. British canned beans are in a comparatively plain, sweet tomato sauce and are eaten on toast, with jacket potatoes, or as part of a fry-up. Greek gigandes plaki bakes giant beans in tomato sauce with olive oil, while Balkan and Serbian prebranac uses onions and paprika. Vegetarian versions of the canned product are standard.

Nutrition

Baked beans are a source of protein, fibre, iron, and folate. British canned varieties are moderate in sugar and salt, which manufacturers have reduced over time in response to public-health guidance.
