Terrane

In geology, a terrane (in full, a tectonostratigraphic terrane) is a fragment of crustal material formed on, or broken off from, one tectonic plate and accreted (sutured) onto crust lying on another plate. The terrane preserves its own distinctive geological history, which differs from that of the surrounding areas; the faults that bound it are therefore evidence of major crustal movement.

Concept and history

The terrane concept was developed in the 1970s from studies of the North American Cordillera, where geologists found that large parts of western North America consist of a collage of fault-bounded blocks whose stratigraphy and fossils could not have formed in their present positions. Many had travelled long distances — in some cases across ocean basins — before being accreted to the continent during subduction and collision. Such far-travelled blocks are called exotic or suspect terranes; Wrangellia, extending from Alaska to Oregon, is a classic example.

Accretion

Terranes may originate as island arcs, oceanic plateaus, microcontinents, or fragments of continental margins. As the plate carrying them reaches a subduction zone, material too buoyant to subduct is instead plastered onto the overriding plate, enlarging the continent in a process called accretion. The boundaries between terranes are typically major faults or suture zones marked by ophiolites and deformed rocks.

Examples

Well-studied terrane collages include the western North American Cordillera (Wrangellia, Stikinia, the Alexander terrane), the Altaids of Central Asia, and the assemblages of the Appalachian orogen. Terrane analysis is now a standard framework for reconstructing the growth of continents through geological time.

Not to be confused with "terrain", which refers to the physical features of land.
