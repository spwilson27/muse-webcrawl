Epi-Olmec culture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Epi-Olmec culture was a cultural area in the central region of the present-day Mexican state of Veracruz, flourishing roughly from 300 BCE to 250 CE, after the decline of the Olmec culture. Centered on sites such as Tres Zapotes and Cerro de las Mesas in the Papaloapan River basin, it is regarded as a successor culture to the Olmec, though with greatly diminished regional influence.

Writing and calendar

The Epi-Olmec are credited with developing the Isthmian script, one of the earliest writing systems in Mesoamerica, and with early use of the Long Count calendar. Important monuments include La Mojarra Stela 1, which bears one of the longest Isthmian inscriptions and Long Count dates corresponding to 143 and 156 CE, and the Tuxtla Statuette, dated to 162 CE. Scholars Justeson and Kaufman proposed a decipherment reading the script as an early Zoquean language, a claim that remains debated.

Characteristics

Epi-Olmec art continued Olmec motifs while becoming more regionalized. Settlements shifted and multiplied in the western Isthmus of Tehuantepec region, and the culture formed a bridge between the Formative Olmec world and the later Classic-period cultures of Veracruz and the Maya region.
