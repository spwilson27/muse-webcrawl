Synodic period

The synodic period of a Solar System body is the time required for that body to return to the same position relative to the Sun as seen from Earth (or, more generally, from another specified vantage point). It is the period of the object's apparent motion — the interval between successive conjunctions or oppositions — and it differs from the sidereal orbital period, which is measured relative to the fixed stars.

Relation to sidereal period

If P is a planet's sidereal period and E is Earth's period (one year), then for a superior planet the synodic period S satisfies

1/S = 1/E − 1/P,

while for an inferior planet (orbiting inside Earth's orbit),

1/S = 1/P − 1/E.

For example, Mars has a sidereal period of about 687 days, giving a synodic period of about 780 days — roughly 26 months, the interval between oppositions of Mars. Venus, with a sidereal period of 225 days, has a synodic period of about 584 days.

The Moon

The most familiar synodic period is the lunar month: the Moon's synodic period of about 29.53 days (the interval between successive new moons) is longer than its sidereal period of 27.32 days, because Earth advances along its orbit during each lunation, so the Moon must travel further to return to the same Sun–Earth–Moon geometry. The phases of the Moon follow the synodic, not the sidereal, period, and lunisolar calendars are based on it.
