Afforestation is the establishment of a forest or stand of trees in an area where there was no previous tree cover — as opposed to reforestation, which is the re-establishment of forest on land that was forested within the recent past. Afforestation is undertaken for timber production, habitat restoration, soil stabilization, and, increasingly, carbon sequestration as a climate-mitigation strategy.

Methods

Afforestation is typically achieved by planting nursery-grown seedlings or direct seeding, sometimes assisted by irrigation, fencing, and weed control during establishment. Assisted natural regeneration — protecting spontaneous seedlings and root suckers from grazing and fire — is cheaper in some landscapes.

Benefits

New forests sequester carbon in biomass and soils, reduce soil erosion, regulate water flows, provide habitat, and supply timber and non-timber products. Strategic afforestation of degraded and marginal land can rehabilitate watersheds and support rural livelihoods.

Criticisms and risks

Afforestation is controversial when it replaces native grasslands, savannas, or wetlands, which have their own biodiversity value and often store more carbon below ground than plantations do above it. Monoculture plantations of fast-growing species such as eucalyptus can deplete water tables and increase fire risk, and critics warn against using afforestation of low-ecological-value plantations to justify continued fossil-fuel emissions ("greenwashing"). Ecologists argue that protecting existing forests and restoring native ecosystems generally delivers more biodiversity benefit per hectare than new plantations.

Major initiatives

Large-scale afforestation programs include China's Grain for Green and Three-North Shelter Forest programs, the African Great Green Wall initiative, and the international Bonn Challenge, which aimed to restore 150 million hectares by 2020 and 350 million by 2030.
