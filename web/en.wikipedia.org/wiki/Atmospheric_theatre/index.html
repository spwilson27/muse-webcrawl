Atmospheric theatre

An atmospheric theatre is a type of movie palace design, popular in the United States during the late 1920s and 1930s, in which the auditorium is decorated to evoke an outdoor setting — typically a Mediterranean or Spanish courtyard, garden or village square — beneath a ceiling painted as a twilight or night sky with twinkling electric stars and clouds drifting across it by means of a projection machine.

Origins

The style was pioneered by the architect John Eberson, an Austrian-born designer who became the leading specialist in American movie palaces. His first fully atmospheric auditorium was the Hoblitzelle Majestic Theatre in Houston, Texas (1923). Eberson reasoned that instead of expensive formal ornament, a theatre could surround its patrons with "a magnificent amphitheatre under a glorious moonlit sky" — an inexpensive plaster illusion that nonetheless felt luxurious.

Features

Atmospheric auditoriums typically feature rough plaster walls finished to imitate stone and stucco, tiled roofs and balconies along the side walls, arches, pergolas, stuffed birds in artificial trees, statuary, and vines. The ceiling is a smooth expanse painted deep blue; small lamps set into it form constellations, and a Brenkert Brenograph or similar projector casts slowly moving clouds across the "sky". Lighting effects suggested the progress of an evening from sunset into night as the film began.

Examples

  • Tampa Theatre, Tampa, Florida (1926), a Mediterranean courtyard fantasy by Eberson, restored and still operating.
  • Majestic Theatre, San Antonio, Texas (1929).
  • Akron Civic Theatre, Akron, Ohio (1929), with a Moorish castle and lagoon theme.
  • Palace Theatre, Louisville, Kentucky (1928), a Spanish Baroque courtyard.
  • Fox Theatre, Atlanta, Georgia (1929), which blends atmospheric and Egyptian/Moorish elements.
  • The Uptown Theatre and other houses in Chicago and the Midwest.

Decline and preservation

The Great Depression ended the era of the grand movie palace, and later auditorium design moved toward streamlined, unornamented boxes. Many atmospheric theatres were demolished or subdivided, but from the 1970s preservation campaigns saved a number of them as performing-arts centres, and several are listed on the National Register of Historic Places.
