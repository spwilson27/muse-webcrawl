Peace research

Peace research is an academic and applied field of study concerned with understanding the causes of violent conflict and the conditions under which peace can be built and sustained. Rooted in the aftermath of the Second World War, it combines insights from political science, sociology, psychology, economics, history and international relations, treating peace as an object of systematic, evidence-based inquiry rather than merely a political aspiration.

Origins

The field took institutional shape in the 1950s and 1960s. In Norway, Johan Galtung founded the Peace Research Institute Oslo (PRIO) in 1959 and launched the Journal of Peace Research in 1964. In the United States, the Journal of Conflict Resolution began publication in 1957, and Kenneth Boulding and others helped establish centres for conflict research at universities such as Michigan. Galtung's distinctions between negative peace (the absence of direct violence) and positive peace (the absence of structural violence and the presence of social justice) became foundational concepts.

Key concepts

Galtung also introduced the idea of structural violence — harm built into social, political and economic arrangements — and later cultural violence, the beliefs and narratives that legitimise other forms of violence. His "violence triangle" links direct, structural and cultural violence as mutually reinforcing. Peace researchers study conflict dynamics, escalation and de-escalation, mediation, peacekeeping, disarmament, post-conflict reconstruction and reconciliation.

Methods and institutions

The field uses quantitative datasets on armed conflict — such as the Uppsala Conflict Data Program (UCDP) — alongside qualitative case studies and fieldwork. Major institutions include PRIO, the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI, founded 1966), the United States Institute of Peace, and university departments at Bradford, Innsbruck, Uppsala and elsewhere. Critics have at times questioned the field's normative commitments, arguing that the explicit aim of promoting peace can bias analysis; practitioners respond that value-aware research can still be rigorous.
