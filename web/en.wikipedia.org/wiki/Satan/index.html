Satan, also known as the Devil, is an entity in the Abrahamic religions who seduces humans into sin or falsehood. In Judaism, Satan is an agent subservient to God — typically a metaphorical figure or an accusing angel (ha-satan, "the adversary") in the heavenly court. In Christianity and Islam, he is usually understood as a fallen angel or jinn who rebelled against God, and who tempts human beings to sin; in Christianity he is identified with the serpent in the Garden of Eden, the dragon of the Book of Revelation, and the ruler of Hell.

Etymology

The Hebrew word śāṭān (שָּׂטָן) means "adversary" or "accuser" and is applied in the Hebrew Bible both to human opponents and to a celestial figure. The Greek Septuagint rendered it diabolos ("slanderer"), the source of the English "devil". In Arabic, Shayṭān (شيطان) serves similarly, while the Quran's proper name for the Devil is Iblis.

Judaism

In the Hebrew Bible, Satan appears most prominently in the Book of Job and in 1 Chronicles, as a member of the divine council who tests human righteousness with God's permission — in Job, famously wagering with God over the piety of Job. Rabbinic Judaism generally interprets Satan not as an independent power of evil but as an angel carrying out God's will or as a personification of the evil inclination (yetzer hara). During the Second Temple period, Jewish apocalyptic literature expanded the figure, describing fallen angels and a leader of evil spirits, influenced in part by contact with Zoroastrian dualism.

Christianity

In the New Testament, Satan is the tempter of Jesus in the desert, the "ruler of this world", an accuser, and the ancient serpent identified in Revelation as "the dragon, that ancient serpent, who is the Devil and Satan". Christian tradition identified him with Lucifer, the "morning star" of Isaiah 14, understood as an angel cast down for pride. Medieval theology elaborated a hierarchy of demons and an infernal kingdom; the Reformation and Counter-Reformation intensified concern with witchcraft and demonic pact. In many modern Christian denominations Satan is still affirmed as a real spiritual being, while liberal theology often treats him as symbolic of evil.

Islam

In Islam, Iblis (also called al-Shaytan) was a jinn raised among the angels who refused God's command to bow before Adam, saying he was made of fire while Adam was of clay. Cast out, he was granted respite until the Day of Judgement to lead humans astray; he whispers temptations but has no power over God's sincere servants. Lesser devils are also called shayāṭīn.

In culture

Satan is among the most depicted figures in Western art and literature: the tragic, defiant protagonist-antagonist of John Milton's Paradise Lost (1667); Mephistopheles in the Faust legend and Goethe's drama; and the tempter in countless works of painting, music, film and folklore. The Romantic movement recast him as a heroic rebel, and in the twentieth century movements such as theistic Satanism and the atheistic Church of Satan adopted him — very differently — as a symbol.
