Osteogenesis imperfecta

Osteogenesis imperfecta
Other names: Brittle bone disease; Lobstein syndrome
Specialty: Medical genetics, orthopaedics
Symptoms: Bones that fracture easily, blue sclerae, short stature, hearing loss, dentinogenesis imperfecta
Causes: Usually mutations in COL1A1 or COL1A2 (type I collagen)
Frequency: About 1 in 15,000–20,000 births

Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI), colloquially known as brittle bone disease, is a group of genetic disorders that all result in bones that break easily. The range of symptoms — on the milder end the symptoms may be few, on the moderate and severe ends the body can be very badly affected. Typically, they include blue sclerae (whites of the eyes), short stature, loose joints, hearing loss, breathing problems and problems with the teeth (dentinogenesis imperfecta).

Genetics

About 85–90% of cases result from dominant mutations in the genes COL1A1 and COL1A2, which encode the chains of type I collagen, the main structural protein of bone. Quantitative defects (less normal collagen) produce the mild type I form; qualitative defects (structurally abnormal collagen, often from glycine substitutions in the triple helix) produce the severe types II, III and IV. A growing number of rarer recessive and X-linked forms involve genes for collagen processing and osteoblast function, such as CRTAP, LEPRE1/P3H1, SERPINF1 and WNT1.

Classification

The Sillence classification, originally proposed in 1979, divides OI into types: type I (mild, with blue sclerae), type II (perinatally lethal), type III (progressively deforming), and type IV (moderately severe, with normal sclerae). Expanded genetic classifications now recognise more than 20 types.

Signs and symptoms

Features vary widely. Recurrent fractures from minimal trauma, bone deformity, and short stature are typical in moderate and severe forms. Blue or grey-blue sclerae result from the thin collagen allowing the underlying choroidal veins to show through. Conductive and sensorineural hearing loss often develops by adulthood. In severe cases, basilar invagination of the skull and restrictive lung disease from rib deformity can be life-threatening.

Treatment

There is no cure; treatment focuses on minimising fractures and maximising mobility. Bisphosphonates such as pamidronate are widely used in children with moderate to severe OI to increase bone density, although their effect on long-term fracture risk is debated. Surgery includes intramedullary rodding of long bones (for example with telescoping Fassier-Duval rods) to correct and prevent deformity. Physiotherapy, occupational therapy and mobility aids are central to management.

Epidemiology and history

OI affects roughly 1 in 15,000 to 20,000 births worldwide. Cases have been identified in an Egyptian mummy of about 1000 BC. The condition was described by the Swedish physician Olof Jakob Ekman in 1788 and given the name osteogenesis imperfecta by Willem Vrolik in 1849. The condition has occasionally been proposed — controversially — for historical figures such as the French entertainer and wartime resister, and in popular culture it is associated with characters such as Mr. Glass in the film Unbreakable.
