Kin selection

Kin selection
FieldEvolutionary biology
Key figuresW. D. Hamilton, J. B. S. Haldane, John Maynard Smith
Core resultHamilton's rule: rB > C
ExplainsAltruism toward relatives; eusociality

Kin selection is the evolutionary strategy that favours the reproductive success of an organism's relatives, even at a cost to the organism's own survival and reproduction. It is a key mechanism of inclusive fitness theory, explaining how altruistic behaviour—such as sterile worker castes in social insects—can evolve by natural selection.

Hamilton's rule

W. D. Hamilton formalised the theory in 1964: a gene for altruism will spread when rB > C, where C is the fitness cost to the actor, B is the fitness benefit to the recipient, and r is the coefficient of relatedness (the probability that actor and recipient share the gene by common descent—0.5 for full siblings and offspring, 0.25 for half-siblings, 0.125 for cousins). J. B. S. Haldane had earlier quipped that he would lay down his life for two brothers or eight cousins.

Evidence and examples

Classic evidence includes alarm calling in Belding's ground squirrels (females with kin nearby call more), cooperative breeding in birds such as Florida scrub-jays, and the extreme altruism of eusocial insects—ants, bees, wasps and termites—where workers forgo reproduction to raise the queen's offspring. Hamilton proposed that haplodiploidy, which makes hymenopteran sisters related by 0.75, predisposed ants and bees to eusociality, though it is neither necessary nor sufficient, and termites are fully diploid.

Debate

Kin selection and inclusive fitness remain central to sociobiology, though their scope has been debated—notably in a 2010 paper by Nowak, Tarnita and E. O. Wilson arguing standard natural selection suffices, which drew strong rebuttals from many evolutionary biologists. Mechanisms of kin recognition include phenotype matching, spatial association and "green-beard" genes.
