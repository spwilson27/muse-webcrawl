Anaphylatoxin

Anaphylatoxins are small peptide fragments — principally C3a, C4a, and C5a — released during activation of the complement system of innate immunity. They are potent inflammatory mediators that trigger mast cell degranulation, histamine release, smooth muscle contraction, and increased vascular permeability. Their name reflects their ability, in excess, to provoke an anaphylaxis-like reaction.

Cleavage of C3 and C5 releases the anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a

Generation

When complement is activated by the classical, lectin, or alternative pathway, the convertase enzymes cleave C3 into C3a and C3b, and later C5 into C5a and C5b. The small "a" fragments diffuse away as anaphylatoxins, while the larger "b" fragments remain bound to surfaces and drive opsonization and the membrane attack complex. C4a, released by C1s cleavage of C4, is the weakest of the three.

Effects

C5a is the most potent anaphylatoxin and a powerful chemoattractant for neutrophils and macrophages, acting through the C5a receptor (CD88). C3a acts on mast cells, basophils, and eosinophils via the C3a receptor. Both stimulate the release of histamine and other mediators, contract bronchial and intestinal smooth muscle, and dilate small blood vessels. Plasma carboxypeptidases rapidly remove their C-terminal arginine, producing des-Arg forms with much reduced anaphylactic activity, which limits the response.

Clinical significance

Uncontrolled anaphylatoxin generation contributes to sepsis, adult respiratory distress syndrome, ischemia–reperfusion injury, and severe allergic reactions. Therapeutic blockade of C5a or its receptor is an active area of drug development; the C5a receptor antagonist avacopan is approved for ANCA-associated vasculitis.
