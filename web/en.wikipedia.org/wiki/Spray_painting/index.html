Spray painting

Spray painting is a painting technique in which a device sprays coating material (paint, ink, varnish, etc.) through the air onto a surface. The most common types employ compressed gas — usually air — to atomize and direct the paint particles. Spray guns evolved from the airbrush; aerosol spray cans, patented in the 1940s and commercialized by Edward Seymour in 1949, made the technique available for small, quick jobs and consumer use.

Methods

Air spray uses a gun mixing paint with compressed air for a fine finish, at the cost of overspray. Airless spray forces paint through a tiny tip at pressures up to 3,000 psi, applying thick coats quickly for houses and ships. HVLP (high volume, low pressure) guns improve transfer efficiency and reduce bounce-back. Electrostatic painting charges paint droplets so they are attracted to a grounded workpiece, wrapping around edges. Spray booths with filtered exhaust manage overspray and fumes in professional settings.

Applications and safety

Spray painting dominates automobile manufacturing and refinishing, appliance and furniture coating, and large construction work because of its speed and smooth finish. Hazards include inhalation of solvents and isocyanates, fire risk from atomized flammables, and environmental emission of volatile organic compounds — prompting waterborne paints, powder coating (itself applied by electrostatic spray) and stricter regulation. As an art medium, aerosol paint underpins graffiti and spray paint art.
