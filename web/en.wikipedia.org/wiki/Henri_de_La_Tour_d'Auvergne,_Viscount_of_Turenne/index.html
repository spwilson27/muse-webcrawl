Henri de La Tour d'Auvergne, Viscount of Turenne

Born11 September 1611, Sedan, France
Died27 July 1675 (aged 63), Sasbach, Baden
RankMarshal General of France
BattlesThirty Years' War; Fronde; Franco-Dutch War

Henri de La Tour d'Auvergne, vicomte de Turenne (11 September 1611 – 27 July 1675), commonly known simply as Turenne, was a French military commander and Marshal of France. One of the most successful generals of the 17th century, he served Louis XIII and Louis XIV and is counted by Napoleon among the greatest captains of history.

Early career

Turenne was born at Sedan, the second son of Henri de La Tour d'Auvergne, Duke of Bouillon, and Elizabeth of Nassau, daughter of William the Silent. Raised a Calvinist, he learned soldiering in Dutch service under his uncles Maurice and Frederick Henry of Orange during the Eighty Years' War, then transferred to French service in 1630, rising quickly during the Thirty Years' War.

Thirty Years' War and the Fronde

His victories at Freiburg (1644, with Condé), Zusmarshausen and the capture of Munich (1648) helped bring the war to an end. During the civil wars of the Fronde he briefly joined the rebels — partly through his attachment to the Duchess of Longueville — but returned to royal service in 1651 and defeated his former ally Condé, securing the young Louis XIV's throne. His Flanders campaign culminating in the Battle of the Dunes (1658) forced Spain to the Treaty of the Pyrenees.

Later campaigns and death

Created Marshal General of France in 1660, Turenne led the lightning conquest of the Spanish Netherlands in the War of Devolution (1667) and commanded in the Franco-Dutch War. After Dutch flooding forced a retreat, he campaigned brilliantly on the Rhine in 1674–75, winning at Sinzheim, Entzheim and Turckheim and driving Imperial forces from Alsace in a celebrated winter campaign. On 27 July 1675, while reconnoitring before the Battle of Salzbach, he was killed by a cannonball at Sasbach.

Legacy

Turenne was buried among kings at Saint-Denis and later reinterred by Napoleon at Les Invalides. Renowned for his humanity toward civilians, his care for logistics, and his maxim of manoeuvre over battle, he was remembered by Napoleon as one of the seven great commanders whose campaigns should be studied again and again.
