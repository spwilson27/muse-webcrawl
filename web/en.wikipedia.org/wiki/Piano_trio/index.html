Piano trio

A piano trio is a group of piano and two other instruments, usually a violin and a cello, or a piece of music written for such a group. It is one of the most common types of chamber music ensemble and a standard genre of classical chamber music, dating from the late 18th century. The term may also refer to a jazz trio of piano, double bass, and drums, but in classical usage it denotes the piano–violin–cello combination.

History

The piano trio grew out of the Baroque trio sonata and the accompanied keyboard sonata. In Haydn's many piano trios, the piano dominates while the violin doubles or comments and the cello largely reinforces the bass line. Mozart raised the strings to greater independence, and Beethoven's trios—from Op. 1 to the "Archduke" Trio, Op. 97 (1811)—made the ensemble a vehicle for his largest chamber-music ideas.

Repertoire

The 19th century made the piano trio a central medium of domestic and concert music: Schubert's two great trios (D. 898 and D. 929); Mendelssohn's two trios; Schumann's three; Brahms's three; Dvořák's four, including the "Dumky" Trio; Tchaikovsky's A-minor Trio "in memory of a great artist"; and trios by Saint-Saëns, Franck, Fauré, and Rachmaninoff (the Trio élégiaque). Twentieth-century landmarks include Ravel's Piano Trio (1914), Shostakovich's Trio No. 2, Op. 67 (1944), and trios by Ives, Copland, and many later composers.

Other combinations

Less common variants substitute other instruments: the clarinet trio (clarinet, cello, piano—Beethoven, Brahms), the horn trio (Brahms, Op. 40), and the flute trio. Ensembles such as the Beaux Arts Trio and the Kalichstein–Laredo–Robinson Trio defined modern standards of performance, while many string quartets' members also form trios.

In jazz

In jazz, "piano trio" usually means piano with double bass and drums, a format central to artists from Art Tatum and Oscar Peterson to Bill Evans, whose trio with Scott LaFaro and Paul Motian redefined group interplay, and the Keith Jarrett "Standards" trio.
