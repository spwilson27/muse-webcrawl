Famadihana

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Famadihana is a funerary tradition of the Malagasy people in Madagascar. Known as the "turning of the bones", people bring forth the bodies of their ancestors from the family crypts, rewrap them in fresh cloth, and dance with the corpses around the tomb to live music.

The custom, celebrated mainly among the Merina and Betsileo peoples of the central highlands, is held during the dry season, typically between June and September, and at intervals of five to seven years for a given family tomb.

Beliefs

Famadihana reflects the Malagasy belief that the spirits of the dead do not join the world of the ancestors until the body has completely decomposed and appropriate ceremonies have been performed. Until then, the razana (ancestors) act as intermediaries between the living and God (Andriamanitra), and honouring them maintains harmony and invites blessing. Family members also use the occasion to pass family news to the ancestors and ask for their blessing.

The ceremony

Families gather at the tomb, which is opened with prayers and speeches. Bodies are carefully removed, sprayed with wine or perfume, and rewrapped in new silk shrouds (lamba). The names of the dead are rewritten on the cloth so they will not be forgotten. Guests feast on rice and zebu meat, musicians play, and relatives dance while holding the shrouded bodies aloft before they are returned to the tomb, often placed upside down at the threshold to "close the circle of life".

Modern practice

The ceremony is costly — new shrouds, food, and tomb repairs can consume a family's savings — and its frequency has declined somewhat due to economic pressure, Christian influence, and public-health concerns. In 2017, Malagasy health officials advised against the practice during a pneumonic plague outbreak, since handling remains could spread infection. Nevertheless, famadihana remains a powerful affirmation of fihavanana (kinship and solidarity) and a central expression of Malagasy identity.
