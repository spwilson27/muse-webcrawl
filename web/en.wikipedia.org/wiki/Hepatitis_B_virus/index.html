Hepatitis B virus

FamilyHepadnaviridae
GenusOrthohepadnavirus
GenomePartially double-stranded circular DNA, ~3.2 kb
HostHumans (and other primates)
DiseaseHepatitis B; chronic infection can lead to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma
PreventionHepatitis B vaccine (available since 1981–82)

The hepatitis B virus (HBV) is a partially double-stranded DNA virus, the type species of the genus Orthohepadnavirus in the family Hepadnaviridae. It infects the liver, causing the disease hepatitis B, and is one of the smallest enveloped animal viruses, with a diameter of about 42 nanometres in its infectious form (the Dane particle).

Structure and genome

The virion consists of an outer lipid envelope bearing the hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) surrounding a nucleocapsid of core antigen (HBcAg) that packages the genome and a viral polymerase. At about 3.2 kilobases, the genome is among the smallest of any animal virus; it has four overlapping reading frames encoding the surface proteins, core and e antigens, the polymerase, and the regulatory X protein. Infected cells also release vast numbers of empty subviral particles of HBsAg — the "Australia antigen" whose discovery by Baruch Blumberg in the 1960s opened the modern study of the virus and won him a Nobel Prize.

Replication

Uniquely among DNA viruses of its kind, HBV replicates through an RNA intermediate: the relaxed circular DNA is repaired in the nucleus into covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA), which serves as a persistent template for pregenomic RNA; that RNA is then reverse-transcribed back into DNA within new capsids. The persistence of cccDNA explains why chronic infection is so difficult to cure completely.

Disease and epidemiology

HBV is transmitted through blood, sexual contact and from mother to child at birth. Acute infection is often asymptomatic but can cause jaundice and, rarely, fulminant liver failure. Chronic infection — far more likely when infection occurs in infancy — affects roughly 250–300 million people worldwide and can progress to cirrhosis and liver cancer; HBV is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen.

Prevention and treatment

A highly effective recombinant vaccine has existed since the 1980s and is recommended at birth in most countries. Chronic hepatitis B is managed, though rarely cured, with antivirals such as tenofovir and entecavir, and with interferon in selected patients.
