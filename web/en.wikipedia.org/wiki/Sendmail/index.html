Sendmail

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sendmail — a general-purpose mail transfer agent, originally written by Eric Allman at UC Berkeley; first released 1983.

Sendmail is a general purpose internetwork mail transfer agent (MTA) for email routing and delivery. Originally developed by Eric Allman at the University of California, Berkeley, as a successor to the delivermail program, Sendmail shipped with BSD Unix and became the dominant MTA of the early Internet, at its peak handling a large majority of the world's email traffic.

Design

Sendmail's great strength was its configurability: it could route mail between networks using dissimilar protocols and address formats — ARPANET, UUCP, BITNET, and later the SMTP Internet — by rewriting addresses according to an elaborate rule system. Its configuration file, sendmail.cf, became notorious for its near-illegible syntax, which administrators usually generated from m4 macros rather than writing directly; Craig Hunt's and Bryan Costales's reference books on Sendmail were correspondingly famous. The program introduced or popularized facilities such as mail aliases, the .forward file, and, later, the milter API for plugging in external mail filters, and SMTP AUTH and STARTTLS support.

History and legacy

The first version was released in 1983. Sendmail's ubiquity made it a high-profile security target — the 1988 Morris worm famously exploited a debug mode in the program — and its monolithic, root-privileged architecture drew criticism as simpler, security-focused rivals such as qmail, Postfix, and Exim appeared in the 1990s. A commercial arm, Sendmail, Inc., was founded in 1998 and later passed through corporate acquisitions, while the open-source MTA continued under community maintenance. Although its market share has declined, Sendmail remains bundled with or available for most Unix-like systems and is a landmark in Internet infrastructure history.
