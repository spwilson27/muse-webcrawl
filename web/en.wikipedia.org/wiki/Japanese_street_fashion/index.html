Japanese street fashion

Harajuku, the symbolic home of Japanese street fashion
CentreHarajuku, Tokyo
Key stylesLolita, gyaru, decora, visual kei, fairy kei, mori kei
Key mediaFRUiTS magazine (1997–2017)

Japanese street fashion (Japanese: ストリートファッション) refers to the distinctive youth fashion subcultures that developed in Japan, centred on the Harajuku district of Tokyo, from the 1980s onward. Unlike fashion dictated by major labels, Japanese street styles are typically mixed and personalized by the wearer, combining vintage clothing, handmade items, brand pieces, and accessories in highly codified subcultural styles.

Major styles

Notable styles include Lolita fashion, inspired by Victorian and Rococo dress and subdivided into Gothic, Sweet, and Classic Lolita; gyaru, with tanned skin, dramatic makeup, and bleached hair; decora, which layers large numbers of colourful accessories; visual kei, drawn from glam- and metal-influenced bands; fairy kei, using pastel 1980s motifs; and mori kei ("forest style"), favouring loose, earthy layers. Men's counterparts include gyaru-o and the host-influenced styles of Shinjuku.

History

The pedestrianization of Harajuku's Omotesandō and the gathering of fashion tribes on Sundays created a spectacle documented from 1997 by Shoichi Aoki's magazine FRUiTS, which photographed individual wearers and recorded exactly what they wore. The magazine's books carried the styles worldwide, influencing Western designers and musicians — Gwen Stefani's Harajuku Lovers line is a prominent example. Street fashion declined as an organized public spectacle after the mid-2010s, when Harajuku's gathering culture faded and styles migrated online; FRUiTS ceased publication in 2017, Aoki citing a lack of stylish kids to photograph.

Influence

Japanese street fashion contributed to global interest in subculture-driven style, layering, and kawaii aesthetics, and remains documented through fashion walks, social media, and museum exhibitions of Lolita and Harajuku dress.
