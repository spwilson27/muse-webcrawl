The Jola (also spelled Diola, Joola, Djola or Ajamat) are an ethnic group found in Senegal (especially the Casamance region), The Gambia and Guinea-Bissau. Predominantly wet-rice farmers of the mangrove lowlands south of the Gambia River, the Jola are notable for an egalitarian, largely stateless social organisation and for having long resisted both Islamisation and centralised political authority.

History

Archaeological and oral evidence suggests the Jola have inhabited the Casamance for many centuries, developing sophisticated techniques of diked, desalinated paddy cultivation in the tidal mangroves. Unlike their neighbours, the Jola built no kingdoms or caste systems and famously rejected the slave trade, resisting raiding and later French colonial rule. Colonial boundaries split the Jola among Senegal, British Gambia and Portuguese Guinea. Since 1982 the Casamance conflict between the Senegalese state and the MFDC separatist movement has been centred in Jola areas, driven by grievances over land, marginalisation and cultural identity.

Society and economy

Jola society is organised in villages of extended-family compounds without hereditary rulers; authority traditionally rested with councils of elders, ritual priests and — in some areas — sacred kings (oeyi) whose role was religious rather than political. The centrepiece of the economy is wet-rice cultivation, traditionally divided by gender (men clearing and diking, women transplanting and harvesting), supplemented by fishing, cattle, palm products and, more recently, cashew and mango growing and migrant labour.

Religion and culture

The traditional religion centres on a supreme creator, Emitai, approached through shrines (ukine) and spirits, with elaborate initiation rites — notably the bukut male initiation held at intervals of a generation — and the women's kabekel and other ritual associations. Many Jola have adopted Roman Catholicism (especially in the Oussouye area) or Islam, often blended with traditional practice. Jola music features the akonting (ekonting), a three-stringed lute considered an ancestor of the American banjo, and the bougarabou drums. In 2002 the Senegalese ferry Le Joola, named for the people, sank with some 1,800 deaths — one of history's worst maritime disasters.

Languages

The Jola languages form the Bak cluster of the Atlantic branch of the Niger–Congo family and include many mutually unintelligible varieties — Fogny, Kasa, Karon, Kwatay, Mlomp, Bayot and others — often treated as separate languages. Most Jola are multilingual in regional lingua francas.
