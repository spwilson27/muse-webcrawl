Maus, often published as Maus: A Survivor's Tale, is a graphic novel by American cartoonist Art Spiegelman, serialised from 1980 to 1991 in the avant-garde comics magazine Raw. It depicts Spiegelman interviewing his father, Vladek, about his experiences as a Polish Jew and Holocaust survivor.

The animal metaphor

The work depicts different groups as anthropomorphic animals: Jews as mice, Germans as cats, ethnic Poles as pigs, Americans as dogs, the French as frogs and the British as fish — a visual metaphor drawn from Nazi propaganda that described Jews as vermin, which Spiegelman both uses and subverts. The device has been widely discussed and sometimes criticised, and Spiegelman explored its implications in his later book MetaMaus (2011).

Plot

The narrative alternates between Vladek's wartime story — his early life in Sosnowiec, marriage to Anja, the German invasion, ghettoes, hiding, and imprisonment in Auschwitz and Dachau — and the fraught present-day relationship between father and son in Rego Park, New York. Anja survived the camps but died by suicide in 1968; her diaries, which Vladek later burned, are a recurring absence in the book.

Publication and reception

Pantheon Books published the first collected volume, Maus I: My Father Bleeds History, in 1986, and the second, Maus II: And Here My Troubles Began, in 1991. In 1992 Maus became the first graphic novel to win a Pulitzer Prize (a Special Award in Letters). It is regularly ranked among the greatest comics ever made and is widely taught in schools and universities; in 2022 a Tennessee school board's removal of the book from an eighth-grade curriculum drew international attention and sent sales sharply upward.

Influence

Along with works by Will Eisner and Harvey Pekar, Maus is credited with establishing the graphic novel as a serious literary form and with pioneering comics memoir and history. Its blend of testimony, family drama and self-reflexive commentary on representation has shaped generations of cartoonists.
