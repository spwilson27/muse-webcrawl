Kabbalah (Hebrew: קַבָּלָה, "tradition" or "received [teaching]") is an esoteric method, discipline and school of thought in Jewish mysticism. It seeks to explain the relationship between the unchanging, infinite God — the Ein Sof ("without end") — and the mortal, finite universe, teaching that God manifests through ten emanations, the sefirot, and that human action can repair a cosmos broken at its creation.

Origins and texts

Jewish esotericism has roots in the merkabah (divine chariot) mysticism of late antiquity and early medieval works such as Sefer Yetzirah (the Book of Formation). Kabbalah proper emerged in twelfth- and thirteenth-century Provence and Spain: Isaac the Blind is regarded as an early systematiser, and around 1280 Moses de León circulated the Zohar ("Splendour"), the tradition's foundational text, which he attributed to the second-century sage Shimon bar Yochai. Written mostly in an artificial Aramaic as a commentary on the Torah, the Zohar describes the inner life of God through the sefirot and the mystical meaning of the commandments.

Core doctrines

The sefirot — Keter (Crown), Chokmah (Wisdom), Binah (Understanding), Chesed (Kindness), Gevurah (Judgement), Tiferet (Beauty), Netzach (Eternity), Hod (Splendour), Yesod (Foundation) and Malkuth (Kingdom) — map the stages by which the infinite becomes manifest, conventionally drawn as the "Tree of Life". In the sixteenth century, Isaac Luria of Safed taught a dramatic cosmology of creation: tzimtzum, God's self-contraction to make space for the world; shevirat ha-kelim, the shattering of the vessels meant to hold divine light, scattering sparks into the realm of evil; and tikkun, the repair of the world through human observance, prayer and ethical action. Lurianic Kabbalah transformed Judaism, influencing prayer, custom and the messianic fervour around Sabbatai Zevi in the seventeenth century.

Hasidism and modern Kabbalah

In eighteenth-century Eastern Europe, Hasidism democratised Kabbalistic ideas, emphasising joyful devotion and the sanctification of everyday life. In the twentieth century, scholarly study was revolutionised by Gershom Scholem of the Hebrew University, while popular interest widened through Yehuda Ashlag's commentaries and, controversially, the celebrity-driven Kabbalah Centre. Christian Cabala and Hermetic Qabalah, by contrast, are parallel Western esoteric traditions that adapted Jewish Kabbalah from the Renaissance onward.
