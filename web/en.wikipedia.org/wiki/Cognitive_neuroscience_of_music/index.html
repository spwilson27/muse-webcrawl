Cognitive neuroscience of music

Neuroimaging shows music engages widely distributed brain networks

Field: Cognitive neuroscience; music psychology; musicology

Methods: fMRI, EEG/MEG, PET, lesion studies

The cognitive neuroscience of music is the scientific study of the brain mechanisms involved in the cognitive processes underlying music — including perception of pitch, rhythm, timbre and harmony, musical memory and emotion, performance, composition and improvisation. Because music engages auditory, motor, emotional and social systems simultaneously, it serves neuroscientists as a model domain for studying brain plasticity, prediction and reward, and as a tool in rehabilitation.

Perception and brain organisation

Sound enters the auditory cortex in the temporal lobes, where different features are processed by partly separable pathways: pitch and melody engage regions of the superior temporal gyrus (with a right-hemisphere bias for spectral detail), while rhythm and meter recruit motor structures including the basal ganglia, cerebellum and supplementary motor area even when the listener is still. Musical syntax — the build-up and violation of harmonic expectations — produces characteristic brain responses (such as the early right anterior negativity, ERAN) in inferior frontal areas overlapping with language regions, demonstrating shared resources for structural processing in music and language.

Emotion and reward

Music reliably evokes strong emotions through mechanisms including brainstem responses to acoustic features, expectancy and its fulfilment or delay, episodic memory, and social contagion. Neuroimaging studies, notably by Robert Zatorre, Valorie Salimpoor and colleagues, show that intensely pleasurable music ("chills") engages the mesolimbic dopamine system, including the nucleus accumbens and caudate — the same reward circuitry involved in food and other primary rewards.

Training, plasticity and disorders

Musicians show structural and functional brain differences — enlarged auditory and motor representations and, in those who began young, a larger anterior corpus callosum — making musical training a leading model of experience-dependent plasticity. Deficits are equally informative: congenital amusia ("tone deafness") affects roughly 1.5–4% of the population and involves impaired pitch networks, while some patients with aphasia or dementia retain striking musical abilities, a dissociation exploited by therapies such as melodic intonation therapy and music-based interventions for Parkinson's gait and stroke rehabilitation.
