Spanish Adoptionism
Periodc. 780s–800s
ProponentsElipandus of Toledo; Felix of Urgell
OpponentsBeatus of Liébana, Alcuin of York; Pope Hadrian I; Charlemagne's councils
Condemned atRegensburg (792), Frankfurt (794), Friuli (796/797), Aachen (799)
ClaimChrist, in his humanity, is the Son of God by adoption

Spanish Adoptionism was a Christological doctrine taught in late 8th-century Spain, principally by Archbishop Elipandus of Toledo and Bishop Felix of Urgell, which held that Jesus Christ, in his human nature, was the Son of God not by nature but by adoption — God "adopting" the man Jesus at his baptism (or conception), while Christ as divine Word was Son by nature. The teaching drew on language in the old Hispanic (Mozarabic) liturgy and on the need, its defenders argued in a land under Muslim rule, to stress Christ's true humanity against docetic tendencies.

Controversy and condemnation

The Asturian monk Beatus of Liébana and Etherius of Osma attacked the doctrine in the Adversus Elipandum (785), and Pope Hadrian I condemned it. Because Felix's diocese of Urgell lay within Charlemagne's Spanish March, the affair became a Carolingian concern: Felix was condemned at Regensburg (792), the great Council of Frankfurt (794) repudiated Adoptionism alongside the image controversy, and Alcuin of York debated Felix in writing and in person, securing his recantation at Aachen (799). Felix later relapsed and died in exile at Lyon in 818.

Significance

Historians note that the Spanish Adoptionists' formulas differed from the ancient Adoptionism of Theodotus and Paul of Samosata — Elipandus affirmed Christ's divinity — and some argue the controversy was as much political as theological, marking the Carolingian church's assertion of authority over the old Visigothic church of Toledo, then under Islamic rule. The episode sharpened Latin Christological vocabulary about the two natures and the hypostatic union.
