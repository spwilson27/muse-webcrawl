Eyring equation

The Eyring equation (occasionally also known as the Eyring–Polanyi equation) is an equation used in chemical kinetics to describe changes in the rate of a chemical reaction against temperature. It was developed almost simultaneously in 1935 by Henry Eyring, Meredith Gwynne Evans, and Michael Polanyi. The equation follows from the transition state theory (also called activated-complex theory) and differs from the empirical Arrhenius equation by deriving the pre-exponential factor from statistical mechanics.

The equation

The rate constant is given by k = κ (kBT / h) (c⊖)1−n exp(ΔS‡/R) exp(−ΔH‡/RT), where ΔH‡ and ΔS‡ are the enthalpy and entropy of activation, κ is a transmission coefficient, kB is the Boltzmann constant, and h is the Planck constant. A plot of ln(k/T) versus 1/T — an Eyring plot — yields a straight line whose slope gives ΔH‡ and whose intercept gives ΔS‡.

Applications and limits

The Eyring equation is widely used to analyse temperature-dependent kinetics in chemistry and biochemistry, and analogous forms appear in theories of viscosity, diffusion, and dielectric relaxation. Its absolute rate predictions depend on the quality of the transition-state partition functions and on assumptions such as the absence of tunnelling and recrossing of the barrier.
