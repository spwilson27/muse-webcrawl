The Copper Age, also called the Chalcolithic (from Greek khalkós, copper, and líthos, stone) or Eneolithic, is an archaeological period characterised by the first widespread use of copper metallurgy alongside stone tools. It forms a transitional phase between the Neolithic and the Bronze Age, when copper began to be alloyed with tin to make bronze.

Regional development

Copper working began in the Fertile Crescent, where native copper had been hammered into beads as early as the 9th millennium BC, and smelting developed by around 5000 BC. In the Balkans, sites such as Vinča and the Varna Necropolis in Bulgaria demonstrate sophisticated copper and gold metallurgy by the 5th millennium BC; the Varna graves contain some of the world's oldest worked gold. In Western Europe, Copper Age cultures include the makers of Beaker pottery, and Ötzi the Iceman, who died around 3300 BC, carried a nearly pure copper axe.

In the Americas, the Old Copper Complex of the Great Lakes region worked native copper for thousands of years without smelting, while in the Andes copper metallurgy developed independently much later.

Society and technology

The period saw increasing social stratification, long-distance trade in metals and prestige goods, the secondary products revolution (use of animal traction, wool and dairy), wheeled vehicles, and the growth of fortified settlements in some regions. Copper tools were softer than later bronze, so stone remained dominant for many tasks; copper's importance was as much social and symbolic as practical.
