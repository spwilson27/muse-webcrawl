Overview

The Montreal Protocol is an international treaty designed to protect the ozone layer by phasing out the production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances (ODS), principally chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and halons. Signed in 1987 under the framework of the 1985 Vienna Convention, it is widely regarded as the most successful environmental treaty ever concluded: it has eliminated about 99% of ODS, and the ozone layer is projected to recover to 1980 levels around the middle of the 21st century.

Mechanism and legacy

The Protocol set binding phase-out schedules (faster for developed countries), created a Multilateral Fund to help developing states comply, and — remarkably — allowed its control schedules to be tightened as science firmed, without renegotiating the treaty. Its success rested on industry alternatives, scientific consensus (the Rowland–Molina hypothesis and the Antarctic ozone hole) and universal participation; Kofi Annan called it "perhaps the single most successful international agreement to date". The 2016 Kigali Amendment added HFCs (potent greenhouse gases, though not ozone-depleting), avoiding up to about 0.5 °C of warming by 2100 by common estimates.
