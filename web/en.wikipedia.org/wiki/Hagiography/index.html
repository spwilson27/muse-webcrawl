Hagiography

Hagiography
DefinitionBiography of a saint or religious leader; by extension, an excessively flattering biography
EtymologyGreek hagios ("holy") + graphia ("writing")
Landmark worksAthanasius, Life of Antony; Sulpicius Severus, Life of Martin; Jacobus de Voragine, Golden Legend

A hagiography (/ˌhæɡiˈɒɡrəfi/; from Greek ἅγιος, hagios, "holy", and γράφειν, graphein, "to write") is a biography of a saint or an ecclesiastical leader, as well as, by extension, an adulatory and idealised biography of a founder, saint, monk, nun or icon in any of the world's religions. Early Christian hagiographies focused on the lives, and particularly the miracles and martyrdoms, of saints, and were intended more for edification than for exact historical record.

Christian hagiography

Origins

The genre grew out of the Acts of the Martyrs, records — part authentic, part legendary — of the trials and deaths of early Christian martyrs read aloud on their feast days. Athanasius of Alexandria's Life of Antony (c. 360) established the model of the saint's life as an account of spiritual combat and monastic virtue, and was enormously influential in both East and West. Sulpicius Severus's Life of St Martin of Tours and the Dialogues of Gregory the Great spread the genre across Latin Europe.

Medieval flowering

In the Middle Ages, saints' lives (vitae) were written for monastic communities, cathedrals and pilgrimage churches, often to promote a shrine or support a claim in a dispute. Collections such as the Golden Legend (Legenda Aurea) of Jacobus de Voragine (c. 1260) gathered lives of some 180 saints and became one of the most copied and printed books of the age. Byzantine hagiography flourished in parallel, with Symeon Metaphrastes' menologion standardising the lives for liturgical reading. In the Celtic and Anglo-Saxon worlds, saints' lives provide much of the surviving narrative record of the early medieval period.

Critical study

The scholarly study of hagiography as a historical source began with the Bollandists, a Jesuit society founded in the 17th century whose Acta Sanctorum subjected the lives to philological criticism; Hippolyte Delehaye's work later codified the distinction between genuine documents and legend. Humanist and Reformation critics had already mocked credulous saint-worship — Erasmus satirised pilgrimage, and Protestant reformers largely rejected the cult of saints — while Catholic reformers at Trent sought to purge the least credible legends.

In other traditions

Analogous genres exist across religions: Islamic sira literature and Sufi tadhkira memorials of saints; Hindu carita poems and the Bhaktamal of Nabha Dass; Buddhist jataka tales and Tibetan namtar spiritual biographies; Jain lives of the Tirthankaras; and Sikh janamsakhi accounts of Guru Nanak's life.

Figurative usage

In modern usage "hagiography" often carries a pejorative sense: a biography so reverential and uncritical that it resembles a saint's life, whether of a politician, celebrity, business leader or artist. The adjective is "hagiographic".
