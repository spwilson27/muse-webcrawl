United States occupation of Veracruz

The United States occupation of Veracruz lasted from 21 April to 23 November 1914, when U.S. naval and Marine forces seized Mexico's principal Gulf port during the Mexican Revolution. President Woodrow Wilson ordered the landing after the Tampico Affair and to prevent a German ship from delivering arms to the government of Victoriano Huerta.

Background

In February 1913 Huerta had overthrown and murdered Francisco Madero, plunging Mexico into civil war. Wilson refused to recognize Huerta's government, which he considered illegitimate, and lifted an arms embargo to aid the Constitutionalists under Venustiano Carranza. On 9 April 1914, Mexican soldiers in Tampico briefly arrested American sailors from USS Dolphin who had landed for supplies; the local commander apologized and released them, but the U.S. admiral Henry Mayo demanded a formal 21-gun salute to the American flag, which Mexico refused in the form demanded.

The landing

Learning that the German steamer Ypiranga was about to land a cargo of machine guns and ammunition at Veracruz for Huerta, Wilson ordered the port and its custom house seized on 21 April 1914. Sailors and Marines occupied the waterfront against unexpectedly stiff resistance from Mexican naval cadets and armed civilians; fighting on 21–22 April left 19 Americans and an estimated 126–300 Mexicans dead. The Ypiranga was turned away but later unloaded its cargo elsewhere.

The United States administered Veracruz for seven months, cleaning streets, improving sanitation, and running the port. Paradoxically, the occupation united Mexicans of all factions against the intervention: even Carranza, its supposed beneficiary, condemned it and demanded withdrawal. Mediation by Argentina, Brazil, and Chile (the "ABC powers") at Niagara Falls helped resolve the crisis, and with Huerta already driven from power in July, U.S. forces withdrew on 23 November 1914.

Legacy

Nineteen Medals of Honor were awarded for the action, a number criticized even at the time as excessive for so limited an operation. The occupation deepened Mexican distrust of the United States and anticipated further intervention, culminating in the Punitive Expedition of 1916–17 against Pancho Villa.
