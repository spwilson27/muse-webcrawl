Autoimmune disease

An autoimmune disease is a condition that results from an anomalous response of the adaptive immune system, in which it mistakenly targets and attacks healthy, functioning parts of the body as if they were foreign organisms. It is estimated that there are more than 80 recognized autoimmune diseases, and research suggests nearly any body part can be involved. Well-known examples include type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, coeliac disease, psoriasis, and Hashimoto's thyroiditis.

Causes and risk factors

The exact causes are incompletely understood. Genetics play a role: certain HLA alleles are strongly associated with particular diseases, and autoimmune conditions cluster in families. Environmental triggers, including infections, may precipitate disease in susceptible individuals, possibly through molecular mimicry, in which pathogen antigens resemble self-antigens. Autoimmune diseases are substantially more common in women than in men, for reasons that may involve sex hormones and X-chromosome biology.

Mechanisms

Autoimmunity arises when self-tolerance mechanisms — central tolerance in the thymus and bone marrow, and peripheral tolerance in the tissues — fail. Autoreactive T cells and B cells produce autoantibodies and inflammatory responses that damage tissues. Diseases may be organ-specific, such as Graves' disease of the thyroid, or systemic, such as lupus.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis typically combines clinical features with laboratory tests for autoantibodies, such as the antinuclear antibody (ANA) test. There is no cure for most autoimmune diseases; treatment aims to suppress the abnormal immune response and control inflammation, using corticosteroids, conventional immunosuppressants such as methotrexate, and biologic agents such as TNF inhibitors and monoclonal antibodies that target specific immune pathways.
