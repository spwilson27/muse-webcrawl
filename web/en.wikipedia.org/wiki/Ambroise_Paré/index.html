Ambroise Paré (c. 1510 – 20 December 1590) was a French barber surgeon who served in that role for kings Henry II, Francis II, Charles IX and Henry III. He is considered one of the fathers of surgery and modern forensic pathology, and a pioneer in surgical techniques and battlefield medicine, especially in the treatment of wounds. He was also an anatomist, invented several surgical instruments, and designed artificial limbs and eyes.

Early life and career

Paré was born around 1510 in Bourg-Hersent, near Laval. The son of a cabinetmaker, he was apprenticed to a barber surgeon and later gained experience at the Hôtel-Dieu hospital in Paris. In 1536 he entered military service as a surgeon, accompanying French armies in Italy. Lacking a university education and knowing little Latin, he wrote his works in French, which helped spread his influence among practising surgeons.

Contributions to surgery

At the siege of Turin in 1537, having run out of the boiling elder oil then standard for cauterising gunshot wounds, Paré dressed the remaining wounds with a mild mixture of egg yolk, oil of roses, and turpentine — and found the next morning that those soldiers were in far less pain, with less inflammation, than those treated with boiling oil. He concluded that gunshot wounds should not be cauterised, a landmark observation in evidence-based surgery.

Paré revived the use of ligatures to stop bleeding after amputation in place of cautery, designed artery forceps, and described procedures for trepanning, hernia repair, and podalic version in obstetrics. He designed prosthetic hands and limbs, including a mechanical hand operated by catches and springs. His famous motto was "Je le pansai, Dieu le guérit" — "I dressed him, and God cured him."

Works and legacy

His major works include La méthode de traicter les playes (1545), Dix livres de la chirurgie (1564), and the collected Œuvres (1575). His writings on monsters, mummies, and poisons also contributed to early forensic medicine. Paré's emphasis on gentle treatment, observation, and experience over dogma made him the most influential surgeon of the Renaissance.
