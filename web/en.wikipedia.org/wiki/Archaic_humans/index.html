Archaic humans

Skulls representing archaic human diversity in the Middle Pleistocene.

Archaic humans comprise a loosely defined group of extinct members of the genus Homo that lived during the Middle and Late Pleistocene and differ in morphology from anatomically modern humans (Homo sapiens). The grouping is informal rather than taxonomic, and the populations it covers — including Homo heidelbergensis, Neanderthals, Denisovans, and various African and Asian fossils — are increasingly understood as regional descendants of a widespread ancestral population.

Evolution and distribution

By around 700,000 to 500,000 years ago, populations of Homo in Africa and Eurasia had evolved larger brains and more robust cranial architecture than Homo erectus. In Europe these populations gave rise to the Neanderthals; in Asia, to the Denisovans and possibly other lineages; and in Africa, archaic populations such as the Kabwe skull (Homo rhodesiensis) are close in time and morphology to the earliest fossils of Homo sapiens at Jebel Irhoud, dated to about 300,000 years ago.

Behaviour

Archaic humans manufactured prepared-core stone tools, hafted spear points, hunted large mammals cooperatively, used fire habitually, and in the case of Neanderthals produced pigments, ornaments, and cave markings. Evidence of care for injured individuals and deliberate burial suggests complex social behaviour.

Legacy

Ancient DNA has demonstrated gene flow from archaic humans into modern populations: Neanderthal ancestry in all non-African populations, Denisovan ancestry concentrated in Oceania and East Asia, and evidence of additional "ghost" archaic lineages within Africa. Archaic introgression has been linked to modern variation in immunity, skin and hair biology, and altitude adaptation.
