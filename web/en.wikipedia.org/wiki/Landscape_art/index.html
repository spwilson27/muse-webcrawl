Landscape art, or landscape painting, is the depiction in art of landscapes — natural scenery such as mountains, valleys, trees, rivers, and forests, especially where the main subject is a wide view. Landscape was long treated as a lowly genre in the academic hierarchy of Western art, below history painting, but it became a major and eventually dominant subject in many traditions.

History

In China, landscape developed into the supreme form of painting: shan shui ("mountain-water") ink painting flourished from the Song dynasty, exemplified by Fan Kuan and later literati painters, expressing Daoist and Confucian ideals rather than topographical accuracy. In the West, landscape backgrounds appeared in medieval and Renaissance art (Joachim Patinir became an early specialist), but the genre came into its own in the 17th-century Dutch Republic, with painters such as Jacob van Ruisdael and Aelbert Cuyp, and in the idealized classical landscapes of Claude Lorrain and Nicolas Poussin.

The 18th and 19th centuries elevated landscape further: the English picturesque and the watercolours of J.M.W. Turner, the naturalism of John Constable, the French Barbizon school, and the plein-air practice that culminated in Impressionism. In North America the Hudson River School celebrated wilderness scenery, while in the 20th century landscape continued through modernism, photography (Ansel Adams), and land art (Robert Smithson).

Types

Related forms include the cityscape, seascape (marine art), and the "veduta" (detailed view, often of a city). Aesthetic categories used in criticism include the beautiful, the picturesque, and the sublime.
