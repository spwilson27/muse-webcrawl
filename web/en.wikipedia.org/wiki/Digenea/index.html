Digenea is a subclass of parasitic flatworms (flukes) in the class Trematoda. Digeneans are endoparasites of vertebrates with complex life cycles typically involving two or more hosts, the first of which is almost always a mollusc, usually a snail. With more than 12,000 described species, they are among the most successful groups of parasitic organisms, infecting fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals, including humans.

Morphology

Adult digeneans are typically flattened and leaf-like, ranging from under a millimetre to several centimetres (the liver fluke Fasciola gigantica can reach 7.5 cm). They characteristically possess two suckers: an oral sucker surrounding the mouth and a ventral sucker (acetabulum) used for attachment. The body is covered by a syncytial tegument that protects against host digestion and immune attack. Most species are hermaphroditic.

Life cycle

The typical life cycle involves eggs shed from the vertebrate definitive host, which hatch into free-swimming miracidia that infect a snail. Within the snail, asexual reproduction through sporocyst and redia stages produces large numbers of cercariae, which leave the snail and either encyst as metacercariae on vegetation or in a second intermediate host (such as a fish or crustacean), or penetrate the definitive host directly, as in the blood flukes (schistosomes). The cycle completes when the definitive host ingests the metacercariae (or is penetrated by cercariae).

Medical and veterinary importance

Digeneans cause major human diseases including schistosomiasis (Schistosoma species, infecting over 200 million people), fascioliasis (Fasciola hepatica), clonorchiasis and opisthorchiasis (liver flukes associated with bile-duct cancer), and paragonimiasis (lung flukes). Livestock fluke infections cause substantial economic losses worldwide.
