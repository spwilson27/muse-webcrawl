Compact car

Compact car is a vehicle size class—predominantly used in North America—that sits between subcompact cars and mid-size cars. "Small family car" is a British term and a part of the C-segment in the European car classification. A compact car is a popular category of vehicle because it offers a good balance between affordability, fuel efficiency, and interior space.

Dimensions and definitions

The United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) defines a compact car as having a combined interior and cargo volume of 100–109.9 cubic feet (2.8–3.1 m³). Typical compact cars have a wheelbase of about 104–110 inches (2.64–2.79 m). In Europe, the equivalent C-segment is defined less by volume than by market position, with hatchbacks such as the Volkswagen Golf setting the template.

History

The term arose in the United States in the 1950s for smaller domestic models such as the Nash Rambler, positioned below the standard full-size cars. Compact cars grew in importance after the 1970s oil crises, as buyers sought fuel economy; Japanese models such as the Toyota Corolla and Honda Civic became best-sellers. The Corolla, introduced in 1966, became the best-selling nameplate in history.

Examples

Well-known compact cars include the Toyota Corolla, Honda Civic, Volkswagen Golf and Jetta, Ford Focus, Hyundai Elantra, Mazda3, and Nissan Sentra. Many are offered as sedans and hatchbacks, and increasingly as compact crossover SUVs, which are classified separately but share platforms with compact cars.
