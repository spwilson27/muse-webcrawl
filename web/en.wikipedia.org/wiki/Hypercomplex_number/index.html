Hypercomplex number

In mathematics, a hypercomplex number is a number belonging to a finite-dimensional algebra over the real numbers with a unit element. The term was coined in the nineteenth century as mathematicians generalised the complex numbers; classic examples include the quaternions, octonions, split-complex numbers, dual numbers and bicomplex numbers.

History

The search for a three-dimensional analogue of the complex numbers led William Rowan Hamilton, in 1843, to the quaternions — famously carved into Brougham Bridge in Dublin — which sacrifice commutativity of multiplication. John T. Graves and Arthur Cayley soon extended the construction to the octonions, which also lose associativity. The Cayley–Dickson construction systematised this doubling process, producing the 16-dimensional sedenions and beyond.

Classification results

Several theorems bound what hypercomplex systems can achieve. The Frobenius theorem (1877) states that the only finite-dimensional associative division algebras over the reals are the real numbers, the complex numbers and the quaternions. Hurwitz's theorem adds the octonions as the only normed division algebras beyond these. Weaker structures, such as Clifford algebras, remain central to geometry and physics, while dual numbers — with a nilpotent unit ε satisfying ε² = 0 — underpin automatic differentiation.
