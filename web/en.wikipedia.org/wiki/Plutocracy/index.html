Plutocracy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A plutocracy (from Greek ploutos, wealth, and kratos, power) is a society ruled or controlled by the small minority of its wealthiest citizens. The term is usually analytical or polemical rather than constitutional: few states call themselves plutocracies, but critics apply it where wealth translates systematically into political power — Aristotle's taxonomy already treated oligarchy as rule by the rich (as distinct from aristocracy, rule by the best), and modern usage follows him.

Mechanisms and measures

Claimed mechanisms include campaign finance and lobbying asymmetries, media ownership, revolving doors between regulators and industry, privileged access to policymakers, and policy-responsiveness skewed toward affluent preferences (the Gilens and Page (2014) finding for the United States — that average citizens' views have near-zero independent effect on policy once economic elites' views are accounted for — is the citation standard, and contested). Historical property qualifications for the franchise made plutocracy literal in early democracies; its modern form is influence without formal bar. Defenders of market systems respond that wealth is contestable and plural, that business interests conflict among themselves, and that democratic counterweights (public opinion, parties needing votes, courts) check conversion of money into rule — the empirical contest continues on data both sides claim.
