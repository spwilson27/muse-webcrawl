The Fortingall Yew is an ancient European yew (Taxus baccata) in the churchyard of the village of Fortingall in Perthshire, Scotland. Widely regarded as one of the oldest trees in Britain — and possibly in Europe — it has been estimated at between 2,000 and 5,000 years old, which would make it a contemporary of the Bronze Age.

History

When the naturalist Daines Barrington measured the tree in 1769, its trunk had a circumference of about 52 feet (15.8 m). Since then the once-massive trunk has hollowed and split into several separate stems, so that it no longer looks like a single tree; souvenir hunters in the 19th century removed pieces of wood, and a wall was built to protect what remains. In 2015 scientists reported that part of the tree, long treated as male, had produced berries and appeared to have changed sex, a phenomenon known in yews under stress.

Significance

The yew stands in a Christian churchyard that may itself have been an early religious site, and local folklore — including an unlikely tale that Pontius Pilate was born in the area — attaches to it. The tree is a protected landmark and a symbol of the remarkable longevity of yews, which regenerate by layering and hollow growth, making precise ageing impossible.
