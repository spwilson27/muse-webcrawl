A spark plug is a device for delivering electric current from an ignition system to the combustion chamber of a spark-ignition engine, where the spark ignites the compressed air–fuel mixture. A spark plug has a metal threaded shell, electrically isolated from a central electrode by a ceramic insulator, and a gap between the central electrode and a ground electrode across which the spark jumps.

Operation

High voltage from the ignition coil (or magneto) is applied to the terminal, ionising the gas in the gap and producing a spark kernel that initiates combustion. Because it sits in the combustion chamber, the plug must withstand pressures of up to about 50 bar, temperatures near 3,000 °C, and chemical attack, while its tip must stay hot enough to burn off deposits but not so hot as to cause pre-ignition — its "heat range".

History

Early ignition systems used flame and hot tubes; practical spark plugs followed the invention of the high-voltage magneto. Étienne Lenoir used an electric spark plug in his 1860 gas engine, and Robert Bosch's company produced the commercially successful plug of 1902 used with Gottlieb Daimler-style engines. Modern plugs use copper, platinum, or iridium electrodes for long service life, sometimes exceeding 160,000 km.

Reading plugs

The colour and condition of a removed plug's firing end is a classic diagnostic: tan or grey deposits suggest normal operation, while oil fouling, carbon fouling, overheating, and worn electrodes indicate specific engine problems.
