Wilhelm Wundt

Born: August 16, 1832, Neckarau

Died: August 31, 1920, Leipzig

Known for: Founding experimental psychology

Wilhelm Maximilian Wundt (August 16, 1832 – August 31, 1920) was a German physiologist, philosopher, and psychologist. He is widely regarded as one of the founding figures of modern psychology, having established psychology as a discipline distinct from philosophy and biology.

Trained in medicine at Heidelberg, Wundt worked as an assistant to Hermann von Helmholtz before becoming professor at Leipzig in 1875. There, in 1879, he founded the first formal laboratory for psychological research — a date often taken as the birth of experimental psychology.

Work

Wundt's Principles of Physiological Psychology (1874) outlined psychology as the experimental study of consciousness, using introspection under controlled conditions to analyse sensations, feelings, and reaction times. His school, sometimes called structuralism through his student Edward Titchener, trained a generation of psychologists from many countries, who carried the laboratory model worldwide.

In his later career Wundt also wrote the ten-volume Völkerpsychologie (1900–1920), arguing that higher mental processes — language, myth, and custom — must be studied through cultural and historical methods rather than experiment alone. This work influenced sociology and anthropology as well as psychology.
