Chantilly lace

Chantilly lace is a handmade bobbin lace named after the city of Chantilly, France, where it was produced from the 17th century onward. It is known for its fine ground, floral patterns outlined in a heavier silk thread (cordonnet), and its delicate, detailed design, traditionally worked in black silk as well as white and cream. Chantilly became one of the most fashionable laces of the 18th and 19th centuries and remains a staple of bridal and haute-couture dressmaking.

Characteristics

True Chantilly is a bobbin lace worked in one piece with a fine hexagonal mesh ground called point chant. The pattern — typically sprays of flowers, ribbons, and scrollwork — is outlined with a flat, untwisted silk thread that gives the design its characteristic slightly raised definition. The edges are usually finished with a delicate picot border. Because the lace is made in narrow strips that are later joined invisibly, large pieces such as shawls and flounces could be assembled from many widths.

History

Lacemaking at Chantilly flourished under the patronage of the Duchess of Longueville in the 17th century and of Madame du Barry in the 18th. Production was disrupted by the French Revolution, when lacemakers were associated with the aristocracy, but revived strongly in the 19th century under Napoleon III's court; black Chantilly shawls, mantillas, and veils were exported throughout Europe and the Americas. From the 19th century, production shifted largely to Bayeux and to machine-made imitations produced in Calais and Caudry on Leavers looms, which are still marketed as Chantilly lace today.

Uses

Chantilly lace is used for wedding dresses and veils, evening gowns, lingerie, and mantillas. Its soft drape and scalloped edges make it a common overlay fabric in couture, frequently layered over silk satin.
