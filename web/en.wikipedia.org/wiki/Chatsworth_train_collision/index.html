The Chatsworth train collision occurred on September 12, 2008, when a Metrolink commuter train collided head-on with a Union Pacific freight train in the Chatsworth neighbourhood of Los Angeles, California. Twenty-five people were killed and 135 injured, making it the deadliest rail accident in the United States in fifteen years and the worst in Metrolink's history.

Collision

Metrolink train 111, bound for Moorpark on the Ventura County Line, departed Union Station in the afternoon carrying about 225 passengers. Near Chatsworth, on a stretch of single track, the passenger train passed a red signal and entered the path of an oncoming Union Pacific freight train. The two trains struck head-on at a combined speed of about 83 mph (134 km/h). The Metrolink locomotive telescoped into the first passenger car, and wreckage caught fire.

Investigation

The National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) determined that the Metrolink engineer, Robert Sanchez, who died in the crash, had been sending and receiving text messages while on duty — including a message sent 22 seconds before impact — and had run the red signal without braking. The NTSB's probable cause was the engineer's failure to observe the signal, attributed to his use of a mobile phone for texting. Sanchez had also allowed unauthorised teenagers interested in trains into the locomotive cab earlier that day and on previous occasions.

Aftermath

Within weeks, Congress passed the Rail Safety Improvement Act of 2008, which mandated positive train control (PTC) — technology that can automatically stop a train before certain accidents — on most US passenger and hazardous-freight lines. The Federal Railroad Administration also issued an emergency order banning the use of personal electronic devices by train crews on duty. Metrolink installed inward- and outward-facing cameras in its locomotives, and litigation arising from the crash was resolved under the federal $200 million liability cap for passenger rail accidents.
