Parabolic trajectory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In astrodynamics or celestial mechanics a parabolic trajectory is a Kepler orbit with the eccentricity equal to 1 and is an unbound orbit that is exactly on the border between elliptical and hyperbolic orbit. When moving away from the source it is called an escape orbit, otherwise a capture orbit. It is also sometimes referred to as a C3 = 0 orbit (see Characteristic energy).

Under standard assumptions a body traveling along an escape orbit will coast along a parabolic shaped trajectory to infinity, with velocity relative to the central body tending to zero, and therefore will never return. Parabolic trajectories are minimum-energy escape trajectories, separating positive-energy hyperbolic trajectories from negative-energy elliptic orbits.

Velocity

Under standard assumptions, for a body moving in this type of trajectory an orbital equation becomes:

r = h²/(μ(1 + cos ν))

where r is the radial distance from the central body, h is the specific angular momentum, ν is the true anomaly, and μ is the standard gravitational parameter. At any point along its orbit, the body has the local escape velocity for that position.

Barker's equation

Barker's equation relates the time of flight to the true anomaly of a parabolic trajectory:

t = ½ √(p³/μ) (D + ⅓D³), where D = tan(ν/2)

Unlike Kepler's equation for elliptical orbits, Barker's equation can be solved directly for the true anomaly as a function of time.

Projectile motion

In the more familiar context of ballistics near the surface of the Earth, where gravity may be treated as uniform and air resistance neglected, a projectile follows a parabolic trajectory. The horizontal and vertical motions are independent, giving a range, height, and time of flight determined by the initial speed and launch angle. Real projectiles deviate from a parabola because of air resistance and the variation of gravity with altitude.
