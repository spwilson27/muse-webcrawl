Opening (morphology)

In mathematical morphology and digital image processing, the opening of an image by a structuring element is the erosion of the image followed by a dilation with the same structuring element. Opening removes small objects and thin protrusions from an image while largely preserving the size and shape of larger objects.

Definition

For a binary image A and structuring element B, the opening A ∘ B is defined as the union of all translates of B that fit entirely inside A. Equivalently, it is computed as (A ⊖ B) ⊕ B, where ⊖ denotes erosion and ⊕ denotes dilation. A classical intuition is rolling the structuring element (for example, a disk) along the inside of the boundary of the shape: the opening is everything the rolling ball can reach.

Properties

Opening is anti-extensive (the result is contained in the original set), increasing (larger inputs give larger outputs), and idempotent (applying it twice has no further effect). Its dual operation, closing, is dilation followed by erosion, which fills small holes and gaps.

Applications

Opening is used to remove noise — small specks — from binary images, to separate objects joined by thin bridges, and to estimate background in grayscale images before subtraction. Successive openings with structuring elements of increasing size form the basis of granulometry, sometimes called "sieving," used to measure the size distribution of grains or particles in an image.
