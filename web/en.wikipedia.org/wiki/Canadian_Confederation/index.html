Canadian Confederation (French: Confédération canadienne) was the process by which three British North American provinces — the Province of Canada, Nova Scotia, and New Brunswick — were united into one federation, called the Dominion of Canada, on 1 July 1867. Upon Confederation, Canada consisted of four provinces: Ontario and Quebec, which had been created by dividing the former Province of Canada, and the former colonies of New Brunswick and Nova Scotia.

Background

Political deadlock in the Province of Canada, fears of American expansion during and after the American Civil War, the end of the Reciprocity Treaty, Fenian raids, and the desire for an intercolonial railway all drove British North American politicians toward union. Britain's Colonial Office favoured consolidation for reasons of defence and economy.

The conferences

Delegates met at the Charlottetown Conference in September 1864, originally called to discuss Maritime union, and reached agreement on a federal union. The Quebec Conference of October 1864 produced the Seventy-Two Resolutions, the constitutional framework, and the London Conference of 1866–67 finalised the text that became the British North America Act, 1867 (now the Constitution Act, 1867).

Fathers of Confederation

Thirty-six men are regarded as the Fathers of Confederation, including John A. Macdonald, George-Étienne Cartier, George Brown, Alexander Tilloch Galt, Charles Tupper, and Samuel Leonard Tilley. Macdonald became the Dominion's first prime minister.

Expansion

Manitoba and the Northwest Territories joined in 1870, British Columbia in 1871, Prince Edward Island in 1873, Alberta and Saskatchewan in 1905, and Newfoundland (now Newfoundland and Labrador) in 1949, completing the modern federation.
