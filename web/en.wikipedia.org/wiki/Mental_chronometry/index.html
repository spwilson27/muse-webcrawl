Mental chronometry

Mental chronometry is the scientific study of processing speed or reaction time on cognitive tasks to infer the content, duration, and temporal sequencing of mental operations. It is one of the foundational methodologies of experimental and cognitive psychology, resting on the assumption that differences in reaction time between experimental conditions reflect the time taken by underlying mental processes.

History

The field originates with the Dutch physiologist Franciscus Donders, who in 1868 proposed the "subtraction method": by comparing reaction times in tasks of increasing complexity (simple reactions, choice reactions, and go/no-go reactions), one could isolate the duration of stages such as stimulus discrimination and response selection. Wilhelm Wundt's Leipzig laboratory made reaction-time measurement a standard tool of the new experimental psychology. In the 20th century, Saul Sternberg's additive factors method (1966) allowed researchers to test whether experimental variables affected the same or different processing stages without relying on pure subtraction.

Key phenomena and laws

  • Hick's law – choice reaction time increases logarithmically with the number of stimulus–response alternatives.
  • Fitts's law – movement time depends on the distance to a target and the target's size.
  • Practice effects – reaction time decreases as a power function of practice.
  • Speed–accuracy trade-off – faster responses tend to be less accurate, a central consideration in interpreting reaction-time data.

Modern use

Mental chronometry remains widely used in cognitive neuroscience, often combined with EEG event-related potentials and neuroimaging to localise processing stages in time and space. Processing-speed measures also feature in clinical assessment, human-factors research, and studies of individual differences, including correlations between reaction time variability and general intelligence.
