An isolating language is a type of language with a very low morpheme-to-word ratio and no inflectional morphology whatsoever. In the extreme case, each word contains a single morpheme. Grammatical relations are expressed by word order and by separate function words (particles, prepositions, auxiliaries) rather than by affixes or changes to word forms.

Characteristics

Isolating languages lack case endings, verb conjugations and agreement markers. In Mandarin Chinese, for example, the sentence Wǒ mǎi shū ("I buy book[s]") uses invariant words; aspect is shown by separate particles such as le (completed action) and plurality or definiteness is inferred from context or expressed by separate words. Vietnamese and Thai show a similar profile, making much of mainland Southeast Asia and southern China a classic area of isolating languages.

Analytic versus isolating

The term analytic language is closely related but broader: analytic languages rely mainly on word order and helper words rather than inflection. A language can be analytic without being fully isolating — English, for example, is largely analytic but retains some inflection (plural -s, past tense -ed). "Isolating" describes the limiting end of the analytic scale, where the morpheme-per-word ratio approaches one.

Historical change

Languages can drift along the typological scale over time. Old Chinese had more derivational morphology than modern Mandarin, and English has become markedly more analytic — hence more isolating — since the Old English period, losing most of its case and agreement inflections.
