The Stanford prison experiment (SPE) was a psychological experiment conducted in August 1971 by a research group led by Philip Zimbardo at Stanford University, funded by the U.S. Office of Naval Research. Student volunteers were randomly assigned to play the roles of "prisoners" or "guards" in a mock prison built in the basement of Stanford's psychology building.

Course of the experiment

Participants adapted to their roles more strongly than expected: guards enforced authoritarian measures and subjected some prisoners to psychological abuse, while prisoners passively accepted the treatment and some suffered emotional breakdowns. Five prisoners were released early because of extreme emotional disturbance, and Zimbardo terminated the study after six days, following the objections of graduate student Christina Maslach. Zimbardo concluded that the participants had conformed to their social roles, supporting a situational rather than dispositional explanation of behavior — a thesis related to the Milgram experiment on obedience.

Criticism

The experiment has long been criticized for ethical problems, lack of informed consent, and demand characteristics. Later archival investigations, notably by Thibault Le Texier, revealed that guards were coached to be "tough" and that some participants' accounts conflicted with the canonical narrative, prompting debate about the study's scientific status. It remains a famous, if contested, fixture of psychology textbooks, and Zimbardo later testified as an expert witness on situational forces in the Abu Ghraib prison abuse scandal, writing The Lucifer Effect (2007).
