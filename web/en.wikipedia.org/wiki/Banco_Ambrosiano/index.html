Banco Ambrosiano was an Italian bank founded in Milan in 1896 by Giuseppe Tovini, a Catholic lawyer, and named after Saint Ambrose, the patron saint of Milan. Its collapse in 1982 was one of Italy's largest banking scandals, involving the Vatican Bank, the Mafia, and the illegal Propaganda Due (P2) Masonic lodge. It is sometimes retroactively nicknamed "the Vatican Bank scandal".

Collapse

Under chairman Roberto Calvi, a member of Licio Gelli's P2 lodge, the bank built a network of offshore shell companies that borrowed heavily using letters of patronage from the Institute for the Works of Religion (IOR, the Vatican Bank), its largest shareholder. In June 1982, after the Bank of Italy uncovered debts estimated at around US$1.2–1.4 billion, Banco Ambrosiano collapsed. On 18 June 1982 Calvi was found hanged beneath Blackfriars Bridge in London; his death was initially recorded as suicide but later treated as murder, though no one was convicted.

Aftermath

The IOR denied legal liability but in 1984 paid about US$224 million to creditors as a "voluntary contribution" recognising moral involvement. A consortium of banks formed the Nuovo Banco Ambrosiano to take over the sound parts of the business, which eventually became part of Intesa Sanpaolo's predecessors. Archbishop Paul Marcinkus, head of the IOR, avoided arrest by remaining within Vatican City. The scandal fed numerous books and conspiracy theories and has been linked in fiction to the plot of The Godfather Part III.
