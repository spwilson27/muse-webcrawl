Serum (blood)

Human blood serum, the clear fluid remaining after blood has clotted and been centrifuged.

Serum (/ˈsɪərəm/) is the fluid and solute component of blood which does not play a role in clotting. It may be defined as blood plasma without the clotting factors, or as blood with the cells and clotting factors removed. Serum includes all proteins not used in blood clotting; all electrolytes, antibodies, antigens, and hormones; and any exogenous substances such as drugs or microorganisms. Serum does not contain white blood cells (leukocytes), red blood cells (erythrocytes), platelets, or clotting factors.

Composition

Serum is about 90% water and contains dissolved proteins (6–8%), including albumin, globulins (among them circulating antibodies, or immunoglobulins), complement proteins, and transport proteins, plus electrolytes, nutrients, metabolites, and hormones. Its slight alkalinity and amber colour come from dissolved solutes and bilirubin. The study of serum is called serology.

Clinical and laboratory uses

Serum is the sample type most commonly used in clinical chemistry and serological testing: assays for glucose, lipids, liver and kidney function markers, infectious-disease antibodies, and drug levels are typically run on serum separated by centrifugation after clotting. In research, fetal bovine serum is a standard supplement for cell culture. Historically, immune serum from recovered patients or animals — containing ready-made antibodies — was used as antiserum to treat infections and envenomation, a practice that began with diphtheria antitoxin in the 1890s.

Serum versus plasma

Plasma is obtained from anticoagulated blood and retains fibrinogen and other clotting factors; serum is obtained after the blood has clotted, so fibrinogen has been consumed in forming the clot. This distinction matters for certain tests: coagulation studies require plasma, while most chemistry and antibody tests can use either.
