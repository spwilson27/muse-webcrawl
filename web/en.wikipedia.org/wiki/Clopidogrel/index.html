Clopidogrel, sold under the brand name Plavix among others, is an antiplatelet medication used to reduce the risk of heart disease and stroke in people at high risk. It is used together with aspirin after coronary stent placement (dual antiplatelet therapy), after myocardial infarction, and in acute coronary syndrome.

Mechanism of action

Clopidogrel is a prodrug. Its active metabolite, produced largely by the liver enzyme CYP2C19, irreversibly blocks the P2Y12 component of ADP receptors on platelet cell membranes, preventing ADP-mediated activation of the glycoprotein IIb/IIIa complex and thus platelet aggregation. Because the blockade is irreversible, platelets are affected for their entire lifespan of about 7–10 days.

Medical use and pharmacogenetics

The landmark CAPRIE trial (1996) showed clopidogrel to be slightly more effective than aspirin in reducing ischaemic events. People carrying loss-of-function variants of CYP2C19 convert clopidogrel poorly and may have reduced benefit; regulatory agencies have added warnings for such "poor metabolisers," and alternative agents such as prasugrel or ticagrelor may be preferred after stenting.

Adverse effects

The principal risk is bleeding, including gastrointestinal haemorrhage; risk increases when clopidogrel is combined with aspirin, anticoagulants, or NSAIDs. Rare but serious effects include thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura.
