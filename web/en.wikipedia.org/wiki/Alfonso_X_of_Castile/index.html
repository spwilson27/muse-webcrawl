Alfonso X (23 November 1221 – 4 April 1284), known as Alfonso the Wise (Spanish: Alfonso el Sabio), was King of Castile and León from 1252 until his death. He is remembered less for his politics than for the remarkable cultural and scientific output of his court, which sought to compile all knowledge of the age.

Cultural achievements

Alfonso sponsored scholars — Christians, Muslims and Jews — at his court in Toledo, promoting Castilian as a language of learning. Works produced under his patronage include the Alfonsine tables of astronomy, the Cantigas de Santa Maria (over 400 songs, many attributed to the king himself), the legal code known as the Siete Partidas, and histories of Spain and the world. His court also translated Arabic scientific texts into Castilian and Latin.

Reign

Politically, Alfonso's ambitions — including his claim to the Holy Roman Empire, elected by some German princes in 1257 — drained his treasury, and heavy taxation provoked noble revolts. His final years were marred by a succession dispute with his son Sancho, who rebelled in 1282; Alfonso died in Seville in 1284 with the kingdom divided.
