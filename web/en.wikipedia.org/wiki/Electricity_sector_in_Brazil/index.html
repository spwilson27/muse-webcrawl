The electricity sector in Brazil is one of the largest in the world, and is distinguished by its heavy reliance on hydroelectricity, which historically provides around 60 percent or more of the country's electricity generation. Brazil's interconnected national grid (Sistema Interligado Nacional, SIN) moves power over continental distances, linking huge Amazonian and Paraná-basin hydro plants to the industrial southeast. The binational Itaipu Dam on the Paraná River, shared with Paraguay, is one of the largest power stations on Earth by generation.

Generation mix

Besides hydro, Brazil generates electricity from natural gas, biomass (notably sugarcane bagasse), wind, solar, coal, oil, and two nuclear reactors at Angra dos Reis. Wind generation, concentrated in the trade-wind-rich northeast, and utility-scale and distributed solar have grown rapidly since the 2010s, making Brazil's grid one of the cleanest among large economies, with renewables supplying roughly 80–90 percent of electricity in most years.

Institutions and market

The sector is regulated by ANEEL, the national electricity agency, and centrally operated by the ONS, which dispatches plants largely by optimizing the use of hydro reservoir water over time (the "water value"). Eletrobras, the former state holding company controlling major generation and transmission subsidiaries (Furnas, Chesf, Eletronorte, Eletrosul), was privatized in 2022. Distribution is handled by regional concessionaires, and large consumers may buy power on the free market (mercado livre).

Challenges

Drought is the sector's central risk: severe dry periods in 2001 forced nationwide rationing (the "apagão" crisis), and droughts in 2014–15 and 2021 pushed reservoirs to record lows and raised tariffs via thermal dispatch and tariff "flags". Long transmission distances from Amazonian projects such as Belo Monte, environmental and indigenous-rights controversies around new dams, and the integration of variable wind and solar are ongoing issues.
