Brisker method

The Brisker method (Hebrew: derekh Brisk) is an analytical approach to Talmud study developed by Rabbi Chaim Soloveitchik (1853–1918) of Brisk (Brest-Litovsk). It emphasizes precise conceptual definitions and sharp distinctions (chakirot) — often dichotomies between two understandings of a law — in place of the casuistic pilpul style that preceded it. It became the dominant method in Lithuanian-style yeshivas in Israel and the United States, notably through the Brisk yeshivas founded in Jerusalem by Chaim's descendants.

Method

Rather than resolving contradictions between texts by narrowing their scope, the Brisker method redefines the categories involved: a single rule may operate on two distinct planes, such as the object (cheftza) versus the person (gavra), or two different legal mechanisms producing superficially similar outcomes. Famous examples include the two kinds of sanctity of Jerusalem and the Land of Israel, and the dual aspects of the laws of testimony. Chaim's analyses were published posthumously-influential form in his commentary on Maimonides' Mishneh Torah.

Influence

The method was carried forward by Chaim's son Yitzchok Zev Soloveitchik ("the Brisker Rav"), by students such as Baruch Ber Leibowitz, and by the Ponevezh and Mir yeshivas; Joseph B. Soloveitchik brought a variant of it to American Modern Orthodoxy. Critics, including some Hasidic and Mussar-oriented scholars, have argued that its abstraction can detach Talmud study from practical halakha and aggadic concerns, but within the yeshiva world it remains the prevailing analytic standard.
