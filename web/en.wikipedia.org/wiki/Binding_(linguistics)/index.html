Binding (linguistics)

Binding relations between anaphors, pronouns and their antecedents

Binding is a subfield of syntax and semantics in linguistics concerned with the conditions that govern the distribution and interpretation of anaphors (such as reflexives and reciprocals), pronouns, and referring expressions. Binding theory was developed most prominently within the framework of generative grammar, in particular in Noam Chomsky's 1981 work Lectures on Government and Binding, where the notions of government and binding gave the framework its name.

Basic concepts

Two expressions are said to be coindexed when they are intended to refer to the same entity. An element binds another when it c-commands it and the two are coindexed. The binding domain of an element is the smallest clause (or similar configuration) within which its reference must, or must not, be determined.

The binding principles

Classical binding theory states three principles. Principle A requires an anaphor such as himself to be bound within its local domain, which is why "John saw himself" is grammatical but "John thinks that Mary saw himself" is not. Principle B requires a pronoun such as him to be free (not bound) within its local domain, ruling out "John saw him" when him refers to John. Principle C requires a full referring expression, such as a name, to be free everywhere, ruling out "He thinks that John is smart" when he and John refer to the same person.

Later developments

Subsequent research refined the classical principles to handle phenomena such as picture noun phrases ("John saw a picture of himself"), logophoricity, and long-distance reflexives in languages such as Chinese and Icelandic. Alternatives include Reinhart and Reuland's reflexivity theory and approaches within minimalism that derive binding effects from feature checking and movement.
