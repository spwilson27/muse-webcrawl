Life satisfaction

Life satisfaction is the way in which people show their emotions, feelings (moods) and how they feel about their directions and options for the future. It is a cognitive, judgement-based evaluation of one's life as a whole, and is a key component of subjective well-being, alongside positive and negative affect.

Measurement

The most widely used instrument is the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS), developed by Ed Diener and colleagues in 1985. It consists of five statements (such as "In most ways my life is close to my ideal") rated on a seven-point scale. Single-item global measures are also used in large surveys, such as the Cantril ladder in the Gallup World Poll, where respondents rate their life from 0 (worst possible) to 10 (best possible).

Determinants

Research finds life satisfaction associated with health, income (with diminishing returns — the Easterlin paradox debates how far income raises satisfaction across whole societies), employment, close relationships, personality traits such as extraversion and low neuroticism, and cultural factors. Life satisfaction tends to follow a U-shape over the lifespan in many countries, dipping in middle age.

Policy use

Governments and international bodies increasingly collect life-satisfaction statistics as complements to GDP, in line with recommendations of the Stiglitz–Sen–Fitoussi commission (2009). National well-being programmes in countries such as the United Kingdom and Bhutan's Gross National Happiness framework reflect this shift toward measuring quality of life directly.
