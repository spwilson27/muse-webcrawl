Nap-of-the-earth

Nap-of-the-earth (NOE) is a type of very low-altitude flight course used by military aircraft to avoid enemy detection and attack in a high-threat environment. During NOE flight, an aircraft follows the contours of the terrain — staying below the skyline of hills, valleys, and tree lines — so that ground radar and observers are shielded by the landscape for as much of the flight as possible.

Technique

NOE flight is typically conducted at heights of less than 60 metres (200 feet) above ground level, and helicopters practising NOE may fly only a few metres above the surface, using trees and buildings as cover. The pilot uses terrain masking: the aircraft remains hidden behind rising ground from the perspective of a threat radar, popping up only briefly to navigate or identify a target — a tactic called "quick pop-up". Because radar detection range is limited by line of sight and ground clutter, even simple terrain reduces warning time dramatically.

Use and risks

The technique is central to attack-helicopter tactics and to low-level penetration missions by fixed-wing strike aircraft; cruise missiles use automated terrain-following radar (TERCOM and its successors) to achieve a similar profile. NOE flight is demanding and dangerous: crews face high workload, bird strikes, wires, and controlled flight into terrain, so it is practised extensively in training and supported by terrain-following radar, radar altimeters, and digital terrain databases.
