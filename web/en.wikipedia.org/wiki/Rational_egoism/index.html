Rational egoism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rational egoism (also called rational selfishness) is the position that an action is rational if and only if it maximises one's self-interest. As a normative ethical theory, it holds that it is both rational and moral to act in one's own long-term interest, properly understood. The position is most closely associated with the philosopher and novelist Ayn Rand, whose ethical system, Objectivism, treats rational self-interest as a virtue and self-sacrifice as a vice.

Distinctions

Rational egoism differs from psychological egoism, the descriptive claim that people always act from self-interest, and from ethical egoism in its hedonistic forms, which Rand rejected as mere whim-worship. For Rand, the rational egoist identifies his interests by reason over the long term: honesty, integrity, productiveness and justice toward others are virtues precisely because they serve the agent's own flourishing. The doctrine therefore claims to forbid theft, fraud and coercion, since a rational person neither seeks unearned values nor initiates force against others.

History

Precursors of rational egoism include the egoistic hedonism of the Cyrenaics, Nietzsche's revaluation of selfishness, and Max Stirner's union of egoists. Rand presented the theory in her novels The Fountainhead (1943) and Atlas Shrugged (1957), and in essays collected in The Virtue of Selfishness (1964). Her collaborator Leonard Peikoff and later scholars such as Tara Smith systematised the view.

Criticism

Critics object that rational egoism offers no principled way to resolve genuine conflicts of interest, that it undervalues duties of aid and benevolence, and that expanding "self-interest" to include honesty, friendship and others' welfare risks making the theory unfalsifiable or indistinguishable from commonsense morality. Defenders reply that emergencies and conflicts are marginal cases and that benevolence toward others one values is fully consistent with egoism.
