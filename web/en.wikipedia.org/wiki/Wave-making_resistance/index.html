Wave-making resistance

FieldNaval architecture, hydrodynamics
CauseEnergy transferred by a moving vessel to surface gravity waves
Key parameterFroude number
RelatedHull speed; Kelvin wake; bulbous bow

Wave-making resistance is a form of drag that affects surface ships and other bodies moving at or near the interface between water and air. As a vessel moves, its bow and stern generate systems of surface gravity waves — the familiar V-shaped Kelvin wake — and the energy carried away by those waves must be supplied by the propulsion system. Wave-making resistance is one component of a ship's total hydrodynamic resistance, alongside frictional (viscous) resistance and form drag.

Physics

The bow produces a wave crest near the stem and the stern a second crest system. The two systems interfere: at certain speeds the stern sits in a trough of the bow wave, sharply increasing resistance, while at others the interference is favourable. The governing dimensionless quantity is the Froude number, the ratio of the ship's speed to the square root of the product of gravitational acceleration and waterline length. Wave resistance rises steeply as the Froude number approaches about 0.4, corresponding to the practical "hull speed" of displacement vessels, at which the wavelength of the transverse wave system equals the waterline length and the vessel effectively tries to climb its own bow wave.

Michell's integral and prediction

In 1898 J. H. Michell derived the first theoretical formula for the wave resistance of a thin ship, an integral over the hull geometry that remains a benchmark in hydrodynamics. Modern designers predict wave resistance with computational fluid dynamics and towing-tank model tests, typically testing at the same Froude number as the full-scale ship.

Reduction

Wave-making can be reduced by lengthening the hull relative to its beam, using fine (slender) hull forms, and fitting a bulbous bow, whose submerged bulb generates its own wave system tuned to cancel the bow wave at the design speed. Multihull vessels, small-waterplane-area twin hulls (SWATH), and fully submerged bodies such as submarines travelling deep largely avoid wave-making altogether. Wave drag of a related kind also affects aircraft near the speed of sound, by analogy, and planing and hydrofoil craft escape displacement wave-making by rising out of the water.
