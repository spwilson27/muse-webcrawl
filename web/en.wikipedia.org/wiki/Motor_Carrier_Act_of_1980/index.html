Motor Carrier Act of 1980

Enacted: July 1, 1980 (signed by President Jimmy Carter)
Public Law: 96-296
Purpose: Deregulation of the U.S. trucking industry

The Motor Carrier Act of 1980 (Public Law 96-296) was a federal law of the United States that significantly deregulated the trucking industry. Signed by President Jimmy Carter on July 1, 1980, the act reduced the authority of the Interstate Commerce Commission (ICC) over entry into the trucking market and over the rates carriers could charge, as part of the broader deregulation movement that also transformed airlines and railroads in the same era. Carter stated at the signing that the act would "bring the trucking industry into the free enterprise system".

Background

Since the Motor Carrier Act of 1935, interstate trucking had been tightly regulated: carriers needed certificates of public convenience and necessity from the ICC, were limited in what commodities they could haul and what routes they could serve, and filed rates collectively through rate bureaus with antitrust immunity. Critics argued this system inflated freight costs, wasted fuel through circuitous routing and empty backhauls, and protected incumbents. Studies in the 1970s, and pressure from economists, the Carter administration, and Senator Edward Kennedy's hearings, built the case for reform.

Provisions and effects

The act eased entry standards (shifting the burden to opponents of a new licence), liberalized route and commodity restrictions, expanded the zone of rate freedom, phased out collective ratemaking antitrust immunity, and deregulated much farm and exempt traffic. The number of licensed carriers roughly doubled within a few years, from about 18,000 in 1980 to well over 40,000 by 1990. Real freight rates fell substantially, logistics practices such as just-in-time delivery expanded, and non-union and owner-operator segments grew while unionized less-than-truckload carriers came under intense cost pressure; the Teamsters' freight membership declined sharply. The ICC itself was abolished in 1995 by the ICC Termination Act, with remaining functions passing to the Surface Transportation Board.
