The blastula (from Greek βλαστός, blastós, "sprout") is a hollow sphere of cells, called blastomeres, surrounding an inner fluid-filled cavity, the blastocoel, formed during an early stage of embryonic development in animals.

Formation

Following fertilisation, the zygote undergoes cleavage — rapid mitotic divisions without growth — producing first a solid cluster of cells, the morula. Continued division and fluid secretion rearrange the cells into the single-layered (in many species) hollow blastula. The form of the blastula varies with the amount of yolk in the egg: sea urchins form a simple hollow ball, frogs a ball with a small cavity displaced toward the animal pole, and birds and reptiles a flat disc (discoblastula) atop the yolk.

Fate: gastrulation

The blastula is the starting configuration for gastrulation, the dramatic reorganisation in which cells migrate and invaginate to lay down the germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm) and the basic body plan. In mammals the corresponding stage is called the blastocyst, which additionally differentiates an inner cell mass and an outer trophoblast specialised for implantation.
