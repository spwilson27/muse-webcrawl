Clinical endpoint

A clinical endpoint is an outcome that can be measured objectively in a clinical trial and that indicates the extent of benefit of an intervention. Regulators such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration define a clinical endpoint as a characteristic or variable that reflects how a patient feels, functions, or survives. Examples include overall survival, relief of symptoms, improved exercise tolerance, and prevention of stroke or hospitalisation.

Primary and secondary endpoints

Trials designate one or more primary endpoints, chosen in advance, on which the success of the trial is principally judged; secondary endpoints provide additional information. Because testing many endpoints increases the risk of false-positive findings, analyses of secondary endpoints are often regarded as exploratory unless multiplicity is controlled.

Surrogate endpoints

A surrogate endpoint is a laboratory value or physical sign — such as blood pressure, cholesterol level, or tumour shrinkage — used as a substitute for a clinical endpoint. Surrogates allow smaller, shorter trials, but a treatment's effect on a surrogate does not reliably predict its effect on how patients feel, function, or survive, so their use in drug approval remains controversial. Composite endpoints combine several outcomes into one measure to increase event rates and statistical power.
