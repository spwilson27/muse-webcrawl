Charleston (dance)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Charleston is a dance named after the harbour city of Charleston, South Carolina. The rhythm was popularised in mainstream dance music in the United States by a 1923 song called "The Charleston", written by James P. Johnson for the Broadway musical Runnin' Wild. The dance became an emblem of the Roaring Twenties and of the flapper era, and its fast, swinging steps with toe-in, heel-out pivots were widely considered scandalous by older generations.

Origins

The basic steps have roots in African-American dances of the South, including the Juba dance, and may have been performed in Charleston's Black community and by dockworkers there as early as 1903. The steps were seen in Harlem revues from 1913 onward; Runnin' Wild (1923) and Johnson's hit song carried the dance nationwide.

Characteristics

The Charleston is danced to ragtime-jazz music in 4/4 time with syncopated rhythms. The solo dance uses swivelling feet, kicked legs, swinging arms and a slight bounce; partner and group versions developed, and competitions and marathon contests were popular in the mid-1920s. In some cities, including Boston, the dance was briefly banned from public dance halls on moral grounds.

Legacy

The Charleston fed directly into the development of the Lindy Hop in Harlem's Savoy Ballroom in the late 1920s, and remains a staple of vintage swing-dance scenes worldwide. Its image — bobbed hair, fringed dresses, speakeasies — is inseparable from popular depictions of 1920s America.
