The Tai peoples are the populations who speak Tai languages, a branch of the Kra–Dai language family, and who historically share a wet-rice agricultural culture organized in small valley principalities called mueang. Tai-speaking peoples are spread across a broad arc from northeast India through southern China and mainland Southeast Asia. They include the Thai (Siamese) of Thailand, the Lao of Laos, the Shan of Myanmar, the Zhuang of Guangxi (the largest minority group in China), the Dai of Yunnan, the Tai groups of northwestern Vietnam, and the Ahom and other Tai groups of Assam, among many others.

Origins and migrations

The Tai homeland is generally placed in the region of present-day northern Vietnam, Guangxi, and Yunnan, where early Tai speakers cultivated rice in river valleys. Between roughly the 7th and 13th centuries CE, Tai groups migrated southwestward along river valleys into what is now Laos, Thailand, and Myanmar's Shan plateau, a movement driven by population growth and by pressure from expanding Chinese states and, later, the Mongol conquests. In the lowlands they encountered and gradually displaced or absorbed Mon, Khmer, and Burman polities. By the 13th and 14th centuries, Tai rulers had founded kingdoms including Sukhothai and Lan Na in modern Thailand, Lan Xang in Laos, and the Shan states, while the Ahom established a kingdom in Assam that endured for 600 years.

Culture

Traditional Tai society centers on irrigated rice cultivation, the village, and the mueang — a valley principality ruled by a lord (chao) and defined by a central settlement and its dependent villages. Kinship is broadly bilateral. Most southwestern Tai peoples adopted Theravada Buddhism, which shapes festivals, temple life, and literacy, while retaining an older stratum of spirit worship (satsana phi) involving guardian spirits of place and ancestors; northeastern Tai groups such as the Zhuang maintain their own traditional religions, and some Tai groups in Vietnam and China practice other faiths. Many Tai languages are written in Brahmic-derived scripts (Thai, Lao, Shan, and various Dai scripts), while Zhuang traditionally used Chinese-derived characters.

Terminology

"Tai" without the "h" denotes the wider ethnolinguistic family, while "Thai" refers specifically to the citizens and dominant ethnic group of Thailand. The peoples called Tai in different states include the Tai Lue, Tai Nuea, Tai Dam (Black Tai), Tai Daeng (Red Tai), Tai Khao (White Tai), Phuan, and many local groups, each with its own language variety, dress, and customs.
