Legionnaires' disease is a severe form of pneumonia — lung inflammation usually caused by infection — caused by any species of Legionella bacteria, most commonly Legionella pneumophila. Signs and symptoms include a cough, shortness of breath, high fever, muscle pains, and headaches; nausea, vomiting, and diarrhoea may also occur. Symptoms typically begin two to ten days after exposure. A milder, self-limiting form of legionellosis without pneumonia is known as Pontiac fever.

History

The disease acquired its name from the original outbreak at a convention of the American Legion held at the Bellevue-Stratford Hotel in Philadelphia in July 1976. Of the roughly 2,000 attendees, 182 developed a mysterious pneumonia and 29 died. The cause was identified in January 1977 by scientists at the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, who isolated a previously unknown bacterium from the hotel's air-conditioning cooling tower. Earlier unexplained outbreaks, including one in Pontiac, Michigan, in 1968, were retrospectively recognised as legionellosis.

Transmission

Legionella bacteria occur naturally in freshwater environments such as lakes and streams, but become a health concern when they grow and spread in human-made water systems: cooling towers, hot tubs, showerheads, decorative fountains, hot-water tanks, and large plumbing systems. People become infected by inhaling tiny droplets of contaminated water (aerosols) or, less commonly, by aspirating contaminated water into the lungs. The disease is not generally transmitted from person to person. Risk factors include older age, smoking, chronic lung disease, and a weakened immune system.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is most commonly made by a urinary antigen test or by culturing the organism from respiratory secretions; PCR testing is increasingly used. Because Legionella does not respond to beta-lactam antibiotics, treatment relies on macrolides such as azithromycin or respiratory fluoroquinolones such as levofloxacin. Even with treatment, mortality is around 10%, and higher in hospital-acquired cases and in immunocompromised patients.

Prevention

Prevention depends on proper maintenance of water systems: keeping hot water above 50 °C, cold water below 25 °C, disinfecting cooling towers, and regular flushing of outlets to prevent stagnation and biofilm formation. Many jurisdictions introduced regulations for cooling towers and building water systems after major outbreaks, such as the 2015 outbreak in the Bronx, New York, which sickened 138 people and killed 16.

Epidemiology

Legionnaires' disease occurs worldwide and is believed to be underdiagnosed. Reported cases in the United States rose sharply between 2000 and 2018, reaching roughly 10,000 cases per year, partly reflecting improved testing and reporting. Most cases are sporadic rather than associated with recognised outbreaks.
