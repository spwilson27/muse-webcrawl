John Ostrom

John Harold Ostrom (February 18, 1928 – July 16, 2005) was an American paleontologist who revolutionized the modern understanding of dinosaurs. His work beginning in the 1960s helped launch the "dinosaur renaissance" and established the now widely accepted theory that dinosaurs were active, warm-blooded animals and that birds are their living descendants.

Deinonychus and the dinosaur renaissance

In 1964, Ostrom's field party discovered the remains of a small, agile predatory dinosaur in Montana, which he named Deinonychus antirrhopus in 1969. Its anatomy suggested an active, fast-moving predator, contradicting the prevailing view of dinosaurs as sluggish, cold-blooded reptiles.

Origin of birds

Ostrom revived Thomas Henry Huxley's nineteenth-century hypothesis that birds evolved from theropod dinosaurs, providing detailed anatomical comparisons between Deinonychus, other small theropods and Archaeopteryx. His student Robert Bakker popularised these ideas, and Ostrom's interpretation is now the scientific consensus.

Career

Ostrom spent his career at Yale University and its Peabody Museum of Natural History, conducting extensive fieldwork in the Cloverly Formation of Montana and Wyoming.
