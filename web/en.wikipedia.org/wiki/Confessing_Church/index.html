The Confessing Church (German: Bekennende Kirche) was a movement within German Protestantism during Nazi Germany that arose in opposition to government-sponsored efforts to unify all Protestant churches into a single pro-Nazi Protestant Reich Church. Founded in 1934, it embodied the main Christian resistance within the Protestant churches to the Nazi regime's attempt to control the church through the Deutsche Christen ("German Christians") movement, though its resistance focused primarily on defending the church's theological independence rather than on opposing the regime politically in general.

Background

After Adolf Hitler came to power in 1933, the regime sought to coordinate (Gleichschaltung) the 28 regional Protestant churches into a unified Reich Church under Reich Bishop Ludwig Müller, a leader of the German Christians, who promoted a Nazified Christianity that accepted the state's "Aryan paragraph" barring Christians of Jewish descent from church office. In response, pastor Martin Niemöller founded the Pastors' Emergency League in September 1933, which grew rapidly and formed the nucleus of the Confessing Church.

Barmen Declaration

In May 1934, the first Confessing Synod met at Barmen and adopted the Barmen Declaration, drafted chiefly by the Reformed theologian Karl Barth. Its six theses rejected the idea that the church could acknowledge any source of revelation other than Jesus Christ as attested in Scripture, and denied that the state could become the "single and totalitarian order of human life" or take over the church's task. The Declaration remains a confessional document of several German regional churches and of the Presbyterian Church (USA) and other churches worldwide.

Persecution and resistance

The regime responded with arrests, dismissal of pastors, and closure of the Confessing Church's seminaries, including the one at Finkenwalde directed by Dietrich Bonhoeffer, who was later executed for his role in the conspiracy against Hitler. Niemöller was arrested in 1937 and spent the war in concentration camps. Many Confessing pastors nonetheless conformed or served in the armed forces, and historians debate the extent of the movement's opposition, noting its limited defence of Jews as such; its resistance was chiefly ecclesiastical.

Legacy

After 1945, members of the Confessing Church played leading roles in rebuilding German Protestantism, and the Stuttgart Declaration of Guilt (1945) acknowledged the church's failure to resist more courageously. The movement became a reference point for later church resistance movements, including Christians opposing apartheid in South Africa.
