James Knox Polk (November 2, 1795 – June 15, 1849) was the 11th president of the United States, serving from 1845 to 1849. A protégé of Andrew Jackson, he was a Democrat best known for overseeing the largest territorial expansion in American history, adding the Republic of Texas, Oregon Country, and the Mexican Cession to the United States during his single term.

Early life and political career

Polk was born in North Carolina and raised in Tennessee. After graduating from the University of North Carolina, he studied law and entered politics, serving fourteen years in the U.S. House of Representatives, including two terms as Speaker (1835–1839) — the only president to have held that office. He was governor of Tennessee from 1839 to 1841.

Election of 1844

Polk emerged as a "dark horse" compromise candidate at the 1844 Democratic convention and defeated Whig Henry Clay in the general election on a platform of expansion ("manifest destiny"), promising to annex Texas and claim the whole of Oregon Country with the slogan "Fifty-four Forty or Fight!" He pledged to serve only one term and to achieve four goals: lower tariffs, an independent treasury, settlement of the Oregon boundary, and the acquisition of California.

Presidency

Polk achieved all four goals. The Oregon Treaty of 1846 divided Oregon Country with Britain at the 49th parallel. After Texas annexation, disputes over the Texas–Mexico border led to the Mexican–American War (1846–1848); the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) added California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, and parts of other states — the Mexican Cession. Polk also signed the Walker tariff reducing rates and restored the Independent Treasury system. He worked punishing hours and micromanaged the war effort, quarrelling with his Whig generals Zachary Taylor and Winfield Scott.

Death and legacy

Exhausted, Polk declined renomination and retired to Nashville, where he died of cholera on 15 June 1849, only 103 days after leaving office — the shortest retirement of any president. Historians rank him highly for his expansionist achievements and executive effectiveness, while noting that the territorial gains intensified the conflict over the expansion of slavery that led to the Civil War.
