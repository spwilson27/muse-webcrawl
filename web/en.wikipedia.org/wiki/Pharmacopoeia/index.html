Pharmacopoeia

A pharmacopoeia, pharmacopeia, or pharmacopoea (from Greek φαρμακοποιία, pharmakopoiia, "drug-making") is a book containing directions for the identification of compound medicines, published by the authority of a government or a medical or pharmaceutical society. It sets legally enforceable standards of identity, strength, quality, and purity for medicines and their ingredients. Major modern pharmacopoeias include the United States Pharmacopeia (USP), the British Pharmacopoeia (BP), the European Pharmacopoeia (Ph. Eur.), the Japanese Pharmacopoeia, and the Pharmacopoeia of the People's Republic of China, alongside the WHO's International Pharmacopoeia.

History

Precursors include the ancient Egyptian Ebers Papyrus and Shennong's herbal in China. Municipal pharmacopoeias appeared in Renaissance Europe — Florence's Nuovo Receptario (1498) and Valerius Cordus's Nuremberg Dispensatorium (1546) — followed by the London Pharmacopoeia (1618), the first official English-language standard. The first United States Pharmacopeia was published in 1820; Britain unified its regional pharmacopoeias in 1864. The 20th century brought international harmonisation, culminating in the European Pharmacopoeia (first edition 1964–) under the Council of Europe.[1]

Contents and legal status

A pharmacopoeia consists of monographs for drug substances, excipients, and finished products specifying identification tests, assay methods, impurity limits, and storage, plus general chapters on methods such as dissolution, sterility, and chromatography. In many jurisdictions, compliance is a legal requirement: in the United States, a drug that fails USP standards may be deemed adulterated or misbranded under the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. Monographs for herbal drugs draw heavily on pharmacognosy.
