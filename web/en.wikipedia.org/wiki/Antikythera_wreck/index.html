The Antikythera wreck is a Roman-era shipwreck dating from the second quarter of the 1st century BC, discovered off the Greek island of Antikythera in 1900. It is famous for the wealth and sophistication of its cargo, which included bronze and marble statues, coins, jewellery, pottery, and the Antikythera mechanism — an intricate geared device often described as an ancient analogue computer, used to predict astronomical positions and eclipses.

Discovery and recovery

In the spring of 1900, sponge divers from the island of Syme, blown off course by a storm, sheltered off Antikythera and discovered the wreck at a depth of about 45 metres. Working with the Greek navy in 1900–1901, they recovered numerous artefacts in what is considered the first major underwater archaeological operation. One diver died and two were paralysed by decompression sickness during the work. Recovered objects are now housed chiefly in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens.

The cargo

Among the most celebrated finds are the Antikythera Ephebe, a bronze statue of a youth dating to about 340–330 BC; a bronze head of a philosopher; marble statues, some badly corroded by the sea; silver and bronze coins; and luxury goods suggesting the ship was carrying booty or a commercial cargo of art and valuables, probably from the Greek east toward Italy. Coins found in later expeditions helped date the wreck to roughly 70–60 BC.

Later expeditions

Jacques Cousteau's team worked the site in 1976, recovering additional coins and objects. Since 2012, the "Return to Antikythera" project, using advanced diving technology including rebreathers and the Exosuit, has conducted new surveys; in 2016 the team recovered a human skeleton from the wreck, from which DNA analysis was attempted, and further bronze artefacts have been raised in subsequent seasons.
