The Wöhler synthesis is the conversion, by heat, of ammonium cyanate into urea. It was first reported in 1828 by the German chemist Friedrich Wöhler in the paper "Ueber künstliche Bildung des Harnstoffs" ("On the artificial formation of urea"), published in the Annalen der Physik und Chemie. Wöhler had attempted to prepare ammonium cyanate by reacting silver cyanate with ammonium chloride (and in related experiments lead cyanate with ammonia), and on evaporating and heating the solution obtained white crystals that proved identical to urea isolated from urine.

Reaction

The transformation is an isomerisation: ammonium cyanate, NH4(OCN), rearranges on heating to urea, CO(NH2)2, the two compounds having the same overall composition. Modern descriptions treat the process as proceeding through dissociation into ammonia and isocyanic acid, which then recombine as urea.

Significance

The result is celebrated as a landmark in the history of chemistry because urea, a product of animal metabolism, had been made from inorganic starting materials without the involvement of a living organism. Textbooks long presented this as the single blow that destroyed vitalism — the doctrine that organic compounds required a special "vital force". Historians note the actual picture is subtler: vitalism declined gradually over following decades, Wöhler himself was cautious in his claims, and his starting cyanates ultimately traced back to organic sources. Nonetheless the experiment is conventionally taken to mark the beginning of modern organic synthesis.
