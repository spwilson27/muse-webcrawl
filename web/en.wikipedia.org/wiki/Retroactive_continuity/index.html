Retroactive continuity

Retroactive continuity, usually shortened to retcon, is a literary and storytelling device in which established facts in a work of serial fiction are adjusted, ignored, amplified or contradicted by a later instalment. Common in comic books, long-running television series, film franchises and soap operas, retcons allow creators to introduce new plot developments, resurrect characters or reconcile inconsistencies — sometimes to the irritation of fans who value consistent canon.

Types

Retcons take several forms. Addition supplies new background that fills gaps without contradicting earlier material (for example, revealing an unknown sibling). Alteration changes an established fact outright, often explained in-story by time travel, dreams, unreliable narrators or reality-altering events; the 1980s television series Dallas notoriously wrote off an entire season as a dream. Subtraction simply ignores inconvenient earlier material and proceeds as if it never happened. Large-scale retcons may reboot a whole continuity, as DC Comics did in Crisis on Infinite Earths (1985–86) and subsequent reboots.

Origins of the term

The phrase "retroactive continuity" was popularised in comics fandom in the 1970s and 1980s — it appeared in the fanzine culture around DC Comics and was used by writer Roy Thomas regarding the retrofitted Golden Age history of the All-Star Squadron — and shortened to "retcon" by fans on early internet forums. Related concepts in other media include George Lucas's revisions to the original Star Wars films and the rewriting of Sherlock Holmes's biography so that Arthur Conan Doyle could resurrect the detective after "The Final Problem" by revealing he had survived.

Reception

Critics distinguish "good" retcons that enrich a story from "bad" ones that cheapen earlier drama (for instance, undoing a meaningful death). The practice is a staple topic of fan debate and continuity scholarship.
