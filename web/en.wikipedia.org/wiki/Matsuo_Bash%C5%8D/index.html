Matsuo Bashō (松尾 芭蕉; 1644 – 28 November 1694) was the most famous poet of the Edo period in Japan and is regarded as the greatest master of haiku, then called hokku. Born Matsuo Kinsaku near Ueno in Iga Province, he took his pen name from a banana plant (bashō) given to him by a disciple. His poetry is internationally renowned, and in Japan many of his poems are reproduced on monuments and at traditional sites.

Life

Bashō was born into a family of low-ranking samurai in the service of the Tōdō clan. After his lord's death he moved to Edo (modern Tokyo), where he studied poetry and gradually built a reputation as a teacher of haikai, attracting many disciples. From 1684 he undertook a series of journeys across Japan, travelling on foot and recording his impressions in travel diaries that combine prose and verse (haibun).

Works

His masterpieces include Nozarashi Kikō (Record of a Weather-Exposed Skeleton), Sarashina Kikō and above all Oku no Hosomichi (The Narrow Road to the Deep North), an account of a long journey through northern Honshu in 1689, published posthumously in 1702 and considered the supreme achievement of haibun literature. His most celebrated poem is the "old pond" haiku: furu ike ya / kawazu tobikomu / mizu no oto — "old pond / a frog jumps in / the sound of water".

Style and legacy

Bashō elevated haikai from comic wordplay to serious literature, developing aesthetic principles such as sabi (the beauty of solitude and age) and karumi (lightness). He died in Osaka in 1694 while on another journey. His influence on Japanese poetry and on modern haiku worldwide remains immense.
