A prime factor of a natural number is a factor of that number which is itself a prime number. Every integer greater than 1 can be expressed as a product of prime factors — its prime factorisation — and by the fundamental theorem of arithmetic this factorisation is unique apart from the order of the factors.

Examples

The number 36 factorises as 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = 22 × 32, so its distinct prime factors are 2 and 3. The number 100 has prime factorisation 22 × 52. A prime number is its own sole prime factor. Factor trees and repeated division by the smallest possible prime are the standard school methods for finding factorisations of small numbers.

Computational difficulty

No efficient (polynomial-time) classical algorithm is known for factorising very large integers, and the hardness of this problem underpins the RSA cryptosystem, whose security rests on the practical infeasibility of recovering the two prime factors of a large semiprime. Peter Shor's 1994 quantum algorithm factorises in polynomial time, which is a principal motivation for post-quantum cryptography.

Related concepts

The number of distinct prime factors of n is denoted ω(n); counting multiplicity gives Ω(n). The largest prime factor appears in smoothness concepts: an integer is called B-smooth if all its prime factors are at most B, a notion used in modern factoring algorithms such as the number field sieve.
