Southern bluefin tuna

Southern bluefin tuna
Thunnus maccoyii (Castelnau, 1872)
Family: Scombridae
Conservation status: Endangered (IUCN)
Max length: about 2.45 m; weight up to 260 kg

The southern bluefin tuna (Thunnus maccoyii) is a tuna of the family Scombridae found in open southern hemisphere waters of all the world's oceans mainly between 30°S and 50°S, to 60°S. At up to 2.45 metres (8 ft) in length and 260 kilograms (570 lb) in weight, it is among the larger bony fish. It is a highly valued food fish, particularly for sashimi in Japan.

Biology

Southern bluefin tuna are slow-growing and long-lived, surviving for up to 40 years. They spawn in the tropical waters south of Java in the northeastern Indian Ocean — the species' only known spawning ground — and juveniles migrate south along the Western Australian coast into the Great Australian Bight, where they spend their summers, before dispersing across the southern oceans as adults. Like other bluefin tunas they are regionally warm-blooded (endothermic), maintaining body temperatures above ambient, which supports sustained fast swimming in cold water.

Fishery and conservation

Intensive fishing from the 1950s, including for the Japanese sashimi market and Australian purse-seine catches of juveniles for tuna ranching at Port Lincoln, reduced the spawning stock to a small fraction of its unfished level. The fishery is managed internationally by the Commission for the Conservation of Southern Bluefin Tuna (CCSBT, established 1994), whose members include Australia, Japan, New Zealand, South Korea, Indonesia, and South Africa. Catch quotas under a management procedure have allowed the stock to rebuild slowly, and the IUCN assessment improved the species from Critically Endangered to Endangered in 2021.
