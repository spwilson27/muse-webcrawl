Umbria

Umbria (Italian: [ˈumbrja]) is one of the twenty regions of Italy, located in the centre of the country. It is the only Italian region that borders neither the sea nor another country. Its capital is Perugia. The region covers about 8,456 km² and has a population of roughly 880,000. Often called "the green heart of Italy" (il cuore verde d'Italia), it is known for its rolling hills, medieval hill towns and religious heritage.

Geography

Umbria is traversed by the upper valley of the Tiber and enclosed by the Apennines to the east, rising to Monte Vettore at 2,476 m on the Marche border. Lake Trasimeno, the largest lake in peninsular Italy, lies in the west. The landscape combines mountain, hill and plain, and is dotted with historic towns including Assisi, Orvieto, Spoleto, Gubbio, Todi, Norcia and Città di Castello.

History

The region takes its name from the Umbri, an Italic people absorbed into the expanding Roman state by the third century BC. After the fall of Rome it was contested by Byzantines and Lombards, whose Duchy of Spoleto ruled much of it. In the Middle Ages its communes flourished and then fell under the Papal States, to which Umbria belonged until Italian unification in 1860–61. The region is inseparable from Saint Francis of Assisi, born in Assisi in 1181/82, whose basilica there is a UNESCO World Heritage site and a major pilgrimage destination.

Economy and culture

Agriculture produces tobacco, wheat, olives, wine grapes (notably Sagrantino di Montefalco and Orvieto), and prized black truffles around Norcia. Industry centres on steel at Terni and textiles and machinery around Perugia, which is also famous for chocolate, manufactured by Perugina. Tourism is drawn by art cities, festivals such as Umbria Jazz in Perugia and the Festival dei Due Mondi in Spoleto, and by the medieval art of painters such as Perugino and Pinturicchio.
