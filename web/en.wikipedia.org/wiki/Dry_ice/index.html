Dry ice is the solid form of carbon dioxide. It is commonly used for temporary refrigeration because at standard atmospheric pressure it does not melt into a liquid but instead sublimes directly into gas at −78.5 °C (−109.3 °F), leaving no residue—hence "dry." The white vapor seen around dry ice is not carbon dioxide gas itself (which is invisible) but condensed water vapor from the chilled surrounding air, forming a low-lying fog.

Production and history

The effect was noted in 1835 by the French inventor Adrien-Jean-Pierre Thilorier, the first to report the solid. Commercial production began in the 1920s; the name "dry ice" was trademarked in 1925 by the DryIce Corporation of America. Industrially, carbon dioxide gas (often captured as a byproduct of ammonia production or fermentation) is pressurized and cooled into liquid, then allowed to expand rapidly, which freezes part of it into "snow" that is compressed into blocks, slabs, or pellets.

Uses

Dry ice is widely used to keep food and medical supplies frozen during shipping—including temperature-sensitive vaccines—because it is colder than water ice and leaves no meltwater. Other uses include fog effects in theatres and concerts, freeze-branding of livestock, carbonating beverages, blast cleaning ("dry-ice blasting"), purging fuel tanks, and shrinking metal parts for press fits. It is also used to trap insects and, controversially and largely ineffectively, in attempts at cloud seeding.

Hazards

Contact can cause severe frostbite within seconds, so insulated gloves are required. Subliming dry ice releases large volumes of carbon dioxide: one kilogram produces roughly 500 litres of gas, which can displace oxygen and cause asphyxiation in confined spaces, and sealing dry ice in an airtight container risks explosion as pressure builds. It must never be ingested.

Extraterrestrial occurrence

Dry ice is abundant in the polar caps of Mars, where seasonal frost and buried deposits of solid CO2 drive springtime geyser-like eruptions of gas and dust when sunlight warms the ice from below.
