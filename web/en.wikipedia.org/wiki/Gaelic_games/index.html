Gaelic games (Irish: Cluichí Gaelacha) are a set of sports originating in Ireland, played principally there and wherever Irish diaspora communities have carried them. They comprise Gaelic football, hurling, camogie (the women's form of hurling), Gaelic handball and rounders. The games are governed by the Gaelic Athletic Association (GAA), founded in Thurles, County Tipperary, in 1884.

The sports

Gaelic football is played by two teams of fifteen with a round ball that may be kicked or hand-passed toward H-shaped goals, scoring a point over the bar or three points for a goal under it. Hurling, played with a wooden stick (hurley or camán) and a small leather ball (sliotar), uses the same goals and scoring and is often described as the fastest field sport in the world. Camogie follows nearly identical rules for women. Gaelic handball is played in a court against a wall, and rounders is a bat-and-ball game related to baseball and softball.

Organisation

The GAA is organised on the parish and county structure of Ireland: players represent their local club and, at the highest level, their county in the All-Ireland championships, whose finals at Croke Park in Dublin — one of Europe's largest stadiums — are among the biggest events in the Irish sporting calendar. Despite their popularity, the games remain amateur: players are not paid, a principle the association has maintained since its founding as part of its mission to promote Irish sport and culture.

International presence

Clubs and county boards exist in Britain, North America, continental Europe, Asia and Australasia, and hybrid codes such as international rules football (against Australian rules) and shinty-hurling have been devised for cross-code matches.
