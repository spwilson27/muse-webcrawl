Goods and services

Goods are items that are usually tangible — such as pens, physical books, salt, apples and hats — whereas services are activities provided by other people, such as teachers, doctors, lawyers, barbers and waiters. Taken together, goods and services are the output of an economic system and the basis of most everyday commerce: consumers buy goods and services to satisfy wants and needs.

Distinguishing characteristics

Economics textbooks conventionally contrast the two categories along several dimensions:

  • Tangibility: goods are physical objects that can be touched and stored; services are intangible actions or performances.
  • Separability: goods can be produced, stored and consumed later; services are typically produced and consumed simultaneously (a haircut cannot be stockpiled).
  • Ownership: buying a good transfers ownership; buying a service usually does not.
  • Perishability and variability: services cannot be inventoried and their quality varies with the provider and occasion.

Classification

Goods are variously classified as consumer goods or capital (producer) goods; durable or non-durable; and, in welfare economics, by rivalry and excludability — private goods, public goods, common goods and club goods. Services comprise the tertiary sector of the economy, with knowledge-based "quaternary" activities sometimes distinguished further. In practice many offerings are bundles: a restaurant meal combines goods (food) with services (preparation, table service, ambience).

National accounts

In statistics such as GDP, production is divided between goods-producing industries (agriculture, mining, manufacturing, construction, utilities) and service-producing industries (trade, transport, finance, health, education, government and others). In most advanced economies services account for the large majority of output and employment — commonly around 70–80 per cent of GDP.
