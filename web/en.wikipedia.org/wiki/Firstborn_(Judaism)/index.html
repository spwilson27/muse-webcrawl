Firstborn (Judaism)

The firstborn (Hebrew: bekhor, בְּכוֹר) in Judaism holds a special religious status arising from the biblical narrative of the Exodus, in which the firstborn of Egypt were slain in the tenth plague while Israel's firstborn were spared. The Torah consecrates the firstborn of humans and animals to God, and Jewish law attaches to the firstborn son — and in some respects to firstborn animals — a set of obligations and privileges that survive in the ceremonies of pidyon haben (redemption of the son), the fast of the firstborn, and the rules of inheritance.

Biblical basis

Exodus 13 commands: "Consecrate to me all the firstborn. Whatever is the first to open the womb among the people of Israel, both of man and of beast, is mine." Because the Levites were taken for Temple service in place of the firstborn, a firstborn Israelite son must be formally "redeemed" from a kohen (priest). In the biblical narratives the firstborn traditionally received a double portion of the inheritance (Deuteronomy 21:17) and a position of family leadership, though the Genesis stories frequently subvert the pattern — Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, Joseph over Reuben and Ephraim over Manasseh.

Redemption of the firstborn son

The pidyon haben ceremony is held when a firstborn son is 31 days old. The father presents the child to a kohen and redeems him with five shekels — in modern practice, five silver coins of specified weight, often specially minted — accompanied by blessings and a festive meal. The obligation applies only when the child is his mother's firstborn, born naturally (not by caesarean section), and when neither parent is a kohen or Levite.

Firstborn animals and the fast of the firstborn

A firstborn male of a kosher domestic animal (bekhor) was given to the priests and, in Temple times, offered as a sacrifice if unblemished; today the rules are largely in abeyance and various devices are used to avoid the sanctity attaching. A firstborn donkey is redeemed with a lamb (peter chamor). Separately, the Fast of the Firstborn (Ta'anit Bekhorim) is observed by firstborn sons on the day before Passover in commemoration of their ancestors' deliverance in Egypt; it is commonly circumvented by attending a siyum, a celebratory meal marking the completion of a tractate of Talmud, which exempts participants from fasting.
