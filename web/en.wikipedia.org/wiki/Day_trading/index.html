Day trading is the practice of buying and selling financial instruments within the same trading day, so that all positions are closed before the market closes. Day traders typically seek to profit from small intraday price movements using leverage, technical analysis, and high trading volume, and they trade stocks, options, futures, currencies, and cryptocurrencies.

History

Day trading became widely accessible in the 1990s with the growth of electronic communication networks (ECNs), online brokerages, and real-time market data. It surged during the dot-com boom, when large numbers of retail traders speculated in technology stocks. Subsequent regulatory changes, including the U.S. pattern day trader rule of 2001, imposed a $25,000 minimum account equity on frequent day traders in margin accounts.

Strategies

Common approaches include scalping (taking very small profits on many trades), momentum trading (following strong intraday moves, often triggered by news or earnings), range trading (buying at support and selling at resistance), and news-based trading. Traders rely on candlestick charts, level II order data, and direct-access brokers for fast execution.

Profitability and risk

Empirical studies consistently find that most day traders lose money. A study of Brazilian futures traders found that the overwhelming majority of those who persisted for more than 300 trading days lost money, and research on Taiwanese day traders similarly shows only a small minority are predictably profitable. High leverage, transaction costs, and emotional decision-making contribute to the poor outcomes.
