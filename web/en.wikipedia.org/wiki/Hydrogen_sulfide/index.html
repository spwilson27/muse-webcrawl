Hydrogen sulfide

Hydrogen sulfide is a chemical compound with the formula H2S. It is a colorless chalcogen-hydride gas, and is poisonous, corrosive, and flammable, with a characteristic foul odor of rotten eggs. It occurs in volcanic gases, natural gas, and some well waters, and is produced by the microbial breakdown of organic matter in the absence of oxygen, such as in swamps and sewers. Exposure to high concentrations can be rapidly fatal.

Properties

Hydrogen sulfide is slightly denser than air and burns with a blue flame to give sulfur dioxide and water. It is a weak diprotic acid (pKa1 ≈ 7.0). The molecule has a bent geometry with a bond angle of about 92°, and is isoelectronic with water though far less hydrogen-bonded, hence its low boiling point.

Occurrence and production

H2S is a significant impurity in natural gas and petroleum ("sour gas"), from which it is removed by amine gas treating and converted into elemental sulfur in the Claus process. In the laboratory it can be generated from iron sulfide and acid in a Kipp's apparatus. The human body itself produces small amounts of hydrogen sulfide as a signaling molecule (gasotransmitter), alongside nitric oxide and carbon monoxide.

Toxicity

Hydrogen sulfide is a broad-spectrum poison that inhibits the enzyme cytochrome c oxidase, blocking cellular respiration in a manner similar to cyanide. The gas is detectable by smell at fractions of a part per million, but olfactory fatigue sets in rapidly; at around 100 ppm the sense of smell is deadened, removing the warning sign. Concentrations above roughly 700–1000 ppm cause rapid unconsciousness, respiratory paralysis, and death. Occupational exposure limits are typically set at 10–20 ppm over a working day. Sewer workers, oil and gas workers, and farm workers near manure pits are among those at risk.
