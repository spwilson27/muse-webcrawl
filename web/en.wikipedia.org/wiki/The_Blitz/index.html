The Blitz


St Paul's Cathedral surrounded by smoke, 29 December 1940

The Blitz (from German Blitzkrieg, "lightning war") was a bombing campaign undertaken by the German Luftwaffe against the United Kingdom during the Second World War. From 7 September 1940 to 11 May 1941, German bombers attacked London and other British cities in a sustained strategic bombing offensive intended to demoralise the population and force Britain to negotiate.

The London Blitz

The campaign began with "Black Saturday", 7 September 1940, when some 350 bombers attacked the London docks. London was then bombed on 57 consecutive nights. The heaviest raid on the capital in this phase came on 29 December 1940, when incendiary bombs started the Second Great Fire of London; Herbert Mason's photograph of St Paul's Cathedral standing amid the smoke became an iconic image of British endurance.

Provincial raids

The Luftwaffe also struck ports and industrial centres across the country, including Coventry (devastated on 14–15 November 1940, an attack that gave German the verb coventrieren), Birmingham, Liverpool, Manchester, Sheffield, Southampton, Portsmouth, Plymouth, Bristol, Cardiff, Belfast and the Clydebank shipyards in Scotland.

Effects and outcome

More than 40,000 British civilians were killed and over a million houses in London alone were damaged or destroyed. Yet the Blitz failed in its strategic aims: British war production continued, civilian morale — mythologised as the "Blitz spirit" — did not collapse, and the invasion of Britain was never attempted. In May 1941 the Luftwaffe turned east for Operation Barbarossa, ending the Blitz, although raids continued at a lower intensity, culminating in the "Baby Blitz" of 1944 and the V-weapon attacks.
