World Cancer Day is an international awareness day held every year on 4 February to raise awareness of cancer, encourage its prevention, detection and treatment, and press governments and individuals to act against the disease. It is led by the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC), a Geneva-based non-governmental organisation with more than a thousand member organisations in over 170 countries, and is supported by the World Health Organization and other international bodies.

History

World Cancer Day was established at the World Summit Against Cancer for the New Millennium, held in Paris on 4 February 2000. The summit adopted the Charter of Paris Against Cancer, which called for a global partnership against the disease and designated 4 February as the day of observance. In 2008 the UICC's World Cancer Declaration set measurable targets for reducing the global cancer burden, and the day became the focal point of a coordinated worldwide campaign involving public events, social-media campaigns, fundraising and illumination of landmarks in the campaign colours of blue and orange.

Themes

Since 2019 the campaign has run in three-year thematic cycles:

  • 2019–2021: "I Am and I Will" — emphasising personal commitment to act against cancer.
  • 2022–2024: "Close the Care Gap" — highlighting inequities in access to diagnosis and treatment between and within countries.
  • 2025–2027: "United by Unique" — placing people and their individual experiences at the centre of cancer care.

Significance

Cancer is one of the leading causes of death worldwide, accounting for nearly ten million deaths in 2020 — roughly one in six of all deaths. The most common cancers are those of the breast, lung, colon and rectum, and prostate. According to the WHO, between 30 and 50 per cent of cancers can currently be prevented by avoiding risk factors such as tobacco use, alcohol consumption, unhealthy diet, physical inactivity and air pollution, and many others can be cured if detected early. World Cancer Day aims to counter misinformation, reduce stigma, and mobilise support for prevention programmes, vaccination against cancer-causing infections such as HPV and hepatitis B, and wider access to screening and treatment.
