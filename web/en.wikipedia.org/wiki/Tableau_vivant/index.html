Tableau vivant

TypePerformance art, living picture
OriginMedieval and early modern European theatre and pageantry

A tableau vivant (French for "living picture"; plural tableaux vivants) is a static scene containing one or more actors or models. The participants are silent and motionless, carefully posed, costumed and lit to imitate a work of art—a painting, sculpture or literary scene. Scenes are typically presented for a short duration, often revealed by the raising of a curtain and framed like a picture.

History

Living pictures appeared in medieval religious drama and royal entries, where allegorical figures posed in staged scenes along processional routes. The form flourished in the late 18th century: Emma Hamilton's "attitudes" in Naples, in which she posed as figures from classical art, became famous across Europe, and Goethe described such performances in Elective Affinities (1809), which includes a celebrated scene of a family staging tableaux from well-known paintings.

Through the 19th century, tableaux vivants were a staple of respectable parlour entertainment in Britain and America, used for charity fund-raising and amateur theatricals, as well as a device within professional drama to mark moments of emotional climax. Because the performers did not move, the form also allowed theatres to present mildly risqué scenes of semi-nude "statues" that would have been censured in motion; music-hall and burlesque versions of the practice attracted periodic controversy and legal attention.

In later culture

The technique survived into the photographic and cinematic eras: early photographers restaged paintings as tableaux, and films have used held poses to quote artworks—famously in Jean-Luc Godard's Passion (1982), which restages paintings by Rembrandt, Delacroix and others with living models. The Pageant of the Masters, held annually in Laguna Beach, California since the 1930s, presents large-scale recreations of famous artworks staffed by local volunteers. Contemporary performance artists continue to use the living picture to comment on stillness, spectatorship and the boundary between art and life.
