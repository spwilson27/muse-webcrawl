Peasants' Revolt

The Peasants' Revolt, also named Wat Tyler's Rebellion or the Great Rising, was a major uprising across large parts of England in 1381. The revolt had various causes, including the socio-economic and political tensions generated by the Black Death in the 1340s, the high taxes imposed by the government to finance the Hundred Years' War, and instability within the local leadership of London.

Causes

The immediate spark was the poll tax of 1380 — the third in four years — a flat-rate levy of one shilling per adult, whose enforcement commissioners provoked resistance in Essex and Kent in May–June 1381. Behind it lay decades of grievance: the Statute of Labourers had frozen wages after the plague, and villeins resented serfdom and manorial dues even as labour scarcity strengthened their bargaining position. Radical preaching, notably by John Ball, spread egalitarian ideas summed up in the rhyme "When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then the gentleman?"

The rising

Rebels from Kent, led by Wat Tyler, and from Essex marched on London, entering the city on 13 June. They burned the Savoy Palace of John of Gaunt, opened prisons, and executed officials including Archbishop Simon Sudbury, the Chancellor, and Treasurer Robert Hales at the Tower. The fourteen-year-old King Richard II met the rebels at Mile End on 14 June and conceded charters abolishing serfdom. The next day, at Smithfield, Tyler was killed by the Lord Mayor William Walworth during a confrontation, and Richard defused the crowd by riding out to address them.

Aftermath

Once the rebels dispersed, the charters were revoked and royal forces suppressed risings in the provinces; leaders including John Ball were executed. Although the revolt failed in its immediate aims, the poll tax was abandoned, and historians see the rising as a landmark in English social history that hastened the decline of serfdom.
