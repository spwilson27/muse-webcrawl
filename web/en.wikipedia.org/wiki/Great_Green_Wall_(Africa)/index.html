The Great Green Wall is an African Union–led initiative to combat desertification and land degradation across the Sahel, the semi-arid belt south of the Sahara. Originally conceived in 2007 as a literal wall of trees stretching some 8,000 kilometres from Dakar, Senegal, to Djibouti, the project has evolved into a mosaic of land-restoration and sustainable livelihood interventions across more than twenty countries.

Evolution of the concept

The idea of a transcontinental shelterbelt dates to Thomas Sankara's Burkina Faso campaigns and was adopted by the African Union in 2007. Experience showed that a single tree wall was ecologically simplistic; the initiative now promotes locally adapted practices — farmer-managed natural regeneration (which restored millions of hectares in Niger), agroforestry, terracing and water harvesting — aimed at restoring 100 million hectares, sequestering 250 million tonnes of carbon and creating 10 million jobs by 2030.

Progress and challenges

Progress has been uneven: estimates in the early 2020s put completed restoration at roughly 15–20% of the target, with Senegal and Ethiopia among the furthest advanced and conflict-affected regions lagging. Funding shortfalls, insecure land tenure and insecurity in the Sahel remain major obstacles, and a 2021 "Great Green Wall Accelerator" sought to unlock pledged international finance.
