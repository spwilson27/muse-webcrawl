The Darian calendar is a proposed system of timekeeping designed to be used by future human settlers on the planet Mars. It was created by aerospace engineer and political scientist Thomas Gangale in 1985 and named by him after his son Darius. It is the best-known and most fully developed of the many Martian calendar proposals, dividing the Martian year of roughly 668.6 sols into 24 months and using a seven-sol week.

Year length and intercalation

A Martian year is approximately 668.59 sols long. The Darian calendar therefore uses ordinary years of 668 sols and leap years of 669 sols, with six leap years in every ten-year period under its basic intercalation formula (leap years are the odd-numbered years and years divisible by ten), supplemented by small corrections at longer intervals to keep the calendar aligned with the Martian seasons over millennia.

Months

The year is divided into 24 months, most of 28 sols, with four months of 27 sols in ordinary years (the last month has 28 sols in leap years). The months are named after the signs of the zodiac, in both Latin and Sanskrit forms alternating: Sagittarius, Dhanus, Capricornus, Makara, and so on through Aquarius, Kumbha, Pisces and Mina. The seven sols of the week are named Solis, Lunae, Martis, Mercurii, Jovis, Veneris and Saturni.

Epoch and variants

Gangale's original epoch began the calendar in 1609, the year of Galileo's telescopic observations and Kepler's laws, to anchor Martian time in the history of astronomy. Variants such as the "Martiana" revision adjust month names and the leap-year rule. Although no Martian calendar is in official use — mission teams simply count sols from landing — the Darian calendar has been widely adopted in science fiction and by Mars enthusiast societies.
