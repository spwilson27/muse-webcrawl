Patient autonomy

Patient autonomy is the principle in medical ethics that patients have the right to make informed decisions about their own medical care, free from coercion. It is one of the four pillars of modern bioethics, alongside beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice, as set out in the influential framework of Tom Beauchamp and James Childress.

Informed consent

The principal expression of patient autonomy is informed consent: before treatment, a patient with decision-making capacity must be told the nature of the proposed intervention, its risks and benefits, and reasonable alternatives, and must agree voluntarily. Correspondingly, patients generally have the right to refuse treatment, even life-saving treatment, and to leave hospital against medical advice.

Historical development

The emphasis on autonomy arose in reaction to medical paternalism and to abuses such as the Tuskegee syphilis study and Nazi medical experiments, which produced the Nuremberg Code (1947). Landmark legal cases — including Schloendorff v. Society of New York Hospital (1914), in which Judge Cardozo wrote that "every human being of adult years and sound mind has a right to determine what shall be done with his own body" — established the doctrine in law. Later instruments such as advance directives and living wills extend autonomy to periods when a patient cannot speak for themselves.

Tensions and limits

Autonomy can conflict with beneficence when a patient's choice seems harmful, and its interpretation varies across cultures, some of which emphasise family-based decision-making. Debates continue over autonomy in contexts such as vaccination mandates, psychiatric commitment, and end-of-life choices including assisted dying.
