The Battle of Orgreave was a violent confrontation between striking miners and police on 18 June 1984 at the Orgreave coking plant near Rotherham, South Yorkshire, during the British miners' strike of 1984–85. It is remembered as one of the most notorious episodes of the strike and of modern British policing.

Background

The National Union of Mineworkers (NUM), led by Arthur Scargill, sought to prevent lorries from carrying coke from the British Steel Corporation plant at Orgreave to steelworks at Scunthorpe. Mass pickets had been organised at the plant since late May, and on 18 June several thousand pickets gathered — estimates of picket numbers range from 5,000 to 10,000 — faced by around 5,000 police officers drawn from forces across the country.

The confrontation

After a morning of pushing and shoving between pickets and police lines, police mounted officers and short-shield units charged into the crowd. Running battles spread into the village of Orgreave and surrounding fields. Ninety-five pickets were arrested and charged with riot and unlawful assembly — offences that could carry life sentences. Stones were thrown by some pickets; many miners and officers were injured, though none fatally.

Legal aftermath

The riot trials collapsed in 1985 after police evidence was discredited, and all the accused were acquitted. In 1991 South Yorkshire Police paid £425,000 in compensation to 39 miners for assault, wrongful arrest and malicious prosecution. No officer was ever disciplined or prosecuted over Orgreave.

Legacy and calls for inquiry

The Orgreave Truth and Justice Campaign has long sought a public inquiry into the policing of the day and the subsequent prosecutions. In 2016 the Home Secretary, Amber Rudd, announced there would be no inquiry, a decision campaigners condemned. In 2024 the Labour government committed to an inquiry, and in 2025 an inquiry into the events at Orgreave was formally announced.
