In physics, hydrodynamics is a sub-discipline of fluid dynamics concerned with the motion of liquids, particularly water, and the forces acting on solid bodies immersed in them. Together with hydrostatics (fluids at rest) it forms the classical field of hydrodynamics in the broad sense, with applications in ship design, hydraulic engineering, oceanography, meteorology and astrophysics.

History

The subject begins with Archimedes' work on buoyancy and floating bodies. Daniel Bernoulli's Hydrodynamica (1738) gave the field its name and stated the principle relating flow speed and pressure; Leonhard Euler derived the equations of motion for an inviscid fluid in the 1750s. In the 19th century Claude-Louis Navier and George Gabriel Stokes added viscosity, producing the Navier–Stokes equations, while Osborne Reynolds clarified the transition between laminar and turbulent flow.

Governing principles

Hydrodynamic analysis rests on conservation of mass (the continuity equation), conservation of momentum and conservation of energy. For incompressible, inviscid flow, Bernoulli's principle relates pressure, velocity and elevation along a streamline:

p + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant

Flow regimes

The dimensionless Reynolds number predicts whether flow is laminar (smooth, layered) or turbulent (chaotic, mixing). Near solid boundaries a thin boundary layer forms, a concept developed by Ludwig Prandtl in 1904 that reconciled ideal-flow theory with observed drag.

Applications

Hydrodynamics underpins hull and propeller design, tidal and wave power, dam and spillway engineering, the study of ocean currents and tsunamis, blood-flow modelling (haemodynamics), and the behaviour of liquid planetary interiors and stellar plasma in magnetohydrodynamics.
