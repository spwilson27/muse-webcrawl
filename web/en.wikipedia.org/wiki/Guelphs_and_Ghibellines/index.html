Guelphs and Ghibellines

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Guelphs and Ghibellines (Italian: Guelfi e Ghibellini) were factions supporting the Pope (Guelphs) and the Holy Roman Emperor (Ghibellines) in the Italian city-states of Central and Northern Italy during the Middle Ages, particularly in the 12th and 13th centuries. Their rivalry structured Italian politics for two centuries and outlasted the original imperial–papal conflict from which it sprang.

Origins

The names derive from the rival German houses of Welf (Italianised as Guelfo) and Waiblingen, the castle of the Hohenstaufen emperors (Ghibellino). They became war cries in the struggle between Emperor Frederick I Barbarossa and the Italian communes, and were adopted in Florence around 1215 following the Buondelmonti–Amidei feud, from which the factional divide spread through Tuscany and beyond.

Course of the conflict

Ghibelline fortunes peaked with Frederick II and with victories such as Montaperti (1260), where Siena's Ghibellines crushed Guelph Florence. But Charles of Anjou's defeat of the Hohenstaufen at Benevento (1266) and Tagliacozzo (1268) established Guelph ascendancy. Within Florence the victorious Guelphs split into Black and White factions; the triumph of the Blacks in 1301 sent Dante Alighieri, a White Guelph, into lifelong exile, a feud he immortalised in the Divine Comedy.

Legacy

By the 14th century the labels were largely inherited badges of local rivalry rather than statements of allegiance to pope or emperor, and they gradually faded as signorial regimes replaced the communes. The names survive in architecture — Guelph battlements are rectangular, Ghibelline battlements swallow-tailed — and in the names of modern orders and political movements.
