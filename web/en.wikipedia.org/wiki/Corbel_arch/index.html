Corbel arch

A corbel arch (or corbeled / corbelled arch) is an arch-like construction method that uses the architectural technique of corbeling to span a space or void in a structure, such as an entranceway in a wall or as the span of a bridge. Unlike a true arch, it does not generate outward thrust and is not self-supporting in the same way; instead, each course of masonry projects slightly further into the opening than the one below until the two sides meet.

History

Corbel arches are among the oldest spanning techniques. Examples include the corbelled roof of the Treasury of Atreus at Mycenae (c. 1250 BC), the galleries of Newgrange in Ireland, and the roofs of early Egyptian and Mesopotamian buildings. The technique is characteristic of Maya architecture, where corbel vaults roofed palaces and temples, and of medieval Indian and Southeast Asian temples, as well as the dry-stone trulli of Apulia and the beehive huts of Ireland.

Comparison with the true arch

A true arch transfers load along a curve to its supports and can span greater widths with less material. A corbel arch is closer in behaviour to a pair of opposing cantilevered brackets; its span is limited, and the resulting profile is typically triangular or stepped rather than smoothly curved, though steps were often dressed smooth in finished buildings.
