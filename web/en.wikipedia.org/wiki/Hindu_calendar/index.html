The Hindu calendar is a collective term for the various lunisolar calendars traditionally used in India and Nepal, together with their regional variants. Rather than a single system, it comprises many related calendars that share concepts—lunar months named for the nakshatras, solar years, intercalation rules and a sexagenary cycle of year names—while differing in whether the month begins with the new moon (amanta) or the full moon (purnimanta), and in the era from which years are counted.

Structure

The basic unit is the tithi, a lunar day defined as the time taken for the Moon to increase its elongation from the Sun by 12 degrees; a lunar month contains 30 tithis but only about 29.5 civil days, so tithis occasionally repeat or are skipped. Twelve lunar months fall about 11 days short of the solar year, so an intercalary month, adhika masa, is inserted roughly every two and a half to three years, in the month bearing the same name as the following month; rarely, a month is omitted (kshaya masa). The months, in order, are Chaitra, Vaishakha, Jyeshtha, Ashadha, Shravana, Bhadrapada, Ashvina, Kartika, Margashirsha, Pausha, Magha and Phalguna.

Eras and regional calendars

The Vikram Samvat era, traditionally founded by King Vikramaditya and epoch-dated to 57 BCE, is the official calendar of Nepal and is widely used in northern and western India. The Shaka era (epoch 78 CE) underlies the Indian national calendar adopted in 1957, a reformed solar calendar whose dates bear a fixed correspondence to the Gregorian calendar (Chaitra 1 falls on 22 March, or 21 March in leap years). Other regional systems include the solar Tamil, Malayalam (Kollam era) and Bengali calendars, and the Odia and Assamese variants. The traditional almanac, the panchanga, tabulates tithis, nakshatras, yogas and karanas for each day and guides the dating of festivals such as Diwali, Holi and Navaratri.

Astronomy

The rules of the calendar were codified in the Surya Siddhanta and related astronomical treatises, and a 60-year cycle of named years (the Jupiter cycle, Brihaspati samvatsara) runs alongside the eras. Modern printed panchangas are computed with contemporary ephemerides, which occasionally produce regional disagreements about festival dates.
