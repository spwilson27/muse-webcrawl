Robert Blake (1598–1657) was an English naval commander and one of the most important military figures of the Commonwealth of England. Appointed one of the three "Generals at Sea" in 1649 despite having no naval experience, he transformed the navy of the Commonwealth into a disciplined, professional force and is often regarded as a founder of the Royal Navy's fighting traditions.

Early life and Civil War

Blake was born in Bridgwater, Somerset, and educated at Wadham College, Oxford. He sat briefly in the Short Parliament of 1640. During the English Civil War he served Parliament as a soldier, achieving notable successes in the defence of Bristol, Lyme Regis, and Taunton, where he held the town through a long siege in 1644–45.

Naval career

In 1649 Blake was made a General at Sea. He pursued the Royalist fleet of Prince Rupert to Portugal and the Mediterranean, blockaded Lisbon, and captured the Scilly Isles and Jersey. During the First Anglo-Dutch War (1652–54) he fought the Dutch admirals Maarten Tromp and Michiel de Ruyter in the battles of Dover, Dungeness — where he was wounded — Portland, the Gabbard, and Scheveningen, helping to establish English sea power.

In 1654–55 Blake cruised the Mediterranean, compelling the Barbary states of North Africa to release English captives, and destroyed a Barbary corsair fleet at Porto Farina — the first time a European fleet had attacked such a harbour with ship guns alone. In 1657, during the Anglo-Spanish War, he destroyed a Spanish treasure fleet at the Battle of Santa Cruz de Tenerife without losing a ship, a feat later praised by Nelson. Wounded and ill, he died aboard his flagship within sight of Plymouth and was given a state funeral in Westminster Abbey (his remains were removed after the Restoration).

Legacy

Blake's Fighting Instructions and Articles of War codified naval discipline and tactics, including the line of battle. Several Royal Navy ships have borne the name HMS Blake.
