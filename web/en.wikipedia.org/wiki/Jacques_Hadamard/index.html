Life

Jacques Salomon Hadamard (8 December 1865 – 17 October 1963) was a French mathematician of extraordinary range and longevity, active into his nineties. Educated at the École Normale Supérieure, he taught at Bordeaux, the Sorbonne, and from 1909 at the Collège de France. The Dreyfus affair — his cousin's husband was Alfred Dreyfus — drew him into civic activism; during the Second World War he fled the Nazi occupation to the United States, lecturing at Columbia, and later visited China and the Soviet Union as a peace activist. His sons were killed in the First World War.

Mathematical work

Hadamard's supreme achievement came in 1896, when — simultaneously and independently of Charles Jean de la Vallée Poussin — he proved the prime number theorem, establishing that the number of primes up to x is asymptotic to x/ln x, using the complex-analytic theory of the Riemann zeta function that he helped create. His name attaches to many tools of analysis: Hadamard matrices (square ±1 matrices with orthogonal rows, central to coding theory), Hadamard's inequality bounding a determinant by the product of row lengths, the Cauchy–Hadamard theorem on radii of convergence, the Hadamard product of power series, and his well-posedness conditions for partial differential equations. He also made foundational contributions to functional analysis, differential geometry, and hydrodynamics.

The psychology of invention

Hadamard's short book The Psychology of Invention in the Mathematical Field (1945), growing out of lectures begun in the 1930s, surveyed how mathematicians actually think — emphasising unconscious incubation and sudden illumination — and included famous testimony from Einstein on thinking in images and feelings rather than words. It remains a classic of creativity studies.
