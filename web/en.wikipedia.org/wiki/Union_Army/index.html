Union Army

The Union Army was the land force that fought for the Union of the United States during the American Civil War (1861–1865). It consisted of the small United States Army (the "Regular Army"), augmented by massive numbers of state-raised volunteer units, and it ultimately defeated the Confederate Army, preserving the Union and ending slavery in the United States.

Organisation

Approximately 2.1 million men served in the Union Army, including some 180,000 African American soldiers in the United States Colored Troops. The army was organised into field armies named for rivers (such as the Army of the Potomac, Army of the Tennessee, and Army of the Cumberland), subdivided into corps, divisions, brigades, and regiments. Commanders included Irvin McDowell, George B. McClellan, Ambrose Burnside, Joseph Hooker, George Meade, William Tecumseh Sherman, and, from 1864 as general-in-chief, Ulysses S. Grant, who directed the coordinated campaigns that won the war.

The war

After initial setbacks in the East at Bull Run, the Union armies secured the Mississippi River with the capture of Vicksburg in July 1863, won the pivotal Battle of Gettysburg the same month, and in 1864–65 ground down Confederate forces through Grant's Overland Campaign, Sherman's March to the Sea, and the siege of Petersburg, culminating in Lee's surrender at Appomattox Court House on April 9, 1865. Union casualties numbered roughly 360,000 dead from all causes.
