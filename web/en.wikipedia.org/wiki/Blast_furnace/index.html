Blast furnace

A blast furnace is a type of metallurgical furnace used for smelting to produce industrial metals, generally pig iron, but also others such as lead or copper. In a blast furnace, fuel (coke), iron ore, and flux (limestone) are continuously supplied through the top while a hot blast of air is blown into the bottom, so that chemical reactions take place throughout the furnace as the material falls downward. Molten pig iron and slag are collected at the bottom and tapped periodically.

Process

Descending ore is dried, heated, and reduced stepwise by rising carbon monoxide: hematite (Fe2O3) passes through magnetite and wüstite to metallic iron. Limestone calcines and combines with impurities to form a molten slag that floats on the iron. Hot stoves preheat the blast air, sometimes enriched with oxygen and injected pulverised coal. The main reaction producing the reducing gas is the partial combustion of coke.

History

Blast furnaces originated in China by around the 1st century AD (with earlier bloomery antecedents) and spread through Europe in the late Middle Ages. Abraham Darby's coke smelting at Coalbrookdale (1709) freed ironmaking from charcoal and helped power the Industrial Revolution. Modern furnaces are enormous — over 8,000 cubic metres in the largest Chinese and Japanese plants — and operate continuously for campaigns of 15–20 years.

Future

Blast furnace ironmaking accounts for a large share of steel's CO2 emissions, driving interest in hydrogen direct reduction, carbon capture, and scrap-based electric arc furnaces. Most world steel, however, still begins in a blast furnace.
