Black pepper (Piper nigrum) is a flowering vine in the family Piperaceae, cultivated for its fruit, known as a peppercorn, which is usually dried and used as a spice and seasoning. The same fruit yields green, white and black pepper depending on when it is harvested and how it is processed. Black pepper is native to the Malabar Coast of India and is one of the most widely traded spices in the world.

Description and production

The pepper plant is a perennial woody vine growing up to 4 m in height on supporting trees or poles. Its small flowers are borne on pendant spikes; the fruits turn red when ripe. For black pepper, unripe green drupes are cooked briefly and dried until the skin shrivels and blackens. Vietnam is now the largest producer, followed by India, Brazil and Indonesia.

History

Pepper has been known in Indian cooking since at least 2000 BCE and was a driving commodity of the ancient spice trade. In classical antiquity it was a luxury good — Pliny complained of its price — and in the Middle Ages it was so valuable it was sometimes used as currency or rent ("peppercorn rent"). The search for a sea route to the pepper coasts of India motivated Vasco da Gama's voyage of 1498 and the ensuing European colonial competition in Asia.

Flavour and uses

Pepper's pungency comes from the alkaloid piperine, concentrated in the outer fruit layer; grinding exposes the volatile aromatics, so pepper is best ground fresh. It is a near-universal table seasoning, paired with salt, and is used medicinally in Ayurvedic tradition.
