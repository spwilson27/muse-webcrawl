Ylide

An ylide (pronounced "ill-id") is a neutral dipolar molecule containing a formally negatively charged atom (usually a carbanion) directly attached to a positively charged heteroatom such as phosphorus, sulfur, or nitrogen. Because the charges sit on adjacent atoms, ylides are also called 1,2-dipoles. They are important reagents in organic synthesis, valued for the nucleophilic character of the negatively charged carbon.

Types and preparation

Phosphonium ylides — the most famous class — are prepared by treating a phosphonium salt with a strong base such as butyllithium or sodium hydride, removing a proton from the carbon adjacent to phosphorus. Sulfonium and sulfoxonium ylides are made similarly from the corresponding salts, while ammonium and pyridinium ylides can be generated from metal carbenoids reacting with amines. Ylides stabilized by neighboring carbonyl or other electron-withdrawing groups are isolable, crystalline solids; "non-stabilized" ylides are typically generated and used in situ.

Reactions

The best-known ylide reaction is the Wittig reaction, in which a phosphonium ylide converts a ketone or aldehyde into an alkene, with triphenylphosphine oxide as the by-product — a transformation recognized by the 1979 Nobel Prize in Chemistry awarded to Georg Wittig. Sulfur ylides feature in the Corey–Chaykovsky reaction, which transfers a methylene group to carbonyls to give epoxides, or to alkenes to give cyclopropanes. Ylides also serve as ligands and as intermediates in rearrangements such as the Stevens and Sommelet–Hauser rearrangements.

Structure

Ylide bonding is described by two resonance forms: a double-bonded "ylene" form and the charge-separated ylide form. Spectroscopic and crystallographic evidence shows the truth is intermediate, with substantial carbanionic character at carbon in most examples.
