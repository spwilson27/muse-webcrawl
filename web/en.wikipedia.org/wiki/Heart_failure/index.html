Heart failure

Heart failure (HF), also known historically as congestive heart failure, is a clinical syndrome in which the heart is unable to pump blood at a rate sufficient to meet the body's metabolic needs, or can do so only at elevated filling pressures. It is caused by structural or functional abnormalities of the heart. Common symptoms include shortness of breath (especially on exertion or lying flat), fatigue, and ankle swelling; signs include raised jugular venous pressure, lung crackles and peripheral oedema.

Causes and classification

Leading causes are coronary artery disease (including prior heart attack), high blood pressure, atrial fibrillation, valvular heart disease, excessive alcohol use, infection (myocarditis), and cardiomyopathies of known and unknown cause. Heart failure is classified by left ventricular ejection fraction: reduced (HFrEF, ≤40%), mildly reduced (41–49%), and preserved (HFpEF, ≥50%), the last driven mainly by impaired relaxation and stiffness, commonly in older patients with hypertension, obesity and diabetes. Functional severity is graded by the New York Heart Association (NYHA) classes I–IV.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on history, examination, electrocardiography, chest imaging and blood tests — notably natriuretic peptides (BNP or NT-proBNP), whose normal levels make heart failure unlikely. Echocardiography is the key investigation, defining ejection fraction, chamber sizes and valve function, and guiding the search for a cause.

Management

Chronic HFrEF is treated with four prognostic drug classes — angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors (or ACE inhibitors), beta blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and SGLT2 inhibitors — plus diuretics for congestion. Selected patients benefit from implantable defibrillators, cardiac resynchronisation therapy, revascularisation, or, in advanced disease, mechanical circulatory support and heart transplantation. Acute decompensation is managed with oxygen, intravenous diuretics and vasodilators. Lifestyle measures (salt restriction, exercise training, vaccination) and treatment of comorbidities such as iron deficiency improve outcomes.

Epidemiology and prognosis

Heart failure affects more than 64 million people worldwide, with prevalence rising steeply with age (about 1–2% of adults in developed countries, over 10% above age 70). Despite modern therapy, prognosis remains poor — roughly half of patients die within five years of diagnosis — and it is a leading cause of hospitalisation in older adults.
