Ethics of technology

The ethics of technology is a sub-field of ethics addressing the ethical questions specific to the Technology Age, when the power of technology transforms human life, society, and the environment. It overlaps with the philosophy of technology and encompasses issues arising from information technology, biotechnology, artificial intelligence, robotics, and environmental technology.

History

Concerns about technology's moral consequences are as old as the myths of Prometheus and Frankenstein, but the field took modern shape in the twentieth century. Hans Jonas, in The Imperative of Responsibility (1979), argued that modern technology's unprecedented reach — including the capacity to harm future generations and the biosphere itself — demanded a new ethics of responsibility. Jacques Ellul and Martin Heidegger offered influential critiques of technological society and of technology as a mode of revealing. Norbert Wiener, founder of cybernetics, anticipated debates about automation and machine ethics in the 1940s and 1950s.

Areas of inquiry

  • Information and computer ethics — privacy, surveillance, intellectual property, misinformation, and the digital divide.
  • Artificial intelligence ethics — bias in algorithmic decision-making, accountability for autonomous systems, machine consciousness, and the alignment of AI systems with human values.
  • Bioethics and medical technology — genetic engineering, cloning, enhancement, and end-of-life care.
  • Environmental ethics of technology — climate engineering, resource extraction, and humanity's obligations to the non-human world.
  • Professional ethics — the codes governing engineers and computing professionals, whistleblowing, and safety-critical design.

Frameworks

Scholars apply consequentialist, deontological, and virtue-ethical frameworks to technological dilemmas, along with approaches such as the precautionary principle, value-sensitive design, and responsible research and innovation. Governance debates concern how ethical norms should be embodied in law, standards, professional codes, and the design of artefacts themselves.
