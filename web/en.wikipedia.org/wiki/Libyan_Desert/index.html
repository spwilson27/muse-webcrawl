The Libyan Desert is a geographical region filling the north-eastern portion of the Sahara, spanning eastern Libya, western Egypt, and north-western Sudan. Covering an area of roughly 1.3 million square kilometres, it is among the most arid and least populated regions on Earth—so dry that parts of its interior have been compared to the surface of Mars as an analogue environment.

Geography

The desert includes several distinct landscapes. The Great Sand Sea straddles the Egyptian–Libyan border with dunes rising up to 100 metres or more. The Calanscio Sand Sea lies in eastern Libya, while gravel plains (serir) and the rocky hamada of the Libyan Plateau cover other areas. Depressions such as the Qattara Depression in Egypt—whose floor lies about 133 metres below sea level—interrupt the plateau. In the south, the landscape rises to highlands including Jebel Uweinat on the Egypt–Sudan–Libya tripoint and the Gilf Kebir plateau in Egypt.

Climate

The climate is hyper-arid. Some stations record mean annual rainfall of less than 5 mm, and rain may not fall for years at a time. Summer daytime temperatures regularly exceed 50 °C in the shade; a temperature of 58 °C recorded at El Azizia in 1922 was long cited as the world's highest, until the World Meteorological Organization invalidated the record in 2012, leaving Death Valley's 56.7 °C as the accepted extreme.

Human history

Rock art in the Gilf Kebir and Jebel Uweinat, including the famous Cave of Swimmers depicted in The English Patient, dates from a wetter early Holocene period when the region supported savanna wildlife and pastoralist communities. A chain of oases—Siwa, Bahariya, Farafra, Dakhla, Kharga, and Kufra—has sustained settlement and caravan routes across the desert. In the twentieth century the region saw exploration by figures such as Ahmed Hassanein and László Almásy, the discovery of Libyan desert glass (a natural silica glass scattered in the Great Sand Sea), and hard-fought desert campaigning during World War II.
