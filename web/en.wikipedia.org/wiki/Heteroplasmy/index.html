Heteroplasmy

Heteroplasmy is the presence of more than one type of organellar genome (mitochondrial DNA or plastid DNA) within a cell or individual. It is an important factor in considering the severity of mitochondrial diseases. Because most cells contain many hundreds of copies of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), it is possible and indeed very frequent for mitochondria to harbour several different alleles, and for individuals to have a mixture of normal and mutated mitochondrial genomes.

Contrast with homoplasmy

The opposite state, in which all copies of the organellar genome in a cell or individual are identical, is called homoplasmy. A cell may be homoplasmic for the wild-type sequence or homoplasmic for a mutant sequence.

Origins

Heteroplasmy can arise through mutations occurring in a single organelle lineage, through the inheritance of a mixture of genomes from the mother (mtDNA is almost always maternally inherited), or through paternal leakage. Because organelles divide and are partitioned somewhat randomly at cell division — a process known as replicative segregation — the proportion of mutant genomes can drift between tissues and across generations, changing rapidly through the germline "bottleneck" in oogenesis.

Clinical significance

Many mitochondrial disorders, such as MELAS and Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy, are caused by mtDNA mutations that may be heteroplasmic. The phenotype typically appears only when the proportion of mutant mtDNA exceeds a tissue-specific threshold, often 60–90%. The level of heteroplasmy in accessible tissues such as blood may not reflect levels in affected organs, complicating diagnosis and genetic counselling.

Detection

Heteroplasmy can be detected by sequencing methods, including Sanger sequencing (sensitive to roughly 15–20% mutant load), pyrosequencing, and deep next-generation sequencing, which can detect mutant fractions below 1%.
