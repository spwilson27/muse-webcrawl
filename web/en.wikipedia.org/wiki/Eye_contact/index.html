Eye contact

Eye contact occurs when two people or animals look at each other's eyes at the same time. In people, eye contact is a form of nonverbal communication and can have a large influence on social behaviour. Coined in the early to mid-1960s, the term emerged from the West to often define the act as a meaningful and important sign of confidence and respect.

Mutual gaze is a powerful channel of social information.

Functions

Eye contact regulates conversation — speakers look away when planning utterances and listeners signal attention with more sustained gaze; it communicates emotion and interest, establishes intimacy or challenge, and helps coordinate joint attention, a developmental milestone in infants. Sustained direct gaze can be read as confidence and honesty in many Western settings, while gaze aversion may signal deference, embarrassment or disengagement depending on context. Physiologically, mutual gaze is arousing and is processed by dedicated social-perception circuitry in the brain.

Cultural variation

Norms differ sharply across cultures. In many East Asian, African, Latin American and Indigenous cultures, avoiding direct eye contact with elders or authority figures is a sign of respect, whereas in much of North America and northern Europe it may be misread as evasiveness. Gender, status and relationship all modulate what counts as appropriate gaze.

Clinical and developmental aspects

Reduced eye contact is a well-known feature of autism spectrum conditions and of social anxiety, and excessive unbroken staring is associated with threat or, in clinical settings, certain neurological conditions. Eye contact between parent and infant supports bonding and the development of social cognition.
