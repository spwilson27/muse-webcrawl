Hákarl (an abbreviation of the Icelandic kæstur hákarl, "treated shark") is a national dish of Iceland consisting of Greenland shark or other sleeper shark that has been cured with a particular fermentation process and hung to dry for four to five months. It has a strong ammonia-rich smell and fishy taste, making it an acquired taste.

Preparation

Fresh Greenland shark meat is poisonous due to its high content of urea and trimethylamine oxide, which it uses for buoyancy and osmoregulation in deep, cold water. To make it safe, the shark is beheaded and gutted, placed in a shallow hole dug in gravelly sand, and pressed with stones so the poisonous fluids drain away. The shark ferments there for six to twelve weeks, depending on the season. It is then cut into strips and hung to dry for several more months, during which a brown crust forms that is removed before serving.

Consumption

Hákarl is traditionally eaten as part of the þorramatur, a selection of traditional Icelandic foods served at the midwinter festival of Þorrablót. It is usually cut into cubes, speared on toothpicks, and often washed down with a shot of the Icelandic schnapps Brennivín. First-time tasters are frequently advised to pinch their noses, as the smell is considered far stronger than the taste. Two varieties exist: the chewy, reddish glerhákarl from the belly and the soft, white skyrhákarl from the body.

Reactions

The dish is notorious among foreign visitors: chef Anthony Bourdain described it as "the single worst, most disgusting and terrible tasting thing" he had ever eaten, and it routinely appears on lists of the world's most challenging foods. Within Iceland it remains a potent symbol of traditional subsistence culture dating to the Viking Age settlement.
