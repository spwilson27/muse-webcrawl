Adiabene

Adiabene (Greek: Ἀδιαβηνή, Adiabēnē; Syriac: ܚܕܝܐܒ, Ḥaḏy'aḇ) was an ancient kingdom in northern Mesopotamia, corresponding to the region around modern Erbil in northern Iraq, between the Upper Zab and Lower Zab rivers. Its capital was Arbela (modern Erbil). For much of its history it was a vassal kingdom within the Parthian Empire, though its rulers periodically asserted independence or shifted allegiance toward Rome.

History

Adiabene is best known for the conversion of its royal house to Judaism in the 1st century AD. Queen Helena of Adiabene and her sons Izates II and Monobaz II adopted Judaism; Helena became famous in Jerusalem for her generosity during a famine and for the palaces and tombs she built there — the complex known today as the Tombs of the Kings. Members of the Adiabenian royal family fought alongside the Jews in the First Jewish–Roman War. The kingdom was invaded by the Roman emperor Trajan in 116 AD and briefly organised as part of the province of Assyria. Later, Adiabene became a centre of Syriac Christianity; under the Sasanians the region formed the province of Nod-Ardashirakan, and its church became an important part of the Church of the East.
