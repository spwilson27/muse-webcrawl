Multinational state

Multinational state
DefinitionA sovereign state comprising two or more nations
ContrastNation state
Historical examplesOttoman Empire; Habsburg monarchy; Soviet Union; Yugoslavia

A multinational state is a sovereign state that comprises two or more nations — communities with their own distinct identities, cultures, languages and often homelands — within a single political structure. It stands in contrast to the nation state, in which the boundaries of the state and the nation roughly coincide. Multinational states may arise through the union of previously separate polities, imperial expansion, or federation, and they manage their diversity through arrangements such as federalism, devolution, official multilingualism and power-sharing.

Examples

The great land empires of the past — the Ottoman Empire and the Habsburg monarchy — governed dozens of peoples, and the 20th-century Soviet Union and Yugoslavia organised themselves, formally, as federations of national republics; both ultimately dissolved along national lines in 1991–92. Among present-day states, the United Kingdom is commonly described as a union of four nations (England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland); Canada recognises the Québécois as a nation within a united Canada; Belgium is divided between Dutch-speaking Flanders and French-speaking Wallonia; and Spain's constitution proclaims the "indissoluble unity of the Spanish Nation" while recognising "nationalities" such as Catalonia and the Basque Country. India, home to many large linguistic nations organised as states, is frequently analysed in similar terms.

Political questions

Multinational states face characteristic tensions: demands for autonomy or independence (as in Scotland's 2014 referendum or Catalonia's 2017 crisis), disputes over language rights and fiscal transfers, and debates over whether loyalty can be built on a shared "state" identity alone. Political theorists such as Will Kymlicka have argued that such states can be stable and just if they grant genuine self-government and recognition to their constituent nations — a position known as multinational federalism — while critics warn that institutionalising national difference can entrench separatism.
