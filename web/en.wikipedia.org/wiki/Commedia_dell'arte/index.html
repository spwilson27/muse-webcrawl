Commedia dell'arte

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A commedia troupe in a town square: Harlequin in his diamond patches mid-leap, Pantalone counting coins, the Innamorata swooning theatrically]

Commedia dell'arte was an early form of professional theatre, originating in Italy, that was popular throughout Europe between the 16th and 18th centuries. Troupes of (traditionally ten) actors performed improvised comedies from a scenario outline (canovaccio), each actor owning a masked stock character for life: the miserly Venetian Pantalone, the pedantic Dottore, the braggart Capitano, the wily servants (zanni) — Arlecchino (Harlequin) chief among them — and the unmasked young lovers (innamorati).

Its set pieces (lazzi — rehearsed comic business) and improvisation within fixed types made the commedia theatre's first professional, touring, actress-employing form, and its DNA runs into Molière, the English harlequinade and pantomime, Punch and Judy, Chaplin and modern improv comedy.

The masks

The commedia crystallised Italy's social types into mask and dialect: Pantalone's Venetian greed, the Dottore's Bolognese Latin-spouting ignorance, the Capitano's Spanish swagger over cowardice, Pulcinella's Neapolitan survivalism. Companies (the Gelosi, the Confidenti) played courts and piazzas across Europe — Isabella Andreini was the age's international star — carrying Italian theatre's craft as far as Paris's Hôtel de Bourgogne and the Théâtre Italien.

Decline and survival

As written comedy (Goldoni, who reformed the form by scripting it) rose, the improvised commedia faded by 1800, but its characters escaped into permanence: Harlequin and Columbine into ballet and pantomime, Punch into the seaside booth, the zanni's timing into every clown since. Twentieth-century practitioners (from the Piccolo Teatro's Arlecchino, 1947–, to Dario Fo and Lecoq's school) revived the masks as actor-training scripture: the body first, the text second.
