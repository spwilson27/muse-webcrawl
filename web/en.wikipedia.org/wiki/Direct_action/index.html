Direct action

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Direct action is a form of activism in which participants act themselves to achieve their goals, rather than petitioning authorities or intermediaries to act for them. It includes strikes, workplace occupations, boycotts, blockades, sabotage, civil disobedience, hacktivism and the direct provision of services (such as mutual-aid clinics or food distribution), and may be violent or nonviolent.

The term was popularised by anarcho-syndicalists and the French CGT in the early twentieth century, in contrast to “political action” through elections; Voltairine de Cleyre's essay Direct Action (1912) gave it a classic defence — every person who ever changed anything, she argued, acted directly. The method was central to the labour, civil rights, anti-nuclear, environmental and global-justice movements, from sit-ins and Freedom Rides to Earth First! and Extinction Rebellion blockades.

Debates

Supporters argue direct action empowers participants, dramatises injustice and wins concessions that lobbying cannot; critics argue it can alienate publics, invite repression, or substitute gesture for organisation, and debate persists within movements over property damage and violence. In industrial law, “direct action” is also a technical term for strikes and lockouts as opposed to legal or political remedies.
