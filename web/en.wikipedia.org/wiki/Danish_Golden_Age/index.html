Danish Golden Age

The Danish Golden Age (Danish: Den danske guldalder) covers roughly the first half of the 19th century, covering approximately 1800 to 1850, when Copenhagen — despite bombardment, bankruptcy, and political turmoil — produced an extraordinary flowering of literature, philosophy, science, and the visual arts.

Principal figures

In literature, the period is defined by Hans Christian Andersen's fairy tales, the philosophical writings of Søren Kierkegaard, and the romantic drama and poetry of Adam Oehlenschläger. In science, Hans Christian Ørsted discovered electromagnetism in 1820. In painting, Christoffer Wilhelm Eckersberg — the "father of Danish painting" — trained a generation including Christen Købke, Martinus Rørbye, and Constantin Hansen, whose clear-lit landscapes and portraits gave Danish art its distinctive character. Sculptor Bertel Thorvaldsen achieved European fame from Rome, architect Michael Gottlieb Bindesbøll designed his museum in Copenhagen, and Niels Wilhelm Gade led the era's music.

Context

The Golden Age unfolded against national adversity: the British attacks on Copenhagen (1801 and 1807), the state bankruptcy of 1813, and the loss of Norway in 1814 shrank Denmark, prompting a turn inward toward cultural and national identity that culminated in the National Liberal movement and the constitution of 1849.
