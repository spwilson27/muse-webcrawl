Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina (c. 1525 – 2 February 1594) was an Italian composer of late Renaissance music. The central representative of the Roman School, with Orlande de Lassus and Tomás Luis de Victoria, he is among the most important composers of 16th-century European music, and his smooth, refined style of polyphony became the model taught in counterpoint classes for centuries.

Life

Palestrina was born in the town of Palestrina, near Rome, from which he took his name. A choirboy at Santa Maria Maggiore, he became organist at Palestrina cathedral in 1544 and in 1551 was appointed maestro di cappella of the Cappella Giulia at St Peter's in Rome. He subsequently served at St John Lateran and Santa Maria Maggiore. He published his first book of Masses in 1554 and composed over 100 Masses and hundreds of motets, madrigals and other works.

Music and the legend

His Missa Papae Marcelli (Pope Marcellus Mass, c. 1562) is his most famous work. A persistent legend holds that it was composed to persuade the Council of Trent not to ban polyphonic music from the liturgy, though historians now treat the story with caution. His music is characterised by careful control of dissonance, smooth melodic lines, and clarity of text setting.

Legacy

The "Palestrina style" was codified by Johann Joseph Fux in Gradus ad Parnassum (1725) and remained the foundation of species counterpoint instruction. Composers from Bach to the present have studied his works as the epitome of Renaissance polyphony.
