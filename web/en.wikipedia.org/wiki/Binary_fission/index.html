Binary fission

Stages of binary fission in a rod-shaped bacterium.

Binary fission ("division in half") is a kind of asexual reproduction and cell division used by all prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea) and by some organelles within eukaryotic organisms (such as mitochondria and chloroplasts). This process results in the reproduction of a living prokaryotic cell (or organelle) by dividing the cell into two parts, each with the potential to grow to the size of the original.

Process

Fission of a prokaryote begins with replication of the single circular chromosome, starting at the origin of replication. The two copies attach to the cell membrane and move apart as the cell elongates. A ring of FtsZ protein assembles at the future division site and directs construction of a septum of new cell wall material, which grows inward until the cell pinches into two genetically identical daughter cells. Under favourable conditions Escherichia coli can complete this cycle about every 20 minutes.

Types

Binary fission may be irregular (as in amoeba, which can divide in any plane), longitudinal (in flagellates such as Euglena), transverse (in ciliates such as Paramecium) or oblique. Some bacteria depart from the simple pattern, producing multiple offspring (multiple fission in some cyanobacteria) or reproducing by budding.

Fission of organelles

Mitochondria and plastids divide by fission, reflecting their origin as endosymbiotic bacteria; in many eukaryotes their division is now controlled by host-cell machinery as well as bacterial-derived proteins.
