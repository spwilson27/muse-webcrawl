Climatology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Climatology (from Greek κλίμα, klima, "slope, zone" and -λογία, -logia) is the scientific study of climate, defined as weather patterns averaged over a period of at least 30 years. It is a branch of the atmospheric sciences and a subfield of physical geography.

Approaches

Climatologists work in several overlapping areas. Descriptive climatology summarises observations and classifies climates, most famously in the Köppen–Geiger system of Wladimir Köppen. Physical climatology examines the energy and water budgets that produce climates. Synoptic climatology relates weather patterns to circulation types, while paleoclimatology reconstructs past climates from proxy evidence such as ice cores and tree rings. Applied climatology serves agriculture, engineering, health, and planning.

Climatology and meteorology

Whereas meteorology focuses on short-term weather systems lasting up to a few weeks, climatology studies the frequency, distribution, and long-term behaviour of those systems. Modern climatology also studies climate variability (such as the El Niño–Southern Oscillation) and anthropogenic climate change, and it depends heavily on global observations and computer modelling.

History

Systematic climate description dates to the nineteenth century, when networks of weather stations made regional averages possible. Alexander Buchan, Julius von Hann, and Köppen established the discipline's statistical foundations; the twentieth century added upper-air observation, satellites, and numerical models.
