Points of sail

The points of sail are the directions a sailing vessel can travel in relation to the direction of the true wind. Because a sailboat cannot sail directly into the wind, the points of sail describe a series of sectors around the wind's direction, each calling for a different trim of the sails and producing different speeds and handling qualities.

The points

In irons (into the wind): Within roughly 45 degrees of the direction from which the wind blows, a conventional sailboat cannot make progress; the sails luff and the boat stalls. This is the "no-go zone".

Close haul: Sailing as close to the wind as possible, with sails trimmed in tight — about 45 degrees off the wind for most modern fore-and-aft rigged boats.

Close reach: Between close haul and a beam reach, with the wind forward of the beam.

Beam reach: The wind comes directly over the side (beam) of the boat, at about 90 degrees to the course; often the fastest point of sail.

Broad reach: The wind comes from behind the beam but not directly astern.

Running (downwind): Sailing directly away from the wind, with the sails let far out; a spinnaker is often set, and the jib may be held out on the opposite side ("wing on wing") to catch the wind.

Apparent wind

The wind felt aboard a moving boat is the apparent wind, the vector sum of the true wind and the wind created by the boat's own motion. As a boat accelerates on a reach, the apparent wind swings forward, which is why fast craft such as ice yachts and foiling multihulls can sail faster than the true wind speed and trim their sails nearly as if close-hauled even when sailing downwind.
