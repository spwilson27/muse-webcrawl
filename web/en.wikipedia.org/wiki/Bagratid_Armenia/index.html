Bagratid Armenia (Armenian: Բագրատունիների թագավորություն) was an independent Armenian kingdom from the late 9th century to 1045, ruled by the Bagratuni dynasty. It emerged as Arab caliphal power waned and both the Abbasid Caliphate and the Byzantine Empire recognised Ashot I as king of Armenia. The kingdom is remembered for a flourishing of trade, architecture and culture, centred on its later capital, Ani, the "city of a thousand and one churches".

Foundations

The Bagratuni family had risen under Arab rule as presiding princes (ishkhans) of Armenia. In 884 or 885 Ashot I was crowned king, receiving crowns from both the Abbasid caliph and (in 886) the Byzantine emperor Basil I. His successors Smbat I and Ashot II "the Iron" defended the kingdom against Arab emirates and secured its frontiers; Ashot II obtained the title "King of Kings" (shahanshah) from Baghdad in 922.

The age of Ani

In 961 Ashot III moved the capital to Ani, which grew into one of the largest cities of the medieval world, rivalling Constantinople and Baghdad in wealth. Under Smbat II and Gagik I (990–1020) the kingdom reached its greatest extent and prosperity. Master builders such as the architect Trdat — who also repaired the dome of Hagia Sophia — raised the Cathedral of Ani, and churches at Marmashen, Amberd and elsewhere displayed a distinctive Armenian architecture that influenced the region.

Fragmentation and fall

From the late 10th century the kingdom fragmented as cadet branches and noble houses established semi-independent kingdoms — Kars, Lori (Tashir-Dzoraget), Syunik and Vaspurakan — weakening central authority. Byzantine pressure intensified under Basil II and his successors; in 1045 the emperor Constantine IX annexed Ani and ended Bagratid rule in Armenia proper, shortly before Seljuk Turkish incursions transformed the region. Bagratid offshoots endured in Georgia, where the cognate Bagrationi dynasty ruled for centuries.

Culture

The period saw a revival of Armenian letters: chroniclers such as Stephen of Taron (Asoghik) wrote universal histories, Catholicos Khosrov Andzevatsi commented on liturgy, and illuminated manuscripts and khachkar carving flourished. Ani's position on trade routes between Persia, the Black Sea and the Mediterranean made the kingdom prosperous and cosmopolitan.
