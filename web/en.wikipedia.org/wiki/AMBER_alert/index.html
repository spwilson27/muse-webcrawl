An AMBER Alert (also styled Amber Alert) is a child abduction emergency alert system that asks the public for help in finding abducted children. It originated in the United States in 1996 following the kidnapping and murder of nine-year-old Amber Hagerman in Arlington, Texas; "AMBER" was later given the backronym "America's Missing: Broadcast Emergency Response".

Operation

Law enforcement issues an alert when a case meets criteria such as: there is reasonable belief an abduction has occurred, the child is 17 or younger, the child is believed to be in imminent danger of serious bodily injury or death, and there is enough descriptive information about the child, abductor or vehicle. Alerts are distributed through the Emergency Alert System, radio and television broadcasts, electronic highway signs, lottery terminals, and as Wireless Emergency Alerts pushed to mobile phones.

Reach and results

All 50 U.S. states operate plans, and similar systems exist in Canada, Mexico, Australia and several European countries. The U.S. Department of Justice credits the system with the successful recovery of well over a thousand children as of the 2020s. The federal PROTECT Act of 2003 established a national Amber Alert coordinator.

Criticism

Researchers have questioned how many recoveries are directly caused by alerts, noted that many alerts involve family (custodial) abductions rather than stereotypical stranger kidnappings, and warned about alert fatigue and the anxiety caused by late-night phone alerts. Criteria restrict issuance in part to keep the alerts rare and effective.
