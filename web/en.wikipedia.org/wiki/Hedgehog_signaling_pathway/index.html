Hedgehog signaling pathway

The Hedgehog signaling pathway is a key regulator of animal development that transmits information to embryonic cells about cell fate and patterning.

The Hedgehog signaling pathway is one of the key signalling pathways regulating animal development, controlling cell proliferation, differentiation, and the patterning of tissues during embryogenesis. It is named after the hedgehog (hh) gene first identified in the fruit fly Drosophila — larvae lacking it were covered in bristles, resembling a hedgehog — in the classic Heidelberg screens of Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard and Eric Wieschaus.

Mechanism

In vertebrates there are three Hedgehog ligands — Sonic hedgehog (Shh), Indian hedgehog (Ihh) and Desert hedgehog (Dhh). In the absence of ligand, the receptor Patched (PTCH1) inhibits the transducer Smoothened (SMO), and Gli transcription factors are processed into repressor forms. Binding of Hedgehog to Patched lifts the inhibition; Smoothened accumulates in the primary cilium and Gli activators enter the nucleus to switch on target genes. Sonic hedgehog, secreted by the notochord and floor plate, patterns the ventral neural tube and limb buds through a concentration gradient.

Medical significance

Pathway disruption causes birth defects such as holoprosencephaly and cyclopia (the latter long produced in lambs by the plant alkaloid cyclopamine, later shown to block Smoothened). Inappropriate activation in adults drives cancers including basal cell carcinoma and medulloblastoma; Smoothened inhibitors such as vismodegib are approved treatments for advanced basal cell carcinoma.
