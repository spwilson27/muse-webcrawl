Laryngoscopy

Laryngoscopy is an endoscopic examination of the larynx (voice box). It allows a clinician to view the vocal cords and the glottis, and is performed to diagnose disorders of the voice and throat, to assist in tracheal intubation, and to guide certain surgical procedures.

Types

Laryngoscopy may be direct or indirect. Indirect laryngoscopy uses a small mirror held at the back of the throat, or a flexible fibre-optic nasendoscope passed through the nose. Direct laryngoscopy uses a rigid instrument called a laryngoscope, usually under general anaesthesia, giving a straight line of sight to the larynx. Videolaryngoscopy, in which a camera at the blade tip displays the view on a screen, has become common in anaesthesia.

History

The Spanish singing teacher Manuel García is credited with the first indirect laryngoscopy in 1854, using a dental mirror and sunlight to view his own vocal cords. The direct laryngoscope was developed in the late 19th and early 20th centuries by physicians such as Gustav Killian and Chevalier Jackson, and the curved and straight blades of Macintosh and Miller (1940s) remain standard in intubation.

Clinical uses and risks

Laryngoscopy is used to investigate hoarseness, chronic cough, throat pain, difficulty swallowing, and suspected tumours, and to obtain biopsies or remove foreign bodies. Risks include sore throat, dental damage, bleeding, and, rarely, injury to the vocal cords. During intubation, laryngoscopy provides the view needed to pass an endotracheal tube between the vocal cords.
