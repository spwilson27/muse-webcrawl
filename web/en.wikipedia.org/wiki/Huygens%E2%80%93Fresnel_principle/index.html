Huygens–Fresnel principle

Huygens–Fresnel principle
Field: Wave optics
Proposed: Christiaan Huygens (1678); extended by Augustin-Jean Fresnel (1816)
Describes: Wave propagation, diffraction
Successor theory: Kirchhoff diffraction theory, quantum electrodynamics

The Huygens–Fresnel principle (named after Dutch physicist Christiaan Huygens and French physicist Augustin-Jean Fresnel) states that every point on a wavefront is itself the source of spherical wavelets, and the secondary wavelets mutually interfere; the new wavefront is the envelope (tangent surface) of these wavelets.

History

Huygens proposed the wavelet construction in his Treatise on Light (1690, written 1678) to explain reflection and refraction, but could not account for diffraction. Fresnel combined Huygens' principle with interference, calculating diffraction patterns quantitatively and winning the Paris Academy's prize in 1819 — famously confirmed by the Poisson/Arago spot. Gustav Kirchhoff later gave the principle a firmer mathematical basis.

Applications

The principle explains straight-line propagation, bending of waves around obstacles and through apertures, and underlies antenna theory and seismology. In modern physics it can be viewed as a precursor to Feynman's path-integral picture, in which a wave explores all paths.
