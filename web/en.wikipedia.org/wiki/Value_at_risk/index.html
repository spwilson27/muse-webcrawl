Value at risk (VaR) is a measure of the risk of loss of investment, widely used in financial risk management. It estimates how much a portfolio could lose, with a given probability, over a specified time horizon under normal market conditions. For example, a one-day 99% VaR of $1 million means that there is a 1% chance the portfolio will lose more than $1 million on a given day. VaR is a single-number summary of downside risk, expressed either in currency units or as a percentage.

Calculation methods

Three main approaches are used. Historical simulation applies past market movements to the current portfolio. The variance–covariance (parametric) method assumes normally distributed returns and uses volatilities and correlations. Monte Carlo simulation generates large numbers of hypothetical market scenarios. VaR became an industry standard in the 1990s, popularised by J.P. Morgan's RiskMetrics system, and was adopted by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision as the basis for banks' market-risk capital requirements (later Basel standards add stressed VaR).

Criticisms

VaR says nothing about how bad losses beyond the threshold can be, and it is not subadditive — diversified portfolios can show higher VaR than their components — so it is not a "coherent" risk measure in the sense of Artzner et al. Expected shortfall, which averages losses beyond the VaR level, remedies both defects and has displaced VaR in the Basel market-risk framework (FRTB). Critics also blamed VaR-based risk management for encouraging dangerous concentrations in seemingly low-risk assets before the 2008 financial crisis, a charge debated among practitioners and regulators.

Backtesting

Regulators require banks to backtest VaR models by counting "exceptions" — days on which actual losses exceeded VaR. Too many exceptions indicate an understated risk model and attract capital penalties (the "traffic light" approach under Basel rules).
