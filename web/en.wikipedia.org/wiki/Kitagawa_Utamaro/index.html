Kitagawa Utamaro (Japanese: 喜多川 歌麿; c. 1753 – 31 October 1806) was a Japanese artist of the Edo period, regarded as one of the greatest masters of ukiyo-e woodblock prints. He is best known for his bijin ōkubi-e — large-headed portraits of beautiful women — produced in the 1790s for the publisher Tsutaya Jūzaburō, which transformed the depiction of women in Japanese art.

Career

Utamaro studied under Toriyama Sekien and first worked illustrating books. His early print designs included actor portraits and scenes of the Yoshiwara pleasure district. In the early 1790s he began the close-cropped ōkubi-e portraits of courtesans and townswomen that made his reputation, using delicate line, mica-dusted backgrounds and an unsentimental observation of gesture and mood. He also produced celebrated nature studies, including illustrated books of insects, birds and shellfish.

In 1804 Utamaro was arrested and manacled for making prints that satirised the shogun's military ruler through depictions of the 16th-century warlord Toyotomi Hideyoshi. The punishment broke his health and spirit, and he died two years later in Edo.

Legacy

Utamaro's work was among the ukiyo-e most admired in the West during the Japonisme craze of the late 19th century, influencing artists such as Toulouse-Lautrec, and his prints remain among the most valuable of the school.
