Hambach Festival

The Hambach Festival (German: Hambacher Fest) was a mass political demonstration held at Hambach Castle, near Neustadt an der Weinstraße in the Palatinate, from 27 to 30 May 1832. Publicly framed as a county fair (Volksfest) to evade the ban on political assemblies, it drew between 20,000 and 30,000 participants from across the German states — and delegations from Poland and France — and became the largest demonstration of the German Vormärz era in favour of national unity, democracy and civil rights.

Background

After the Napoleonic Wars, the German Confederation created by the Congress of Vienna frustrated hopes for a united, liberal Germany; the Carlsbad Decrees of 1819 imposed censorship and surveillance of universities and the press. The Palatinate, ruled by Bavaria but retaining liberal French-era laws including the Code civil, was especially restive, and heavy taxes and a poor harvest in 1831 heightened discontent. Journalists Johann Georg August Wirth and Philipp Jakob Siebenpfeiffer, founders of the banned Press Association (Preßverein), issued the invitation to a "festival" at the ruined hilltop castle of Hambach.

The festival

On 27 May 1832 a long procession climbed to the castle carrying the black-red-gold flag — the colours of the Lützow Free Corps, later the flag of German unity. Speakers including Siebenpfeiffer and Wirth demanded a united Germany organised as a democratic republic, freedom of the press, popular sovereignty and solidarity with other peoples struggling for freedom, in particular the Poles after their failed November Uprising against Russia. Polish and French flags flew alongside German ones, and the festival adopted a resolution envisioning a "confederated republican Europe". Women, unusually for the time, participated in large numbers.

Aftermath and significance

The authorities responded with repression: the Bundestag of the German Confederation adopted the Six Articles (and later the Ten Articles), tightening censorship and banning political associations and assemblies; troops occupied the Palatinate, and leading participants were arrested, tried or driven into exile. Despite its failure, the Hambach Festival is remembered as a founding moment of German democracy — the first time a mass movement publicly demanded a united democratic state — and Hambach Castle today houses a permanent exhibition on its history and serves as a venue for democratic commemorations.
