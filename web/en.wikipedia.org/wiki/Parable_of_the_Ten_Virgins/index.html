The Parable of the Ten Virgins, also known as the Parable of the Wise and Foolish Virgins, is one of the parables of Jesus. It appears in Matthew 25:1–13, within the Olivet Discourse, and is unique to Matthew's Gospel. It is among the best-known parables and a frequent subject of art, drama and hymnody.

The parable

Ten virgins take their lamps and go out to meet the bridegroom. Five are wise and bring extra oil in flasks; five are foolish and bring none. The bridegroom is delayed, and all ten fall asleep. At midnight a cry announces his arrival. The wise virgins trim their lamps and go in with him to the wedding feast, and the door is shut. The foolish virgins, who have gone to buy oil, return too late and are turned away with the words "Truly, I say to you, I do not know you." Jesus concludes: "Watch therefore, for you know neither the day nor the hour."

Interpretation

In Christian interpretation the bridegroom is Christ and his delayed arrival represents the interval before the Second Coming; the parable exhorts believers to constant spiritual readiness, since the moment of judgement cannot be predicted and cannot be remedied once it arrives. The oil has been read allegorically as faith, good works, or the Holy Spirit — interpretations that divided commentators from the early Church onward. Modern scholars place the parable among the eschatological teachings emphasising vigilance, alongside the Parable of the Faithful Servant and the Parable of the Talents, which follows it in Matthew 25.

In art and culture

The wise and foolish virgins were a favourite subject of medieval sculpture, appearing on the portals of cathedrals such as Strasbourg, Magdeburg and Basel, where the smiling, lamp-bearing wise virgins contrast with the downcast foolish ones whose lamps are overturned. The parable also generated the Advent hymn "Wake, Awake, for Night Is Flying" (Wachet auf) by Philipp Nicolai (1599), later harmonised by J. S. Bach, and inspired works by painters including William Blake.
