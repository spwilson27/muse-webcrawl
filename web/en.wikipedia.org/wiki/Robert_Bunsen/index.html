Robert Wilhelm Eberhard Bunsen (30 March 1811 – 16 August 1899) was a German chemist. With Gustav Kirchhoff he founded the science of spectrum analysis, with which the two discovered the elements caesium (1860) and rubidium (1861) in mineral waters. He perfected the laboratory gas burner that bears his name, developed methods of gas analysis, pioneered photochemistry with Henry Enfield Roscoe, and did early work in organoarsenic chemistry that cost him the sight of one eye.

Life and career

Bunsen was born in Göttingen, where his father was a professor, and took his doctorate there in 1831. After travels and posts at Kassel, Marburg and Breslau, he became professor at the University of Heidelberg in 1852, remaining there until his retirement in 1889. His Heidelberg laboratory attracted students from across Europe; among those he trained or influenced were Dmitri Mendeleev, Lothar Meyer and Adolf von Baeyer. He never married and was famous for his devotion to laboratory work and his indifference to honours.

Scientific work

Early research

Bunsen's investigation of cacodyl compounds — foul, spontaneously inflammable organoarsenic substances — nearly killed him by arsenic poisoning, and an explosion of cacodyl cyanide destroyed the sight of his right eye in 1843. He then turned to the analysis of blast-furnace gases, showing how much fuel was wasted, and to photochemistry: with Roscoe he developed the Bunsen–Roscoe actinometer and established the law of reciprocity for photographic exposure.

The burner and spectrum analysis

Around 1855 Bunsen, with his mechanic Peter Desaga, developed a premixed gas burner producing a hot, clean, nearly colourless flame — the Bunsen burner — ideal for heating and for flame tests. In 1859 he and Kirchhoff built the first spectroscope, demonstrating that each element emits a characteristic spectrum. Applying it to Dürkheim mineral water, they announced caesium in 1860 (named for its blue lines) and rubidium in 1861 (red lines). Spectrum analysis soon revealed elements on Earth and, from sunlight, in the stars.

Other contributions

Bunsen invented the carbon–zinc Bunsen cell (1841), the grease-spot photometer, the ice calorimeter (1870), and methods to measure the speed of explosions and the heating power of fuels. He also worked out the composition of Icelandic geyser waters during an 1846 expedition.

Legacy

The Bunsen burner remains a fixture of school and university laboratories, and the spectroscope he and Kirchhoff devised underlies modern analytical chemistry and astrophysics. The lunar crater Bunsen and the asteroid 10361 Bunsen are named for him.
