Demurrage (currency)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. For the shipping term, see Demurrage.

Demurrage is a cost imposed on the holder of a currency over time — in effect, a carrying cost or negative interest rate on money. The concept was central to the monetary theory of the German economist Silvio Gesell, who proposed "free money" (Freigeld) that would lose value at a fixed rate unless the holder affixed stamps to the notes to maintain their face value. Because holding money becomes costly, demurrage discourages hoarding and encourages spending and lending, increasing the velocity of money.

Gesell's proposal

In his book The Natural Economic Order (1916), Gesell argued that conventional money enjoys an unfair advantage over goods: goods rot, rust, and cost money to store, whereas money can be withheld from the market at no cost, allowing its holders to demand interest. Demurrage would equalise the carrying costs, removing what Gesell saw as the basic cause of interest, unemployment, and economic crises. He proposed currency that depreciated by about 5.2% per year, maintained by weekly stamps of 0.1% of face value.

Experiments

The most famous experiment took place in the Austrian town of Wörgl in 1932–33, during the Great Depression. The mayor, Michael Unterguggenberger, issued local stamp scrip with a monthly demurrage of 1%. The scrip circulated rapidly, funded public works, and sharply reduced local unemployment, until the Austrian National Bank, defending its issuing monopoly, had the experiment stopped in 1933. Similar stamp-scrip schemes appeared in Germany (the Wära) and in hundreds of American communities during the Depression. The economist Irving Fisher promoted stamp scrip in his 1933 book Stamp Scrip, and John Maynard Keynes discussed Gesell's ideas with qualified sympathy in The General Theory.

Modern relevance

Demurrage is closely related to negative interest rates on bank reserves and deposits, implemented by several central banks after 2008. It also features in some local and complementary currencies — the German Chiemgauer applies a demurrage fee — and in proposals for expiring stimulus payments and digital currencies designed to lose value if unspent.
