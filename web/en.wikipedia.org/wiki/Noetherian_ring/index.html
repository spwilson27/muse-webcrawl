Noetherian ring

In mathematics, a Noetherian ring is a ring that satisfies the ascending chain condition on ideals: every increasing sequence of ideals I₁ ⊆ I₂ ⊆ I₃ ⊆ … eventually stabilises. Equivalently, every ideal of the ring is finitely generated. The concept is named after Emmy Noether, who developed its theory in the 1920s and made it a foundation of modern commutative algebra.

Characterisations

For a ring R, the following are equivalent: (1) R satisfies the ascending chain condition on ideals; (2) every ideal of R is finitely generated; (3) every non-empty set of ideals of R has a maximal element. A module with the analogous properties is called a Noetherian module.

Key theorems

The Hilbert basis theorem states that if R is Noetherian, then the polynomial ring R[x] is also Noetherian; consequently every finitely generated algebra over a field is Noetherian. The Lasker–Noether theorem says that in a Noetherian ring every ideal can be written as a finite intersection of primary ideals. Artinian rings, principal ideal domains, fields and the ring of integers are all Noetherian.

Examples and non-examples

The polynomial ring in infinitely many variables is not Noetherian, since the ideal generated by all the variables is not finitely generated. Noetherian hypotheses underlie much of algebraic geometry: coordinate rings of algebraic varieties are Noetherian, which guarantees that varieties have finitely many irreducible components.
