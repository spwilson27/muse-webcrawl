Surname

A surname, also known as a family name or last name, is the portion of a personal name that indicates a person's family, tribe or community. In most Western cultures the surname follows the given name, while in many East Asian cultures (China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam) and in Hungary it precedes it.

Origins

Hereditary surnames developed at different times around the world. In China, family names date back thousands of years. In Europe, surnames became common between the 11th and 16th centuries as populations grew and single given names no longer sufficed to distinguish individuals. English surnames typically derive from occupations (Smith, Baker), places (Hill, Ford), patronyms (Johnson, Williams), or personal characteristics (Brown, Short). Iceland still largely uses patronymics rather than hereditary family names.

Types

  • Patronymic — derived from the father's name (e.g., Ivanov, Fitzgerald).
  • Occupational — from a trade (e.g., Miller, Ferrari).
  • Toponymic — from a place (e.g., London, van der Berg).
  • Descriptive — from a nickname or trait (e.g., Armstrong, Petit).

Usage and change

In many countries a spouse traditionally adopts the other's surname on marriage, though keeping one's birth name, hyphenating, or choosing a new name are increasingly common. Legal name changes, double-barrelled names, and compound surnames (as in Spanish naming customs, where a child receives both parents' surnames) are recognised in many jurisdictions.
