History of Armenia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Mount Ararat, the national symbol of Armenia.

The history of Armenia covers the Armenian Highlands and the Armenian people from prehistory to the present republic. Armenia was the first state to adopt Christianity as its official religion, in 301 CE, and developed a distinct alphabet, literature and church while lying between the Roman/Byzantine, Persian, Arab, Mongol, Ottoman and Russian empires.

Antiquity

The Bronze Age kingdom of Urartu flourished around Lake Van in the 9th–6th centuries BCE. Under the Artaxiad dynasty, Tigranes the Great (95–55 BCE) built a short-lived empire stretching from the Caspian to the Mediterranean. Armenia then became a buffer between Rome and Parthia/Persia. King Tiridates III adopted Christianity under Gregory the Illuminator; around 405 CE Mesrop Mashtots created the Armenian alphabet, inaugurating a golden age of literature.

Middle ages

Divided between Byzantium and Sasanian Persia, much of Armenia later passed under Arab rule. The Bagratid kingdom (885–1045), with its capital at Ani, restored independence before Byzantine annexation and the Seljuk invasions. In Lesser Armenia, the Cilician kingdom (1080–1375) allied with the Crusader states. Subsequent centuries brought Mongol, Timurid, and Turkmen rule, then partition between the Ottoman Empire and Safavid Persia.

Ottoman and Russian rule; genocide

Eastern Armenia was annexed by the Russian Empire in 1828. In the Ottoman west, Armenians suffered the Hamidian massacres of the 1890s and, during the First World War, the Armenian genocide of 1915–1917, in which the Ottoman government deported and killed an estimated one million or more Armenians.

Soviet period and independence

A short-lived First Republic (1918–1920) was sovietised; the Armenian SSR formed part of the USSR from 1922. The devastating Spitak earthquake struck in 1988, the year the Karabakh movement began. Armenia declared independence in 1991 amid war with Azerbaijan over Nagorno-Karabakh, which ended in a 1994 ceasefire, renewed war in 2020, and Azerbaijan's 2023 offensive and the exodus of the region's Armenian population.
