Colin Pitchfork

Colin Pitchfork
Born: 1960, Leicestershire, England
Conviction: Murder (2 counts)
Sentence: Life imprisonment (minimum term 30 years, later reduced)
Known for: First person convicted of murder using DNA evidence

Colin Pitchfork (born 1960) is a British double murderer and rapist who was the first person in the world to be convicted of murder based on DNA fingerprinting evidence, and the first to be caught following a mass DNA screening. His case, involving the murders of two teenage girls in Leicestershire in the 1980s, is a landmark in the history of forensic science.

The murders

In November 1983, fifteen-year-old Lynda Mann was raped and murdered on a footpath in Narborough, Leicestershire. In July 1986, fifteen-year-old Dawn Ashworth was killed in similar circumstances near the neighbouring village of Enderby. Semen samples from both scenes indicated the same attacker.

Investigation and DNA breakthrough

A local youth, Richard Buckland, initially confessed to the second murder, but DNA profiling — a technique newly developed by Alec Jeffreys at the University of Leicester — proved that he was innocent and that the same man had committed both crimes. Buckland thus also became the first person exonerated by DNA evidence. Police then undertook the first mass DNA screening ("the blooding"), asking some 5,000 local men to give blood or saliva samples.

Pitchfork persuaded a colleague, Ian Kelly, to give a sample in his place. When the deception was overheard and reported, Pitchfork was arrested in September 1987; his DNA matched the crime-scene samples, and he confessed. In January 1988 he pleaded guilty to both murders and to other offences, and was sentenced to life imprisonment with a minimum term of 30 years, later reduced to 28 years on appeal.

Release controversy

Pitchfork became eligible for release after serving his minimum term, and his case generated sustained controversy, with the victims' families campaigning against his release. The Parole Board directed his release in 2021, subject to strict licence conditions; he was recalled to prison a few months later for breaching those conditions, and the lawfulness and politics of his release remained a matter of public debate in subsequent parole reviews.
