127 film and a Vest Pocket Kodak camera

127 film is a roll film format for still photography introduced by Kodak in 1912 for its Vest Pocket Kodak folding camera. The film is 46 mm wide, between 35 mm and 120 "medium format" film in size, and is carried on a spool with backing paper.

History

The format was created for the Vest Pocket Kodak, a compact folding camera marketed as "the soldier's camera" during the First World War. After the war, 127 became popular for inexpensive box and folding cameras. Originally the format produced eight exposures of 4×6.5 cm, but smaller frame sizes became common: 4×4 cm "superslide" format yielding 12 exposures, and 4×3 cm half-frame yielding 16 exposures.

Decline and revival

The rise of 35 mm film and later the Instamatic 126 cartridge eroded 127's market. Kodak discontinued its 127 colour films in the 1990s and black-and-white production ended around 1995. Despite this, the format survives in niche production: manufacturers such as Rollei and small specialty firms continue to cut and spool 127 film for enthusiasts, and the format's 4×4 cm negatives remain prized for producing "superslides" that fit in medium-format projectors.

Cameras

Notable cameras for 127 include the Vest Pocket Kodak series, the Kodak Brownie range, the Yashica 44 twin-lens reflex (4×4), and the Baby Rolleiflex. Many toy and novelty cameras, including Diana variants, also used the format.
