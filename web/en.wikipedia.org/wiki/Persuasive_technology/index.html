Persuasive technology

Persuasive technology is technology designed to change attitudes or behaviours of its users through persuasion and social influence, but not necessarily through coercion. The field was named and systematised by Stanford researcher B. J. Fogg, whose 2003 book Persuasive Technology: Using Computers to Change What We Think and Do coined the term "captology" for the study of computers as persuasive technologies.

Design strategies

Fogg described computers as filling three roles — tools, media, and social actors — and catalogued strategies such as reduction (making a task easier), tunnelling (guiding the user through a process), tailoring, suggestion, self-monitoring, surveillance, and conditioning. Persuasive design is applied in health and fitness applications, energy-conservation feedback, education, e-commerce, and workplace safety systems.

Ethics

The field raises ethical questions about manipulation, informed consent, and the asymmetry between designers and users. Critics note that the same techniques underlie dark patterns in interface design and addictive features of social media; Fogg and others have proposed ethical guidelines requiring that persuasive intent be disclosed and that designers consider whether they would want to be persuaded themselves.
