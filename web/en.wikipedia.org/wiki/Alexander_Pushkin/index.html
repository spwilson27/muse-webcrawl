Alexander Pushkin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Portrait of Alexander Pushkin (1827) by Vasily Tropinin: the poet in a loose dressing gown, quill at hand, gaze turned aside]

Alexander Sergeyevich Pushkin (6 June 1799 – 10 February 1837) was a Russian poet, playwright and novelist, widely considered Russia's greatest poet and the founder of modern Russian literature. In a short life he forged the Russian literary language — supple, ironic, exact — across lyric poetry, the novel in verse Eugene Onegin, the drama Boris Godunov, and prose tales of crystalline economy.

His works include the narrative poems Ruslan and Ludmila (1820), The Bronze Horseman (1833) and Poltava, the short story The Queen of Spades (1834), and the historical novel The Captain's Daughter (1836). Exiled for his liberal verses, harassed by court intrigue, he was killed at 37 in a duel with his brother-in-law Georges d'Anthès — a national trauma Russians still commemorate.

Life and exile

Pushkin was born in Moscow, the great-grandson of Abram Petrovich Gannibal, an African page raised at Peter the Great's court. Educated at the Imperial Lyceum at Tsarskoye Selo, he won fame while still a schoolboy. His early ode 'Liberty' and epigrams on the powerful brought exile to the Caucasus, Crimea and then his estate at Mikhailovskoye (1820–26) — his most productive years, yielding Onegin's first chapters, Boris Godunov and the southern romantic poems that made Byron's Russian reputation his own.

Later work and death

Recalled to a gilded cage under Nicholas I — who made himself Pushkin's personal censor — the poet married Natalya Goncharova, founded the journal The Contemporary, and turned to prose and history (a life of the Pugachev rebellion). Court whispers about his wife and the French officer d'Anthès drove him to the duel on the Black River, 27 January 1837 (O.S.); he died two days later.

Legacy

Every Russian writer of consequence descends from Pushkin: Gogol ('our everything'), Turgenev, Dostoevsky and Tolstoy built on his language. Onegin's stanza form is still used by poets; Tchaikovsky and Mussorgsky carried his works into opera houses worldwide.
