Karplus equation

The Karplus equation, first described by Martin Karplus in 1959, relates the three-bond (vicinal) spin–spin coupling constant 3J in nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy to the dihedral angle between the coupled nuclei. It is one of the principal tools for determining molecular conformation from NMR data, especially in peptides, proteins and carbohydrates.

3J(φ) = A cos²φ + B cosφ + C

Here φ is the dihedral angle (for example H–C–C–H), and A, B and C are empirical parameters that depend on the atoms and substituents involved. Typical parameterisations for H–N–C–H coupling in proteins use values around A ≈ 6–7 Hz, B ≈ −1.5 Hz and C ≈ 1.5–2 Hz.

Shape and interpretation

The curve has its maximum at angles of 0° and 180°, where the coupled bonds are eclipsed or anti-periplanar and overlap of the intervening bonding orbitals is greatest, and its minimum near 90°, where coupling nearly vanishes. Because the function is quadratic in cos φ, a measured coupling constant generally corresponds to several possible angles, so Karplus analysis is combined with other data such as NOEs.

Applications

In protein NMR, the 3J(HN, Hα) coupling is used to restrain the backbone φ angle in structure determination. Analogous relations exist for other nuclei and bond systems, including couplings across glycosidic bonds in carbohydrates and couplings involving phosphorus or fluorine. Electronegative substituents systematically modify the curve, a refinement treated by the generalised Karplus equations of Altona and others.
