Fermat's little theorem

Fermat's little theorem states that if p is a prime number, then for any integer a, the number ap − a is an integer multiple of p. In the notation of modular arithmetic, this is expressed as ap ≡ a (mod p).

For example, if a = 2 and p = 7, then 27 = 128, and 128 − 2 = 126 = 7 × 18, which is an integer multiple of 7. If a is not divisible by p, Fermat's little theorem is equivalent to the statement that ap−1 − 1 is an integer multiple of p: ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p).

History

Pierre de Fermat first stated the theorem in a letter dated October 18, 1640, to his friend Frénicle de Bessy, without giving a proof. The first published proof was given by Leonhard Euler in 1736; an earlier proof by Leibniz survives in an unpublished manuscript. Fermat's little theorem is distinct from his famous "Last Theorem" concerning sums of like powers.

Proofs

The theorem admits several elementary proofs. One uses mathematical induction on a together with the binomial theorem, noting that the binomial coefficients of (a + 1)p are all divisible by p. Another uses the combinatorial fact that the number of necklaces of p beads in a colours, counting rotations, must be an integer. A third proof uses group theory: the multiplicative group of integers modulo p has order p − 1, so by Lagrange's theorem every element raised to the power p − 1 equals the identity.

Generalisations

Euler's theorem generalises the result to arbitrary moduli using Euler's totient function. Carmichael's theorem gives a further refinement. Numbers that satisfy the congruence without being prime, called Fermat pseudoprimes, are exceptions used to test probabilistic primality criteria such as the Fermat primality test.

Applications

The theorem is fundamental in number theory and is widely used in RSA cryptography, primality testing, and computer algebra.
