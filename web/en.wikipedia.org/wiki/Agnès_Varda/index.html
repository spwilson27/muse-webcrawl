Agnès Varda

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Agnès Varda in her eighties, white-and-auburn bowl haircut, beaming beside a camera — the godmother of the New Wave in her late fame]

Agnès Varda (born Arlette Varda; 30 May 1928 – 29 March 2019) was a Belgian-born French film director, screenwriter and artist, whose work pioneered the French New Wave — her first feature La Pointe Courte (1955) predates the movement's official start. Over six decades she moved between fiction (Cléo from 5 to 7, 1962; Le Bonheur, 1965; Vagabond, 1985, Golden Lion at Venice) and the essay-documentaries (The Gleaners and I, 2000; Faces Places, 2017, with JR) that made her last decades a second spring.

Trained as a photographer (official photographer of the Théâtre National Populaire), she brought still photography's eye to cinema: real time, real streets, real faces — and a feminist intelligence decades ahead of the industry's.

Career shape

Cléo from 5 to 7 follows a singer in near-real time through Paris awaiting cancer results — a formal tour de force made when its director was dismissed as a precursor while the boys got the movement's name. Vagabond's portrait of a drifter found dead, reconstructed from witnesses, is her harshest fiction. Marriage to Jacques Demy, his death in 1990, and her films about him (Jacquot de Nantes) thread the personal through the work.

Late fame

The Gleaners and I — Varda with a small digital camera, gleaning images of gleaners — invented a new documentary intimacy; travels, installations at the Venice Biennale and the Fondation Cartier, and Faces Places (Oscar-nominated at 89) made her a beloved public figure, 'the grandmother of the New Wave', a title she alternately accepted and corrected. She died in Paris in 2019.
