Agglutinative language

An agglutinative language has a system in which words are formed by joining together morphemes, each of which retains its meaning and phonetic shape. Agglutinative languages are a type of synthetic language, distinguished from fusional languages, in which affixes often carry several meanings at once and fuse with the stem, and from isolating languages, which use separate words instead.

Characteristics

In a prototypically agglutinative language, each affix expresses a single grammatical category — such as tense, number, case, or possession — and affixes are strung together in a fixed order after (or before) the root. Because the boundaries between morphemes remain clear, a single word can be equivalent to a whole sentence in an analytic language such as English.

A famous example comes from Turkish: evlerinizden ("from your houses") decomposes into ev "house" + -ler plural + -iniz "your" + -den "from". Agglutination is often accompanied by vowel harmony, in which the vowels of affixes adjust to match those of the root.

Distribution

Agglutinative morphology is found on every inhabited continent. Well-known examples include the Turkic languages, the Uralic languages (Finnish, Estonian, Hungarian), Mongolian, Japanese, Korean, the Dravidian languages of southern India, Swahili and other Bantu languages, Quechua and Aymara in the Andes, and many Indigenous languages of the Americas. Linguists stress that agglutination is a tendency rather than an absolute category: most languages display some fusional or isolating features as well.
