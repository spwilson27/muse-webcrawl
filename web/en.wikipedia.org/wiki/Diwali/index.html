Diwali

Diwali (also spelled Deepavali) is the Hindu festival of lights, celebrated in the Hindu lunisolar month of Kartika (typically October or November). It symbolises the victory of light over darkness and good over evil, and is observed by Hindus, Jains, Sikhs, and some Buddhists. Celebrations usually last five days and feature rows of oil lamps (diyas) and candles, fireworks, feasting, gift-giving, and prayers to Lakshmi, the goddess of prosperity.

Religious significance

The festival is associated with several narratives. In northern India it marks Rama's return to Ayodhya after defeating Ravana, when citizens are said to have lit lamps to guide him home. Elsewhere it is linked with Krishna's victory over the demon Narakasura and with Lakshmi's emergence from the churning of the ocean of milk. Jains commemorate Mahavira's attainment of moksha (in 527 BCE by tradition), and Sikhs observe Bandi Chhor Divas, marking Guru Hargobind's release from imprisonment.

The five days

  • Dhanteras – day of wealth and health; purchases of metal goods.
  • Naraka Chaturdashi (Choti Diwali) – commemorates the defeat of Narakasura.
  • Lakshmi Puja – the main day: worship of Lakshmi and Ganesha; lamps and fireworks.
  • Govardhan Puja / Padwa – associated with Krishna lifting Mount Govardhan.
  • Bhai Dooj – celebrates the bond between brothers and sisters.

Celebrations worldwide

Diwali is a public holiday in India, Nepal, Fiji, Guyana, Malaysia, Mauritius, Myanmar, Singapore, Sri Lanka, Suriname, and Trinidad and Tobago, and is celebrated by the South Asian diaspora around the world, including large public events in cities such as London, New York, and Leicester.
