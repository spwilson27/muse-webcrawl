Japanese economic miracle

The Japanese economic miracle refers to Japan's record period of high economic growth between the end of the Second World War and the end of the Cold War. From the mid-1950s to 1973, the Japanese economy grew at average annual rates of around 10%, transforming a war-devastated country into the world's second-largest economy by 1968 — a position it held until overtaken by China in 2010. Growth moderated but remained strong through the 1980s before the asset bubble burst in the early 1990s, ushering in the "Lost Decades".

Background and reforms

Postwar Japan began from ruins: industrial output in 1946 was a fraction of prewar levels. The Allied occupation imposed land reform, zaibatsu dissolution and labour reforms; the "Dodge Line" of 1949 stabilised finances, and procurement demand from the Korean War (1950–53) reignited heavy industry. Under the Ikeda government's Income Doubling Plan (1960), growth became explicit state policy.

Drivers of growth

Scholars cite a combination of factors: a young, well-educated and disciplined workforce; very high savings rates channelled into investment; rapid adoption and improvement of foreign technology; export promotion and industrial guidance by the Ministry of International Trade and Industry (MITI); close bank–industry ties within keiretsu groups; the fixed exchange rate of 360 yen to the dollar, which long undervalued the yen; lifetime employment and enterprise unions in large firms; and a protected domestic market behind which exporters — Toyota, Sony, Honda, Nippon Steel — conquered world markets in steel, ships, cars, motorcycles, cameras and consumer electronics.

Turning points

The 1973 oil shock ended the era of double-digit growth and pushed Japan toward energy efficiency and higher-value industries; growth continued near 4% in the later 1970s and 1980s. The Plaza Accord of 1985 sharply revalued the yen, and the Bank of Japan's response fed a vast stock and real-estate bubble whose collapse around 1991 began decades of stagnation and deflation — the "Lost Decades" that followed the miracle.

Legacy

The miracle made Japan a model of state-guided development studied (and adapted) by South Korea, Taiwan and later China, and remains central to debates over industrial policy. It also brought severe pollution episodes in the 1950s–60s, famously the Minamata mercury-poisoning disaster, before environmental regulation tightened.
