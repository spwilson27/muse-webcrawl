Hyperkalemia

Hyperkalemia is an elevated level of potassium (K+) in the blood. Normal potassium levels are between 3.5 and 5.0 mmol/L, with levels above 5.5 mmol/L generally defining hyperkalemia. Hyperkalemia may cause no symptoms, but it can produce dangerous cardiac arrhythmias and is a medical emergency at high levels because of the risk of fatal abnormal heart rhythms.

Characteristic ECG changes in hyperkalemia include peaked T waves.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms, when present, are often nonspecific: weakness, fatigue, palpitations and muscle paralysis. The electrocardiogram may show peaked (tented) T waves, flattened P waves, a widened QRS complex and, terminally, a sine-wave pattern preceding ventricular fibrillation or asystole. ECG changes correlate imperfectly with the potassium level, so treatment is guided by both.

Causes

The main categories of cause are decreased renal excretion (acute kidney injury, chronic kidney disease, hypoaldosteronism, and drugs such as ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, potassium-sparing diuretics and trimethoprim), shift of potassium out of cells (acidosis, insulin deficiency, tissue breakdown in rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis and burns), and increased intake, often iatrogenic. Pseudohyperkalemia is a laboratory artefact caused by haemolysis or prolonged tourniquet use.

Treatment

Emergency management stabilises the cardiac membrane with intravenous calcium, shifts potassium into cells with insulin and glucose, beta-2 agonists and sometimes sodium bicarbonate, and removes potassium from the body with potassium binders (patiromer, sodium zirconium cyclosilicate), loop diuretics, or haemodialysis in refractory cases.
