Dacia (Latin: [ˈdaːki.a]) was the land inhabited by the Dacians, a Thracian people, in the area of the Carpathian Mountains and the lower Danube, corresponding roughly to modern Romania and Moldova, together with parts of neighboring countries. The Dacian kingdom reached its height under King Burebista (82–44 BC) and again under Decebalus, whose wars with Rome ended in conquest by the emperor Trajan in AD 106.

Roman Dacia became an imperial province valued for its gold mines. Rome withdrew administration south of the Danube under Aurelian around AD 271–275. The ethnogenesis of the Romanian people — whether by continuity of Romanized Dacians north of the Danube or by later migration — remains a debated historical question.

Geography

Dacia was bounded approximately by the Danube to the south, the Tisza to the west, the Dniester to the east, and the Carpathians across its center. Its mountainous core in Transylvania held fortified sanctuaries and the capital, Sarmizegetusa Regia, while the plains of Wallachia and Moldavia supported agriculture and trade with the Greek cities of the Black Sea coast.

History

Greek authors knew the Getae from the 5th century BC; Herodotus called them "the bravest and most just of the Thracians". Burebista united the Dacian and Getic tribes into a powerful kingdom that raided as far as the Balkans and the Pontic coast. After his assassination the kingdom fragmented. Decebalus reunified much of it and fought Domitian to a negotiated peace before Trajan's two campaigns (101–102 and 105–106) destroyed the kingdom; Decebalus took his own life, and his head was displayed in Rome. The campaigns are commemorated on Trajan's Column.

Society and religion

Dacian religion centered on the god Zalmoxis, associated in Greek accounts with doctrines of immortality. Society was divided between an aristocracy (tarabostes) and commoners, with fortified settlements (davae) dotting the landscape. The Dacians were skilled metalworkers, and their gold and silver hoards remain famous.

Roman Dacia

As a province, Dacia received colonists, legions (XIII Gemina at Apulum), roads, and cities such as Ulpia Traiana Sarmizegetusa. Gold mining at Alburnus Maior (Roșia Montană) was a major imperial interest. The province was reorganized several times and finally abandoned under Aurelian amid Gothic pressure.

Legacy

The name survives in Romania's national car manufacturer, Automobile Dacia, and the Dacian heritage is a central element of Romanian national identity.
