Technological unemployment

[Image: Automation replaces some tasks while creating others]

Technological unemployment is the loss of jobs caused by technological change. It is a type of structural unemployment in which new machines, processes or software reduce the demand for human labour in particular occupations, even if the economy as a whole later generates new kinds of work.

Historical debate

Anxiety about machine displacement is centuries old: the Luddites smashed power looms in 1810s England, and David Ricardo added a famous chapter "On Machinery" to the 1821 edition of his Principles conceding that mechanisation could harm workers. John Maynard Keynes coined the term "technological unemployment" in 1930, calling it a temporary "phase of maladjustment" but warning of its social costs. Through the later 20th century, mainstream economists generally held that productivity gains lower prices, raise incomes and create new demand, so aggregate employment recovers — the so-called "lump of labour fallacy" argument.

Modern research

Empirical work since the 2000s has nuanced that consensus. Studies of industrial robots (for example by Acemoglu and Restrepo) find significant local labour-market losses where robots are adopted, and the "job polarisation" literature documents the hollowing-out of routine middle-skill work by computing. Debate intensified with advances in artificial intelligence: Frey and Osborne's 2013 study estimated 47% of US jobs at high risk of computerisation, a figure later studies revised sharply downward when analysing tasks rather than whole occupations. Responses proposed include retraining, shorter working weeks, wage insurance and universal basic income.
