The Panic of 1873 was a financial crisis that triggered the Long Depression in the United States and Europe, a period of economic contraction lasting until about 1879 (and, by some measures in Europe, much longer). At the time it was called the "Great Depression," a name it kept until the 1930s.

Causes and outbreak

The panic followed a post-Civil War speculative boom in railroads, financed heavily with bonds sold to European investors. In May 1873 the Vienna Stock Exchange crashed amid speculative excess in Austria-Hungary and Germany, drying up European capital. On 18 September 1873 the great Philadelphia banking house Jay Cooke & Co., overextended on the Northern Pacific Railway, suspended payments. The New York Stock Exchange closed for ten days, and bank runs spread.

Effects

Dozens of railroads went bankrupt — some 89 of the country's 364 railway companies failed within a year — along with thousands of businesses and many banks. Unemployment may have reached 14 percent by 1876; wages fell sharply, and the crisis culminated in the Great Railroad Strike of 1877. Deflation and the return to the gold standard (Resumption Act, 1875) became central political battles, fuelling the Greenback and free-silver movements and agrarian populism.
