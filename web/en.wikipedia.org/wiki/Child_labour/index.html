Child labour

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Child labour is the employment of children in work that deprives them of their childhood, interferes with their schooling, or is mentally, physically, socially or morally harmful. International law distinguishes child labour from permissible light work or work experience: the International Labour Organization (ILO) defines child labour as work that is dangerous, that interferes with education, or that is performed by children below a minimum age.

Definitions and scale

ILO Convention No. 138 sets a general minimum working age of 15 (14 in some developing economies), with 18 as the minimum for hazardous work. Convention No. 182, adopted in 1999 and ratified by every ILO member state, commits countries to eliminating the "worst forms of child labour", including slavery, trafficking, forced recruitment into armed conflict, prostitution, and work likely to harm health or safety.

Joint ILO and UNICEF estimates put the number of children in child labour at about 160 million in 2020 — roughly one in ten children worldwide — with about half in hazardous work. Agriculture accounts for the large majority of cases, followed by services and industry. Sub-Saharan Africa has the highest prevalence, and most child labour takes place within families, on farms and in informal work rather than in factories supplying export markets.

Historical development

Children had always worked in agrarian households, but the factory system of the Industrial Revolution in Britain drew very young children into textile mills, mines and chimney sweeping for long hours in dangerous conditions. Public outrage, spurred by parliamentary inquiries and writers such as Charles Dickens, produced the Factory Acts of the nineteenth century, which progressively restricted children's hours and set minimum ages. Similar legislation followed in other industrialising countries, and compulsory schooling reinforced the decline of child labour in the developed world.

Causes and debates

Poverty is the principal driver: households rely on children's earnings, and shocks such as illness or crop failure push children into work. Lack of accessible, quality schooling, weak enforcement and informal economies sustain the practice. Economists debate the pace and design of bans, noting that a prohibition without income support can push children into worse, hidden work, while conditional cash transfers and free schooling have shown measurable success. Target 8.7 of the Sustainable Development Goals called for ending child labour in all its forms by 2025, a deadline that was not met.
