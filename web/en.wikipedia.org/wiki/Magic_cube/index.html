Magic cube

Magic cube
TypeCombinatorial object
Related toMagic square, magic hypercube

In mathematics, a magic cube is the 3-dimensional equivalent of a magic square: an arrangement of integers in an n × n × n pattern such that the sums of the numbers on each row, each column, each pillar and the four space diagonals are all equal to the same constant, the magic constant of the cube.

Definition

A magic cube of order n contains the integers 1 through n³. Its magic constant is n(n³ + 1)/2. A cube in which, additionally, every planar diagonal of every cross-section layer is also magic is called a pandiagonal or perfect magic cube; terminology varies between authors, and some reserve "perfect" for cubes in which all possible lines through each cell sum to the constant.

Examples and history

No magic cube of order 2 exists; the smallest possible order is 3, with magic constant 42. Magic cubes were studied from the 19th century onward; Pierre de Fermat claimed an order-4 cube, and large catalogues of cubes of orders 5 and above were produced with the aid of computers. The order-3 cube is essentially unique up to symmetry when the centre cell takes the mean value 14.

Multimagic cubes

A cube whose numbers remain magic when each entry is raised to a power is called multimagic; bimagic (squared) and trimagic (cubed) cubes are known, beginning at orders 16 and 64 respectively in published constructions.
