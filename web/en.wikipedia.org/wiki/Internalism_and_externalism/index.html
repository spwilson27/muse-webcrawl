Internalism and externalism

The internalism–externalism debate concerns whether justification depends on factors accessible within the mind or on external facts about reliability.

Internalism and externalism are contrasting approaches in the philosophical study of knowledge and justification. In epistemology, internalism holds that a person either has, or has reflective access to, all the factors that justify a belief: nothing external to the believer's perspective is needed for justification. Externalism denies this, holding that factors outside the believer's awareness — such as whether the belief was produced by a reliable process — can contribute to justification or knowledge.

Internalism

Internalist theories are often divided into access internalism, which requires that a believer be able to access the grounds of a belief through reflection, and mentalist internalism, which requires that justification supervene on the believer's mental states. Laurence BonJour defended a coherentist form of internalism, arguing that a belief is justified only if the believer has good reasons for it available from within their own perspective. Internalists often appeal to the "new evil demon problem": a victim of a Cartesian demon whose experiences are identical to ours would seem just as justified in their beliefs, even though those beliefs are formed unreliably.

Externalism

The leading externalist theory is reliabilism, associated with Alvin Goldman, according to which a belief is justified if and only if it is produced by a cognitive process that reliably yields true beliefs. Externalists respond to the evil demon intuition by noting that justification may come in different senses, and they raise counterexamples to internalism such as the case of the reliable clairvoyant who has no idea how their beliefs are formed.

Content externalism

A related but distinct distinction concerns the philosophy of mind and language. Semantic or content externalism, defended by Hilary Putnam with his Twin Earth thought experiment and by Tyler Burge, holds that the contents of a person's thoughts are partly determined by their physical and social environment: "meanings just ain't in the head". This thesis is independent of the epistemological debate, though the similar terminology is a frequent source of confusion.
