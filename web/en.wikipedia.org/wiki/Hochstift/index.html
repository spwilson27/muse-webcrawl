Hochstift

Hochstift (plural: Hochstifte) is a German term for the territory of a prince-bishopric within the Holy Roman Empire — the lands over which a bishop or archbishop ruled not merely as a churchman but as a secular prince (Fürstbischof) with temporal authority, a seat in the Imperial Diet, and the rights of an imperial estate.

Meaning and scope

The Hochstift comprised the lands directly administered by the bishop as territorial sovereign, which were distinct from, and often much smaller than, his diocese — the ecclesiastical jurisdiction over souls and parishes. A bishop's diocese might extend into the territories of neighbouring princes, while his Hochstift could contain areas religiously administered by other bishops. Alongside the Hochstifte stood the analogous territories of prince-abbots and prince-provosts (Fürststift).

History

Prince-bishoprics arose from early medieval grants of land and immunity to bishoprics by Frankish and German kings. By the late Middle Ages there were dozens of Hochstifte; the largest included the archbishoprics of Mainz, Cologne, and Trier — whose holders were also imperial electors — and the bishoprics of Würzburg, Münster, Salzburg (an archbishopric), Paderborn, and Bamberg. Their rulers built courtly residences, administered justice, coined money, and waged war like other princes.

Secularisation

Nearly all the Hochstifte were abolished in the great secularisation of 1803, codified by the Reichsdeputationshauptschluss, which transferred ecclesiastical principalities to secular rulers to compensate German princes for lands lost to France west of the Rhine. The last surviving prince-bishopric arrangement, the Hochstift of the Bishop of Basel's temporal remnants, faded in the same period. The term survives in regional and institutional names, such as the former Hochstift of Fulda.
