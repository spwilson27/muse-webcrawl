Invasion literature

Invasion literature (or invasion-scare literature) is a literary genre that flourished in Britain and elsewhere between the 1870s and the First World War. Its stories imagined a sudden invasion of the homeland by a foreign power — most often Germany after about 1900, and France or Russia earlier — and typically argued for stronger armed forces, conscription, or other preparedness measures.

Origins

The genre is usually traced to George Tomkyns Chesney's "The Battle of Dorking" (1871), published in Blackwood's Magazine after Prussia's victory over France in the Franco-Prussian War. Its vivid account of a German invasion and the defeat of an unprepared Britain was enormously successful and provoked widespread public debate.

Development and influence

Hundreds of invasion novels followed, including William Le Queux's "The Invasion of 1910" (1906), serialised in the Daily Mail, and works by authors such as H. G. Wells, whose "The War in the Air" (1908) imagined aerial warfare. Erskine Childers' "The Riddle of the Sands" (1903), a related spy novel, influenced British naval policy and the establishment of new bases. The genre reflected and stoked public anxiety, fed spy scares, and contributed to the climate in which Britain created its Secret Service Bureau in 1909. After 1918 the invasion scenario declined, though Wells's "The War of the Worlds" (1898) had already transformed the invader into an extraterrestrial one, seeding a major science-fiction tradition.
