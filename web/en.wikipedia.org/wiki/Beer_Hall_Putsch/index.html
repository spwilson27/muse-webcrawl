Beer Hall Putsch

Supporters of the putsch in Munich, November 1923.

The Beer Hall Putsch, also known as the Munich Putsch, was a failed coup d'état by the Nazi Party (NSDAP) under Adolf Hitler in Munich, Bavaria, on 8–9 November 1923, during the Weimar Republic. Approximately two thousand Nazis marched on the Feldherrnhalle in the city centre but were confronted by police; sixteen Nazi supporters and four police officers were killed.

Events

On the evening of 8 November, Hitler and armed stormtroopers burst into a meeting at the Bürgerbräukeller beer hall where Bavaria's leaders were speaking, and attempted to coerce them into supporting a march on Berlin modelled on Mussolini's March on Rome. General Erich Ludendorff lent his prestige to the attempt, but the Bavarian authorities withdrew their support overnight. The next day the putschists' march was stopped by police gunfire at the Odeonsplatz; Hitler fled, was arrested two days later, and many leading Nazis were tried for treason in early 1924.

Aftermath

Hitler used his trial as a propaganda platform and, during his imprisonment at Landsberg, dictated Mein Kampf. He concluded that power had to be sought by legal means rather than by force. The sixteen Nazis killed were later venerated as martyrs ("blood witnesses") of the Nazi movement, and the anniversary of the putsch was commemorated annually in the Third Reich.
