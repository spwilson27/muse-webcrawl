Sardine

Sardine and pilchard are common names for various small, oily forage fish in the herring family Clupeidae. The term "sardine" was first used in English in the early 15th century, possibly from the Mediterranean island of Sardinia, around which sardines were once abundant.

Sardine
Family: Clupeidae
Type: Forage fish
Use: Canned food, bait, fishmeal

Species

True sardines include the European pilchard (Sardina pilchardus); other species of Sardinops, Sardinella and related genera are marketed as sardines in many countries. Codex Alimentarius lists over twenty species that may be sold as sardines.

Fisheries and food

Sardines form huge schools, feed on plankton, and are prey for larger fish, seabirds and marine mammals. They are canned in oil, water or sauces, and are rich in omega-3 fatty acids, vitamin D and calcium. Major fisheries have included California's Monterey fishery (immortalised by Steinbeck's Cannery Row), which collapsed in the mid-20th century.

Idiom

"Packed like sardines" alludes to the tight packing of fish in cans.
