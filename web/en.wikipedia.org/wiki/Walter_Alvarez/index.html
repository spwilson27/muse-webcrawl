Walter Alvarez (born October 3, 1940) is an American geologist and professor emeritus at the University of California, Berkeley. He is best known, together with his father, the Nobel Prize–winning physicist Luis Alvarez, for proposing the Alvarez hypothesis: that the extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs 66 million years ago was caused by the impact of a large asteroid.

The impact hypothesis

While studying limestone strata near Gubbio, Italy, Alvarez noticed a thin clay layer marking the boundary between the Cretaceous and Paleogene periods (the K–Pg boundary). In 1980, Walter and Luis Alvarez, with chemists Frank Asaro and Helen Michel, published a paper in Science reporting that this layer was enriched in the rare element iridium — far more abundant in asteroids than in Earth's crust — and proposed a giant impact as the extinction mechanism. The hypothesis was at first controversial, but evidence accumulated worldwide, and in 1991 the ~180 km Chicxulub crater in Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula was identified as the impact site.

Other work

Alvarez has also worked on the tectonics of the Mediterranean, Roman history and archaeology, and "big history." His popular book T. rex and the Crater of Doom (1997) recounts the discovery of the impact evidence. He is a co-founder of the Big History Project and author of A Most Improbable Journey (2016).
