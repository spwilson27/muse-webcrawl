Upāsaka (feminine upāsikā) is the title of a lay follower of Buddhism — literally "one who sits close by" (to hear the teaching). Together with monks (bhikkhu), nuns (bhikkhunī) and female lay followers, male lay devotees form one of the four assemblies (parisā) of the Buddhist community. A person becomes an upāsaka by formally taking refuge in the Three Jewels — the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha — and undertaking the Five Precepts.

Duties and practice

The classical lay disciple supports the monastic community with the four requisites (food, clothing, shelter and medicine), observes the five precepts against killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying and intoxication, keeps the uposatha observance days with the eight precepts, and practises generosity (dāna), morality (sīla) and meditation (bhāvanā). The Sigālovāda Sutta is often called the "layperson's Vinaya" for its advice on household ethics and reciprocal duties.

In the canon

The Buddha's first lay disciples were the merchants Tapussa and Bhallika, who took refuge in the Buddha and Dhamma before the Sangha existed. Famous upāsakas in the texts include the banker Anāthapiṇḍika, the Buddha's chief male lay benefactor, and the physician Jīvaka; prominent upāsikās include Queen Sāmāvatī and the benefactress Visākhā.

Modern usage

In Theravada countries the term remains in everyday use for devout laypeople, especially white-clad devotees who keep eight precepts at monasteries on observance days. In East Asian Buddhism the corresponding terms (Chinese youposai, Japanese ubasoku) likewise denote formally committed lay practitioners.
