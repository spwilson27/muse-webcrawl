Spanish missions in California

Mission San Carlos Borromeo at Carmel

The Spanish missions in California (Spanish: Misiones españolas en California) were a series of 21 religious outposts established by Catholic priests of the Franciscan order between 1769 and 1833, along or near the Pacific coast of what is now the US state of California. Founded from San Diego in the south to Sonoma in the north, a day's journey apart along the route later called El Camino Real, the missions were intended to convert the Indigenous peoples of California to Christianity, to teach European agriculture and crafts, and to secure Spain's claim to Alta California.

History

The enterprise began with the Sacred Expedition of 1769, led on land by Gaspar de Portolá and spiritually by Junípero Serra, who founded the first mission, San Diego de Alcalá. Serra, now a canonised saint, founded nine missions before his death in 1784; his successor Fermín Lasuén founded nine more, and the chain was completed with San Francisco Solano in 1823. Each mission was accompanied by military presidios and was expected to become self-sustaining through farming and stock-raising carried on by converted Native people, the neophytes.

Impact on Indigenous peoples

The missions transformed Indigenous life catastrophically. Native Californians were drawn or coerced into mission settlements, where regimented labour, corporal punishment, suppression of native religion and language, and — above all — epidemic diseases against which they had no immunity produced very high mortality. Historians and California Native peoples continue to debate the system's legacy; in 2015 the canonisation of Serra drew protests from Indigenous groups, while the missions remain celebrated features of California's heritage landscape.

Secularization and legacy

After Mexican independence, the Secularization Act of 1833 dismantled the mission system; mission lands were distributed, often to Californio ranchers, and the buildings fell into ruin. Many were later restored, several as active Roman Catholic parishes. The surviving mission churches — built in the style that inspired Mission Revival architecture — are among California's most visited historic sites.
