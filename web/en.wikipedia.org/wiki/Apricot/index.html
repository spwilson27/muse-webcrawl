Apricot

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Ripe apricots with their soft orange blush on the branch]

The apricot (Prunus armeniaca) is a small stone fruit of the rose family — velvet-skinned, orange-gold, sweet-tart, and first to ripen of the orchard fruits in many regions. Its Latin name honours Armenia, but the apricot's deeper roots are Chinese: it was cultivated in China over 3,000 years ago and travelled the Silk Road west, so that dried apricots became one of the great caravan foods. Fresh apricots are fleeting — fragile, quick to soften, and bred in most commerce for travel rather than flavour — which is why the dried fruit, dominated by Turkey's Malatya region, carries the apricot's global name.

History and lore

Apricots appear in Chinese medicine and legend (the physician Dong Feng was said to take apricot trees as payment, leaving a "apricot forest" as a byword for medical kindness), in Persian poetry, and in the orchards of Samarkand and Damascus. The Hunza valley's apricot-centred diet fed durable longevity myths in the 20th century. Apricot kernels are a food and a caution: sweet kernels flavour amaretti and the liqueur amaretto's story, while bitter kernels concentrate amygdalin — the basis of the discredited "laetrile" cancer remedy and a genuine poisoning risk in quantity.

Uses

Fresh apricots are a June luxury in Europe; elsewhere the fruit is jam (the baker's apricot glaze shines on a thousand tarts), nectar, compote, and a partner for lamb in Persian and Moroccan tagines, where its sweetness meets spice. Dried apricots — sulphured to keep their sunset colour or left to darken naturally — plump into stuffings, rice dishes and trail mix. Apricot oil from the kernels scents cosmetics.
