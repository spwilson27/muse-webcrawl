Flint sit-down strike

The Flint sit-down strike, also known as the General Motors sit-down strike, was a sitdown strike by autoworkers at General Motors plants in Flint, Michigan, United States, that lasted from December 30, 1936, to February 11, 1937. It was one of the most significant strikes in American labour history: its victory led General Motors to recognise the United Auto Workers (UAW) union and spurred the rapid growth of unionism in the American automobile industry.

Sit-down strikers occupying a GM plant in Flint

Background

Autoworkers in the 1930s faced speed-ups, arbitrary layoffs, unsafe conditions, and an elaborate system of company spies that made organising dangerous. Protected on paper by the National Labor Relations Act of 1935, workers in Flint began joining the fledgling UAW. When GM moved dies out of the Fisher Body No. 1 plant — apparently preparing to shift work elsewhere — workers sat down inside the plant rather than walking out, preventing the company from using strikebreakers or easily removing machinery.

Course of the strike

The occupation spread to other plants. On January 11, 1937, police attempted to cut off food deliveries to Fisher Body No. 2, provoking the pitched "Battle of the Running Bulls", in which strikers repelled police with hinges, bottles, and fire hoses. Michigan governor Frank Murphy mobilised the National Guard — but as a peacekeeping force rather than to evict the strikers, and he refused to enforce an injunction ordering evacuation. A clever diversion allowed the union to seize the crucial Chevrolet Plant No. 4, which made engines for most GM cars.

Settlement and significance

On February 11, 1937, GM agreed to recognise the UAW as the bargaining agent for its members, to rehire strikers, and to refrain from discriminating against union members. UAW membership soared, Chrysler recognised the union soon after, and the strike became a landmark demonstration of the sit-down tactic and of industrial unionism under the CIO.
