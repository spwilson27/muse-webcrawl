Kruskal's tree theorem

In mathematics, Kruskal's tree theorem states that the set of finite trees over a well-quasi-ordered set of labels is itself well-quasi-ordered under homeomorphic embedding. The theorem was conjectured by Andrew Vázsonyi and proved by Joseph Kruskal in 1960. It has since become a prominent example in reverse mathematics, since the statement and its proof are significantly stronger than Peano arithmetic.

Statement

The finite version given by Kruskal is:

Given n, there is some m such that if T1, ..., Tm is any finite sequence of trees with at most n vertices, then Ti ≤ Tj for some i < j, where ≤ denotes homeomorphic embedding.

The strength of even this finitary version is enormous: the maximal length of such sequences grows faster than any primitive recursive function, a phenomenon analysed by Harvey Friedman and related to the small Veblen ordinal.

History

A weaker statement for binary trees was proved by Graham Higman in the context of Higman's lemma. Nash-Williams later gave a simpler proof using his theory of "minimal bad sequences". In 2004 the result was extended from trees to graphs in the celebrated Robertson–Seymour theorem.

Ordinal analysis

Kruskal's theorem exceeds the strength of ATR0 and its proof-theoretic ordinal is associated with the small Veblen ordinal, far beyond the Feferman–Schütte ordinal Γ0 of predicative mathematics.
