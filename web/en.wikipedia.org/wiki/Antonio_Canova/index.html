Antonio Canova (1 November 1757 – 13 October 1822) was an Italian Neoclassical sculptor, famous for his marble sculptures that conveyed a remarkable illusion of flesh. He is widely regarded as the greatest of the Neoclassical sculptors and was commissioned by popes, emperors, and royalty across Europe, including Napoleon, whose family he portrayed repeatedly. His workshop in Rome became one of the sights of the city for Grand Tour visitors.

Life and career

Canova was born in Possagno in the Venetian Republic, the son of a stonecutter who died when he was young. Trained in Venice, he achieved early success with works such as Daedalus and Icarus and the monument to Pope Clement XIV, and in 1781 settled in Rome. There his mythological groups — above all Psyche Revived by Cupid's Kiss (1793) — made his European reputation. He sculpted Napoleon's mother, his sister Paolina Borghese as a reclining Venus, and Napoleon himself as a colossal nude Mars. After Napoleon's fall he acted as the papal envoy who secured the return to Italy of artworks looted by the French.

Style and legacy

Canova worked up his figures in clay and plaster, transferring them to marble with the help of assistants and finishing the surfaces himself, sometimes tinting the marble with wax to soften its whiteness. His art was praised for grace and sensuous surface and later criticised by Romantics and modernists as cold, but his fame has endured; the Museo Canova in Possagno preserves his models and the Tempio Canoviano, the church he designed as his own mausoleum.
