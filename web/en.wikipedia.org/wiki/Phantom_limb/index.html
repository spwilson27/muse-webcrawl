Phantom limb

A phantom limb is the sensation that an amputated or missing limb is still attached to the body and moving appropriately with the other limbs. Nearly all amputees experience phantom sensations at some point; for many these are painless feelings of position, movement, warmth or itching, but a large proportion experience phantom limb pain, which can be severe and chronic.

History

The phenomenon was described by the French surgeon Ambroise Paré in the sixteenth century and named by the American neurologist Silas Weir Mitchell, who treated Civil War amputees and published on the "phantom limb" in the 1870s. Similar sensations can occur after removal of other body parts, including breasts, teeth and internal organs, and even in some people born without limbs.

Mechanisms

Phantom sensations are thought to arise from the brain's persistent internal map of the body. After amputation, the region of the somatosensory cortex that formerly received input from the limb is deprived of its usual signals and is partly invaded by neighbouring cortical representations — famously, stimulation of the face may be felt in a phantom hand, since the face and hand areas lie adjacent in the cortical homunculus. This cortical remapping, studied extensively by V. S. Ramachandran and others, correlates with the intensity of phantom pain. Peripheral factors, including neuromas in the stump, also contribute.

Many amputees report "telescoping", in which the phantom limb gradually shrinks so that the phantom hand or foot seems to hang close to the stump.

Treatment

Phantom limb pain is difficult to treat. Approaches include medications (anticonvulsants, antidepressants, opioids), prosthesis use, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, and mirror therapy, in which the reflection of the intact limb is visually superimposed on the missing one, allowing the patient to "move" and unclench the phantom and often relieving pain. Virtual-reality versions of mirror therapy and targeted muscle reinnervation surgery are also used.
