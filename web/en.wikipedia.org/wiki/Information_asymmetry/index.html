Information asymmetry

Information asymmetry (or asymmetric information) deals with the study of decisions in transactions where one party has more or better information than the other. This imbalance creates an unevenness in the transaction that can cause markets to malfunction or fail. The concept became central to modern economics through the work of George Akerlof, Michael Spence and Joseph Stiglitz, who shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for their analyses of markets with asymmetric information.

The market for lemons

In his influential 1970 paper "The Market for 'Lemons'", George Akerlof used the used-car market to show how asymmetric information degrades the quality of goods traded. Sellers know whether their car is a good car (a "plum") or a defective one (a "lemon"), but buyers cannot tell the difference and so will only pay a price reflecting average quality. Owners of good cars then withdraw them from the market, lowering average quality further, and in the extreme the market can unravel entirely. This process is known as adverse selection.

Signalling and screening

Michael Spence showed that informed parties can credibly convey their private information through costly actions called signals. In his job-market model, education serves as a signal of a worker's ability: because education is less costly for high-ability workers to obtain, employers can interpret a degree as evidence of productivity even if the education itself adds little. Conversely, the uninformed party can induce the informed party to reveal information by offering a menu of choices — a practice called screening, seen for example in insurance contracts with varying premiums and deductibles.

Moral hazard

A related problem is moral hazard, where one party changes its behaviour after a contract is signed because the other party cannot observe or bear the consequences of that behaviour — for instance, an insured driver taking greater risks. Information asymmetry thus underlies the principal–agent problem studied across economics, contract theory and political science.

Remedies

Markets and institutions respond to asymmetric information with warranties and money-back guarantees, brand reputations, licensing and professional certification, mandatory disclosure rules, third-party ratings and reviews, and regulation. In financial markets, insider trading laws aim to prevent traders with privileged information from exploiting uninformed counterparties.
