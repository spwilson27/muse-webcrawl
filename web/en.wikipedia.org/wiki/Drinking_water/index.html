Drinking water, or potable water, is water that is safe for human consumption and food preparation. In developed countries it is typically supplied to homes through a piped distribution system after treatment, and must meet legally enforceable quality standards covering microbiological contaminants, chemicals and physical properties. Water obtained from untreated sources can transmit diseases including cholera, typhoid fever, dysentery and hepatitis A.

Sources and treatment

Drinking water is drawn from surface sources (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) and from groundwater in aquifers. Conventional treatment coagulates and settles out suspended matter, filters the water — by sand, membranes or both — and disinfects it, most commonly with chlorine or chloramine, but also with ozone or ultraviolet light. Fluoride is added in some countries to reduce tooth decay. Desalination supplies a growing share in arid regions and island states.

Access

Access to safe drinking water is a central target of the United Nations' Sustainable Development Goal 6. Around the early 2020s, roughly three-quarters of the world's population used safely managed drinking water services, but about 2 billion people still lacked them, relying on unimproved sources, surface water or water contaminated with faecal matter or chemicals such as arsenic and excess fluoride. Contaminated water and poor sanitation are estimated to cause hundreds of thousands of diarrhoeal deaths each year, especially among children under five.

Regulation and quality issues

Notable regulatory frameworks include the WHO guidelines, the European Union's Drinking Water Directive and the United States' Safe Drinking Water Act (1974). Persistent challenges include lead leaching from old pipes — dramatised by the Flint water crisis — nitrate and pesticide runoff, and emerging contaminants such as PFAS ("forever chemicals").
