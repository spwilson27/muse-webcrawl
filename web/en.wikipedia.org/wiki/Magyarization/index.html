Magyarization

Magyarization is the process of assimilation by which non-Hungarian peoples adopted the Hungarian (Magyar) language and culture, in particular as promoted by state policies in the Kingdom of Hungary during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, especially after the Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867.

Policies

Although the Nationalities Law of 1868 formally recognized the rights of Hungary's national minorities, successive governments pursued policies that privileged Hungarian in administration, railways, and education. The Education Acts of 1879 and 1883 made Hungarian compulsory in schools, and the Apponyi education laws of 1907 tightened requirements for Hungarian-language instruction in church and community schools. Place names were systematically Hungarianized, and the use of Hungarian was encouraged in public life and surnames.

Effects and criticism

The proportion of the kingdom's population recorded as Hungarian-speaking rose substantially between 1880 and 1910, through a combination of assimilation, migration, and census methods. Magyarization policies generated deep resentment among Slovaks, Romanians, Croats, Serbs, and others, contributing to tensions that erupted at the end of the First World War, when the kingdom was dismantled under the Treaty of Trianon (1920).
