Weather forecasting is the application of science and technology to predict the conditions of the atmosphere for a given location and time. Modern forecasts rest on the collection of enormous quantities of observations — from surface stations, weather balloons, radar, aircraft, and satellites — which are assimilated into numerical models of the atmosphere run on supercomputers.

History

Organised forecasting began in the 19th century with the telegraph, which allowed observations to be gathered faster than weather systems moved; Admiral Robert FitzRoy issued the first public forecasts for the British Meteorological Office in 1861. In 1922 Lewis Fry Richardson proposed forecasting by numerical solution of the governing equations, a vision realised only with electronic computers after the Second World War — the ENIAC produced the first successful numerical forecast in 1950.

Numerical weather prediction

Numerical weather prediction (NWP) divides the atmosphere into a three-dimensional grid and integrates the equations of fluid motion and thermodynamics forward in time. Global models such as the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts' IFS and NOAA's GFS produce outlooks up to about two weeks ahead; higher-resolution regional models resolve thunderstorms and local effects. Because the atmosphere is chaotic, small errors grow with time, limiting deterministic predictability.

Ensemble and short-range forecasting

Ensemble forecasting runs a model many times with slightly different initial conditions or physics to estimate uncertainty and the probability of outcomes. Very short-range prediction (nowcasting, 0–6 hours) relies heavily on radar and satellite extrapolation, crucial for severe-weather warnings.

Uses

Forecasts serve agriculture, aviation, shipping, energy management, disaster preparedness, and daily public life, and are communicated through weather apps, broadcasts, and warning systems.
