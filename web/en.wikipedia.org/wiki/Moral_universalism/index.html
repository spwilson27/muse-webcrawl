Moral universalism

Moral universalism (also called moral objectivism or universal morality) is the meta-ethical position that some system of ethics, or a universal ethic, applies universally — that is, to all individuals regardless of culture, race, sex, religion, nationality, sexual orientation, or any other distinguishing feature. Moral universalism is opposed to moral nihilism and moral relativism, according to which moral judgments are relative to particular cultures or individuals.

Overview

Universalist frameworks hold that certain moral truths and obligations bind everyone. The idea has ancient roots: the Stoics taught a cosmopolitan ethic based on universal reason, and many religious traditions teach divinely given moral laws for all humanity. In modern philosophy, Immanuel Kant's categorical imperative is a paradigmatic universalist principle: one should act only according to maxims that one can consistently will to become universal law. Utilitarianism is also universalist in scope, weighing the welfare of all affected persons impartially.

Universal Declaration of Human Rights

The most prominent modern institutional expression of moral universalism is the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in 1948. The Declaration asserts rights that belong to all human beings "without distinction of any kind", and underlies subsequent international human rights law.

Criticism

Critics argue that supposed universal values may in fact reflect the norms of dominant cultures, and that descriptive moral diversity across societies tells against a single universal ethic. Defenders reply that moral disagreement does not itself disprove objective moral truth, and that some prohibitions — such as those against slavery and torture — command near-universal assent.
