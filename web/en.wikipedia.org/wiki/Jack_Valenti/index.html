Jack Joseph Valenti (September 5, 1921 – April 26, 2007) was an American political advisor and lobbyist who served as a special assistant to President Lyndon B. Johnson and, for 38 years, as president of the Motion Picture Association of America (MPAA). He is best remembered for creating the MPAA film rating system in 1968, which replaced the restrictive Hays Code and remains the framework for film classification in the United States.

Early life and White House service

Born in Houston, Texas, to Italian immigrant parents, Valenti flew 51 combat missions as a bomber pilot in Italy during the Second World War, earning the Distinguished Flying Cross. After the war he co-founded an advertising agency in Houston and became an aide to then-Senator Lyndon B. Johnson. Valenti was in the presidential motorcade in Dallas on November 22, 1963, and appears in the famous photograph of Johnson taking the oath of office aboard Air Force One. He became one of Johnson's closest aides at the White House.

MPAA presidency

In 1966 Valenti left the administration to lead the MPAA, a post he held until 2004. His signature achievement was the voluntary rating system introduced on November 1, 1968, with the original categories G, M (later PG), R, and X; PG-13 was added in 1984 following controversy over films such as Gremlins and Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom, and NC-17 replaced X in 1990. Valenti became Hollywood's chief advocate in Washington, campaigning against video piracy — in 1982 testimony he famously compared the VCR to "the Boston Strangler" as a threat to the film industry — and later championing strong copyright enforcement in the digital era and the Digital Millennium Copyright Act.

Legacy

Valenti's memoir, This Time, This Place, was published posthumously in 2007. He is widely regarded as one of the most effective trade-association executives in American history, and the rating system he designed endures as a central institution of the film business.
