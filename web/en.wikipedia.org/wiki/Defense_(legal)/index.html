Defense (legal)

For the party presenting a defense, see defense attorney. For other uses, see Defense (disambiguation).

In civil proceedings and criminal prosecutions under the common law, a defendant may raise a defense (or defence) in an attempt to avoid criminal or civil liability. Besides contesting the accuracy of any allegations made against them in a criminal or civil proceeding, a defendant may also make allegations against the prosecutor or plaintiff, or raise a defense that, even if the allegations against the defendant are true, the defendant is nevertheless not liable.

The defense phase of a trial occurs after the prosecution phase: that is, after the prosecution, or the plaintiff in a civil case, has presented its case. The defense may present evidence of its own, cross-examine the opposing witnesses, and argue that the other side has failed to carry its burden of proof.

Common law defenses

In common law jurisdictions a defense may be classified in several ways. A negative defense (or failure-of-proof defense) simply argues that the prosecution has not proved one of the elements of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt — for example, contesting identity or disputing that the accused performed the prohibited act. An affirmative defense, by contrast, introduces new facts which, if true, will defeat or mitigate liability even if the prosecution proves its case; the defendant ordinarily bears the burden of production, and sometimes the burden of persuasion, on such defenses.

Criminal defenses

  • Alibi — the defendant was elsewhere when the act occurred.
  • Self-defense — the use of force was justified to protect against unlawful force.
  • Insanity — the defendant lacked the requisite mental state or capacity due to mental disease or defect.
  • Necessity — the defendant acted to avoid a greater harm.
  • Duress — the defendant acted under an unlawful threat.
  • Entrapment — the crime was induced by law enforcement agents.
  • Mistake of fact or mistake of law in limited settings.
  • Statute of limitations and other procedural defenses, including double jeopardy and lack of jurisdiction.

Civil defenses

In civil litigation, common defenses include failure to state a claim, lack of personal or subject-matter jurisdiction, improper venue, insufficiency of process or service, payment, accord and satisfaction, release, contributory or comparative negligence, assumption of risk, and the statute of limitations. In contract cases a defendant may plead impossibility, frustration of purpose, or the plaintiff's own prior material breach.

The defense in adversarial systems

The adversarial system of common law countries assigns each party responsibility for developing its own case. The defense is conducted by a defense attorney (defence counsel), who owes duties of loyalty and confidentiality to the client while remaining an officer of the court. Indigent criminal defendants in the United States are entitled to appointed counsel under the Sixth Amendment, as held in Gideon v. Wainwright (1963) and its progeny; in England and Wales, public defenders and duty solicitors serve analogous functions.

Because the prosecution bears the ultimate burden of proof in criminal cases, the defense may prevail without presenting any evidence at all, by persuading the factfinder that a reasonable doubt exists. Defendants also enjoy the right against self-incrimination, and no adverse inference may ordinarily be drawn from a defendant's decision not to testify.

Civil law systems

In civil law jurisdictions operating under an inquisitorial model, the notion of a "defense" is less sharply separated from the rest of the proceedings, because the court itself directs much of the investigation. Nonetheless, accused persons have comparable rights to present exculpatory evidence, to challenge the lawfulness of the proceedings, and to invoke substantive justifications and excuses recognized by the applicable penal code.
