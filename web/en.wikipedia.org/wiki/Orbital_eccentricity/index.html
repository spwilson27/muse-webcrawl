Orbital eccentricity

In astrodynamics, the orbital eccentricity of an astronomical object is a dimensionless parameter that determines how much its orbit deviates from a perfect circle. It is denoted by e and defined from the shape of the conic section describing the orbit.

An eccentricity of 0 is a circular orbit; values between 0 and 1 give elliptical orbits, with more elongated ellipses as e approaches 1; e = 1 is a parabolic trajectory, and e greater than 1 is a hyperbolic trajectory, as taken by some interstellar visitors. For an ellipse, e can be computed from the semi-major axis a and semi-minor axis b as e = √(1 − b²/a²), or from the apsidal distances.

Earth's orbital eccentricity is currently about 0.0167 and varies over cycles of about 100,000 years, contributing to the Milankovitch cycles that influence climate. Mercury has the most eccentric planetary orbit in the Solar System, at about 0.205.
