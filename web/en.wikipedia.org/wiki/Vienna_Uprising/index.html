Vienna Uprising (October Uprising)
Date6–31 October 1848
LocationVienna, Austrian Empire
ResultImperial victory; revolution in Vienna suppressed
BelligerentsAustrian Empire (Imperial Army) / Revolutionary Viennese citizens, students and National Guard
CommandersAlfred I, Prince of Windisch-Grätz; Josip Jelačić / Wenzel Messenhauser

The Vienna Uprising (German: Wiener Oktoberaufstand) of October 1848 was the final revolutionary insurrection in the Austrian capital during the Revolutions of 1848. It began on 6 October 1848 when Viennese crowds tried to prevent Imperial troops from leaving the city to suppress the Hungarian Revolution, and it ended with the Imperial Army's bloody recapture of Vienna at the end of the month.

Outbreak

When a battalion of grenadiers was ordered to entrain for Hungary, revolutionary sympathizers, students of the Academic Legion, and workers attempted to stop the departure at the Tabor bridge. Fighting escalated; the unpopular Minister of War, Count Theodor Baillet von Latour, was lynched by a mob and hanged from a lamppost. Emperor Ferdinand I and the court fled to Olomouc (Olomouc) in Moravia for the second time that year.

The siege

The city was defended by the National Guard, the Academic Legion, and armed workers under the Polish general Józef Bem and the journalist Wenzel Messenhauser. Field Marshal Alfred I, Prince of Windisch-Grätz, assembled an army of some 70,000 men, joined by Croatian forces under Ban Josip Jelačić. After an ultimatum expired, Windisch-Grätz bombarded and stormed the suburbs beginning on 26 October; Vienna capitulated on 31 October. A Hungarian relief army under János Móga was defeated at Schwechat on 30 October, too late to save the city.

Aftermath

Repression followed: Messenhauser and other leaders were executed by firing squad, and the deputy Robert Blum — despite parliamentary immunity — was shot on 9 November, an act that made him a martyr of the German democratic movement. Martial law was imposed, the revolutionary Kremsier Parliament was later dissolved, and in December Ferdinand abdicated in favour of his nephew Franz Joseph I. The suppression of Vienna, together with the defeat of Hungary in 1849 with Russian help, ended the revolutionary era in the Habsburg lands and ushered in a decade of neo-absolutism.
