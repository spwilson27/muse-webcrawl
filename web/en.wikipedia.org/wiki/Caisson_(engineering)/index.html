Caisson (Engineering)

In civil engineering, a caisson is a watertight retaining structure used to work on the foundations of bridges, piers, dams, and other structures built in or near water, or a prefabricated hollow structure floated into place and sunk to form part of a permanent foundation.

Types

Open caissons are open at the top and bottom and are sunk by excavating material from inside. Box caissons are closed at the bottom, floated out, and sunk onto a prepared bed. Pneumatic (pressurized) caissons have a sealed working chamber kept dry by compressed air; workers enter through airlocks and excavate beneath the cutting edge. Historically, workers in pneumatic caissons suffered decompression sickness — "caisson disease" — famously during construction of the Eads Bridge and the Brooklyn Bridge, whose chief engineer Washington Roebling was crippled by it.

Uses

Caissons allow foundations to reach bedrock or firm strata below water or soft ground. Suction caissons, a modern variant used offshore, are large inverted buckets embedded by pumping water out from inside, and anchor many floating wind turbines and oil platforms. In a different sense, drilled shafts are sometimes called caissons in North American practice.
