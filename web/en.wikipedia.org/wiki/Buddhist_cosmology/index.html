Buddhist cosmology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Subject: Buddhist view of the universe
Key texts: Abhidharma, sutras, commentaries

Buddhist cosmology is the description of the shape and evolution of the Universe according to Buddhist scriptures and commentaries. It consists of a temporal cosmology—cycles of creation and dissolution spanning immense periods (kalpas)—and a spatial cosmology describing the realms in which beings are reborn according to their karma.

Planes of existence

The Buddhist universe is traditionally divided into three planes (triloka), subdivided into thirty-one planes of existence:

  • Kāmadhātu, the desire realm — including the hells (naraka), the realm of hungry ghosts (pretas), animals, humans, the asuras, and the lower heavens of the devas, including the Heaven of the Thirty-Three (Trāyastriṃśa) atop Mount Meru.
  • Rūpadhātu, the form realm — higher heavens inhabited by brahmās, reached through meditative absorption (dhyāna).
  • Ārūpyadhātu, the formless realm — the four highest, purely mental states of existence.

Rebirth into these realms is temporary and conditioned by karma; liberation (nirvana) lies outside the cycle of rebirth (saṃsāra) altogether.

The world system

At the centre of each world-system stands Mount Meru, surrounded by concentric oceans and continents, including Jambudvīpa, the southern continent inhabited by humans. Innumerable such world-systems arise, endure, dissolve and re-form across successive kalpas.

Interpretation

Traditional teachers present the cosmology literally, while some modern interpreters read the realms psychologically, as states of mind. The Buddha himself declined to answer certain speculative questions about the universe, regarding them as not conducive to liberation.
