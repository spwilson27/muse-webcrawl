Actus reus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Actus reus (Latin for "guilty act") is one of the elements normally required to prove a criminal offence — the external, physical component of the crime, as distinct from the mental state (mens rea). The actus reus may be a voluntary act (punching, taking, driving), an omission where the law imposes a duty to act (parental care, contractual duty, responsibility for a danger one created), or a state of affairs (being in possession of a prohibited item, being an illegal entrant).

Elements

Analysis divides the actus reus into conduct, circumstances and consequences: result crimes (murder, criminal damage) require proof the conduct caused the result — applying factual ("but for") and legal (operating and substantial cause; intervening-act doctrines) causation tests, with notorious problems around medical intervention, victim refusal of treatment and eggshell-skull vulnerability. Involuntary conduct (automatism, reflex, duress of circumstance) negates the voluntary-act requirement. The concurrence of actus reus and mens rea in time is itself a requirement, softened by doctrines treating a series of acts as one transaction.
