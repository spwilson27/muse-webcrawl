Mastodon

A mastodon (Mammut) is a term for any proboscidean belonging to the extinct genus Mammut in the family Mammutidae. Mastodons inhabited North and Central America from the late Miocene (or early Pliocene) until their extinction at the end of the Pleistocene epoch, about 10,000 to 11,000 years ago.

Description

Mastodons had a shorter, stockier build than mammoths, with a relatively low and long body, a broader head, and straighter tusks — usually more gently curved than those of mammoths, and in the American mastodon reaching lengths of up to 3 metres (10 feet). Their molar teeth bore cone-shaped cusps adapted to browsing on trees and shrubs, quite unlike the ridged teeth of mammoths and elephants, which grazed on grasses. The name mastodon, coined by Baron Georges Cuvier (from Greek mastos, "breast", and odon, "tooth"), refers to these nipple-shaped tooth projections.

Distribution and behaviour

The best-known species is the American mastodon (Mammut americanum), widespread in North America from Alaska to Mexico, particularly in forested and woodland habitats. Mastodons were browsers, favouring coniferous forests, and ate twigs, leaves, and aquatic plants. They probably lived in family groups similar to those of modern elephants.

Extinction

Mastodons disappeared during the Quaternary extinction event at the end of the Pleistocene, in which many species of megafauna became extinct. Both climate change and hunting by Paleoamerican peoples have been proposed as causes, though the relative importance of each remains debated. Well-preserved specimens, some with preserved hair and soft tissues, have been recovered from bogs in the Great Lakes region.
