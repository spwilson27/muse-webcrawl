Polar vortex

A polar vortex is a persistent, large-scale, upper-level low-pressure area lying near one or both of the Earth's poles. More technically, there are two kinds: the stratospheric polar vortex, about 15–50 km above the surface, and the tropospheric polar vortex, extending from the surface to roughly 10–15 km. The bases of the two vortices are located near the middle and upper troposphere and extend into the stratosphere. Surrounding the polar vortexes are strong westerly winds — a polar jet stream — that keep bitterly cold air bottled up near the poles.

Stratospheric polar vortex

The stratospheric polar vortex forms in the autumn as solar heating declines, strengthens through winter, and breaks down in spring. The Arctic vortex is weaker and more variable than the Antarctic one because of greater planetary-wave activity in the Northern Hemisphere. Occasionally it is disrupted by a sudden stratospheric warming, in which temperatures in the polar stratosphere rise rapidly; major warmings can split the vortex or displace it off the pole, and are often followed, weeks later, by colder-than-average weather in parts of the mid-latitudes.

Tropospheric polar vortex and cold outbreaks

The term entered the public vocabulary in North America during the severe cold outbreak of January 2014, when a weakened and distorted tropospheric polar vortex allowed a lobe of Arctic air to plunge into the central and eastern United States, bringing record-low temperatures. Similar events affected North America in 2019 and, combined with other factors, contributed to the February 2021 Texas cold wave and power crisis.

Climate change

Research continues into whether Arctic amplification — the faster warming of the Arctic relative to the rest of the globe — weakens the polar vortex and makes mid-latitude cold outbreaks more or less likely; the question remains an active area of scientific debate.

Ozone

The Antarctic stratospheric polar vortex isolates the air within it during winter; chemical reactions on polar stratospheric clouds within the cold vortex destroy ozone each spring, producing the Antarctic "ozone hole". The warmer, more disturbed Arctic vortex generally prevents a comparable Arctic ozone hole, though significant Arctic ozone loss has occurred in unusually cold winters such as 2011 and 2020.

Other planets

Polar vortices are observed elsewhere in the Solar System, notably the long-lived polar cyclone systems of Jupiter and Saturn — including Saturn's hexagonal polar jet — and the polar vortices of Venus and Mars.
