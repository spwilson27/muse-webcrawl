N-body problem

In physics, the n-body problem is the problem of predicting the individual motions of a group of celestial objects interacting with each other gravitationally. Solving this problem has been motivated by the desire to understand the motions of the Sun, Moon, planets, and stars. The n-body problem in the general-relativistic setting is considerably more difficult.

For n = 2 the problem is completely solvable (the two-body problem, with Keplerian orbits). For n ≥ 3 no general closed-form solution exists: Henri Poincaré showed in 1887 that the three-body problem admits no additional analytic integrals of motion, a result that seeded the modern theory of chaos. Karl Sundman found a convergent series solution for the three-body problem in 1912, but it converges so slowly as to be useless for practical prediction; in practice n-body systems are computed numerically.

Formulation

For n point masses mi at positions ri, Newton's laws give a system of second-order differential equations in which each body accelerates under the summed inverse-square attraction of all the others. The system has only ten classical conserved quantities: total energy, the three components of linear momentum, the three of angular momentum, and the three coordinates of the uniformly moving centre of mass. These are insufficient to reduce the problem to quadratures for n ≥ 3, and in 1887 Heinrich Bruns proved that no other independent algebraic integrals exist.

History

Newton treated the mutual perturbations of planets in the Principia (1687) and worried that the Solar System might be unstable. Through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, perturbation methods by Euler, Lagrange, and Laplace gave approximate solutions adequate for astronomy. In 1887 King Oscar II of Sweden offered a prize for a solution of the n-body problem; Poincaré won the prize in 1889 with a memoir that, while not solving the problem, revealed the complexity of homoclinic orbits — the first description of deterministic chaos. Sundman's series solution for three bodies appeared in 1912, and Qiu-Dong Wang generalised it to n bodies in 1991, excluding singular initial conditions.

Special solutions

Particular configurations can be solved exactly. In central configurations the bodies keep fixed relative positions while rotating or pulsating; Lagrange's equilateral-triangle solution of the three-body problem is the classic example. In 1993 Christopher Moore discovered a "figure-eight" orbit in which three equal masses chase each other along the same figure-eight curve, proved to exist by Alain Chenciner and Richard Montgomery in 2000; many further choreographic orbits are now known.

Numerical integration

Because analytic solutions are impractical, n-body dynamics is studied by numerical integration: direct summation for modest n, tree codes (Barnes–Hut) and fast multipole methods for large systems, and symplectic integrators that conserve energy well over long times. Such simulations underlie modern work on planetary-system stability, globular-cluster evolution, and galaxy formation; the gravitational many-body problem in astrophysics is often called the gravitational n-body problem.

Other n-body problems

Analogous problems arise for charged particles interacting by Coulomb forces, in plasma physics, and in quantum mechanics, where the n-body problem concerns the Schrödinger equation for n interacting particles and is handled by approximation methods such as Hartree–Fock and coupled-cluster theory.
