Emission nebula

Emission nebula
TypeInterstellar cloud
CompositionIonised gas, chiefly hydrogen
ExampleOrion Nebula (M42)

An emission nebula is a nebula formed of ionised gases that emit light of various wavelengths. The most common source of ionisation is high-energy ultraviolet photons emitted by a nearby hot star. Emission nebulae glow in characteristic colours: the hydrogen-alpha line at 656.3 nm gives many of them a red colour in photographs, while doubly ionised oxygen produces greenish and blue hues.

Types

H II regions are large clouds of ionised hydrogen surrounding young, massive stars; they are the birthplaces of stars. Planetary nebulae are shells of gas expelled by dying low-mass stars and ionised by the hot white dwarf at their centre. Supernova remnants such as the Crab Nebula are the expanding, shock-heated debris of stellar explosions.

Observation

Among the most famous emission nebulae are the Orion Nebula (M42), the Lagoon Nebula (M8), the Eagle Nebula (M16) — site of the "Pillars of Creation" — and the Carina Nebula. Because emission nebulae radiate in discrete spectral lines, astronomers use narrowband filters to isolate their light even from light-polluted skies.
