Adi Shakti

Adi Shakti (Sanskrit: आदि शक्ति, "Primordial Power"), also called Adi Parashakti, is the supreme goddess in the Shakta tradition of Hinduism. She is regarded as the primordial cosmic energy and the source from which the universe and all deities — including the Trimurti of Brahma, Vishnu, and Shiva — emanate. In Shakta theology she is identified with the ultimate reality (Brahman) conceived in feminine form, and all goddesses such as Parvati, Lakshmi, Saraswati, and Durga are understood as her manifestations.

Theology

Shakta texts such as the Devi Mahatmya and the Devi-Bhagavata Purana present Adi Shakti as both transcendent and immanent: she is beyond all attributes (nirguna) yet also takes form with attributes (saguna) to create, preserve, and dissolve the cosmos. The famous hymn of the Rigveda, the Devi Sukta, is read by Shaktas as the Goddess's own declaration that she pervades and rules all creation. In this framework the male deities are inert without her energizing power; the union of Shiva and Shakti symbolizes consciousness and energy, neither complete without the other.

Manifestations

Adi Shakti is said to manifest in three principal forms corresponding to the cosmic functions: Mahakali (destruction and time), Mahalakshmi (preservation and prosperity), and Mahasaraswati (creation and knowledge). She further manifests as the ten Mahavidyas, as the warrior goddess Durga who slays the buffalo demon Mahishasura, and in fierce forms such as Kali. In Kundalini yoga traditions, the same Shakti lies dormant at the base of the spine in every person and rises through the chakras to unite with Shiva at the crown.

Worship

Devotion to Adi Shakti is expressed in festivals such as Navaratri, when her nine forms (Navadurga) are honored over nine nights, and at Shakti Pitha pilgrimage sites marking places where parts of the goddess Sati's body are believed to have fallen. The symbol of Adi Shakti — a sword vertically bisecting a circle flanked by two curved swords — was popularized in the Sikh tradition by Yogi Bhajan as the emblem of the Khalsa's feminine divine power, and it is also used in Kundalini yoga as taught in the West.
