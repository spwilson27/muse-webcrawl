Johannes Diderik van der Waals (23 November 1837 – 8 March 1923) was a Dutch theoretical physicist and thermodynamicist famous for his pioneering work on the equation of state for gases and liquids. His name is associated with the van der Waals forces (intermolecular attractions) and the van der Waals equation. He won the 1910 Nobel Prize in Physics "for his work on the equation of state for gases and liquids."

Life and career

Largely self-taught — he worked as a schoolteacher while studying at Leiden in his spare time — van der Waals received his doctorate in 1873 for a dissertation, On the Continuity of the Gaseous and Liquid State, that introduced an equation correcting the ideal gas law for the finite volume of molecules and the attractive forces between them:

(p + a/V²)(V − b) = RT

The work immediately drew praise from James Clerk Maxwell and guided Kamerlingh Onnes's later liquefaction of helium. In 1877 van der Waals became the first professor of physics at the newly founded University of Amsterdam, where he remained until his retirement in 1908; his son Johannes Diderik van der Waals Jr. succeeded him in the chair.

Contributions

Beyond the equation of state, van der Waals formulated the law of corresponding states (1880), showing that all fluids behave similarly when scaled by their critical temperature, pressure, and volume; developed a thermodynamic theory of capillarity; and contributed to the theory of binary mixtures. The weak intermolecular attractions he postulated — now called van der Waals forces — and the van der Waals radius of atoms remain standard concepts in chemistry and physics.
