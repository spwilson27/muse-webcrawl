In poetry, meter (Commonwealth spelling) or metre is the basic rhythmic structure of a verse or lines in a verse. Many traditional verse forms are based on a recurring pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables, or, in classical Greek and Latin poetry, on the arrangement of long and short syllables (quantitative meter).

Feet and line length

The basic unit of meter is the foot, a combination of stressed and unstressed syllables. The most common feet in English are the iamb (unstressed–stressed), trochee (stressed–unstressed), dactyl (stressed–unstressed–unstressed), anapaest (unstressed–unstressed–stressed) and spondee (stressed–stressed). Lines are named by the number of feet they contain: monometer (1), dimeter (2), trimeter (3), tetrameter (4), pentameter (5), hexameter (6), heptameter (7) and octameter (8).

Iambic pentameter

The most common meter in English-language poetry is iambic pentameter: five iambic feet per line. It is the meter of Chaucer's Canterbury Tales, Shakespeare's plays and sonnets, and Milton's Paradise Lost (in blank verse, i.e. unrhymed iambic pentameter).

Shall I compare thee to a summer's day? (Sonnet 18, William Shakespeare)

History

Classical Greek and Latin epic used the quantitative dactylic hexameter. After the shift in European languages toward stress-based rhythm, accentual-syllabic verse predominated in English, German and other traditions, while Romance-language poetry is generally syllabic. In the 20th century, free verse abandoned regular meter in much modern poetry, though metrical composition continues alongside it.

Scansion

Scansion is the analysis of a line of verse into its metrical feet, marking stressed (/) and unstressed (∪) syllables. Scansion reveals substitutions — departures from the base pattern, such as a trochee in the first foot of an iambic line — which poets use for emphasis and variety.
