The Seneca (Seneca: Onöñda'gaga', "People of the Great Hill") are a group of Indigenous people native to North America, historically inhabiting the area south of Lake Ontario in what is now western New York State. They were the westernmost nation of the Five Nations of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy), and were known as the "Keepers of the Western Door" because they guarded the confederacy's western approaches.

History

Seneca villages centred on the Genesee River valley and the Finger Lakes. During the 17th-century Beaver Wars, the Seneca became the most populous and powerful of the Five Nations, expanding their influence across the Great Lakes and Ohio Country. In the American Revolutionary War the Seneca largely sided with the British; the retaliatory Sullivan Expedition of 1779 destroyed dozens of Seneca towns and crops, and post-war treaties sharply reduced their land base.

Modern nations

Today Seneca people belong principally to the federally recognized Seneca Nation of Indians (with territories at Allegany, Cattaraugus, and Oil Springs in New York), the Tonawanda Band of Seneca, the Seneca-Cayuga Nation in Oklahoma, and the Seneca community within the Six Nations of the Grand River in Ontario. The Seneca language, an Iroquoian language, is critically endangered but the subject of active revitalization efforts.
