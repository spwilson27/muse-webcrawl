Roman Inquisition

The Roman Inquisition, formally the Supreme Sacred Congregation of the Roman and Universal Inquisition, was a system of tribunals developed by the Holy See of the Roman Catholic Church during the second half of the 16th century, responsible for prosecuting individuals accused of a wide array of crimes relating to religious doctrine or alternative religious teachings.

Foundation

Pope Paul III established the institution in 1542 with the bull Licet ab initio, creating a permanent congregation of cardinals charged with maintaining and defending the integrity of the faith and examining doctrines throughout the Catholic world. It formed part of the Church's response to the Protestant Reformation and was distinct from the earlier Medieval Inquisition and from the Spanish Inquisition, which was controlled by the Spanish monarchy.

Activities

The Roman Inquisition supervised local tribunals in Italy and elsewhere, oversaw the censorship of books through the Index of Prohibited Books, and conducted prominent trials, most famously that of Galileo Galilei in 1633, who was condemned for his advocacy of heliocentrism. Over time the congregation's role narrowed; in 1908 it was renamed the Holy Office, and in 1965 it became the Congregation (now Dicastery) for the Doctrine of the Faith.
