Gaius Licinius Macer Calvus (82 – c. 47 BC) was a Roman orator and poet, the closest friend and literary ally of Catullus among the neoteric poets. Though small in stature, he was feared in the law courts as a brilliant, biting speaker and ranked by later critics with the great Attic orators.

Calvus was a formidable advocate despite his youth and short stature.

Life

Calvus was the son of Gaius Licinius Macer, the annalist and politician who was prosecuted by Cicero and took his own life in 66 BC. The son chose the opposite side of the forum's battles: as an advocate he prosecuted Vatinius (defended by Cicero) in 54 BC with such force that Vatinius joked, mid-speech, "Am I to be condemned, judges, just because this fellow is eloquent?" His speeches — twenty-one were known — were studied models of the plain, precise "Attic" style against Cicero's fullness; Cicero, in the Brutus, both praised his learning and blamed his austerity for a lack of blood and body. Calvus died young, in his mid-thirties.

Poetry and Catullus

Calvus wrote an epyllion Io, wedding poems (epithalamia), lampoons against Caesar and Pompey, and elegies mourning his dead wife or mistress Quintilia. Catullus addresses him in poems 14, 50, 53 and 96 — in poem 50 the two spend a whole day exchanging verses on their tablets; in poem 96 Catullus consoles him that if the dead can feel anything, Quintilia rejoices in his love. Only fragments of Calvus survive, including lines from the Io and an epigram on Pompey's self-satisfied thumb-scratching.
