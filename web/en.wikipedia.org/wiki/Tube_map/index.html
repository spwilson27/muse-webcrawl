The Tube map (sometimes called the London Underground map) is a schematic transport map of the lines, stations and services of the London Underground, known colloquially as "the Tube", hence the map's name. The diagram is not to geographic scale and does not show relative positions of stations, but rather their topological relationships: it pioneered the schematic style now used by metro systems worldwide.

History

Early Underground maps attempted to overlay the lines on street geography, producing cramped and confusing diagrams in central London. In 1931, electrical draughtsman Harry Beck, an Underground employee, produced in his spare time a radical redesign modelled on electrical circuit diagrams: stations evenly spaced, lines running only horizontally, vertically or at 45 degrees, with distances distorted to make the centre legible. Initially rejected as too revolutionary, the map was trialled in 1932 and issued to the public in 1933, becoming an immediate success.

Design and legacy

Beck continued to revise the map until the 1960s. Successive versions have added the Victoria and Jubilee lines, fare zones, step-free access symbols, and services such as the Docklands Light Railway, London Overground and the Elizabeth line. The map is a celebrated work of 20th-century information design, widely reproduced on merchandise, and its approach has been imitated by transit agencies around the world. Transport for London guards the design as a registered trademark.
