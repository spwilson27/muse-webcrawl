Seal of confession

The seal of confession (also sacramental seal or seal of the confessional) is the absolute duty of confidentiality imposed on priests in the Catholic Church — and in various forms in other churches that practise auricular confession — forbidding them from revealing anything learned in the sacrament of Penance. Under the Catholic Church's canon law (canon 983), the seal is inviolable: a confessor may not betray a penitent "in words or in any manner and for any reason", and violation incurs automatic excommunication reserved to the Holy See.

History and scope

The obligation developed with private confession in the early medieval church and was solemnly affirmed by the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215, which mandated annual confession. Confessors have historically suffered imprisonment and death rather than break the seal; St John of Nepomuk is venerated as a martyr of the seal, and modern cases include priests imprisoned in the 20th century for refusing to disclose confessions.

Legal conflicts

Many legal systems recognise some form of clergy–penitent privilege, but the absoluteness of the seal has produced conflict, especially over mandatory reporting laws for child abuse. Several Australian states and proposed laws elsewhere have required clergy to report abuse disclosed in confession, which the Church has resisted, maintaining that the seal admits no exceptions and that a priest must accept legal penalties instead.
