Continuously variable transmission

A continuously variable transmission (CVT) is an automatic transmission that can change seamlessly through a continuous range of gear ratios, in contrast to conventional transmissions that offer a fixed set of discrete ratios. This allows the engine to operate near its most efficient or most powerful speed across a wide range of vehicle speeds, improving fuel economy and providing smooth acceleration without shift shocks.

CVT
ClassificationAutomatic transmission
Main typesBelt/chain pulley; toroidal; hydrostatic; electric
ApplicationsPassenger cars, scooters, snowmobiles, agricultural and industrial machinery

Types

  • Pulley-based (belt or chain) CVT: two variable-diameter pulleys connected by a steel push-belt or chain; changing the pulley widths changes the ratio. This is the dominant automotive design (e.g., in many Nissan, Honda, Toyota and Subaru models).
  • Toroidal CVT: rollers transmit power between input and output discs; tilting the rollers varies the ratio (e.g., Nissan's Extroid).
  • Hydrostatic CVT: a variable-displacement pump drives a hydraulic motor, common in tractors and lawn equipment.
  • Electric CVT (eCVT): power-split devices using a planetary gearset and motor-generators, as in Toyota's Hybrid Synergy Drive, achieve a variable effective ratio without a belt.

History

Leonardo da Vinci sketched a stepless transmission in 1490, and friction-disc CVTs appeared on early automobiles. The modern belt CVT was commercialized by the Dutch firm DAF, whose 1958 DAF 600 used the rubber-belt Variomatic; the metal push-belt developed by Van Doorne made higher-power applications practical. A Williams Formula One test car with a CVT in 1993 lapped so effectively that the technology was banned from the sport.

Advantages and disadvantages

Benefits include smooth acceleration, optimized engine operating points and fewer parts than multi-speed automatics. Drawbacks include limited torque capacity, belt losses at some operating points, a characteristic sustained high engine note under acceleration (the "rubber-band" feel disliked by some drivers), and historically higher repair costs; many manufacturers add simulated "steps" in software to mimic conventional gear changes.
