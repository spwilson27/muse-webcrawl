Underworld

The underworld as imagined in classical mythology: a gloomy realm below the earth.

The underworld, also known as the netherworld, is the supernatural world of the dead in various religious traditions and myths, located below the world of the living. The concept is found in nearly all civilisations and "may be as old as humanity itself". Common features of underworld myths include the journey of a hero or deity into the realm of the dead, a river or boundary separating the living from the dead, a judge or ruler of the dead, and varying regions of reward or punishment.

In world mythologies

Ancient Egypt

In Egyptian mythology the underworld is the Duat, through which the sun god Ra travelled each night. The dead were judged by Osiris, their hearts weighed against the feather of Maat; those who failed were devoured by the monster Ammit.

Mesopotamia

Sumerian and Akkadian tradition placed the dead in Kur (Irkalla), a dark, dreary cavern where all the dead ate dust and clay, ruled by the goddess Ereshkigal. The descent of the goddess Inanna (Ishtar) into the underworld is one of the oldest surviving myths.

Greek and Roman

In Greek mythology the underworld is the realm of Hades, reached by crossing the river Styx in the boat of the ferryman Charon. It contained the Asphodel Meadows for ordinary souls, Tartarus for the wicked, and the Elysian Fields for the blessed. Heroes such as Odysseus, Heracles and Aeneas were said to have visited it while alive.

Other traditions

Norse mythology has Hel, ruled by the goddess of the same name; Aztec tradition the nine-layered Mictlan; Chinese religion the courts of Diyu; Hindu and Buddhist traditions the realm of Yama (Naraka); and Hebrew tradition the shadowy Sheol. In Christianity, related concepts include Hades, Gehenna and Hell.
