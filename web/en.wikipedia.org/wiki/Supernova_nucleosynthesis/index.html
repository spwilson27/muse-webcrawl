Supernova nucleosynthesis

Supernova nucleosynthesis is the creation of new chemical elements during a supernova explosion. It is responsible for the production and dispersal of many of the elements heavier than those formed in ordinary stellar nucleosynthesis, and it seeds the interstellar medium with the material from which later generations of stars, planets, and life are formed.

Explosive burning in the star

In a massive star approaching core collapse, successive shells burn hydrogen, helium, carbon, neon, oxygen, and silicon, building an iron core. When the shock wave from the core's collapse races outward, it heats the surrounding shells to billions of degrees for a few seconds, causing explosive nucleosynthesis: explosive oxygen and silicon burning synthesise large quantities of iron-group elements, notably nickel-56, whose radioactive decay (through cobalt-56 to iron-56) powers the supernova's light curve. In Type Ia supernovae, the runaway fusion of a white dwarf produces a similar, even larger yield of iron-group elements.

The r-process

The intense flux of free neutrons in the hot, neutron-rich material near the collapsed core (and in neutron star mergers) drives the r-process (rapid neutron capture), in which seed nuclei absorb many neutrons before beta decay can occur, building the heaviest elements such as gold, platinum, uranium, and thorium. Astronomers long attributed the r-process principally to supernovae; the 2017 kilonova GW170817 confirmed that neutron star mergers are also a major site, and the relative contributions remain an active research question.

Other processes

Neutrino interactions in the supernova envelope drive the ν-process, producing rare isotopes such as boron-11 and fluorine-19, while the p-process (photodisintegration) in hot oxygen/neon layers accounts for certain proton-rich stable isotopes. Supernova ejecta also carry the products of the star's earlier hydrostatic burning, including carbon, oxygen, and the alpha elements.

Observational evidence

Gamma-ray lines from radioactive titanium-44 and aluminium-26, the composition of supernova remnants, presolar grains in meteorites bearing supernova isotopic signatures, and the abundance patterns of ancient metal-poor stars all testify to supernova nucleosynthesis and allow astronomers to test theoretical yields.
