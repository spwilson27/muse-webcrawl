Computer network

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A network diagram of routers and computers linked across a map]

A computer network is a set of computers sharing resources located on or provided by network nodes: they communicate using common protocols over digital interconnections, wired (Ethernet, fibre) or wireless (Wi-Fi, cellular). The Internet is the global system of interconnected networks using the TCP/IP suite.

Networks are classified by scale: personal (PAN), local (LAN), campus, metropolitan, and wide-area (WAN) networks, and by topology (bus, star, ring, mesh) and architecture (client–server versus peer-to-peer).

Foundations

Packet switching (Baran, Davies, 1960s) — splitting messages into independently routed blocks — beat circuit switching for data and underlies all modern networks; the ARPANET (1969) first demonstrated it at scale, and Ethernet (1973, Metcalfe) standardised the LAN.

Performance is characterised by bandwidth, latency, jitter, and reliability; queueing theory and information theory supply the mathematics. Network engineering has become invisible infrastructure: homes, hospitals, exchanges, and governments presume it, and its failures (fibre cuts, BGP misconfigurations, DNS outages) are news events.

Security

Attacks scale with connectivity: worms, distributed denial of service, and routing hijacks are network-native threats, countered by encryption (TLS, VPNs), firewalls, and increasingly zero-trust designs that assume the network itself is hostile.
