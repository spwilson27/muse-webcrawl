Forensic identification

Forensic identification is the application of forensic science to establish the identity of a person, living or dead, or to link a person to a crime scene, object, or event. It draws on a range of methods — including fingerprint analysis, DNA profiling, dental comparison, and anthropological examination of skeletal remains — and its conclusions are used in criminal investigations, disaster victim identification, and legal proceedings.

Methods

Fingerprints

Friction ridge patterns on the fingers are unique to an individual and persistent over a lifetime. Latent prints recovered from scenes are compared against known prints, historically by trained examiners and increasingly with the aid of automated fingerprint identification systems (AFIS).

DNA profiling

Since its development in the 1980s by Alec Jeffreys, DNA profiling has become the most powerful identification tool. Short tandem repeat (STR) profiles generated from blood, semen, hair roots, or other biological material can be compared with reference samples or searched against offender databases such as the FBI's CODIS system.

Forensic odontology

Dental records — charting, radiographs, and restorations — are compared with the teeth of unidentified remains. Bite mark analysis, once commonly presented in court, has been widely criticised and its reliability is now heavily disputed.

Forensic anthropology

When remains are skeletonised or badly decomposed, forensic anthropologists estimate sex, age at death, stature, and ancestry from skeletal features, and may assist with facial approximation.

Disaster victim identification

Mass fatality events follow standardised protocols, notably INTERPOL's Disaster Victim Identification (DVI) system, which reconciles ante-mortem records with post-mortem findings, using fingerprints, dental records, and DNA as primary identifiers.

Reliability and reform

Identification disciplines have come under scrutiny: a landmark 2009 report by the US National Academy of Sciences criticised the weak scientific foundations of several pattern-matching fields, including bite marks and hair microscopy, and even fingerprint examination — long presented as infallible — has documented cases of erroneous identification, most famously that of Brandon Mayfield in connection with the 2004 Madrid train bombings. DNA evidence has also exonerated many wrongly convicted people, prompting reforms in how forensic identification is validated and presented in court.
