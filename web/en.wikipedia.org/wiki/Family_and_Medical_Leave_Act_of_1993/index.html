The Family and Medical Leave Act of 1993 (FMLA) is a United States labor law requiring covered employers to provide employees with job-protected, unpaid leave for qualified medical and family reasons. These reasons include personal or family illness, family military leave, pregnancy, adoption, or the foster care placement of a child. The FMLA was a major part of President Bill Clinton's first-term agenda and was the first bill he signed into law, on February 5, 1993.

Provisions

The Act entitles eligible employees of covered employers to take up to 12 workweeks of unpaid, job-protected leave in a 12-month period for the birth or adoption of a child, to care for a spouse, child or parent with a serious health condition, or because of the employee's own serious health condition. Up to 26 weeks are available to care for a covered servicemember with a serious injury or illness. During leave, group health benefits must be maintained.

Eligibility

The FMLA applies to public agencies and private employers with 50 or more employees. An employee is eligible after working for the employer for at least 12 months and at least 1,250 hours, at a worksite where at least 50 employees work within 75 miles. In 2015, regulations extended FMLA spousal rights to same-sex marriages regardless of the state of residence.

History

Family leave legislation was debated in Congress from the mid-1980s; bills passed in 1991 and 1992 were vetoed by President George H. W. Bush before the 1993 act was signed. The United States remains unusual among wealthy nations in mandating only unpaid leave at the federal level, though several states and the District of Columbia have since enacted paid family leave insurance programs.
