Medea is a figure in Greek mythology, an enchantress and princess of Colchis, daughter of King Aeëtes and granddaughter of the sun god Helios. She is best known for helping Jason win the Golden Fleece and, after he abandoned her, for exacting a terrible revenge — the story dramatised by Euripides in his tragedy Medea, first performed in 431 BC.

Medea, sorceress of Colchis.

Myth

Struck by love for Jason through the machinations of Hera and Aphrodite, Medea betrayed her father to help the Argonauts: she gave Jason the means to yoke the fire-breathing bulls, sow the dragon's teeth and overcome the sleepless dragon guarding the Fleece. Fleeing Colchis, she delayed pursuit — in some versions by killing and dismembering her young brother Absyrtus. In Iolcus she tricked the daughters of the usurper Pelias into killing their father by a false rite of rejuvenation.

In Corinth

Exiled to Corinth, Medea lived with Jason for ten years and bore him two sons. When Jason left her to marry Glauce, daughter of King Creon, Medea sent the bride a poisoned robe and crown that burned Glauce and Creon to death — and then, in Euripides' harrowing climax, killed her own children to complete Jason's ruin before escaping in a chariot drawn by dragons, a gift of her grandfather Helios. She later found refuge in Athens with King Aegeus.

Interpretation and legacy

Medea has been read as a barbarian outsider wronged by Greek faithlessness, a terrifying image of female passion and intellect, and — in modern feminist readings — a protest against the powerlessness of women in marriage. Her story was retold by Apollonius Rhodius in the Argonautica, by Seneca, and in countless operas, plays and films.
