Hall–Héroult process

The Hall–Héroult process is the major industrial process for smelting aluminium. It involves dissolving aluminium oxide (alumina), obtained from bauxite via the Bayer process, in molten cryolite and electrolysing the mixture to produce pure aluminium metal. It was invented independently and nearly simultaneously in 1886 by the American Charles Martin Hall and the Frenchman Paul Héroult — both aged 23 at the time.

Process

Electrolysis takes place in carbon-lined steel cells ("pots") at about 940–980 °C. Alumina is dissolved in cryolite (Na3AlF6), which lowers its melting point from over 2,000 °C. When a strong direct current is passed between carbon anodes and the cell lining, molten aluminium collects at the cathode at the bottom of the pot and is siphoned off, while oxygen released at the anodes burns the carbon to carbon dioxide, so the anodes are gradually consumed. The process is very electricity-intensive — roughly 13–15 kWh per kilogram of metal — which is why smelters are usually sited near cheap power supplies such as hydroelectric plants.

Impact

Before 1886 aluminium was a precious metal; the Hall–Héroult process collapsed its price and turned it into a ubiquitous structural material. It remains, with only incremental improvements, the sole commercial route to primary aluminium, although its perfluorocarbon and CO2 emissions have driven research into inert anodes.
