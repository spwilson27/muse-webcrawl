Lure coursing

TypeDog sport
Invented1970s, California, United States
EquipmentMotor-driven lure machine, artificial lure, open field
Primary breedsSighthounds (Greyhound, Whippet, Saluki, etc.)

Lure coursing is a sport for dogs that involves chasing a mechanically operated lure. Competition lure coursing, designed primarily for sighthound breeds, simulates the chase of live prey — usually a rabbit or hare — by dragging an artificial lure, typically a white plastic bag, across an open field along a predetermined course. The course is laid out with pulleys so that the lure zig-zags, mimicking the evasive movements of fleeing game.

History

The modern sport was developed in the early 1970s in California by Lyle Gillette and other coursing enthusiasts who wanted a safe, controlled way to test the hunting instincts of sighthounds without live game. The American Sighthound Field Association (ASFA) was founded in 1972 to sanction trials, and the sport later spread internationally, with the American Kennel Club (AKC) and other registries also offering events. Many events now admit all breeds, not only sighthounds.

Competition

In a trial, dogs usually run in pairs or trios of the same breed or stake. Judges score the dogs on criteria such as speed, agility, endurance, enthusiasm and follow — the ability to stay with the lure through cuts and turns. The lure is pulled by a continuous loop of line driven by a motor, and the lure operator adjusts speed to keep the lure just ahead of the dogs. Courses typically measure 600 to 1,000 yards.
