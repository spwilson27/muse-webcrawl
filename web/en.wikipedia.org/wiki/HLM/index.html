An habitation à loyer modéré (HLM, French pronunciation: [aʃ.ɛl.ɛm]; "housing at moderated rent"), is a form of low-income housing in France, essentially social housing let at below-market rents by dedicated public or private non-profit landlords (organismes HLM). HLMs house around ten million people — roughly one in six households in France — and are a major, politically sensitive component of the French housing system and of the collective memory of post-war urbanisation.

History

The origins of French social housing lie in the habitations à bon marché (HBM, "cheap housing") encouraged by the Siegfried law of 1894, which allowed municipalities and companies to build workers' housing with tax advantages. After the Second World War, France faced a severe housing crisis: war damage, a baby boom and rural exodus left millions inadequately housed. The HLM designation replaced HBM in 1950, and the state launched vast construction programmes. Under plans such as the 1953 Plan Courant, industrialised building methods produced the great estates of tower blocks and slab blocks — the grands ensembles — on the outskirts of Paris, Lyon, Marseille and other cities, housing much of the new industrial workforce, including many immigrant families.

From the 1970s the emphasis shifted from building huge estates to renovating the existing stock and constructing smaller, more dispersed schemes. Many of the grands ensembles became associated in the public mind with poverty, unemployment and the social tensions of the banlieues. Successive urban renewal programmes, notably those run by the national agency ANRU since 2003, have demolished the most stigmatised towers and replaced them with lower-rise mixed housing.

Organisation

HLM dwellings are built and managed by about 600 organisations: public offices (offices publics de l'habitat) attached to local authorities, and private non-profit companies (entreprises sociales pour l'habitat). Construction is financed largely through long-term loans funded by the Livret A savings scheme via the Caisse des Dépôts. Rents are set according to regulated formulas and tenants' incomes; allocations are made from waiting lists, with quotas reserved for the state, municipalities, employers and others who contribute to financing. Demand far exceeds supply in Paris and other pressured areas, where waiting times can exceed a decade.

Legal obligations and policy

The SRU law of 2000 (loi relative à la solidarité et au renouvellement urbains) requires larger towns in urban areas to ensure that at least 20% — raised to 25% for many communes by later legislation — of their housing is social housing, with financial penalties for those that fall short; enforcement against affluent suburbs that resist building HLMs is a recurring theme in French politics. The 2007 DALO law created an enforceable right to housing for the most vulnerable households. Debates continue over allocation fairness, concentration of poverty, energy renovation of the stock, and the balance between social mix and housing those in greatest need.

In popular culture

The HLM estates are the setting of much French film, literature and rap music about the banlieues, from the film La Haine (1995) onwards, and the initials HLM have become a byword — both affectionate and stigmatising — for working-class suburban France.
