The Jewish–Roman wars were a series of large-scale revolts by the Jews of Judaea against the Roman Empire between AD 66 and 136. They comprise the First Jewish–Roman War (66–73/74), the Kitos War (115–117, fought largely in the diaspora provinces), and the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–136). Together the wars ended Jewish political sovereignty in the region until the modern era, destroyed the Second Temple, transformed Judaism toward its rabbinic form, and reshaped the demography of Judaea.

Background

Rome had ruled Judaea since Pompey's capture of Jerusalem in 63 BC, first through Hasmonean and Herodian client rulers, then (from AD 6) through prefects and procurators of equestrian rank. Resentment grew over taxation, procuratorial corruption and insensitivity, Roman symbols in Jerusalem, and tensions between Jews and Greeks in mixed cities. Messianic expectations and movements such as the Zealots and Sicarii fed cycles of unrest punctuated by prophetic movements and unrest under procurators including Pontius Pilate, Cuspius Fadus, and Gessius Florus.

First Jewish–Roman War (66–73)

The revolt erupted in 66 after Florus seized funds from the Temple treasury and clashes in Caesarea and Jerusalem escalated; the rebel victory over Cestius Gallus's legion at Beth Horon turned unrest into war. A provisional government in Jerusalem minted coins and organised defences, while factions (moderates, Zealots, John of Gischala, Simon bar Giora) fought a civil war inside the city. Vespasian, and after his accession as emperor, his son Titus, campaigned through Galilee (fall of Jotapata, where Josephus was captured) and besieged Jerusalem in 70: the city and the Second Temple were destroyed, with mass death from famine, fighting, and enslavement — Josephus gives figures modern scholars treat as inflated but indicative of catastrophe. Rebel strongholds fell successively, ending with Masada (73/74). Titus's triumph in Rome, commemorated by the Arch of Titus, displayed the Temple menorah and vessels.

Kitos War (115–117)

While Trajan campaigned in Parthia, revolts erupted among the Jewish diaspora of Cyrenaica, Egypt, and Cyprus (115–117), named after the Roman general Lusius Quietus ("Kitos") who suppressed them. Ancient sources describe extreme violence against Greek and Roman populations and equally brutal Roman reprisals; Jewish communities in Alexandria and Cyrenaica were devastated, and the great synagogue of Alexandria was destroyed. A related uprising accompanied the fighting in Mesopotamia and unrest in Judaea.

Bar Kokhba revolt (132–136)

The final revolt was triggered under Hadrian — associated in tradition with his foundation of the pagan colony Aelia Capitolina on Jerusalem's site and (in some sources) a ban on circumcision. Under the leadership of Simon bar Kosiba, acclaimed as "Bar Kokhba" (Son of the Star) by Rabbi Akiva, the rebels established an independent administration that minted coins, issued documents, and held much of Judaea for about three years. Rome deployed large forces under Sextus Julius Severus; the war ended with the fall of Betar (135) and mass killing, enslavement, and depopulation — Cassius Dio reports 580,000 killed in raids and battles, with Judaea largely desolated.

Consequences

After 136, Judaea was merged into the province of Syria Palaestina; Jews were prohibited from entering Jerusalem (Aelia Capitolina) except, by later tradition, on Tisha B'Av; the Jewish population centre shifted to Galilee, where the Mishnah was compiled under Judah the Prince (c. 200). The destruction of the Temple in 70 had already ended sacrifice; rabbinic Judaism, centred on prayer, study, and law, replaced the temple cult. The wars also widened the separation between Judaism and the early Christian movement. The demographic and political consequences made the wars one of the defining catastrophes of ancient Jewish history, commemorated in fasts and liturgy to this day.
