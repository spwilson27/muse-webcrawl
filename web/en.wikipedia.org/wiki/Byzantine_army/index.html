Byzantine army

The Byzantine army was the primary military body of the Byzantine armed forces, serving alongside the Byzantine navy. A direct continuation of the Roman army, it maintained a similar level of discipline, strategic prowess and organization. It was among the most powerful and effective military forces in the western world for much of the Middle Ages, evolving from the late Roman field armies through the thematic system of the middle period to the professional tagmata and, finally, the largely mercenary forces of the Palaiologan era.

History

After the division of the Roman Empire, the Eastern army preserved the legionary tradition while adapting to new threats from Persia, the Arabs, Bulgars and Turks. Under Justinian I, generals such as Belisarius reconquered North Africa, Italy and part of Spain. The Arab conquests of the 7th century forced a defensive reorganization into themes (themata), military districts in which soldier-farmers were settled in return for hereditary service.

The thematic system

From the later 7th century, Anatolia was divided into themes commanded by a strategos. The system provided large numbers of locally recruited troops for defense, and formed the backbone of Byzantine military power until the 11th century. From the 8th century, permanent professional regiments, the tagmata, were stationed in and around Constantinople as an elite central reserve under officers such as the Domestic of the Schools.

Composition and tactics

The army combined heavy cavalry (cataphracts), horse archers, heavy infantry and light troops. Byzantine military manuals such as the Strategikon attributed to Maurice and the Tactica of Leo VI codified combined-arms doctrine, emphasizing intelligence, logistics, diplomacy and the avoidance of pitched battle when possible. Elite units included the Varangian Guard, recruited first from Rus' and Scandinavian, later also Anglo-Saxon, warriors.

Decline

The defeat at Manzikert in 1071 and the loss of Anatolia undermined the thematic system. The Komnenian emperors rebuilt a smaller, professional army dependent on pronoia grants and foreign mercenaries. After 1204 the empire's armies shrank steadily; by the fall of Constantinople in 1453 the Byzantine army numbered only a few thousand men.
