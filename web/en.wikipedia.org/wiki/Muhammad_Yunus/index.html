Muhammad Yunus (born 28 June 1940) is a Bangladeshi economist, entrepreneur, and political leader, widely credited as the founder of modern microcredit and microfinance. He founded the Grameen Bank in 1983, which lends small amounts of money to poor borrowers — overwhelmingly women — without collateral. In 2006, Yunus and Grameen Bank jointly received the Nobel Peace Prize "for their efforts to create economic and social development from below."

Early life and career

Yunus was born in Chittagong and studied economics at Dhaka University and, on a Fulbright scholarship, at Vanderbilt University in the United States, where he earned a PhD in 1969. During the Bangladesh Liberation War of 1971 he campaigned abroad for Bangladeshi independence. He later headed the economics programme at Chittagong University.

Microcredit

Shocked by the famine of 1974, Yunus began making small personal loans to destitute basket weavers in the village of Jobra. The experiment showed that tiny loans at manageable terms allowed the poor to sustain small businesses and escape moneylenders. The project became Grameen Bank ("Village Bank") in 1983. Its group-lending model was replicated worldwide and helped inspire the global microfinance movement, though later studies and controversies debated the depth of its poverty impact and the practices of the sector it spawned.

Later career

Yunus popularised the concept of "social business" — enterprises designed to solve social problems rather than maximise profit — through books such as Banker to the Poor (1999) and the Yunus Centre. After years of legal and political conflict with the government of Sheikh Hasina, including his removal from Grameen Bank in 2011, Yunus was appointed Chief Adviser (head) of Bangladesh's interim government in August 2024, following the student-led uprising that ended Hasina's rule.
