The Brittonic languages (also Brythonic or British Celtic; Welsh: ieithoedd Brythonaidd) form one of the two branches of the Insular Celtic languages, the other being the Goidelic (Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Manx). They derive from Common Brittonic, the Celtic language spoken across Great Britain in the Iron Age and Roman period. The living Brittonic languages are Welsh, Breton and the revived Cornish. Together with Goidelic, they are sometimes grouped as "P-Celtic" and "Q-Celtic" respectively, after their treatment of the Proto-Celtic sound *kʷ: in Brittonic it became p (Welsh pen, "head") where Goidelic kept a velar (Irish ceann).

History

Common Brittonic was spoken throughout Britain south of the Antonine Wall. After the Roman withdrawal and the Anglo-Saxon settlements from the 5th century, Brittonic retreated westward and fragmented into distinct languages: Welsh in Wales, Cumbric in the north-west (extinct by about the 12th century), Cornish in the south-west, and Breton in Brittany — carried across the Channel by British migrants, which is why Breton is an Insular, not Continental, Celtic language.

Features

Brittonic languages share the characteristically Celtic initial consonant mutations (Welsh soft, nasal and aspirate mutations), verb-subject-object patterns in early forms (modern Welsh is largely VSO in literary registers), inflected prepositions, and a vigesimal counting element. They preserve a wealth of place-names across England — rivers such as the Thames, Avon and Derwent, and towns in -combe and -chester overlay.

Survival

Welsh, with several hundred thousand speakers, is by far the strongest Brittonic language, official in Wales and supported by Welsh-medium education and media. Breton, spoken in western Brittany, is classified by UNESCO as severely endangered despite immersion schooling. Cornish died out as a community language by the late 18th century (Dolly Pentreath, d. 1777, is often called its last fluent native speaker) but has been revived since the early 20th century and now has a small community of speakers and official recognition.
