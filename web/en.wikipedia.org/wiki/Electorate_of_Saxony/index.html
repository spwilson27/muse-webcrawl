Electorate of Saxony

Electorate of Saxony
Status: State of the Holy Roman Empire
Capital: Dresden (from 1464); Wittenberg (earlier)
Period: 1356–1806
Religion: Roman Catholic; Lutheran from the Reformation

The Electorate of Saxony (German: Kurfürstentum Sachsen) was a state of the Holy Roman Empire whose ruler, the Elector of Saxony, was one of the prince-electors entitled to elect the Holy Roman Emperor. It existed from the Golden Bull of 1356 until 1806, when it was raised to the Kingdom of Saxony.

History

The Golden Bull of 1356 confirmed the Ascanian dukes of Saxe-Wittenberg as electors. When the Ascanian line died out in 1423, Emperor Sigismund granted the electorate to Frederick I of the House of Wettin, Margrave of Meissen. The Leipzig Partition of 1485 split the Wettin lands between the Ernestine and Albertine lines; after the Schmalkaldic War, the electoral dignity passed in 1547 from the Ernestines to the Albertine line, which ruled from Dresden until 1806.

Saxony was a cradle of the Protestant Reformation: Elector Frederick the Wise protected Martin Luther, and Wittenberg became the movement's centre. Under Augustus the Strong (elector from 1694), who also became King of Poland, Dresden was rebuilt as a baroque capital. Saxony suffered heavily in the Seven Years' War against Prussia. In 1806 Elector Frederick Augustus III joined the Confederation of the Rhine and was raised to king, ending the electorate.
