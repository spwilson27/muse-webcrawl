Earth's crust is the thin, solid outer shell of the planet Earth, lying above the mantle. It is the part of the Earth on which all known life exists. The crust is distinguished chemically from the underlying mantle and makes up less than 1 percent of Earth's volume. Together with the uppermost mantle, it forms the rigid lithosphere, which is broken into tectonic plates.

Types of crust

There are two distinct kinds of crust. Oceanic crust underlies the ocean basins; it is thin (about 5–10 km), dense, and composed mainly of basalt and gabbro rich in iron and magnesium. It is geologically young — nowhere older than about 200 million years — because it is continually created at mid-ocean ridges and destroyed by subduction. Continental crust forms the continents and continental shelves; it is much thicker (typically 30–50 km, and up to about 70 km beneath high mountain ranges), less dense, and composed largely of granitic rocks rich in silicon and aluminium. Parts of it are nearly 4 billion years old.

Composition

By mass, Earth's crust is about 46–47 percent oxygen, 27–28 percent silicon, 8 percent aluminium, 5 percent iron, with smaller amounts of calcium, sodium, potassium and magnesium. Most crustal rocks are silicates — minerals built around silicon–oxygen tetrahedra. Igneous rocks dominate by volume, with sedimentary rocks forming a thin veneer over much of the surface.

Structure and dynamics

The base of the crust is marked by the Mohorovičić discontinuity (the Moho), where seismic wave velocities increase sharply into the denser mantle. Through plate tectonics the crust is constantly recycled: new oceanic crust forms at spreading centres, while continental crust grows by accretion and volcanism and is eroded at the surface. Isostasy — the buoyant floating of the crust on the denser mantle — explains why thickened crust stands high as mountain ranges and why it rebounds after ice sheets melt.
