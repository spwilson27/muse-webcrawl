Recombinant DNA

Recombinant DNA (rDNA) molecules are DNA molecules formed by laboratory methods of genetic recombination (such as molecular cloning) that bring together genetic material from multiple sources, creating sequences that would not otherwise be found in the genome. Recombinant DNA is the general name for a piece of DNA that has been created by combining two or more fragments from different sources.

Recombinant DNA is possible because DNA molecules from all organisms share the same chemical structure and differ only in nucleotide sequence. Recombinant DNA molecules are sometimes called chimeric DNA, because they can be made of material from two different species, like the mythical chimera. RDT uses a palette of technologies — restriction endonucleases, ligases, polymerases and vectors such as plasmids, bacteriophages and artificial chromosomes.

History

The idea of recombinant DNA was first proposed by Peter Lobban, a graduate student at Stanford University. The first publications describing its successful production and intracellular replication appeared in 1972 and 1973, from the laboratories of Paul Berg and of Stanley Cohen and Herbert Boyer. Berg's group spliced DNA from the simian virus SV40 into a lambda phage vector; Cohen and Boyer showed that restriction-cut plasmid fragments could be joined and propagated in Escherichia coli, and in 1973 constructed a biologically functional bacterial plasmid containing genes from two different plasmids and an amphibian ribosomal RNA gene. Boyer co-founded Genentech in 1976, and in 1978 the company produced human insulin in bacteria. Concern over safety led to the 1975 Asilomar Conference and subsequent NIH guidelines governing recombinant DNA research.

Production and cloning

In a typical cloning procedure, the DNA fragment of interest is cut with restriction enzymes and joined (ligated) into a similarly cut vector, often a plasmid bearing an antibiotic-resistance marker. The recombinant molecule is introduced into a host cell by transformation; cells that take it up are selected, and as they divide the recombinant DNA is copied along with the host DNA. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) can also amplify target sequences for insertion, and modern methods such as Gibson assembly and Golden Gate cloning join multiple fragments in a single reaction without restriction-site scars.

Applications

  • Medicine — production of human insulin, human growth hormone, erythropoietin, clotting factor VIII, monoclonal antibodies and recombinant vaccines such as the hepatitis B vaccine.
  • Agriculture — genetically modified crops with herbicide tolerance or insect resistance (Bt crops), and animals engineered for research or production traits.
  • Industry — enzymes for detergents and food processing (e.g. chymosin for cheese), and engineered microbes producing biofuels and chemicals.
  • Research — gene knockouts, reporter constructs, DNA libraries and sequencing templates; the foundation of genomics and synthetic biology.

Controversy and regulation

Recombinant DNA technology has been debated since its inception, over human germline modification, ecological release of engineered organisms, food safety and patenting of life forms (addressed in the 1980 U.S. Supreme Court case Diamond v. Chakrabarty). Regulation varies: the United States regulates by product under the Coordinated Framework, while the European Union regulates by process and requires labelling of genetically modified food.
