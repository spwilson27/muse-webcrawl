Color temperature

Color temperature
Unit: Kelvin (K)
Candlelight: ~1,900 K
Noon daylight: ~5,500–6,500 K
Overcast sky: ~7,000 K and above

Color temperature is a characteristic of visible light describing the colour appearance of a light source by comparison with an ideal black-body radiator heated to a given temperature, expressed in kelvins. Low colour temperatures correspond to warm, reddish light such as candlelight, while high values correspond to cool, bluish light such as an overcast sky.

Black-body radiation

As a black body is heated it glows dull red, then orange, yellow, white, and finally blue-white, following Planck's law and the Planckian locus on the chromaticity diagram. Real sources that are not perfect black bodies are described by their correlated color temperature (CCT), the temperature of the black body whose colour appears closest.

Common values

A candle flame is about 1,900 K, household incandescent lamps 2,700–3,000 K, studio lamps about 3,200 K, direct sunlight at noon roughly 5,600 K, and standard daylight illuminants D65 about 6,500 K. Lamps are marketed as warm white, neutral, or daylight according to these values.

Applications

Color temperature guides lighting design, aquarium and horticultural lighting, and photography, where camera white balance settings are chosen to match the scene illuminant. In astronomy, a star's colour temperature indicates its surface temperature and spectral type. Deviations from the Planckian locus are described by a green–magenta tint measure (Duv).
