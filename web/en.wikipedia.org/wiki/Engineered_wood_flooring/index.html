Engineered wood flooring
Type: Flooring product
Composition: Hardwood veneer over plywood, HDF or softwood layers
Invented: 1960s (commercial)

Engineered wood flooring is a type of hardwood flooring made up of multiple layers of wood or wood-derived materials bonded together. It consists of a top layer (lamella) of real hardwood — the "wear layer" — adhered to a core of plywood, high-density fibreboard (HDF), or softwood strips. Unlike solid wood flooring, which is milled from a single piece of timber, engineered flooring is a composite manufactured to be more dimensionally stable.

Construction

Engineered flooring typically comprises three to twelve layers. The top veneer layer is generally 0.6 mm to 6 mm thick; thicker wear layers can be sanded and refinished multiple times, much like solid wood. The core layers are laid with their grain running at right angles to one another ("cross-ply" construction), which counteracts the tendency of wood to expand and contract across the grain with changes in humidity and temperature.

Advantages

Because of its layered construction, engineered flooring is less susceptible to warping, cupping and gapping than solid hardwood. It can be installed below grade (in basements), over radiant heating systems, and directly over concrete slabs — situations in which solid wood is discouraged. It also uses slow-growing hardwood more sparingly, since only the thin surface layer is of the decorative species.

Installation

Common installation methods include gluing down, nailing or stapling, and "floating" floors in which planks are joined by tongue-and-groove or click-lock systems without attachment to the subfloor. Planks are available in a wide range of species, including oak, maple, hickory and walnut, as well as exotic hardwoods.
