Syphilis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Syphilis is a sexually transmitted infection caused by the bacterium Treponema pallidum subspecies pallidum. The signs and symptoms depend on the stage it presents: primary, secondary, latent or tertiary. The primary stage classically presents with a single painless ulcer (chancre); the secondary stage with a diffuse rash often involving the palms and soles; latent syphilis with little to no symptoms; and tertiary syphilis with gummas, neurological, or heart symptoms. Syphilis has been known as "the great imitator" because its presentations can resemble many other diseases.

Signs and symptoms

Primary stage

After an incubation of about three weeks, a firm, painless skin ulcer (chancre) appears at the site of infection, usually the genitals, accompanied by painless regional lymph node swelling. The chancre heals spontaneously within several weeks, but infection persists.

Secondary stage

Weeks to months later, a widespread rash, condylomata lata, mucous patches, fever, sore throat and hair loss may develop as the bacterium disseminates through the blood. These symptoms also resolve without treatment.

Latent and tertiary stages

Latent syphilis is asymptomatic but detectable by blood tests. In a minority of untreated cases, tertiary disease appears years to decades later as gummatous lesions, cardiovascular syphilis (notably aortitis), or neurosyphilis, which can occur at any stage and includes tabes dorsalis and general paresis.

Cause and diagnosis

The causative organism is a thin, spiral-shaped spirochaete that cannot be cultured on artificial media. Diagnosis is usually by blood tests — non-treponemal tests such as VDRL and RPR confirmed by specific treponemal tests — supplemented by dark-field microscopy of lesion fluid in early disease.

Treatment and prevention

A single intramuscular dose of benzathine penicillin G cures most early cases; doxycycline is an alternative for the penicillin-allergic. Late latent and tertiary disease require longer courses, and neurosyphilis intravenous aqueous penicillin. Prevention includes condom use and screening, particularly in pregnancy, since congenital syphilis transmitted from mother to child can cause stillbirth and serious neonatal disease.

History

Syphilis appeared in epidemic form in Europe in the 1490s; whether it arrived from the Americas with Columbus's crews (the Columbian hypothesis) or existed earlier in the Old World remains debated. Historical treatments included mercury — giving rise to the saying "a night with Venus, a lifetime with Mercury" — and later Salvarsan (1910), before penicillin transformed therapy in the 1940s. The disease's natural history in untreated patients was documented by the Oslo study and, controversially and unethically, by the Tuskegee study in the United States (1932–1972).
