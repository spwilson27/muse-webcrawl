Negative verb

In Finnish, the negative verb carries person and number while the main verb appears in a special connegative form.

A negative verb is a type of auxiliary verb used in some languages to express negation. In these languages the negative element itself is conjugated — typically for person, number, tense or mood — while the lexical main verb appears in a reduced, invariant form (often called the connegative). Negative verbs are a well-known feature of the Uralic languages and occur in various other languages of northern Eurasia and beyond.

Finnish

The classic example is Finnish, where the verb ei negates a clause and inflects for person and number: en (I do not), et (you do not), ei (he/she does not), emme (we do not), ette (you pl. do not), eivät (they do not). The main verb follows in its connegative form: Minä en puhu — "I do not speak". Past tense is expressed by putting the main verb's participle after the negative verb (en puhunut, "I did not speak").

Other languages

Related negative verbs are found in Estonian (ei, now largely uninflected), the Sami languages, and Mordvin, and similar constructions occur in some Tungusic, Mongolic and Paleosiberian languages, as well as in languages of other regions. The Estonian negative verb has lost most of its conjugation, illustrating how such systems can erode over time.

The existence of negative verbs is typologically significant because in most of the world's languages negation is expressed by an uninflected particle or by an affix on the main verb rather than by a fully inflecting auxiliary verb.
