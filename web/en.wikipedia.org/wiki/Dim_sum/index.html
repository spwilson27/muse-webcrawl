Dim sum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A yum cha table crowded with bamboo steamers and small plates]

Dim sum is the Cantonese tradition of small dishes served with tea — dumplings, buns, rolls and little plates in bewildering variety — shared at the meal called yum cha ("drinking tea"). The name, in Cantonese dím sām, means roughly "touch the heart": small things, ordered in rounds, that add up to a feast. Its twin benchmarks are har gow, shrimp dumplings in a translucent wheat-starch skin, and siu mai, open-topped pork-and-shrimp dumplings — the two items by which Cantonese tea houses have been judged for a century.

History and ritual

The custom grew from tea houses on the Silk Road routes through Guangzhou, where travellers rested over tea and snacks; by the 19th and early 20th centuries the city's great tea restaurants had turned it into a morning institution of trolleys, stamp cards and multi-generational tables. Etiquette is its own cuisine: pour for others before yourself, tap two fingers on the table in thanks (a gesture legend ties to a disguised Qianlong emperor), flip the teapot lid to ask for a refill. Hong Kong codified the modern form and exported it; in the West, dim sum became many diners' first Cantonese meal, arriving on carts exactly as in Guangzhou.

The repertoire

Beyond the twin standards: char siu bao (barbecue-pork buns, steamed fluffy or baked glazed), cheung fun (silken rice-noodle rolls), lo bak go (turnip cake), Phoenix claws (chicken feet in black-bean sauce — the table's courage test), congee, glutinous rice in lotus leaf, and the sweet finish of egg tarts (a Cantonese-Portuguese handshake via Macau) and mango pudding. Dim sum kitchens run on specialisation — a dumpling master may fold one item for decades — and the meal's economics run on volume, turnover and tea.
