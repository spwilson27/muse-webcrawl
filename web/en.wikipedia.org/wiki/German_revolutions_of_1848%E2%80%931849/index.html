German revolutions of 1848–1849

The German revolutions of 1848–1849, part of the wider Revolutions of 1848 across Europe, were a series of loosely coordinated protests and rebellions in the states of the German Confederation demanding liberal reforms, national unification, and constitutional government.

Course of events

Following the February Revolution in Paris, unrest spread through the German states in March 1848 ("March Revolution"). Rulers conceded reforms and appointed liberal "March ministries". In May 1848 the Frankfurt National Assembly, the first freely elected parliament for all of Germany, convened in St. Paul's Church to draft a constitution for a unified German state.

Failure

The Assembly's proposed constitution was rejected by Frederick William IV of Prussia, who refused the imperial crown in April 1849. Prussian and Austrian armies suppressed remaining uprisings, including the Palatine and Baden insurrections, and the Assembly was dispersed in 1849. Many revolutionaries, the "Forty-Eighters", emigrated, notably to the United States.

Legacy

Although the revolutions failed, they abolished some feudal remnants, prompted lasting constitutional changes in several states, and established ideals of German unity and democracy later invoked by the Weimar Republic and the Federal Republic; the black-red-gold flag of the revolutionaries became Germany's national flag.
