Scleroderma

Sclerodactyly and Raynaud's phenomenon are common features of systemic sclerosis.

Scleroderma is a group of autoimmune diseases that may result in changes to the skin, blood vessels, muscles, and internal organs. The disease can be either localized to the skin or involve other organs as well. Systemic scleroderma is also known as systemic sclerosis. Symptoms may include areas of thickened skin, stiffness, feeling tired, and poor blood flow to the fingers or toes with cold exposure (Raynaud's phenomenon).

The limited form of systemic sclerosis is often summarized as CREST syndrome: Calcinosis, Raynaud's phenomenon, Esophageal dysfunction, Sclerodactyly, and Telangiectasia. The diffuse form progresses more rapidly and can affect the lungs (interstitial lung disease, pulmonary arterial hypertension), kidneys (scleroderma renal crisis), heart, and gastrointestinal tract. Localized forms include morphea and linear scleroderma, which affect the skin and underlying tissues but spare internal organs.

Cause and treatment

The cause is unknown, but it is believed to involve genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers leading to immune-mediated vascular injury and excess collagen deposition (fibrosis). There is no cure; treatment targets symptoms and organ complications — immunosuppressants, vasodilators for Raynaud's, ACE inhibitors for renal crisis, and proton pump inhibitors for reflux. Prognosis varies widely by subtype and organ involvement.
