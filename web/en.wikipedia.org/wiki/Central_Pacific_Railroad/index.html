The Central Pacific Railroad was a rail company chartered by the U.S. Congress in 1862 to build a railroad eastward from Sacramento, California, as the western half of the first transcontinental railroad in North America. Together with the Union Pacific Railroad building westward from Omaha, it completed the Pacific Railroad authorised by the Pacific Railroad Acts of 1862 and 1864.

Founding and the Big Four

The company was promoted by engineer Theodore Judah, who surveyed a route over the Sierra Nevada via Donner Pass, and financed by Sacramento merchants known as the "Big Four" (also called "the Associates"): Leland Stanford, Collis Potter Huntington, Mark Hopkins and Charles Crocker. Groundbreaking took place in Sacramento on 8 January 1863.

Construction

Building eastward meant crossing the Sierra Nevada with tunnels, trestles and snow sheds, then racing across Nevada and Utah. Labour shortages in California led the company, under construction chief Charles Crocker and superintendent J. H. Strobridge, to employ thousands of Chinese immigrant workers — eventually some 10,000–12,000 — whose work at Cape Horn, the summit tunnel and the final push was essential; in April 1869 Central Pacific crews laid ten miles of track in a single day, a record that still stands. The two railroads met at Promontory Summit, Utah, on 10 May 1869, where Stanford drove the ceremonial golden spike.

Later history

The Central Pacific expanded through California and was leased to the Southern Pacific Railroad in 1885, remaining a corporate entity into the 20th century. Its original line over Donner Pass remains a major freight corridor, and Amtrak's California Zephyr follows much of the route.
