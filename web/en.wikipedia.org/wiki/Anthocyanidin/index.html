Anthocyanidin

Anthocyanidins are common plant pigments, the aglycone (sugar-free) counterparts of the anthocyanins. They are based on the flavylium cation, an oxonium ion, with a characteristic 2-phenylchromenylium skeleton. Anthocyanidins change colour with pH — red in acid, purple at neutral pH, and blue in alkaline conditions.

Common anthocyanidins

More than twenty anthocyanidins are known, but six dominate in foods and flowers, differing in the hydroxylation and methylation pattern of the B-ring:

  • Cyanidin — magenta-red; the most widespread.
  • Delphinidin — blue-purple.
  • Pelargonidin — orange-red.
  • Peonidin — a methylated cyanidin derivative.
  • Petunidin and Malvidin — methylated delphinidin derivatives, abundant in grapes and wine.

Occurrence and stability

Anthocyanidins rarely occur free in nature; they are almost always glycosylated as anthocyanins, which are more stable and water-soluble. Free anthocyanidins are formed by acid hydrolysis of anthocyanins in the laboratory. Their colour depends on pH, metal ions and co-pigmentation, which accounts for much of the red-to-blue variation in flowers and autumn leaves.
