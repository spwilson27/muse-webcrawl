C*-algebra

In mathematics, especially functional analysis, a C*-algebra (pronounced "C-star") is a Banach algebra together with an involution satisfying properties similar to those of the adjoint of a linear operator. A C*-algebra A is a Banach algebra over the complex numbers with a map a ↦ a* satisfying the C* identity: ‖a**a*‖ = ‖a‖² for all a in A.

History

C*-algebras were first considered by John von Neumann and Francis Murray in their work on rings of operators (now von Neumann algebras) in the 1930s and 1940s, and were axiomatised by Israel Gelfand and Mark Naimark in 1943. The Gelfand–Naimark theorem states that every C*-algebra is isometrically *-isomorphic to a closed subalgebra of the bounded operators on some Hilbert space.

Commutative case

By the Gelfand representation, every commutative C*-algebra is isomorphic to the algebra C0(X) of continuous functions vanishing at infinity on a locally compact Hausdorff space X. This duality between commutative C*-algebras and topology underlies the programme of noncommutative geometry, which treats general C*-algebras as "noncommutative topological spaces".

Applications

C*-algebras provide the mathematical framework for the observables of quantum mechanics, and they appear in representation theory, operator K-theory, quantum field theory and the theory of quantum groups.
