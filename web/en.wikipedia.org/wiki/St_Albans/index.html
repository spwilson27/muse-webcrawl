St Albans is a cathedral city in Hertfordshire, England, about 20 miles (32 km) northwest of London. It is named after Saint Alban, traditionally regarded as the first British Christian martyr, who was executed on the hill where St Albans Cathedral now stands. The city grew around the great Benedictine Abbey of St Albans, one of the wealthiest and most powerful monasteries of medieval England.

History

Before the Roman conquest the area was the territory of the Catuvellauni, whose capital at Verlamion stood nearby. The Roman city of Verulamium, founded around AD 50, became one of the largest towns in Roman Britain; its remains include a theatre, hypocaust, and sections of the city wall. After the Roman withdrawal the Saxon abbey was founded, traditionally in 793 by Offa of Mercia. The medieval town was a centre of pilgrimage and played a role in the Peasants' Revolt of 1381. Two battles of the Wars of the Roses were fought at St Albans, in 1455 and 1461. The abbey church became a cathedral in 1877, when the city received its charter.

The city today

St Albans is a popular commuter city with fast rail links to London St Pancras, and is known for its historic market, Roman museum (Verulamium Museum), and high concentration of pubs — it lays claim to England's oldest pub, Ye Olde Fighting Cocks. The local economy mixes professional services, tourism, and education, and the city consistently ranks among the most expensive places to live in the United Kingdom outside London.
