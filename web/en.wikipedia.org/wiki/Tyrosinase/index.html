Tyrosinase

EC number1.14.18.1
NamesMonophenol monooxygenase; polyphenol oxidase
CofactorCopper (binuclear Cu centre)
SubstratesL-tyrosine, L-DOPA
ProductsDopaquinone → melanin
Human geneTYR

Tyrosinase is a copper-containing oxidase enzyme (EC 1.14.18.1) that catalyses the rate-limiting steps in the production of melanin and other pigments from tyrosine. It is widely distributed in plants, animals, fungi, and bacteria. In mammals it resides in melanocytes, where its activity determines skin, hair, and eye pigmentation; in plants and fungi, related enzymes (polyphenol oxidases) cause the enzymatic browning of cut fruit and vegetables.

The binuclear copper centre of tyrosinase binds oxygen and oxidises phenolic substrates.

Reactions catalysed

Tyrosinase carries out two distinct oxidations using molecular oxygen: the hydroxylation of the monophenol L-tyrosine to the o-diphenol L-DOPA (monophenolase or cresolase activity), and the oxidation of L-DOPA to the o-quinone dopaquinone (diphenolase or catecholase activity). Dopaquinone then reacts spontaneously and through further enzymatic steps to form the dark polymers eumelanin and, in the presence of cysteine, the reddish pheomelanin.

Structure

Tyrosinases belong to the type-3 copper protein family, along with hemocyanins and catechol oxidases. The active site contains two copper ions, each coordinated by three histidine residues, which cycle between met, deoxy, and oxy states as they bind O2 and substrate. The first crystal structures of a tyrosinase, from the bacterium Streptomyces castaneoglobisporus, were reported in 2006.

Medical and industrial relevance

Mutations in the human TYR gene that abolish or reduce tyrosinase activity cause oculocutaneous albinism type 1, characterised by absent or reduced pigmentation of skin, hair, and eyes and associated visual defects; temperature-sensitive variants produce the colour pattern of Siamese cats. Conversely, overproduction of melanin is involved in hyperpigmentation disorders, so tyrosinase inhibitors — such as kojic acid, arbutin, and hydroquinone — are widely used in cosmetics and dermatology. In food science, inhibiting tyrosinase-related polyphenol oxidases (e.g., with sulfites, ascorbic acid, or blanching) prevents the browning of produce.
