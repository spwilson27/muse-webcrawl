Château de Vincennes

The keep (donjon) of the Château de Vincennes, the tallest medieval fortified structure in Europe.

The Château de Vincennes is a former fortress and royal residence in Vincennes, on the eastern edge of Paris, France. Built chiefly in the 14th century under the Valois kings, it was for centuries one of the principal residences of the French monarchy and remains the largest surviving medieval royal castle in France. Its 52-metre keep (donjon) is the tallest medieval fortified structure in Europe.

History

The site began as a hunting lodge in the forest of Vincennes used by Louis VII in the 12th century. King John II and Charles V transformed it in the 14th century: Charles V completed the great keep around 1370, built the surrounding fortified wall with nine towers, and founded the Sainte-Chapelle de Vincennes, a royal chapel modelled on the Sainte-Chapelle in Paris, which was finished in the 16th century. In the 17th century, the architect Louis Le Vau added two classical pavilions for the King and the Queen Mother, in the early days of Louis XIV's reign, before the court definitively favoured Versailles.

Over the centuries the castle also served as a state prison, holding prisoners including the Marquis de Sade and Denis Diderot, and later as an arsenal and military post. During the Second World War it was occupied by German forces, who executed hostages there in August 1944. Today the keep and chapel are open to the public, and the castle houses the historical archives of the French armed forces.

Architecture

The fortress forms a rectangle of roughly 330 by 175 metres, enclosed by walls and a moat. The keep stands near the middle of the western wall, a square tower with turrets at its corners, containing vaulted halls on six levels, including the king's apartments and council chamber. The Sainte-Chapelle, begun in 1379 and completed in the 1550s, is a single-nave Flamboyant Gothic chapel notable for its large stained-glass windows depicting the Apocalypse.
