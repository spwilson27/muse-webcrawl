William F. Friedman

Born: 24 September 1891, Kishinev, Russian Empire
Died: 12 November 1969, Washington, D.C.
Known for: Father of American cryptanalysis; breaking the Japanese PURPLE machine
Spouse: Elizebeth Smith Friedman

William Frederick Friedman (September 24, 1891 – November 12, 1969) was a US Army cryptologist who ran the Signal Intelligence Service in the 1930s and helped shape American codebreaking through the Second World War. He is often called the father of American cryptanalysis.

Early career

Born Wolf Friedman in Kishinev (now Chișinău, Moldova), he emigrated to the United States as a child and studied genetics at Cornell University. At Riverbank Laboratories in Illinois he was drawn into cryptography, met his future wife and collaborator Elizebeth Smith, and worked on the famous (and mistaken) claim that Francis Bacon wrote Shakespeare's plays.

Signal Intelligence Service

In 1930 Friedman was appointed to lead the Army's new Signal Intelligence Service, where he recruited and trained a small team that included Frank Rowlett, Abraham Sinkov, and Solomon Kullback. He coined the term "cryptanalysis" and introduced statistical methods such as the index of coincidence and the kappa test.

PURPLE and later work

Under Friedman's direction, the SIS in 1940 broke PURPLE, the Japanese diplomatic cipher machine, without ever seeing the machine itself. Friedman suffered a nervous breakdown in 1941 under the strain. After the war he served as a special assistant at the National Security Agency, and with his wife wrote The Shakespearean Ciphers Examined (1957), which definitively debunked Baconian cipher theories.
