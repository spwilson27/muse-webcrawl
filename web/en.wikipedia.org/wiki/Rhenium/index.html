Rhenium is a chemical element with the symbol Re and atomic number 75. It is a silvery-gray, heavy, third-series transition metal in group 7 of the periodic table. With an estimated average concentration of 1 part per billion, rhenium is one of the rarest elements in the Earth's crust. It has one of the highest melting points of all elements, exceeded only by tungsten and carbon, and one of the highest boiling points.

History

Rhenium was discovered in 1925 by Walter Noddack, Ida Tacke (later Ida Noddack), and Otto Berg in Germany, who detected it spectroscopically in platinum ores and the mineral columbite; the team also reported the element in gadolinite. It was the last stable element to be discovered. The discoverers named it after the river Rhine (Latin: Rhenus). In 1928 the team extracted 1 gram of rhenium from 660 kg of molybdenite. The same group also claimed to have discovered element 43, which they called "masurium", but that claim could not be verified and the element (technetium) was later synthesized artificially.

Occurrence and production

Rhenium does not occur free in nature and has no rich ore mineral of its own. It is recovered commercially as a by-product of copper and molybdenum mining: molybdenite concentrates contain small amounts of rhenium, and roasting them drives off rhenium heptoxide, which is captured from flue gases and processed into ammonium perrhenate and metal powder. Chile, with its vast porphyry copper deposits, is the largest producer, followed by the United States, Poland, and Kazakhstan; world production is on the order of 50 tonnes per year.

Applications

Most rhenium — roughly 70 percent — is used in nickel-based superalloys for high-temperature turbine engine parts, such as the single-crystal blades of jet engines, where additions of 3–6 percent rhenium improve creep strength; scarcity and price have driven aero-engine makers to reduce rhenium loadings. Platinum–rhenium catalysts are central to catalytic reforming of petroleum, producing high-octane, lead-free gasoline. Rhenium is also used in filaments, electrical contacts, thermocouples (W–Re), X-ray tubes, and the radioisotopes 186Re and 188Re are used in nuclear medicine and for treating liver cancer.

Compounds

Rhenium forms compounds in oxidation states from −1 to +7, the widest range of any element group; rhenium(VII) oxide (Re2O7) and perrhenic acid are important starting materials, and methyltrioxorhenium is a versatile oxidation catalyst. Natural rhenium consists of the stable isotope 185Re and 187Re, which is primordial but very slightly radioactive with a half-life of about 4×10¹⁰ years; the 187Re–187Os decay system is used for geological dating.
