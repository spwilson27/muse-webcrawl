First Indochina War
Date: 19 December 1946 – 20 July 1954
Location: French Indochina (Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia)
Result: Việt Minh victory; Geneva Accords; partition of Vietnam
Belligerents: French Union (France, State of Vietnam, Kingdom of Laos, Kingdom of Cambodia) — against the Việt Minh (Democratic Republic of Vietnam), supported by China and the USSR
Casualties: French Union c. 75,000–110,000 dead; Việt Minh c. 175,000–300,000 dead; large civilian losses

The First Indochina War (in Vietnam: Kháng chiến chống Pháp, the Resistance War against the French) began in French Indochina on 19 December 1946 and lasted until 20 July 1954. Fighting between French Union forces and the Việt Minh, led by Hồ Chí Minh and commanded militarily by Võ Nguyên Giáp, began after negotiations over Vietnam's future within the French Union broke down. The war ended with the French defeat at the Battle of Dien Bien Phu and the Geneva Accords of 1954, which partitioned Vietnam at the 17th parallel.

Background

France had ruled Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia as French Indochina since the late 19th century. During the Second World War, Japanese occupation shattered French authority; on 2 September 1945 Hồ Chí Minh proclaimed the Democratic Republic of Vietnam. France returned in force in 1945–46, and after the Haiphong incident of November 1946 — in which French shelling killed thousands — full-scale war erupted in Hanoi on 19 December 1946.

Course of the war

The Việt Minh waged guerrilla war from rural base areas (the Việt Bắc), while the French held the cities and deltas and sponsored an anti-communist State of Vietnam under former emperor Bảo Đại from 1949. The communist victory in China in 1949 transformed the war: the Việt Minh gained a secure border, Chinese advisers, artillery and training, and the conflict became a Cold War proxy war, with the United States eventually funding the great majority of the French war effort. Giáp's forces won a major victory in the Border Campaign of 1950, then suffered costly defeats in open battle in the Red River Delta in 1951 against General de Lattre. French attempts to force a decisive battle in the northwest led to the fortified air-land base at Dien Bien Phu, which the Việt Minh besieged and overran on 7 May 1954 after 57 days.

Aftermath

The Geneva Accords of July 1954 ended French rule: Cambodia and Laos became independent, and Vietnam was temporarily divided at the 17th parallel pending elections in 1956 that were never held. Some 800,000–1,000,000 people, mostly anti-communist northerners, moved south, while Việt Minh forces regrouped in the north. The partition set the stage for the Second Indochina War — the Vietnam War — as the United States took over support of the southern Republic of Vietnam.
