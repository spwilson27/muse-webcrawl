Opposition (astronomy)

Opposition
FieldPositional astronomy
DefinitionTwo celestial bodies at 180° difference in ecliptic longitude as seen from a third body (usually Earth)
Related termsConjunction, quadrature, syzygy

In positional astronomy, two celestial bodies are said to be in opposition when they are positioned at opposite sides of the sky as viewed from a given location (usually the Earth). A body is "in opposition" to the Sun when its ecliptic longitude differs from the Sun's by 180°, so that it crosses the meridian at roughly midnight local time, rises at sunset and sets at sunrise.

Planets

Only bodies that orbit beyond the Earth — the superior planets Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune, and also asteroids and other outer Solar System objects — can come to opposition. Around opposition such a planet is closest to Earth for that apparition, appears brightest, has its largest apparent diameter, and is visible all night, making it the most favourable time for observation. For Mars, whose orbit is markedly eccentric, oppositions vary greatly in distance; particularly close "perihelic" oppositions occur every 15–17 years. During opposition a planet also exhibits retrograde motion as Earth overtakes it.

The Moon and related phenomena

The full Moon is in opposition to the Sun. A lunar eclipse can occur only at opposition, when the Moon passes through Earth's shadow. The opposition effect (opposition surge) is a brightening of a rough surface, such as the lunar regolith, when viewed at near-zero phase angle, contributing to the conspicuous brightness of the full Moon.
