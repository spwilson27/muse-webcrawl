Scutum (shield)

A reconstructed Imperial-era scutum.

The scutum (Latin for 'shield', plural scuta) was the large, curved rectangular shield used by Roman legionaries. It formed a central part of the Roman infantry's equipment and tactics for centuries, from the early Republic through the Empire, protecting the soldier's body from chin to knees when held in close order.

Type: Shield

Used by: Roman legionaries

Dimensions: c. 1.05–1.25 m tall, c. 0.6 m wide

Weight: c. 10 kg (22 lb)

Materials: Laminated wood (plywood-like), canvas, leather edging, iron boss

Construction

The Imperial scutum was built like plywood: three layers of wooden strips glued at right angles to one another, curved into a semi-cylindrical shape, and covered with canvas and leather. A central horizontal handgrip sat behind an iron boss (umbo). Surviving examples, such as the Fayum scutum from Egypt, confirm the layered construction described by the historian Polybius.

Use

In battle, legionaries advanced behind their scuta, using the shield both defensively and offensively — punching with the boss before striking with the short sword (gladius). Overlapping scuta formed the famous testudo (tortoise) formation, which protected against missiles from all sides, including from above during sieges. Painted designs, such as the wings and lightning bolts of Jupiter, identified units and honoured the gods.

History

Earlier Republican scuta were oval; the rectangular curved form became standard around the 1st century BC. By the 3rd century AD the scutum was gradually replaced by flat oval or round shields as Roman tactics and recruitment changed.
