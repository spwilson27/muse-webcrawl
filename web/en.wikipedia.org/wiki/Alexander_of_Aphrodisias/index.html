Alexander of Aphrodisias (fl. late 2nd to early 3rd century CE) was a Peripatetic philosopher and the most celebrated commentator on Aristotle in antiquity, known in later tradition simply as "the Commentator" (a title Latin scholastics later gave to Averroes as well). He held the imperial chair of Aristotelian philosophy at Athens, dedicating his treatise On Fate to the emperors Septimius Severus and Caracalla.

Commentaries

Alexander wrote commentaries on Aristotle's Prior Analytics, Topics, Meteorology, On Sense and Sensibilia and the first book of the Metaphysics, among others, several of which survive and remain standard tools for interpreting Aristotle's text. His commentaries combine close exegesis with independent argument, and they strongly influenced the later Greek, Arabic and Latin commentary traditions.

Psychology: the intellect

In his treatise De Anima Alexander developed his famous doctrine of the intellect. The "material" or potential intellect is, in his reading of Aristotle, not an innate faculty with content but a pure disposition or suitability of the soul, arising from the mixture of the body — and therefore mortal. Only the "active intellect", identified with the divine first cause of Aristotle's Metaphysics Λ, is separate, eternal and immortal. This interpretation — under which the individual human soul does not survive death — became a central issue in medieval philosophy, opposed by Averroists, Thomists and many others in the Renaissance "controversy over the intellect".

Fate and providence

In On Fate Alexander defended Aristotelian contingency against Stoic determinism, arguing that fate is simply nature and that human deliberation and choice are genuinely "up to us". He also held that providence extends only to the celestial order and the preservation of species, not to individual human affairs — a view that provoked rebuttals from Christian and Neoplatonic authors.

Influence

Through translations into Syriac and Arabic, Alexander's works shaped the development of falsafa; al-Farabi, Avicenna and Averroes all engaged with his readings. In the Latin West his commentaries, printed alongside Aristotle in the Renaissance, underpinned the Paduan Aristotelianism of Pomponazzi and his contemporaries.
