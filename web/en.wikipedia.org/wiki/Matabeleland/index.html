Matabeleland

The Matobo Hills, a defining landscape of Matabeleland.

Matabeleland is a region located in southwestern Zimbabwe, between the Limpopo and Zambezi rivers. It is named after the Ndebele people (called Matabele by early European writers), who migrated there in the 19th century under Mzilikazi, a general who broke away from Shaka's Zulu kingdom. The region comprises the modern provinces of Matabeleland North, Matabeleland South, and the metropolitan province of Bulawayo, the region's capital and Zimbabwe's second-largest city.

History

Mzilikazi founded the Ndebele kingdom in the late 1830s after the long migration known as part of the Mfecane, establishing his capital at Bulawayo. His son Lobengula succeeded him in 1870. Lobengula signed the Rudd Concession of 1888, granting mining rights to Cecil Rhodes's associates — a document whose interpretation helped precipitate the First Matabele War (1893–94), in which the British South Africa Company destroyed the kingdom. The Second Matabele War (1896–97), known in Zimbabwe as the First Chimurenga, saw Ndebele and Shona risings against settler rule, ending with negotiations between Rhodes and Ndebele chiefs in the Matobo Hills.

During Zimbabwe's liberation war Matabeleland was the stronghold of Joshua Nkomo's ZAPU and its ZIPRA army. After independence, tensions between ZAPU and Robert Mugabe's ZANU government erupted into the Gukurahundi (1983–87), in which the North Korean-trained Fifth Brigade killed thousands of civilians in Matabeleland — estimates range from several thousand to 20,000 — until the Unity Accord of 1987.

Geography and people

The region is a plateau of savanna and granite kopjes, including the Matobo Hills, a UNESCO World Heritage Site and the burial place of Mzilikazi, Lobengula's royal tradition, and Cecil Rhodes. Hwange National Park, Zimbabwe's largest game reserve, lies in Matabeleland North, near Victoria Falls on the Zambezi. The majority population is Ndebele (amaNdebele), speaking isiNdebele, closely related to Zulu; minorities include Kalanga, Tonga, and Shona speakers. Complaints of political and economic marginalization by the Harare government remain a recurrent theme in the region's politics.
