Taffy (candy)

Taffy is a type of candy invented in the United States, made by stretching and/or pulling a sticky mass of boiled sugar, butter or vegetable oil, flavourings, and colourings until it becomes aerated (that is, small air bubbles are produced), resulting in a light, fluffy and chewy candy. When this process is complete, the taffy is rolled, cut into small pieces and wrapped in wax paper to keep it soft. It is usually pastel-coloured and fruit-flavoured.

Salt water taffy

Salt water taffy is a variety originally produced and marketed in Atlantic City, New Jersey, from the 1880s. Despite its name it contains no significant amount of seawater; the name is generally attributed — in a popular though probably apocryphal story — to an incident in 1883 when a flood soaked the stock of candy seller David Bradley, who jokingly sold the flooded sweets as "salt water taffy". The candy became a famous souvenir of Atlantic City and other seaside resorts.

Production

Traditionally taffy was pulled by hand on a hook, and taffy pulls were social events in nineteenth-century America. Today the pulling is done by machines with oscillating arms, and taffy is sold in many flavours, from vanilla, chocolate and molasses to watermelon and maple.
