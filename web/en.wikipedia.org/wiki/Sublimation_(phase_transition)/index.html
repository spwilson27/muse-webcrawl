Sublimation (phase transition)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Sublimation
TypePhase transition (solid → gas)
Reverse processDeposition
EnergyEndothermic (enthalpy of sublimation)

Sublimation is the transition of a substance directly from the solid to the gas state, without passing through the liquid state. Sublimation is an endothermic process that occurs at temperatures and pressures below a substance's triple point in its phase diagram, which corresponds to the lowest pressure at which the substance can exist as a liquid.

The reverse process of sublimation is deposition (or desublimation), in which a substance passes directly from a gas to a solid phase. At normal pressures, most chemical compounds and elements possess three different states at different temperatures. In these cases, the transition from the solid to the gaseous state requires an intermediate liquid state. The pressure referred to is the partial pressure of the substance, not the total pressure of the entire system.

Examples

  • Dry ice: solid carbon dioxide sublimes at −78.5 °C at atmospheric pressure, producing the familiar fog used for stage effects.
  • Snow and ice: water ice can sublime slowly below 0 °C, which is why snow can disappear in cold, dry, windy weather without melting, and why freezer burn occurs.
  • Iodine, naphthalene and camphor: these solids give off vapour directly at room temperature; naphthalene mothballs shrink by sublimation.
  • Comets: as a comet approaches the Sun, its ices sublime and produce the coma and tail.

Applications

Sublimation is used as a purification technique in chemistry: a solid is heated under vacuum, vaporised, and condensed as purified crystals on a cold surface, leaving non-volatile impurities behind. Freeze-drying (lyophilisation) removes water from frozen food and pharmaceuticals by sublimation under vacuum. Dye-sublimation printing transfers dyes as gases into polyester fabrics and coated surfaces, and forensic science uses iodine fuming—which relies on sublimed iodine vapour—to develop latent fingerprints.
