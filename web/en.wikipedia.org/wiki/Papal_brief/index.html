Papal brief

A papal brief (Latin: breve, "short") is a formal document issued by the pope in the Roman Catholic Church. The brief is a less solemn form of papal letter than a papal bull: it is written in a simpler style, typically on a single sheet, sealed with the Ring of the Fisherman in red wax, and signed by the Cardinal Secretary of State or issued through the Secretariat of Briefs.

Characteristics

Unlike bulls, which were traditionally written in an elaborate chancery script on parchment and sealed with a leaden bulla, briefs are drafted in ordinary Latin (or a vernacular language), begin with the pope's name in a distinctive format — for example, "Clemens PP. XI" — and are dated by the day and the year of the pontificate, "sub annulo Piscatoris" ("under the ring of the Fisherman").

The use of the brief developed in the fifteenth century, under Pope Eugene IV and his successors, as the volume of papal correspondence grew and a quicker, less ceremonious instrument was needed for routine matters. Briefs came to be used for dispensations, graces, appointments, and communications with rulers and bishops, while the most solemn doctrinal and canonical acts continued to take the form of bulls or apostolic constitutions.

Modern usage

Since the reforms of the Roman Curia in the twentieth century, much papal correspondence has taken other forms — apostolic letters, motu proprio documents, and rescripts — but the brief remains a recognized instrument for certain grants and communications, still bearing the impression of the Fisherman's Ring rather than a lead seal.
