Tulum

Tulum (Yucatec Maya: Tulu'um) is the site of a pre-Columbian Maya walled city that served as a major port for Cobá, on the Caribbean coast of Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, in the modern state of Quintana Roo. The ruins stand on 12-metre cliffs above the Caribbean Sea and are among the best-preserved coastal Maya sites; the modern town of Tulum Pueblo and its resort zone lie nearby.

History

Tulum reached its height between the 13th and 15th centuries, late in the Maya Postclassic period. Earlier inscriptions suggest the site, originally called Zamá ("dawn"), was occupied from the 6th century. As a seaport it traded in turquoise, jade, obsidian, copper bells, cotton, and salt by canoe along the coast. A defensive wall protected the landward sides while cliffs guarded the sea approach. The city was still inhabited when the Spanish arrived in the early 16th century but was abandoned by the end of that century, likely due to introduced disease.

Principal buildings

El Castillo ("The Castle"), the tallest structure, served partly as a lighthouse guiding canoes through the reef. The Temple of the Frescoes retains mural paintings depicting Maya deities, and the Temple of the Descending God is named for a recurring figure shown head-downward, associated with the setting sun.

Modern tourism

Tulum is one of Mexico's most visited archaeological sites, and the surrounding "Riviera Maya" has developed rapidly since the 1990s, raising both economic benefits and environmental concerns.
