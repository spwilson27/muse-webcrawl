Memorial Day

Memorial Day (originally Decoration Day) is a federal holiday in the United States for honouring and mourning the military personnel who died while serving in the United States Armed Forces. It is observed on the last Monday of May. From 1868 to 1970 it was observed on May 30. Memorial Day is distinct from Veterans Day, which honours all military veterans, living and dead.

History

After the American Civil War, communities across the country began holding spring tributes to fallen soldiers, decorating their graves with flowers. In 1868, General John A. Logan of the Grand Army of the Republic proclaimed May 30 as Decoration Day, first observed that year at Arlington National Cemetery. After World War I the day expanded to honour Americans killed in all wars. The Uniform Monday Holiday Act (effective 1971) moved the observance to the last Monday in May, creating a three-day weekend.

Observance

On Memorial Day the US flag is raised briskly to the top of the staff, then solemnly lowered to half-staff until noon, when it is raised to full-staff for the remainder of the day. Volunteers place flags at the graves of service members in national cemeteries, most famously at Arlington. The National Moment of Remembrance, established in 2000, asks Americans to pause at 3 p.m. local time. The weekend also marks the unofficial beginning of summer in the United States and is associated with parades, family gatherings, and the Indianapolis 500 and Coca-Cola 600 races.
