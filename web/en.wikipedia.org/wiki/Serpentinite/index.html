Serpentinite's mottled green surface resembles a serpent's skin—hence its name.

Serpentinite is a metamorphic rock composed predominantly of one or more serpentine group minerals—antigorite, chrysotile, and lizardite. It forms by the hydration and metamorphic transformation of ultramafic rocks from the Earth's mantle, especially peridotite and dunite, in a process called serpentinization.

Formation

Serpentinization occurs when water penetrates ultramafic rock, typically at mid-ocean ridges, in subduction zones, or in ophiolite complexes emplaced on land. Olivine and pyroxene react with water at temperatures below about 500 °C to produce serpentine minerals plus brucite, magnetite, and hydrogen gas. The reaction increases rock volume substantially, is exothermic, and creates highly reducing, alkaline conditions of scientific interest for hypotheses about the origin of life—for example at hydrothermal systems such as the Lost City vent field.

Properties and occurrences

Serpentinite is typically green, soft (hardness 2.5–4), and has a greasy or waxy lustre and a slick, soapy feel. Major occurrences include the ophiolites of Cyprus (Troodos), Oman, New Caledonia, and the Franciscan Complex of California, where serpentinite is the state rock. Soils derived from serpentinite ("serpentine barrens") are rich in nickel, chromium, and cobalt but poor in calcium and nutrients, supporting distinctive endemic flora.

Uses and hazards

Attractive varieties of serpentinite are used as dimension stone and in sculpture (sometimes marketed as "serpentine marble", e.g. verde antique). Chrysotile-bearing rock is the principal source of asbestos, with attendant health risks when disturbed. Because serpentinization consumes water and produces hydrogen, and because carbonation of serpentinite can sequester CO2, the rock figures in research on carbon capture and natural hydrogen resources.
