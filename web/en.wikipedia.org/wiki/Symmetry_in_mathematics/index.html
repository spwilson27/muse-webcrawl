Symmetry in mathematics

Symmetry occurs not only in geometry but in all branches of mathematics. In modern terms, a symmetry of a mathematical object is a transformation that preserves the object — formally, an automorphism in the appropriate category. The set of all symmetries of an object forms a group, the object's symmetry group, making symmetry a unifying concept that connects geometry, algebra, analysis and beyond.

In geometry

Geometric symmetry includes reflection (mirror) symmetry, rotational symmetry, translational symmetry and glide reflection, which combine in the classification of patterns: there are 7 frieze groups for one-dimensional patterns and 17 wallpaper groups for two-dimensional repeating patterns. Finite objects are described by point groups, including the symmetry groups of the Platonic solids.

In algebra

The permutations of the roots of a polynomial that preserve all algebraic relations among them form its Galois group, and symmetry in this sense governs which equations can be solved by radicals. Symmetric functions and symmetric polynomials — unchanged under any permutation of their variables — form a classical field of study.

In calculus and analysis

Functions may be even (symmetric about the vertical axis) or odd (symmetric under 180° rotation about the origin), a distinction that simplifies integration and Fourier analysis. In differential equations and physics, symmetries of an equation correspond, via Noether's theorem, to conservation laws — time-translation symmetry to conservation of energy, spatial translation to momentum, and rotation to angular momentum.

In other areas

Symmetry appears in probability (exchangeable random variables), statistics, combinatorics (symmetric designs), and logic. In each case, identifying the symmetries of a problem typically reduces it to a simpler one.
