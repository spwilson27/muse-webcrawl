Well-order

In mathematics, a well-order (or well-ordering) on a set S is a total order on S with the property that every non-empty subset of S has a least element in this ordering. A set together with a well-order is called a well-ordered set.

Examples

The natural numbers in their usual order are well-ordered, which underlies proofs by mathematical induction and its generalisation, transfinite induction. The integers in their usual order are not well-ordered (they have no least element overall, and subsets unbounded below have no least), nor are the positive real numbers — the open interval (0, 1), for example, has no least element.

Well-ordering theorem

The well-ordering theorem, proved by Ernst Zermelo in 1904, states that every set can be well-ordered; it is equivalent to the axiom of choice. Ordinal numbers are the canonical representatives of well-ordered sets up to order isomorphism.
