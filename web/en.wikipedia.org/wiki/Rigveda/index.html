The Rigveda (Sanskrit: ऋग्वेद, "praise-knowledge") is the oldest of the four Vedas and the foundational scripture of Hinduism: a collection of 1,028 hymns (suktas) in Vedic Sanskrit, composed — by scholarly consensus — between roughly 1500 and 1200 BCE in the Punjab, and preserved orally with extraordinary fidelity for three thousand years before being widely written down. Addressed to the gods of the early Indo-Aryans — above all Indra, slayer of the serpent Vritra, and Agni, the fire who carries the hymns to heaven — the Rigveda is among the oldest religious texts in continuous use anywhere, and the earliest major document of any Indo-European language.

Structure and hymns

The hymns are arranged in ten books (mandalas), the family books (2–7) oldest at the core. Most praise particular deities and petition them for cattle, rain, sons and victory; but the collection also holds creation hymns of startling reach — the Nasadiya Sukta ("There was neither non-existence nor existence then...") ends in radical doubt about whether even the gods know the world's origin — the Purusha Sukta's vision of the cosmos sacrificed from a primal Man, wedding hymns, gambling laments, dialogue poems, and the dawn hymns to Ushas. The deity Soma, the pressed and deified sacred drink, has the whole ninth book to himself.

Preservation and significance

The text was transmitted by priestly families through mnemonic techniques of formidable sophistication — the hymns recited forwards, backwards, and in woven permutations to detect error — and UNESCO has recognised the Rigvedic chanting tradition as a masterpiece of the oral heritage of humanity. Philologists from the 18th century onward made the Rigveda central to comparative linguistics: its language stands close to Avestan and illuminates the whole Indo-European family. For Hindu tradition the Vedas are shruti, "what is heard" — authorless revelation — and the Rigveda their head.
