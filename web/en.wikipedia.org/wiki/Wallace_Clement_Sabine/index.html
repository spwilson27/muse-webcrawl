Wallace Clement Sabine (June 13, 1868 – January 10, 1919) was an American physicist who founded the field of architectural acoustics. A professor at Harvard University, he derived the fundamental relationship between a room's volume, its surface absorption, and its reverberation time, an equation that remains the standard starting point of auditorium design.

The Fogg Lecture Hall problem

In 1895 Sabine, then a young physics instructor, was asked to fix the notoriously poor acoustics of the lecture hall in Harvard's new Fogg Art Museum. Working at night with seat cushions borrowed from a nearby theatre as movable absorbers, a stopwatch, and an organ pipe, he measured how long sound took to decay to inaudibility as he varied the amount of absorbing material. Over several years he extended the measurements to other rooms and established that reverberation time is proportional to the room's volume and inversely proportional to its total absorption.

Sabine's formula

The result, published in 1900, is expressed as T = 0.161 V/A in metric units, where T is the reverberation time in seconds, V the room volume in cubic metres, and A the total absorption in square-metre "sabins". The unit of sound absorption, the sabin, is named in his honour. Sabine applied his method as consultant for Boston's Symphony Hall (opened 1900), widely regarded as one of the finest-sounding concert halls in the world and the first designed using quantitative acoustics.

Later work

Sabine wrote extensively on sound insulation and theatre acoustics, and during the First World War he worked on aircraft detection and camouflage problems. He died in 1919 following surgery. His collected papers on acoustics were published posthumously by Harvard University Press in 1922.
