Enantiomer

The two enantiomers of a generic chiral amino acid — mirror images that cannot be superimposed.

In chemistry, an enantiomer is one of a pair of stereoisomers that are non-superimposable mirror images of each other, like a left and a right hand. Enantiomers arise in molecules that are chiral — most commonly organic molecules containing a carbon atom bonded to four different substituents (a stereocenter). A mixture containing equal amounts of the two enantiomers is called a racemic mixture (or racemate).

Properties

Enantiomers have identical physical properties — melting point, boiling point, solubility, and spectra in achiral environments — with one exception: they rotate the plane of polarised light by equal amounts in opposite directions, a property called optical activity. The enantiomer rotating light clockwise is labelled (+) or dextrorotatory (d-), the other (−) or levorotatory (l-). Absolute configurations are designated R or S by the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog priority rules. Enantiomers react identically with achiral reagents but often very differently with other chiral substances — including the enzymes and receptors of living organisms.

Biological importance

Life is homochiral: proteins are built almost exclusively from L-amino acids and nucleic acids from D-sugars. Consequently the two enantiomers of a drug can differ dramatically in effect. The classic (though partly mythologised) example is thalidomide, one enantiomer of which was prescribed as a sedative while the other was associated with severe birth defects — complicated by the fact that the enantiomers interconvert in the body. Modern examples include ibuprofen, whose S-enantiomer is the active anti-inflammatory, and levofloxacin, the active enantiomer of ofloxacin marketed separately. Regulators now generally require the stereochemistry of new drugs to be characterised, and many modern pharmaceuticals are sold as single enantiomers.

History and separation

Louis Pasteur's manual separation of sodium ammonium tartrate crystals in 1848 first demonstrated molecular chirality. Separating enantiomers (resolution) remains a practical challenge achieved by crystallisation of diastereomeric salts, chiral chromatography, enzymatic methods, or asymmetric synthesis — for which the 2001 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded (Knowles, Noyori, and Sharpless).
