Negligence per se

In the tort law of the United States, negligence per se is a doctrine under which an act is considered negligent because it violates a statute or regulation. The duty in such cases is defined by the legislative enactment rather than by the common-law standard of the reasonable person.

Requirements

For negligence per se to apply, courts generally require that:

  • the defendant violated a statute or regulation;
  • the statute was designed to protect against the type of harm that occurred; and
  • the plaintiff was within the class of persons the statute was designed to protect.

Depending on the jurisdiction, an unexcused statutory violation may be treated as conclusive evidence of negligence, as creating a rebuttable presumption of negligence, or simply as evidence for the jury to consider. Recognized excuses can include incapacity, reasonable attempts to comply, or an emergency not of the defendant's making.

Origins

The doctrine was famously discussed by Justice Benjamin Cardozo in Martin v. Herzog (1920), in which driving at night without lights, as required by statute, was held to be negligence in itself rather than mere evidence of negligence.
