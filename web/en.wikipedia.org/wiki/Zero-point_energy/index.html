Zero-point energy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Graph of a quantum harmonic oscillator's energy levels, the lowest sitting above zero]
The ground state of a quantum oscillator retains energy ħω/2.

Zero-point energy (ZPE) is the lowest possible energy that a quantum mechanical system may have. Unlike in classical mechanics, quantum systems constantly fluctuate in their lowest energy state, as described by the Uncertainty principle; hence even at absolute zero, systems have nonzero energy. The concept was developed by Max Planck (1911–13, his 'second theory') and by Einstein and Stern, and first clearly formulated for the harmonic oscillator: E₀ = ½ħω.

Zero-point fluctuations of the electromagnetic field have measurable consequences: the Lamb shift of hydrogen levels, spontaneous emission of light by atoms, the Casimir effect between conducting plates, and van der Waals forces between molecules. Summed over all field modes, the vacuum energy density appears to be enormous — the mismatch with the observed Cosmological constant (by up to ~120 orders of magnitude) is the worst quantitative disagreement in physics and a clue that quantum field theory and gravity are not yet reconciled. Claims to extract usable energy from the vacuum ('free energy' devices) contradict thermodynamics as understood and have never withstood testing.

History

Planck introduced a half-quantum of residual energy in 1911 while revising his radiation law; Einstein and Otto Stern (1913) used zero-point energy to explain the specific heat of hydrogen gas. With the advent of full quantum mechanics the concept became unavoidable: a particle confined to a point would have definite position and momentum, forbidden by uncertainty. The vacuum of Quantum field theory is accordingly full of virtual processes, visualised by Feynman diagrams with closed loops.
