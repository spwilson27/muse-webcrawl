Assimilation (phonology)


Nasal place assimilation: /n/ becomes [m] before /p/, as in "input".

In phonology, assimilation is a sound change in which some phonemes (distinct speech sounds) change to become more similar to other nearby sounds. A common type of phonological process across languages, assimilation can occur either within a word or between words, and can be synchronic (an active process in the language's phonology) or diachronic (a historical sound change). The opposite process, in which sounds become less alike, is dissimilation.

Types

Assimilation is classified by direction. In regressive (or anticipatory) assimilation, a sound changes under the influence of a following sound; this is the most common type cross-linguistically. In progressive (or perseverative) assimilation, a sound is influenced by a preceding one. Assimilation may also be total, yielding a sound identical to its neighbour, or partial, in which only some features are copied.

By feature, common kinds include:

  • Nasal place assimilation — a nasal adopts the place of articulation of a following consonant: Latin in- + portare gives import, but in- + legalis gives illegal, and in- + regularis gives irregular.
  • Voicing assimilation — an obstruent becomes voiced or voiceless to match an adjacent obstruent, as in Russian and many other languages' final clusters.
  • Vowel harmony — sometimes analysed as long-distance vowel-to-vowel assimilation, in which vowels within a word agree in features such as frontness or rounding, as in Turkish and Finnish.
  • Coalescence — two sounds fuse into one combining features of both, as when English /t/ + /j/ yields [tʃ] in "tune" in some accents.

Examples

English provides many everyday examples. The prefix in- appears as im- before labials (impossible), il- before /l/ (illegal) and ir- before /r/ (irregular). In connected speech, "ten bikes" is commonly pronounced [tem baɪks] and "good boy" with a doubled or assimilated consonant. In the history of English, palatalisation turned /k/ into [tʃ] before front vowels, giving cheese beside German Käse.

Assimilation and sound change

Many historical sound changes originate as gradient coarticulation — the articulatory overlap between neighbouring sounds — which listeners reinterpret as deliberate targets, a mechanism John Ohala formalised in theories of sound change. Once categorical, such assimilations may become part of a language's phonemic system, as with the umlaut changes in the history of Germanic languages.
