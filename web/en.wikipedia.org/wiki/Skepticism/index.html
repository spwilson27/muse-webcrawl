Skepticism (British spelling scepticism) is a questioning attitude toward knowledge claims, and in philosophy a family of positions that challenge the possibility or justification of certain knowledge. The word derives from the Greek skepsis, meaning inquiry or examination.

Ancient skepticism

In the Hellenistic world two sceptical traditions flourished: the Pyrrhonism of Pyrrho and Sextus Empiricus, which suspended judgement on all non-evident matters in pursuit of tranquillity, and the Academic skepticism of the Platonic Academy under Arcesilaus and Carneades, which argued against the Stoic claim to certain knowledge and developed accounts of the plausible or reasonable as a guide to action.

Modern philosophy

René Descartes used methodical doubt as a tool — doubting everything that could be doubted in order to find an indubitable foundation, arriving at the cogito ("I think, therefore I am"). David Hume raised the Problem of induction and questioned knowledge of causation, the self and the external world; his mitigated scepticism influenced Kant and the empiricist tradition. Philosophical skepticism as a problem — how do you know you are not a brain in a vat, or dreaming? — remains a central test case in Epistemology.

Scientific skepticism

In contemporary usage, scientific skepticism denotes the practice of questioning claims lacking empirical evidence and evaluating them by the standards of science; organised sceptical movements examine paranormal and pseudoscientific claims. Philosophers distinguish such local, methodological doubt from global philosophical skepticism about knowledge as such.
