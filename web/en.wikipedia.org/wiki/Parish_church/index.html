Parish church

A parish church is a church serving a parish, the basic territorial unit of pastoral care in many Christian denominations. As the local centre of worship, the parish church is where baptisms, marriages and funerals are held for the community, and historically it was the focus of village and town life.

Function

In the Catholic Church, a parish church is the seat of a parish presided over by a parish priest under the authority of the diocesan bishop. In the Church of England, every parish has a parish church, and residents traditionally have the right to be married and buried there; the parochial church council shares responsibility for its maintenance. Similar arrangements exist in the Church of Scotland, where the parish church serves a defined parish area, and in Lutheran and other traditions.

History

The parish system developed in western Europe during the early Middle Ages as bishoprics were subdivided for pastoral care. Parishioners historically owed tithes to support the church, and the patronage — the right to appoint the priest — belonged to local lords, monasteries or bishops. England retains some 16,000 parish churches, many of medieval origin, and the churchyard around them long served as the community burial ground. Some parish churches later became cathedrals when a diocese was created around them.

Architecture

Parish churches range from tiny rural chapels to substantial town churches. The classic English plan comprises a nave, chancel, and west tower or spire, often expanded over centuries with aisles, transepts and porches, preserving a layered record of local devotion and craftsmanship.
