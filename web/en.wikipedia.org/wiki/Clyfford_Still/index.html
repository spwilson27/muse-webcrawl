Clyfford Still (November 30, 1904 – June 23, 1980) was an American painter and one of the leading figures in the first generation of Abstract Expressionists, who developed a new, powerful approach to painting in the years immediately following World War II. He is credited with laying the groundwork for the movement's shift from figurative and surrealist imagery toward the radically abstract, large-scale colour field works that defined postwar American art.

Life and career

Still was born in Grandin, North Dakota, and raised in Spokane, Washington, and in southern Alberta, Canada. He studied at Spokane University and later at the California School of Fine Arts in San Francisco. During the 1930s he painted the farmers and landscapes of the Pacific Northwest and Colville Indian Reservation in a somber, figurative style marked by elongated, gaunt forms.

By the early 1940s Still had arrived at the mature style for which he is known: monumental canvases covered with jagged, flame-like fields of dense pigment, applied with a palette knife, in which forms appear torn or flayed, as if the surface of the earth had cracked open. He exhibited with Peggy Guggenheim's Art of This Century gallery in New York in 1946 and was closely associated with Mark Rothko, with whom he taught at the California School of Fine Arts in the late 1940s.

Withdrawal and estate

Increasingly hostile to the commercial art world, Still withdrew from gallery representation in the 1950s and moved to Maryland in 1961, where he painted in relative isolation for the rest of his life. He retained some 95 percent of his output — more than 800 paintings and 1,500 works on paper — in his own estate.

Still's will stipulated that his entire estate be given to an American city that would build a museum dedicated exclusively to his work. In 2004 the city of Denver, Colorado, was selected, and the Clyfford Still Museum opened there in November 2011, displaying the vast majority of his life's work. In 2011, four works from the estate sold at auction to help fund the museum's endowment, with his 1949 canvas 1949-A-No. 1 fetching $61.7 million, then a record for the artist.
