Palamidi (Greek: Παλαμήδι) is a fortress to the east of the Acronauplia in the town of Nafplio in the Peloponnese region of southern Greece. Nestled on the crest of a 216-metre-high hill, the fortress was built by the Venetians during their second occupation of the area (1686–1715) and is regarded as one of the finest achievements of Venetian military architecture.

History

The fortress was constructed between 1711 and 1714 to designs by the French military engineer Lasalle, in the remarkably short period of three years. It consists of eight self-sufficient bastions, each named — mostly after ancient Greek heroes such as Achilles, Leonidas, Miltiades, Epaminondas, Themistocles and Phocion — connected by curtain walls and each able to hold out independently if the others fell.

The strength of the fortress was never fully tested in Venetian hands: in 1715, during the Seventh Ottoman–Venetian War, Ottoman forces captured it through the treachery of its defences, and the garrison was massacred. During the Greek War of Independence, Palamidi was seized in a celebrated night attack on 29–30 November 1822 led by Staikos Staikopoulos, an event commemorated by the liberation of Nafplio. The fortress later served as a barracks and, under King Otto and afterwards, as a prison — the revolutionary hero Theodoros Kolokotronis was imprisoned there in 1833–34.

Description

The fortress is approached either by road or by a steep stairway from the town traditionally said to have 999 steps (in reality fewer, around 857–913 depending on the count). Within the walls stand barracks, cisterns, powder magazines, and the small church of Agios Andreas in the bastion of the same name. Palamidi commands sweeping views over Nafplio, the Argolic Gulf and the Argive plain, and is the town's most prominent landmark alongside the Bourtzi island castle.
