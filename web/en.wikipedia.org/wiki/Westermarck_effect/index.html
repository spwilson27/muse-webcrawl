Westermarck effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Westermarck effect, named after the Finnish anthropologist Edvard Westermarck, is a psychological hypothesis that people who live in close domestic proximity during the first few years of life become desensitized to sexual attraction to each other. It is one proposed mechanism behind the near-universal incest taboo.

History

Westermarck proposed the effect in his 1891 book The History of Human Marriage, arguing against the view of Sigmund Freud, who held that children naturally develop sexual attraction to their close relatives. Westermarck suggested instead that early co-residence produces a natural aversion, making a cultural taboo unnecessary as a primary explanation.

Evidence

Evidence for the effect has come from studies of Israeli kibbutzim, where unrelated children raised together in communal peer groups rarely married each other, and from sim-pua marriages in Taiwan, where girls adopted into their future husband's family as infants had lower fertility and higher divorce rates than other marriages. The phenomenon of genetic sexual attraction, in which relatives separated at birth sometimes report strong attraction on reunion, is also cited as consistent with the hypothesis.
