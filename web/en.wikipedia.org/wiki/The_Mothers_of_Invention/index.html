The Mothers of Invention were an American rock band from California. Formed in 1964, their work is marked by the use of sonic experimentation, innovative album art, and elaborate live shows. Fronted by composer and guitarist Frank Zappa, the band served as the vehicle for Zappa's compositions, which blended rock, jazz, classical music, musique concrète and satirical lyrics aimed at American culture and politics.

Originally an R&B band called the Soul Giants, the group changed its name after Zappa joined and became its leader. Their debut album, Freak Out! (1966), was one of the first double albums in rock music and combined conventional rock songs with extended avant-garde collages. Though the Mothers never achieved major commercial success in the United States, they were popular in Europe during the late 1960s.

History

The group's original line-up included vocalist Ray Collins, bassist Roy Estrada, drummer Jimmy Carl Black and guitarist Elliot Ingber. Zappa disbanded the original Mothers in 1969, later reforming versions of the band with new musicians, including keyboardist Ian Underwood, violinist Jean-Luc Ponty, keyboardist George Duke, drummer Aynsley Dunbar, and vocalists Mark Volman and Howard Kaylan ("Flo & Eddie").

Key albums include Absolutely Free (1967), We're Only in It for the Money (1968) — a satire of hippie culture and the Beatles' Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band — Uncle Meat (1969), Burnt Weeny Sandwich (1970) and Weasels Ripped My Flesh (1970). The band was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame as part of Zappa's induction in 1995.

Legacy

The Mothers of Invention are regarded as one of the most innovative bands of the rock era, noted for virtuosic musicianship, complex time signatures and biting social satire. Their influence extends across progressive rock, jazz fusion and alternative music.
