Backward compatibility

In telecommunications and computing, backward compatibility (or backwards compatibility) is a property of a system, product, or technology that allows for interoperability with an older legacy system, or with input designed for such a system, especially in telecommunications and computing.

Modifying a system to not allow backward compatibility is sometimes called "breaking" backward compatibility. A complementary concept is forward compatibility: a design that is forward-compatible usually has a roadmap for compatibility with future standards and products.

Usage

In hardware

A simple example of both backward and forward compatibility is the introduction of FM radio in stereo. FM radio stations often broadcast in stereo, and receivers decode the signal accordingly; older mono receivers can still reproduce the broadcast because the stereo signal is constructed so that mono sets receive the sum of the channels. In video games, console manufacturers have frequently marketed new machines by their ability to play games written for a previous generation, such as the PlayStation 2 playing PlayStation games, and the Wii playing GameCube games.

In software

Software is backward compatible if programs written for earlier versions of an operating system, library, or file format continue to work on later versions. Maintaining this property preserves users' investment in existing software and data, but it can also perpetuate design flaws and security weaknesses. The Intel x86 architecture is a prominent example of a long-lived instruction set kept compatible across decades of processors.

Tradeoffs

Backward compatibility provides benefits to users, who can upgrade without abandoning their tools and documents, and to vendors, who protect their installed base. However, it imposes costs: added complexity, testing burden, and the inability to remove obsolete features. Some vendors, such as Apple with various hardware transitions, have deliberately dropped compatibility after a transition period, while others, notably Microsoft with the Windows API, have treated compatibility as a core product requirement.
