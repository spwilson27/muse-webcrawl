Human Action: A Treatise on Economics is a work by the Austrian economist and philosopher Ludwig von Mises, published in 1949 and widely considered his magnum opus and the foundational treatise of the modern Austrian School of economics. An expanded English version of his German-language Nationalökonomie (1940), it presents a comprehensive theory of the market economy based on methodologically individualist premises.

Overview

Mises builds economics on "praxeology," the deductive study of human action. From the axiom that human beings act—employing means to attain ends—he claims to derive, by a priori reasoning, the whole structure of economic theory: value, exchange, prices, money, interest, and the business cycle. The book defends laissez-faire capitalism, argues that economic calculation is impossible under socialism (developing his 1920 calculation argument), analyzes interventionism as inherently unstable, and sets out the Misesian theory of money and the trade cycle based on credit expansion.

Human Action runs to nearly 900 pages in most editions and has remained in print, published in later editions by the Ludwig von Mises Institute. It influenced later Austrian economists including Murray Rothbard and Israel Kirzner, while mainstream economists have generally rejected its a priori methodology even when acknowledging its influence.
