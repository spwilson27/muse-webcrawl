Betty Friedan

Betty Friedan (born Bettye Naomi Goldstein; February 4, 1921 – February 4, 2006) was an American feminist writer and activist. A leading figure in the women's movement in the United States, her 1963 book The Feminine Mystique is often credited with sparking second-wave feminism. In 1966 she co-founded and became the first president of the National Organization for Women (NOW), which aimed to bring women "into full participation in the mainstream of American society". She also helped found the National Women's Political Caucus and the National Association for the Repeal of Abortion Laws (later NARAL).

Early life and career

Friedan was born in Peoria, Illinois, to a Jewish family; her father ran a jewellery store and her mother had given up newspaper work for domestic life — a frustration Friedan later wrote about. She graduated summa cum laude from Smith College in 1942, studied psychology briefly at Berkeley under Erik Erikson, and then worked as a journalist, including for labour-movement publications. Married in 1947 and raising children in the suburbs, she began writing for women's magazines, and in 1957 surveyed her Smith classmates for a reunion, finding widespread dissatisfaction with the housewife role among educated women.

The Feminine Mystique

The survey grew into The Feminine Mystique (1963), which named "the problem that has no name": the unhappiness of American housewives told that fulfilment lay solely in domesticity, femininity, and consumerism. Friedan attacked the Freudian and functionalist ideas underpinning that ideal and urged education and meaningful work for women. The book was a bestseller, sold millions of copies, and is credited with launching the modern women's movement — though critics, including later feminists and the historian Daniel Horowitz, noted its focus on educated white suburban women and Friedan's downplaying of her own labour-movement past.

Activism

Friedan co-founded NOW in 1966 and served as its president until 1970, pressing for enforcement of sex-discrimination provisions of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, legal abortion, and childcare. She organised the Women's Strike for Equality of 26 August 1970, which drew tens of thousands in New York and across the country. A forceful and sometimes divisive leader, she clashed with younger radical feminists, whom she considered dismissive of family and of men's support. Later books included The Second Stage (1981), arguing the movement should embrace family concerns, and The Fountain of Age (1993), on ageing. She died of heart failure on her 85th birthday, 4 February 2006.
