In the Hebrew Bible, a Nazirite (or Nazarite; Hebrew: נזיר, nazir) was an Israelite who voluntarily took a vow described in Numbers 6:1–21, consecrating himself or herself to God for a set period. The term derives from a root meaning "to separate" or "to consecrate". During the vow the Nazirite observed three restrictions: abstaining from wine, vinegar, grapes and all grape products; allowing the hair of the head to grow uncut; and avoiding all contact with corpses, even those of close family members.

Biblical accounts

The regulations in Numbers stipulate that if a Nazirite was accidentally defiled by sudden contact with a corpse, the vow was broken: the hair was shaved, offerings were brought, and the vow restarted. On completion of the term, the Nazirite brought a series of sacrifices at the Tabernacle (later the Temple), shaved the consecrated hair and burned it on the altar, and was then free to drink wine again.

Most Naziriteship was temporary — rabbinic rules set a default term of thirty days — but the Bible also describes Nazirites for life. Samson was declared a Nazirite from the womb by an angel (Judges 13); his uncut hair was the source of his strength, lost when Delilah had it shaved. The prophet Samuel is regarded in rabbinic tradition as a lifelong Nazirite on the basis of his mother's vow (1 Samuel 1:11). The prophet Amos condemns Israel for making Nazirites drink wine and commands the prophets not to prophesy (Amos 2:11–12). In the New Testament era the practice continued: Acts 21 describes Paul of Tarsus participating in the completion rites of Nazirite vows in Jerusalem, and John the Baptist has often been considered a Nazirite, though this is debated.

In rabbinic Judaism

The Mishnah devotes the tractate Nazir to the laws of the vow, including vows by women, vows taken outside the Land of Israel, and the Naziriteship of Samson (which had its own special rules). After the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE the concluding sacrifices could no longer be offered, and the practice effectively ceased, though taking Nazirite vows has occasionally been revived in modern times.
