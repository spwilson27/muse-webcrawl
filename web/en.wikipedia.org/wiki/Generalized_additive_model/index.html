Generalized additive model

In statistics, a generalized additive model (GAM) is a generalized linear model in which the linear response variable depends linearly on unknown smooth functions of some predictor variables, and interest focuses on inference about these smooth functions. GAMs were developed by Trevor Hastie and Robert Tibshirani in the 1980s, blending the properties of generalized linear models with additive models.

Model specification

A GAM has the form g(E(Y)) = β₀ + f₁(x₁) + f₂(x₂) + … + fₘ(xₘ), where g is a link function, Y follows an exponential-family distribution, and the fⱼ are unspecified smooth functions, commonly represented as penalized regression splines. Because each predictor enters through its own smooth curve, GAMs can capture nonlinear effects while remaining more interpretable than fully nonparametric models; the additivity assumption excludes general interactions, though pairwise smooths can be added.

Estimation

Early GAMs were fitted by the local scoring algorithm using scatterplot smoothers such as loess. Modern implementations represent smooths with spline bases and estimate smoothing parameters by generalized cross-validation (GCV), AIC, or restricted maximum likelihood (REML), balancing fit against wiggliness via quadratic penalties. Simon Wood's mgcv package in R is a widely used implementation, handling large datasets, random effects (GAMMs), and many response distributions.

Applications

GAMs are widely used in ecology (species distribution modelling), epidemiology (time-series studies of air pollution and health), fisheries, and actuarial and environmental statistics, wherever flexible dose–response curves are needed without imposing a parametric form.
