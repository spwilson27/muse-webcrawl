Erdős–Kac theorem

The Erdős–Kac theorem, named after Paul Erdős and Mark Kac, is a theorem in number theory which relates the number of distinct prime factors of a natural number to the normal distribution. It is often described as the fundamental theorem of probabilistic number theory.

Statement

Given a natural number n, write ω(n) for the number of distinct prime divisors of n. The theorem states that, as n ranges over the integers up to N with N large, the values of ω(n) are distributed approximately like a normal distribution with mean ln ln N and variance ln ln N. More precisely, the proportion of integers n ≤ N for which (ω(n) − ln ln n) / √(ln ln n) lies between a and b tends, as N → ∞, to the corresponding probability for a standard normal random variable.

History

The result, proved in 1940, grew out of the earlier work of Godfrey Hardy and Srinivasa Ramanujan, who showed in 1917 that the normal order of ω(n) is ln ln n, and of Pál Turán, who gave a simpler proof of that fact in 1934. Kac, a probabilist, recognised the central limit theorem character of the problem, and the collaboration with Erdős produced a proof using methods from probability theory, including the sieve of Brun and moment calculations.

Significance

The theorem is a prototype for the probabilistic method in number theory: arithmetic functions can behave like sums of nearly independent random variables. It later inspired extensions such as the general central limit theorems for additive arithmetic functions.
