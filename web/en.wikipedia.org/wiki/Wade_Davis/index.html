Edmund Wade Davis CM (born December 14, 1953) is a Canadian cultural anthropologist, ethnobotanist, author, and photographer. He has published popular articles and books on topics ranging from the traditional uses of plants in Indigenous cultures to explorations of remote regions and the loss of cultural and linguistic diversity. Davis served as Explorer-in-Residence at the National Geographic Society from 2000 to 2013, and is a professor of anthropology and the BC Leadership Chair in Cultures and Ecosystems at Risk at the University of British Columbia.

Work

Davis received his PhD from Harvard University, where he studied under the ethnobotanist Richard Evans Schultes. His early research in Haiti investigated folk preparations implicated in the creation of so-called "zombies", including the pufferfish toxin tetrodotoxin; his findings were published in the book The Serpent and the Rainbow (1985), a bestseller that was loosely adapted into a 1988 horror film of the same name directed by Wes Craven.

His later books include One River (1996), an account of Schultes's Amazonian travels and the exploration of the Amazon and Orinoco basins; The Wayfinders (2009), based on his Massey Lectures for CBC Radio, arguing that the world's Indigenous cultures constitute an "ethnosphere" as vital to human life as the biosphere; and Into the Silence (2011), a history of British expeditions on Mount Everest in the 1920s, which won the Samuel Johnson Prize for non-fiction. In 2020 he published Magdalena: River of Dreams, about Colombia's principal river.

Views

A recurring theme in Davis's work is the rapid disappearance of languages and cultures: of the roughly 7,000 languages spoken in the world, he notes that many are no longer being taught to children and may vanish within a generation or two. He argues that each language represents a distinct way of understanding the world — an "old-growth forest of the mind". Davis was made a Member of the Order of Canada in 2016 for his work as an anthropologist and explorer.
