The Lamiales are an order in the asterid group of dicotyledonous flowering plants. It includes about 23,810 species, 1,059 genera, and is divided into about 24 families. Well-known or economically important members of this order include lavender, lilac, olive, jasmine, the ash tree, teak, snapdragon, sesame, psyllium, garden sage, and a number of table herbs such as mint, basil, and rosemary.

Description

Species in the order Lamiales are usually herbs, shrubs or trees with opposite leaves. The flowers are often bilaterally symmetrical (zygomorphic), with a two-lipped corolla formed from five fused petals. The ovary is typically superior, and the fruit may be a capsule, drupe, berry or schizocarp. Many species produce distinctive secondary compounds such as iridoid glycosides.

Taxonomy

The circumscription of Lamiales has changed substantially with molecular phylogenetic studies. Under the APG IV system the order contains around 24 families, the largest of which are Lamiaceae (the mint family), Acanthaceae, Plantaginaceae, Scrophulariaceae, Orobanchaceae, Gesneriaceae, Verbenaceae, Bignoniaceae and Oleaceae. Families such as Plantaginaceae have absorbed many genera formerly assigned to a broadly defined Scrophulariaceae.

Uses

The order is of considerable economic importance: olives and olive oil come from Olea europaea (Oleaceae); teak is a major tropical timber; sesame is an oilseed crop; and numerous ornamentals, including snapdragons, foxgloves, lilacs and jacarandas, belong to Lamiales. Several families include parasitic plants, notably the broomrapes of Orobanchaceae, some of which are serious agricultural pests.
