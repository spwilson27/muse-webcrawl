Topsoil


A soil profile: the dark upper layer is topsoil, lying above the subsoil.

Topsoil is the upper layer of soil. It has the highest concentration of organic matter and microorganisms and is where most of the Earth's biological soil activity occurs. In soil science, topsoil generally corresponds to the A horizon (and, where present, the O horizon of accumulated organic material) of a soil profile.

Composition

Topsoil is composed of mineral particles, organic matter, water, and air. The mineral fraction typically consists of a mixture of sand, silt, and clay; a balanced mixture known as loam is especially valued for plant growth. The organic component is derived from decomposed plant and animal material and can range from less than 1% in arid soils to more than 10% in rich grassland and forest soils. A single teaspoon of healthy topsoil may contain billions of microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes, as well as larger organisms such as earthworms.

Importance

Plants obtain most of their nutrients from topsoil, and it is the layer in which seeds germinate and roots proliferate. Because it is biologically active and rich in nutrients, topsoil is critical to agriculture and gardening. It also plays important roles in the carbon cycle — soils store more carbon than the atmosphere and all plant life combined — and in filtering and storing water.

Formation

Topsoil forms extremely slowly through the weathering of parent rock combined with the accumulation and decomposition of organic matter. Rates of formation are commonly estimated at roughly an inch (about 2.5 cm) per 200 to 1,000 years under natural conditions, depending on climate, organisms, relief, and parent material — the factors of soil formation identified in classic pedology.

Erosion and loss

Because topsoil forms so slowly, its loss through erosion by water and wind, and through practices such as intensive tillage, overgrazing, and deforestation, is a serious environmental concern. Global estimates suggest that billions of tonnes of topsoil are lost from farmland each year, threatening long-term food security — an issue highlighted during the American Dust Bowl of the 1930s. Conservation measures include cover crops, contour ploughing, terracing, reduced tillage, and windbreaks.

Commercial topsoil

Topsoil is sold commercially, usually in bulk or in bags, for landscaping, gardening, and site remediation. Commercial products are typically screened to remove stones and debris and may be blends of soil, sand, and composted organic matter. Quality varies widely, and standards for what may be sold as "topsoil" differ between jurisdictions.
