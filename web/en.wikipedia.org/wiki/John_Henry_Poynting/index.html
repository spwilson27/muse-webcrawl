John Henry Poynting FRS (9 September 1852 – 30 March 1914) was an English physicist. He formulated the Poynting vector, which describes the direction and magnitude of electromagnetic energy flow, and Poynting's theorem, the corresponding statement of energy conservation for the electromagnetic field. He also made precision measurements of Newton's gravitational constant and of the mean density of the Earth, and predicted the effect of radiation pressure later known as the Poynting–Robertson effect.

Life and career

Poynting was born at Monton, near Eccles in Lancashire, the son of a Unitarian minister. He was educated at Owens College, Manchester, and won a scholarship to Trinity College, Cambridge, where he studied under James Clerk Maxwell and was a pupil of Edward Routh; he graduated as third wrangler in 1876. In 1880 he became the first professor of physics at the newly founded Mason Science College in Birmingham (later the University of Birmingham), a post he held until his death.

Scientific work

Electromagnetic energy flow

In 1884 Poynting published his celebrated paper "On the Transfer of Energy in the Electromagnetic Field", showing that the flow of electromagnetic energy at any point can be represented by the vector

S = E × H,

now called the Poynting vector. The result resolved conceptual puzzles about how energy travels in circuits — showing, for example, that in a simple direct-current circuit the energy flows through the space around the wires rather than within the wires themselves.

Gravitation and radiation pressure

Using a delicate torsion balance, Poynting measured the gravitational constant and the mean density of the Earth, work recognised by the Royal Society's Royal Medal in 1905. In 1903–1904 he analysed the effect of radiation pressure on small bodies orbiting the Sun; the drag component of this effect, elaborated by Howard P. Robertson in 1937, is known as the Poynting–Robertson effect and is important in the dynamics of interplanetary dust.

Honours

Poynting was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1888 and served as president of the Physical Society (1905–1906). The Poynting Building at the University of Birmingham and a lunar crater are named after him.
