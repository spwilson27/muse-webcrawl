Software review

A software review is a process in which software work products — such as requirements documents, designs, source code, and test plans — are examined by peers or stakeholders to find defects, verify quality, or assess readiness. Reviews are a form of static verification: unlike testing, they do not execute the software.

Types

Reviews range from informal to highly formal:

  • Code review — examination of source code by peers, today often tool-assisted through pull requests.
  • Walkthrough — the author leads participants through the material to gather feedback and reach a shared understanding.
  • Technical review — a peer group evaluates the technical quality and conformance of a product.
  • Inspection — the most formal type, pioneered by Michael Fagan at IBM in the 1970s, with defined roles, entry and exit criteria, checklists, and defect metrics.
  • Management review and audit — evaluations of project status or of compliance with processes and standards.

Effectiveness

Studies of inspections and code reviews consistently find them to be among the most cost-effective defect-removal techniques, because defects are cheapest to fix when found close to where they were introduced. Modern lightweight reviews, integrated into version control workflows, trade some of the formality of Fagan inspections for speed and continuous practice.
