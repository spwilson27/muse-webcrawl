Random dot stereogram

A random dot stereogram (RDS) is a pair of images made of random dots which, when viewed with a stereoscope or with the eyes diverged or converged appropriately, produces a sensation of depth, with shapes appearing to float above or recede behind the background. Invented by the vision scientist Bela Julesz in 1960, the technique demonstrated that depth perception can arise purely from binocular disparity, without any recognizable contours in either eye's image alone.

Principle

Each image consists of a random pattern of dots; in one image, a region corresponding to a hidden shape is shifted horizontally by a few dots and the resulting gaps are filled with fresh random dots. Neither image by itself shows the shape — it carries no monocular cues at all. When the visual system fuses the two patterns, the horizontal disparity of the shifted region is interpreted as depth, and the hidden figure (Julesz called the effect cyclopean perception) pops out vividly.

Legacy

Random dot stereograms revolutionized the study of binocular vision and are used clinically to test stereopsis in children, since the test cannot be passed with one eye. The related single-image autostereogram, popularized in the 1990s by the Magic Eye books, adapts the same principle so that the hidden three-dimensional image can be seen without a viewer by diverging or crossing the eyes.
