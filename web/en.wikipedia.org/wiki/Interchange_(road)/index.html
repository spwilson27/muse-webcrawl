Interchange (road)

In the field of road transport, an interchange is a road junction that uses grade separation, with one or more ramps, to permit traffic on at least one highway to pass through the junction without interruption from any other crossing traffic stream. It differs from a standard intersection, at which roads cross at grade. Interchanges are characteristic of controlled-access highways such as motorways and freeways, though they are also used where ordinary roads meet railways or other roads under heavy traffic.

Types

  • Cloverleaf: two levels, with loop ramps for left turns; compact and cheap but prone to weaving conflicts.
  • Stack: typically four levels of direct and semi-direct ramps; handles all movements at high speed but is large and expensive.
  • Directional T / trumpet: used where one highway ends at another.
  • Diamond and its variants (diverging diamond, single-point urban interchange): used where a freeway meets a surface street, with signalized junctions on the cross road.
  • Roundabout interchange and dumbbell: the cross road junctions are handled by roundabouts.

Terminology

An interchange that permits all movements between two highways is a system interchange; one connecting a freeway to local roads is a service interchange. A junction lacking some movements is a partial interchange. The first cloverleaf in the United States opened in New Jersey in 1929; the world's largest interchanges, such as those in the American Sun Belt, can occupy several hundred acres.
