Capacity factor

The capacity factor of a power generating unit is the ratio of its actual electrical energy output over a period of time to the maximum possible output if it had operated at full nameplate capacity continuously over the same period. It is usually expressed as a percentage and averaged over a year.

Calculation

Capacity factor = actual energy produced ÷ (nameplate capacity × hours in the period). A 1 GW plant producing 5 TWh in a year (8,760 hours) has a capacity factor of about 57%.

Typical values

Nuclear plants often exceed 90%; natural-gas combined-cycle plants commonly run at 40–60%; coal varies widely. Variable renewables are lower and resource-dependent: onshore wind around 25–40%, offshore wind 40–50%+, and solar photovoltaics roughly 10–25% depending on latitude and tracking.

Factors

Capacity factor is affected by fuel or resource availability, maintenance and refuelling outages, equipment reliability, and economics — for example, peaking plants are deliberately run only when demand and prices are high. A low capacity factor does not by itself imply inefficiency.
