Romulus Augustus (c. 465 – after 507), nicknamed Augustulus ("little Augustus") on account of his youth, was Roman emperor of the West from 31 October 475 until his deposition by the barbarian general Odoacer on 4 September 476. His abdication is traditionally taken to mark the end of the Western Roman Empire and, in many periodisations, the end of ancient history and the beginning of the Middle Ages.

Reign

Romulus was the son of Orestes, a former secretary of Attila the Hun who had become master of soldiers in Italy. After deposing the emperor Julius Nepos in August 475, Orestes placed his teenage son on the throne rather than ruling himself. The boy's authority barely extended beyond Italy, and his reign was never recognised by the Eastern emperor Zeno. When Orestes refused to grant federate troops — Heruli, Sciri and Turcilingi — one-third of Italian land, they proclaimed their leader Odoacer king. Orestes was defeated and killed at Pavia in August 476; Ravenna fell days later.

Deposition and later life

On 4 September 476 Romulus was forced to abdicate, formally renouncing the imperial office before the Senate; the imperial regalia were sent to Constantinople. Odoacer spared him — reportedly because of his youth and beauty — granting him an annual pension of 6,000 solidi and exile to the Castellum Lucullanum in Campania. Romulus was still alive in 507, when Theodoric the Great confirmed his pension; some sources suggest he lived into the 520s or later and may have founded a monastery. Meanwhile Julius Nepos retained a claim to the western throne from Dalmatia until his murder in 480, a fact that complicates the conventional narrative of 476.

Significance

Historians debate how far 476 was a true rupture: Roman law, administration and culture continued under Odoacer and the Ostrogoths. Nevertheless, no further emperor was appointed in the West, and Romulus — sharing, by coincidence, the names of Rome's founder and its first emperor — remains the symbolic last emperor of the Western Roman Empire.
