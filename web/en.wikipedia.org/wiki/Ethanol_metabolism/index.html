Ethanol metabolism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ethanol metabolism is the process by which the human body breaks down ingested ethanol, principally in the liver. Ethanol is eliminated at a roughly constant (zero-order) rate in most people, commonly corresponding to a fall in blood alcohol concentration of about 0.015 g/dL per hour.

Pathway

The main pathway has two oxidation steps. First, the enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH), in the presence of the cofactor NAD+, converts ethanol to acetaldehyde, a toxic and carcinogenic intermediate. Second, aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH, chiefly mitochondrial ALDH2) converts acetaldehyde to acetate, which is then broken down in peripheral tissues to carbon dioxide and water or used in metabolism. A secondary route, the microsomal ethanol-oxidising system centred on cytochrome P450 enzyme CYP2E1, becomes more important at high concentrations and with chronic drinking, and catalase provides a minor third route.

Variation and effects

Genetic variants strongly affect metabolism. Many people of East Asian ancestry carry an inactive ALDH2 variant (ALDH2*2); acetaldehyde accumulates, causing facial flushing, nausea and palpitations — the "alcohol flush reaction" — and elevated risk of oesophageal cancer in those who drink despite it. Variants in ADH that speed the first step have similar protective effects against alcohol dependence. Because acetaldehyde accumulates if ALDH is blocked, the drug disulfiram is used to deter drinking. Chronic ethanol metabolism raises the NADH/NAD+ ratio, contributing to fatty liver, lactic acidosis and hypoglycaemia, and CYP2E1 induction generates reactive oxygen species implicated in alcoholic liver disease.

Elimination

A small percentage of ethanol is excreted unchanged in breath, urine and sweat — the basis of the breathalyser. Gastric ADH provides limited "first-pass" metabolism before ethanol reaches the liver.
