Jet lag, also called desynchronosis and rarely circadian dysrhythmia, is a physiological condition that results from alterations to the body's circadian rhythms caused by rapid long-distance trans-meridian (east–west or west–east) travel across three or more time zones. For example, someone flying from New York to London (five hours ahead) feels as though the time were five hours earlier than local time, and their body must adjust accordingly.

Jet lag was previously classified as one of the circadian rhythm sleep disorders. The condition is generally temporary, with adjustment taking roughly one day per time zone crossed, though individuals vary. Jet lag is distinct from the fatigue of travel itself ("travel fatigue"), which accompanies even north–south journeys without time-zone changes.

Causes and physiology

The master circadian clock in the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus runs with an intrinsic period slightly longer than 24 hours in most people and is entrained chiefly by light. After rapid time-zone transitions, internal rhythms (sleep–wake, core body temperature, melatonin and cortisol secretion, appetite) remain aligned to the origin time zone and re-entrain only gradually — and at different rates, producing internal desynchrony as well as misalignment with the environment. Eastward travel requires a phase advance (shortening the day), which is usually harder than the phase delay required by westward travel.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms include difficulty falling asleep (after eastward travel) or early awakening (after westward travel), excessive daytime sleepiness, fatigue, headache, irritability, mild depression, reduced concentration and athletic or cognitive performance, loss of appetite, and gastrointestinal disturbance such as indigestion or altered bowel habit. Symptoms usually appear within a day or two of travel and are worse with more zones crossed, older age, and poor sleep before or during the flight.

Direction of travel

Most travellers find eastward journeys harder: flying east compresses the day, requiring them to sleep earlier than the body clock permits. A common rule of thumb is that recovery takes about one day per time zone crossed eastward and about two-thirds to one day per zone westward. Trips crossing fewer than three zones usually produce negligible jet lag.

Management

Strategies include gradually shifting sleep schedules before departure, appropriately timed bright-light exposure and light avoidance at the destination, short naps, hydration, and avoidance of alcohol and heavy meals. Melatonin (typically 0.5–5 mg taken near the target bedtime) has evidence for reducing jet lag after eastward flights crossing five or more zones; short-acting hypnotics are sometimes used for sleep but do not shift the clock. Caffeine can sustain daytime alertness. Apps and protocols calculate personalised light/sleep schedules based on itinerary and chronotype.

Effects on performance

Jet lag measurably impairs athletes and business travellers; studies of professional sports teams have reported reduced performance in teams travelling east across multiple zones, though effects are modest and contested. Aircrew on long-haul rotations experience cumulative circadian disruption, and chronic circadian misalignment has been studied for metabolic and cardiovascular effects; permanent night-shift and frequent time-zone work is classified differently (shift work sleep disorder).
