The Yogyakarta Principles are a set of principles on the application of international human rights law in relation to sexual orientation and gender identity. They were drafted by a group of 29 international human rights experts who met at Gadjah Mada University in Yogyakarta, Indonesia, in November 2006, and were launched in Geneva in March 2007. The document affirms binding international legal standards with which, its drafters argue, all states must comply, while creating no new rights.

Content

The original text sets out 29 principles. They cover the rights to universal enjoyment of human rights and non-discrimination; rights to recognition before the law (including legal recognition of gender identity); rights to security of the person and protection from medical abuses; economic, social and cultural rights such as work, housing, health and education; rights to expression, opinion, association and asylum; and rights to accountability and redress for violations. Each principle is accompanied by detailed recommendations to states, UN bodies and other actors.

The YP+10

In November 2017, a supplement known as the Yogyakarta Principles plus 10 was published, adding nine further principles and additional state obligations. The supplement responds to developments including greater explicit recognition of gender expression and sex characteristics (intersex status), and addresses matters such as protection from forced medical procedures and the right to sanitation.

Reception and use

The Principles have been cited by courts, legislatures, UN agencies and treaty bodies in several countries as an authoritative interpretation of existing human rights law, including in landmark gender-recognition and decriminalisation cases. They have also been controversial: some governments have declined to endorse them, and critics argue they go beyond state consensus. The signatories and supporters respond that the Principles merely restate obligations already binding under treaties such as the ICCPR.
