Subject–verb–object word order

Subject–verb–object (SVO)
Example languagesEnglish, Mandarin Chinese, French, Spanish, Russian, Swahili
Example"She loves him"
FrequencySecond most common order by number of languages; most speakers worldwide

In linguistic typology, subject–verb–object (SVO) is a sentence structure in which the subject comes first, the verb second, and the object third: "Sam ate oranges." English is an SVO language, as are many European languages, Mandarin Chinese, and numerous languages of Africa and Southeast Asia. By number of native speakers, SVO is the most widely used word order, largely because of the huge speaker populations of English, Chinese, Spanish, and Hindi's SOV rivals.

Properties

SVO languages typically use prepositions (as opposed to postpositions in many SOV languages), place auxiliary verbs before the main verb, and position relative clauses after the nouns they modify. SVO order has arisen independently in several language families, often from verb-second (V2) stages, as in the history of English and the Romance languages, or from loosening of earlier SOV patterns.

Differences among SVO languages

Languages sharing SVO order can differ greatly: English relies heavily on word order to signal grammatical roles, having largely lost case marking, whereas Russian combines SVO with rich inflection and freer ordering. Mandarin is strongly topic-prominent despite its SVO base order. Analytic creole languages are also strongly SVO.
