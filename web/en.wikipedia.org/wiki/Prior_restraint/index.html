Prior restraint

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Prior restraint is government action that prohibits speech or other expression before it can take place — licensing schemes, injunctions against publication, and pre-publication review — as opposed to punishing speech afterwards. In United States constitutional law it bears a “heavy presumption” of unconstitutionality: Near v. Minnesota (1931) struck down a state scheme for suppressing “malicious” newspapers, and in New York Times Co. v. United States (1971), the Pentagon Papers case, the Court refused to enjoin publication of a classified history of the Vietnam War, the government having failed to meet its burden.

Blackstone treated freedom of the press as precisely the absence of prior restraint (with subsequent punishment permitted), and the Anglo-American tradition remains especially hostile to it; European systems, while protective of the press, have been somewhat more willing to allow injunctions, for example to protect privacy or fair trials — the British “super-injunction” practice attracting controversy. Exceptions in US doctrine are narrow: wartime troop movements (the classic hypothetical), obscenity with procedural safeguards, and prior review accepted by government employees and contractors as a condition of access to secrets (Snepp, 1980).
