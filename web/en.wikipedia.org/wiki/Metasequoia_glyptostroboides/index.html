Metasequoia glyptostroboides, the dawn redwood, is a fast-growing deciduous conifer and the sole living species of the genus Metasequoia. It is famous as a "living fossil": the genus was described from fossils in 1941 by Japanese paleobotanist Shigeru Miki and was thought to be extinct, before living trees were discovered in China in the 1940s, near Modaoxi (now in Lichuan County, Hubei). Seeds collected by an expedition partly funded by the Arnold Arboretum were distributed worldwide in 1948.

Description

The dawn redwood is a large tree, commonly 30–40 metres tall and potentially exceeding 50 metres, with a straight trunk, fluted and buttressed base, and reddish-brown bark that exfoliates in strips. Unlike most conifers it is deciduous: its soft, feathery, opposite branchlets bear linear leaves that turn a striking orange-red to russet in autumn before falling. Small round cones, about 2 cm across, hang on long stalks.

Distribution and ecology

The natural population survives in a limited area on the border of Hubei and Hunan provinces and Chongqing municipality in central China, in moist valley bottoms and along streams at elevations of roughly 750–1500 m. The wild population is small and fragmented, and the species is listed as endangered. Fossil evidence shows that Metasequoia was once widespread across the Northern Hemisphere, including high Arctic latitudes, during the Late Cretaceous and Cenozoic.

Cultivation

Because virtually all cultivated trees descend from the 1948 seed distribution, genetic diversity in cultivation is low, but the species has proven adaptable and is widely planted as an ornamental and street tree in temperate regions worldwide. The cultivar 'Gold Rush' has golden foliage.
