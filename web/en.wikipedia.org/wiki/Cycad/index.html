Cycads are seed plants that typically have a stout, woody trunk with a crown of large, hard, stiff, evergreen, pinnate leaves. They are gymnosperms — their unfertilised seeds are exposed to the air — and form the division Cycadophyta, with about 360 living species in two families. Although superficially resembling palms or ferns, they are not closely related to either.

Evolution and distribution

Cycads are an ancient lineage, with fossils dating to the early Permian, roughly 280 million years ago. They flourished in the Mesozoic era alongside the dinosaurs, then declined as flowering plants spread; molecular studies suggest the living species are a comparatively recent radiation from a few survivors. Today they are found in tropical and subtropical regions of the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Australia, often in dry or nutrient-poor soils.

Biology

Cycads are dioecious: individual plants are either male, producing pollen cones, or female, producing seed cones or leaf-like megasporophylls. Many are pollinated by specialist beetles or thrips attracted by heat and scent produced by the cones. Their roots host nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria in specialised coralloid roots. Cycad tissues contain toxins such as cycasin and the neurotoxic amino acid BMAA, though some cultures traditionally prepare cycad seeds as food after careful detoxification.

Conservation

Most cycad species are threatened by habitat loss and over-collection for the ornamental trade; they are among the most endangered groups of plants, and all are listed under CITES.
