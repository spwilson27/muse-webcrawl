Genome browser

A genome browser displaying genes and annotation tracks along a chromosome.

A genome browser is a graphical interface for displaying information from a biological database for genomic data. Genome browsers enable researchers to visualize and browse entire genomes—with annotated data such as gene prediction and structure, proteins, expression, regulation, variation, and comparative genomics—along a linear representation of chromosomes or assembled contigs.

Features

Genome browsers display multiple horizontally layered "tracks" of annotations aligned to genomic coordinates, which users can zoom from whole-chromosome views down to single nucleotides. Typical tracks include gene models and transcripts, sequence alignments (such as RNA-seq reads), genetic variants (SNPs), conservation scores across species, regulatory elements from projects such as ENCODE, and repeats. Most browsers let users search by gene name or coordinate, upload their own data as custom tracks (in formats such as BED, BAM, bigWig, and VCF), and export images or underlying data.

Major systems

The UCSC Genome Browser, developed at the University of California, Santa Cruz and released in 2000 around the draft human genome, is one of the most widely used. Ensembl, from the European Bioinformatics Institute, provides integrated genomes for many species. Desktop tools such as the Broad Institute's Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) allow fast local browsing of large sequencing datasets, while newer web frameworks such as JBrowse 2 support plugin-based, embeddable browsing.

Significance

By integrating heterogeneous data types against a common coordinate system, genome browsers became essential infrastructure for genomics, enabling hypothesis generation, validation of sequencing results, and interpretation of clinically relevant variants.
