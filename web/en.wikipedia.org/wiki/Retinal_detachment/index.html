Retinal detachment

Retinal detachment is a disorder of the eye in which the neurosensory retina separates from the underlying retinal pigment epithelium, accumulating fluid in the potential space between them. It is an ophthalmic emergency: if untreated, prolonged separation deprives photoreceptors of their blood supply and leads to permanent vision loss.

Types and causes

In rhegmatogenous detachment, the commonest type, a tear or hole in the retina allows liquefied vitreous to pass underneath and lift the retina off. It is associated with posterior vitreous detachment, myopia, previous cataract surgery and trauma. Tractional detachment occurs when fibrovascular membranes, as in proliferative diabetic retinopathy, pull the retina away. Exudative (serous) detachment results from fluid leaking under an intact retina, for example from tumours or inflammatory disease.

Symptoms

Warning symptoms include a sudden shower of floaters, flashes of light (photopsia) and a shadow or "curtain" spreading across the visual field. A detachment that has not yet reached the macula ("macula-on") requires especially urgent repair to preserve central vision.

Treatment

Surgical options include pneumatic retinopexy (injecting a gas bubble and sealing the break with laser or cryotherapy), scleral buckling, and pars plana vitrectomy with internal tamponade by gas or silicone oil. Anatomical success rates of modern surgery exceed 90%, though visual recovery varies.
