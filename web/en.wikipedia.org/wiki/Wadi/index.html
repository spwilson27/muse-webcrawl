Wadi

Definition: A dry riverbed or valley in arid regions of North Africa and the Middle East

Related terms: Arroyo (Spanish), oued (French/Maghreb), nullah (South Asia)

A wadi (Arabic: وَادِي, romanised wādī) is a valley, ravine or channel that is dry except during periods of rain, when it may carry a temporary stream. Wadis are characteristic landforms of the deserts of North Africa, the Arabian Peninsula and the Levant, and the word appears in countless place names across the Arab world, including Wadi Rum in Jordan and Wadi Hadhramaut in Yemen.

Formation and hydrology

Wadis form in arid and semi-arid environments where rainfall is infrequent but intense. During rare storms, runoff concentrates in the channel and can produce dangerous flash floods that arrive with little warning, travelling far from the storm that caused them. Between floods, the bed is typically filled with gravel and sand, and wadi floors often retain moisture that supports vegetation and shallow groundwater long after surrounding areas have dried, making them important corridors for wildlife, grazing and travel.

Human use

Settlements and agriculture in desert regions have long clustered along wadis, where hand-dug wells and modern dams capture floodwater. Ancient caravan and pilgrimage routes frequently followed wadi systems. In several countries, notably Oman and Jordan, engineered check dams and recharge structures are built in wadis to slow floodwater and replenish aquifers.

Etymology

The Arabic word has passed into Spanish as part of many river names from the period of Muslim rule in Iberia — for example, Guadalquivir derives from wādī al-kabīr, "the great valley/river." The Spanish term arroyo and the South Asian nullah describe comparable ephemeral watercourses in other regions.
