Maidenhair fern

Maidenhair fern is the common name for ferns of the genus Adiantum, in the family Pteridaceae, comprising roughly 250 species found worldwide, mostly in tropical and subtropical regions. The name refers to the fine, hair-like black stalks (stipes) of the fronds, fancifully compared to maiden's hair. The genus name Adiantum comes from the Greek for "unwetted", because the leaflets repel water and remain dry even when dipped.

Description

Maidenhair ferns are delicate plants with slender, dark, wiry stems bearing light-green, fan-shaped or oblong leaflets (pinnae) arranged along branching fronds. The sori (spore-producing structures) are borne on the undersides of the leaflet margins, protected by the reflexed edge of the leaflet, which acts as a false indusium. Many species grow on moist rock faces, near waterfalls, on shaded banks and in humid forests.

Cultivation and uses

Several species, notably the southern maidenhair (Adiantum capillus-veneris) and the delta maidenhair (Adiantum raddianum), are popular houseplants prized for their lacy foliage, though they require constant humidity and are notoriously fussy indoors. Some species have traditional medicinal uses, and A. capillus-veneris has a long history in herbal preparations for coughs and hair care.
