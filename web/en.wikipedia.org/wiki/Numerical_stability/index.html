Numerical stability

In the mathematical subfield of numerical analysis, numerical stability is a generally desirable property of numerical algorithms. It describes how errors in the input or arising during computation — such as rounding errors from floating-point arithmetic — propagate through an algorithm. An algorithm is stable if small perturbations produce only small changes in the final result, roughly no larger than one would expect from the sensitivity (conditioning) of the problem itself.

Forward and backward stability

Forward error analysis measures how far the computed answer is from the exact answer. Backward error analysis, developed extensively by James H. Wilkinson, asks instead what slightly perturbed problem the computed answer solves exactly. An algorithm that is backward stable produces, for every input, the exact solution to a "nearby" problem, which combined with a well-conditioned problem guarantees small forward error.

Growth factor and examples

In Gaussian elimination, stability depends on the growth factor of intermediate entries, which motivates partial pivoting. The classical Gram–Schmidt process is numerically unstable for orthogonalization, whereas the modified Gram–Schmidt and Householder methods are stable. Naive summation of many floating-point numbers accumulates error proportional to the number of terms; pairwise summation and Kahan compensated summation greatly reduce it.

Stability in differential equations

For numerical solution of differential equations, related notions include zero-stability and A-stability of time-stepping methods, and the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition for partial differential equations, which restricts the time step so that numerical propagation keeps pace with physical propagation.
