Camelford water pollution incident

The Camelford water pollution incident occurred on 6 July 1988 at the Lowermoor water treatment works near the town of Camelford in Cornwall, England. A delivery driver mistakenly emptied a tanker load of aluminium sulphate, used in water treatment, into a tank containing treated water ready for supply, rather than into the correct storage tank. Around 20 tonnes of the chemical entered the public water supply, contaminating the water of some 20,000 people.

The incident

Residents reported that their tap water tasted strange and turned a blue-green colour, stained hair and skin, and spoiled drinks. Despite numerous complaints, the South West Water Authority initially assured customers that the water was safe to drink. Aluminium concentrations in some samples reached many times the permitted limit.

Health effects

In the days after the incident, many residents reported nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, skin rashes, mouth ulcers and joint pains. Longer-term complaints included memory problems and fatigue, and the incident has been the subject of continuing scientific debate about possible lasting health effects, including a controversial theory linking the exposure to a rare brain disorder in one victim. Official inquiries concluded that serious long-term harm was unlikely, though the opacity of the response was widely criticised.

Aftermath

The incident led to a major public inquiry chaired by Dame Barbara Clayton and to the prosecution of the South West Water Authority, which was fined for causing a public nuisance. It remains one of the worst water contamination incidents in British history and prompted tighter controls on chemical deliveries at treatment works.
