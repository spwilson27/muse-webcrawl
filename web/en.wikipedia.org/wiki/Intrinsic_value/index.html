Intrinsic value

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Intrinsic value is used in finance, ethics and philosophy

Intrinsic value may refer to several distinct concepts, in finance, ethics, philosophy and numismatics, generally denoting the value a thing has "in itself" rather than in relation to something else.

In finance

In financial analysis, intrinsic value is the perceived or calculated value of an asset, investment or company, based on its fundamentals — expected future cash flows discounted to the present — rather than its current market price. Value investors such as Benjamin Graham and Warren Buffett seek to buy securities trading below their intrinsic value. Methods of estimation include discounted cash flow analysis and dividend discount models. For options, intrinsic value is the amount by which an option is in the money: for a call, the excess of the underlying price over the strike price; for a put, the reverse; it is never less than zero, and contrasts with extrinsic (time) value.

In ethics and philosophy

In ethics and value theory, intrinsic value is the value something has in itself, for its own sake, as opposed to instrumental value, which derives from its usefulness as a means to an end. Philosophers debate which things possess intrinsic value: candidates include pleasure, happiness, knowledge, virtue, life and nature. The concept is central to environmental ethics, where the claim that ecosystems or species have intrinsic value is used to argue they merit protection regardless of their utility to humans. G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica (1903) famously treated "good" as the paradigmatic intrinsic value.

In numismatics and economics

For coins and commodity money, intrinsic value is the value of the material content — for example the melt value of the metal in a bullion coin — as distinct from face value. In classical economics, the labour theory of value attempted to ground a commodity's value in something intrinsic, a view largely superseded by marginal utility theory.
