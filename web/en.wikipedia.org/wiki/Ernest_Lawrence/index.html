Lawrence in 1939.

Ernest Orlando Lawrence (8 August 1901 – 27 August 1958) was an American physicist who invented the cyclotron, for which he received the 1939 Nobel Prize in Physics, and founded the Radiation Laboratory at Berkeley (now Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory) and the laboratory that became Lawrence Livermore — effectively creating the American system of big-team national laboratories.

Lawrence's cyclotrons (the first, 13 cm across, in 1931) made Berkeley the world's centre for artificial radioactivity and new elements; during the war his electromagnetic separation method (the calutrons, adapted cyclotron magnets) enriched uranium at Oak Ridge for the Manhattan Project, and his postwar advocacy shaped US science funding and the hydrogen bomb programme. Element 103, lawrencium, is named for him. His model — charismatic director, foundation and industrial funding, engineers and physicists in one team — is the template of big science.

Legacy

Lawrence's name is attached to two national laboratories, an element and the cyclotron in every medical-isotope facility. His wartime and Cold War roles — calutrons, the Livermore laboratory founded with Edward Teller, advocacy of the H-bomb — also made him a central, debated figure in the politics of American physics.
