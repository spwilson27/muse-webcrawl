Taxation in England

The history of taxation in England — and, after the Acts of Union 1707, in Great Britain and the United Kingdom — stretches from Anglo-Saxon tribute to the modern fiscal state, and taxation disputes repeatedly reshaped the constitution, from Magna Carta to the English Civil War and the community charge riots of 1990.

Early and medieval taxation

Anglo-Saxon kings levied the danegeld, originally protection money against Viking raids, which became a regular land tax assessed on hides. After 1066 the Normans compiled the Domesday Book (1086) as a survey of taxable resources. Medieval royal revenues came from feudal incidents — reliefs, wardships, scutage in lieu of military service — supplemented by extraordinary grants voted by Parliament, notably the lay subsidies on movables and, from 1334, fixed community quotas. Richard II's graduated poll tax of 1379–1381 helped provoke the Peasants' Revolt of 1381. Customs duties on wool and wine (tonnage and poundage) were a crown mainstay.

Early modern conflicts

The Tudors relied on parliamentary subsidies and forced loans; Stuart attempts to raise revenue without Parliament — impositions, the forced loan of 1626, and Charles I's ship money — became central grievances leading to the Civil War. The war itself brought England's first excise (1643) and monthly assessments; the Commonwealth and Protectorate ran a strikingly modern fiscal machine. After 1688 the financial revolution — funded national debt, the Bank of England (1694), the land tax, and an expanding excise — underpinned Britain's rise as a fiscal-military state.

Modern taxes

William Pitt the Younger introduced income tax in 1799 to fund the Napoleonic Wars; repealed in 1816, it was reintroduced by Robert Peel in 1842 as a temporary measure and has remained ever since. The 19th century saw the abolition of most customs duties under free trade and the growth of death duties (1894). The two world wars brought mass income taxation (PAYE from 1944), purchase tax, and excess profits duties. Postwar innovations include capital gains tax (1965), corporation tax (1965), value-added tax (1973, replacing purchase tax on EEC entry), and the replacement of domestic rates by the short-lived community charge (1989–1993) and then council tax. Since 1999, devolved taxes are handled separately for Scotland and Wales; England itself has no separate tax administration, taxes being levied UK-wide by HM Revenue and Customs.
