Derived series

In mathematics, specifically in group theory, the derived series (or commutator series) of a group is a particular descending sequence of subgroups obtained by repeatedly taking commutator subgroups. It is a central tool in the theory of solvable groups: a group is solvable if and only if its derived series reaches the trivial subgroup after finitely many steps.

Definition

Let G be a group. The derived subgroup (or commutator subgroup) of G, written G′ or [G, G], is the subgroup generated by all commutators [x, y] = x−1y−1xy for x, y in G. The derived series is defined recursively by

G(0) = G,   G(n+1) = [G(n), G(n)],

giving a descending chain G = G(0) ⊵ G(1) ⊵ G(2) ⊵ ⋯ in which each term is a normal (indeed characteristic) subgroup of the preceding one, and each quotient G(n)/G(n+1) is abelian.

Solvable groups

A group G is called solvable (or soluble) if G(n) = {1} for some finite n; the smallest such n is the derived length of G. Abelian groups have derived length 1, and metabelian groups have derived length at most 2. Non-abelian simple groups, such as the alternating group A5, are not solvable because their derived series stabilises at the whole group. The derived series is the fastest-descending series with abelian quotients, and so it provides the canonical test for solvability.

Abelianisation

The first step of the series has independent significance: the quotient G/G′ is the abelianisation of G, the largest abelian quotient of G.
