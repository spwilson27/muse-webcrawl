Apocatastasis

Apocatastasis is the doctrine of the eventual restoration of all creation.

In theology, apocatastasis (from Ancient Greek ἀποκατάστασις, apokatástasis, "restoration, re-establishment") is the doctrine that all things — in its fullest form, all rational creatures including the fallen angels and the damned — will ultimately be restored to unity with God and saved. The term is connected with the phrase "restoration of all things" (Greek apokatastasis pantōn) in Acts 3:21 of the New Testament.

Origins

The concept appears in Stoic cosmology, where the cosmos periodically returns to its original state after being consumed by fire (ekpyrosis). In Christian thought it is most famously associated with the third-century theologian Origen of Alexandria, who speculated that in the end all free creatures, having been purified through successive ages of correction, would return to God, so that God would be "all in all" (1 Corinthians 15:28).

Development and controversy

The doctrine was elaborated by Gregory of Nyssa and influenced Maximus the Confessor and John Scotus Eriugena. Aspects of Origen's speculative system, possibly including apocatastasis, were condemned in the sixth century, traditionally linked to the Second Council of Constantinople (553), though the extent of the condemnation remains debated by scholars.

In the Reformation era, some Anabaptists taught the restoration of all things, and the idea reappeared in pietist and later universalist movements. Modern theologians such as Karl Barth have been cautiously sympathetic without teaching it dogmatically, while Hans Urs von Balthasar argued that Christians may hope that all will be saved.

Related views

Apocatastasis is closely related to, but not identical with, Christian universalism, the belief that all human beings will ultimately be saved. It typically also includes a cosmology of the final renewal of creation (see new heaven and new earth).
