Papillomaviridae is a family of small, non-enveloped DNA viruses that infect the skin and mucous membranes of a wide range of vertebrates, including humans, other mammals, birds, reptiles, and fish. They are best known for causing warts (papillomas), and certain types are strongly associated with cancers, most notably cervical cancer in humans.

Virology

Papillomavirus particles are about 52–55 nm in diameter, with an icosahedral capsid composed principally of the L1 protein enclosing a circular double-stranded DNA genome of about 8,000 base pairs. The genome encodes early proteins (E1–E7) involved in replication and cell transformation and late proteins (L1, L2) forming the capsid. The viral life cycle is tightly linked to the differentiation program of keratinocytes in stratified epithelium: virions are produced only in the upper, terminally differentiated layers of the skin or mucosa.

Human papillomaviruses

More than 200 types of human papillomavirus (HPV) have been described. They are broadly classed as low-risk types, which cause benign warts (e.g., HPV 6 and 11, causing genital warts), and high-risk types (e.g., HPV 16 and 18) whose E6 and E7 oncoproteins inactivate the tumor suppressors p53 and Rb, respectively. High-risk HPVs cause nearly all cases of cervical cancer and a substantial fraction of anal, oropharyngeal, penile, vulvar, and vaginal cancers.

Evolution and taxonomy

Papillomaviruses are ancient viruses that have largely co-evolved with their hosts over hundreds of millions of years, with individual types showing strong host specificity. The family contains more than fifty genera; human viruses fall mainly into the alpha, beta, gamma, mu, and nu genera. Because papillomaviruses do not grow readily in conventional cell culture, their classification is based on the nucleotide sequence of the L1 gene.

Prevention

Prophylactic HPV vaccines (such as Gardasil and Cervarix), based on virus-like particles of the L1 protein, protect against the most important high- and low-risk types and have substantially reduced HPV infections and precancerous lesions in countries with high coverage. Harald zur Hausen received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2008 for the discovery that HPV causes cervical cancer.
