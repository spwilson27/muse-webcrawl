Salamanca is a city in western Spain, capital of the province of the same name in Castile and León. It stands on the River Tormes and is famous for its historic university, founded in 1218 and among the oldest in the world still in operation, and for its golden-hued sandstone architecture, which earned it the nickname La Dorada ("The Golden City").

History

The site was a fortified settlement of the Vaccaei, taken by Hannibal in 220 BC, and later a Roman town on the Vía de la Plata. After periods of Visigothic and Muslim rule, Salamanca was repopulated under Christian rule in the 11th–12th centuries. King Alfonso IX of León founded the university in 1218; it received royal and papal recognition and became one of Europe's great centres of learning, home to the School of Salamanca — thinkers such as Francisco de Vitoria and Domingo de Soto who shaped international law and economics. Students have traditionally included figures such as Miguel de Cervantes (who set part of the picaresque tradition in the city) — and the city appears in La Celestina and Lazarillo de Tormes. During the Peninsular War, the Battle of Salamanca (1812) saw Wellington defeat the French under Marmont nearby.

Landmarks

The Plaza Mayor (1730s), built in Baroque style under the architect Alberto Churriguera, is considered one of Spain's most beautiful squares. The city has two cathedrals joined together — the Romanesque Old Cathedral (12th century) and the late Gothic New Cathedral (begun 1513) — and notable buildings including the Casa de las Conchas, covered in carved scallop shells, the University facade famous for its carved frog (rana) that students seek for luck, and the Roman bridge over the Tormes.

Modern city

Salamanca is a major destination for students learning Spanish, hosting tens of thousands of language students each year, and tourism is central to its economy. It was a European Capital of Culture in 2002.
