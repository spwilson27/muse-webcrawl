Saffir–Simpson scale

The Saffir–Simpson hurricane wind scale (SSHWS) classifies hurricanes — Western Hemisphere tropical cyclones that exceed the intensities of tropical depressions and tropical storms — into five categories distinguished by the intensities of their sustained winds. It is used in the Atlantic and in the Pacific Ocean east of the International Date Line.

History

The scale was developed in 1971 by civil engineer Herbert Saffir, who devised rankings based on wind speed and expected structural damage. It was expanded in 1973–1974 by Robert Simpson, director of the US National Hurricane Center, who added classifications for storm surge and flooding; public use began in 1975. Between 2009 and 2012 the storm-surge, flooding, and central-pressure criteria were removed, leaving a pure wind scale, formally renamed the Saffir–Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale, because surge and flooding depend strongly on local geography and storm size rather than on wind speed alone.

Hurricanes of Category 3 and above are described as major hurricanes. The measurements are one-minute maximum sustained winds at 10 metres above the surface. Category 5 is open-ended: any hurricane with sustained winds of at least 157 mph (252 km/h) qualifies, and the strongest Atlantic hurricanes on record have reached about 190 mph (305 km/h).

Limitations and criticism

Meteorologists note that wind category does not capture a hurricane's rainfall, storm surge, size, or forward speed, which often cause more death and destruction than wind. Hurricane Katrina (2005) made landfall as a Category 3 by wind but produced a catastrophic surge; Hurricane Harvey (2017) caused its worst damage through freshwater flooding after weakening. Such cases have prompted debate about supplementing or replacing the scale with indices of overall impact.
