Talus slope

A talus slope, also called a scree slope, is an accumulation of broken rock fragments (talus or scree) at the base of a cliff or steep mountainside, produced by physical weathering and rockfall from the face above.

Formation

Processes such as frost wedging loosen blocks from a rock face; the fragments fall, bounce, and roll until they come to rest on the slope below. Over time the debris forms a sloping apron that rests at the angle of repose, typically about 33–37 degrees for coarse angular material. Larger fragments generally travel farther and accumulate toward the base, giving talus slopes a characteristic sorting pattern. As the talus grows, it buries the lower part of the cliff, slowing further erosion.

Terminology and habitat

"Scree" is often used for finer or looser material and "talus" for coarser blocks, though the terms overlap. Talus slopes provide habitat for specialised plants and animals, such as pikas, that shelter among the rocks.
