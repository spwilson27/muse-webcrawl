Medieval theatre

Medieval theatre refers to the theatrical performance traditions of Europe between the fall of the Western Roman Empire and the Renaissance, roughly the 5th to the 16th centuries. Though formal classical theatre largely disappeared, performance survived in folk customs, minstrelsy, and — most importantly — the drama of the medieval Church.

Liturgical drama

Drama re-emerged within Christian worship. The Quem quaeritis trope — a short sung dialogue about the women at Christ's empty tomb, documented by the 10th century — is often cited as the seed of liturgical drama. Short Latin plays performed in churches expanded to include scenes from the Nativity, the Passion, and the lives of saints, gradually acquiring stage machinery, costumes, and vernacular dialogue.

Plays in the vernacular

By the later Middle Ages, performance had moved outdoors and into the hands of lay guilds:

  • Mystery plays (in England) or Corpus Christi cycles dramatized the whole of biblical history from Creation to the Last Judgment. Famous cycles survive from York, Chester, Wakefield (Towneley), and Coventry. They were staged on processional pageant wagons or fixed scaffolds, funded and performed by craft guilds, often with plays matched to their trades.
  • Miracle plays presented episodes from the lives of saints.
  • Morality plays, such as Everyman (c. 1510), used allegorical characters — Good Deeds, Knowledge, Death — to teach moral lessons.
  • Interludes and farces provided secular entertainment in halls and at court.

Performance conditions

Staging used "mansions" (small structures representing locations) arranged around an open playing space, or processional wagons moving between stations. Special effects could be elaborate: trapdoors, flying angels, hellmouths belching smoke, and floods. Performers were usually amateurs from the community, though professional minstrels and fools also performed widely.

Decline

Religious drama was suppressed in many places during the Reformation and Counter-Reformation for its Catholic associations, but its conventions — direct address, episodic structure, mixing of tragedy and comedy — carried into the professional theatre of the Elizabethan age.
