Satellite constellation

A satellite constellation is a group of artificial satellites working together as a system. Unlike a single satellite, a constellation can provide permanent global or near-global coverage, such that at any time everywhere on Earth at least one satellite is visible. Satellites are usually placed in sets of complementary orbital planes and connect to geographically distributed ground stations. They may also use inter-satellite communication.

Overview

Satellite constellations may operate at low Earth orbit (LEO), medium Earth orbit (MEO), or geostationary orbit (GEO). LEO constellations require the largest number of satellites because each satellite's footprint is small, but offer low latency and low path loss. MEO constellations, used by navigation systems such as GPS, use fewer satellites at higher altitudes. A constellation in GEO needs only three equally spaced satellites for near-global coverage, excluding the polar regions.

The design of a constellation is typically described by the Walker constellation notation, which specifies the total number of satellites, the number of orbital planes, the number of satellites per plane, and the phasing between planes.

Applications

  • Navigation: GPS (United States), GLONASS (Russia), Galileo (European Union), and BeiDou (China) are MEO constellations providing global positioning.
  • Communications telephony: the Iridium and Globalstar constellations provide voice and low-rate data services, including to polar regions.
  • Broadband internet: Starlink (SpaceX), OneWeb, and Project Kuiper (Amazon) are large LEO constellations intended to deliver high-speed internet access worldwide.
  • Earth observation: constellations such as Planet Labs' flock of CubeSats image the entire land surface of the Earth daily.
  • Weather and disaster monitoring: the A-Train and COSMIC/FORMOSAT constellations coordinate observations from multiple satellites.
