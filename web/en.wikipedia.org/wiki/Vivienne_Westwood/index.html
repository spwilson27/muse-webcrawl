Dame Vivienne Isabel Westwood (née Swire; 8 April 1941 – 29 December 2022) was an English fashion designer and businesswoman, largely responsible for bringing modern punk and new wave fashions into the mainstream. With her then-partner Malcolm McLaren, she clothed and styled the Sex Pistols from their King's Road shop — successively named Let It Rock, Too Fast to Live Too Young to Die, SEX, Seditionaries, and Worlds End — defining the visual language of punk.

Punk years

A former primary-school teacher, Westwood began designing with McLaren in 1971, selling Teddy Boy revival clothes before pioneering punk's ripped T-shirts with provocative slogans and graphics, bondage trousers, safety-pin jewellery, and fetish wear. Their shop at 430 King's Road became the epicentre of the punk scene. After punk, Westwood's first runway collection, Pirate (1981), launched the New Romantic look and her solo design career.

Design career

Westwood built a global fashion house famous for reworking British traditions — tartan, tweed, Harris cloth, corsetry, crinolines, and the orb-and-Saturn-ring logo. Signature collections include Mini-Crini (1985), Harris Tweed (1987), Anglomania (1993), and the towering platform shoes from which Naomi Campbell famously fell on the runway in 1993. Her bridal and red-carpet designs reached a mass audience when Carrie Bradshaw wore a Westwood gown in the Sex and the City film (2008). From the 1990s she worked with her husband Andreas Kronthaler, who became creative director of the main line (Andreas Kronthaler for Vivienne Westwood).

Activism and legacy

Westwood was a prominent campaigner against climate change ("Climate Revolution"), fracking, and consumerism — urging people to "buy less, choose well, make it last" — and supported causes from civil liberties to WikiLeaks founder Julian Assange. Appointed OBE in 1992 (she famously attended Buckingham Palace reportedly without underwear) and DBE in 2006, she died peacefully at home in Clapham in December 2022, aged 81. Her work is held by museums including the Victoria and Albert Museum, and she is widely ranked with Alexander McQueen among the most influential British designers of the late twentieth century.
