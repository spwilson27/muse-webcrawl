In obstetrics, gestational age is a measure of the age of a pregnancy, conventionally taken from the beginning of the woman's last menstrual period (LMP). Gestational age is expressed in completed weeks and days and is the standard clinical yardstick for tracking fetal development, scheduling prenatal tests, and timing delivery.

Measurement

Gestational age can be estimated from the LMP using Naegele's rule (adding 280 days), but the most accurate method is first-trimester obstetric ultrasonography measuring the crown–rump length, which is accurate to within about 5–7 days. Later ultrasound measurements and physical examination (such as fundal height) are less precise. When pregnancy results from assisted reproductive technology, the known fertilization date gives an exact starting point: embryonic (fertilization) age is about two weeks less than gestational age.

Classification of births

  • Preterm: before 37 weeks (with subcategories including very preterm, before 32 weeks, and extremely preterm, before 28 weeks).
  • Early term: 37 weeks 0 days through 38 weeks 6 days.
  • Full term: 39 weeks 0 days through 40 weeks 6 days.
  • Late term: 41 weeks 0 days through 41 weeks 6 days.
  • Postterm: 42 weeks 0 days and beyond.

Newborns are also classified by size for gestational age: small (below the 10th percentile), appropriate, or large (above the 90th percentile) for gestational age.

Clinical significance

Gestational age at birth is one of the strongest predictors of neonatal outcome; prematurity is a leading cause of infant mortality and long-term disability. Accurate dating guides decisions about induction of labor, antenatal corticosteroids, and screening tests such as the anatomy scan typically performed at 18–22 weeks.
