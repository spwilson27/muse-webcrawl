Greyhound

The Greyhound is a British breed of dog, a sighthound which has been bred for coursing, racing, and hunting. Since the rise in large-scale adoption of retired racing Greyhounds, the breed has seen a resurgence in popularity as a family pet. Greyhounds can reach speeds of up to 40–45 miles per hour (64–72 km/h) in full pursuit, making them among the fastest of all dogs.

Characteristics

Greyhounds are tall, slender, and aerodynamic, with long legs, a deep chest housing a large heart, a flexible spine, and a lean build with little body fat. Males typically stand 71–76 cm (28–30 in) at the shoulder and weigh 27–40 kg; females are slightly smaller. Their coat is short and smooth and may be any color. Despite their racing heritage, Greyhounds are calm, gentle, and famously inactive indoors, often described as "45-mile-per-hour couch potatoes".

History

The breed descends from ancient sighthounds depicted in art from Egyptian tombs and classical antiquity. In Britain, Greyhounds were historically associated with the nobility and used for coursing deer and hare; under medieval forest laws, only nobles were permitted to keep them. Organized coursing became popular in the 18th and 19th centuries, and the modern racing Greyhound developed in the early 20th century with the invention of the mechanical lure in the United States and Britain.

Uses

Greyhounds have been used for racing and coursing, and are increasingly kept as companion animals. Retired racers are commonly rehomed through adoption programs. The breed is generally healthy but can be sensitive to anesthesia and prone to bloat; bone cancer (osteosarcoma) occurs more often than in many breeds.
