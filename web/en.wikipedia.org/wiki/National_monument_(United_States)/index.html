National monument (United States)

In the United States, a national monument is a protected area that can be created by presidential proclamation under the Antiquities Act of 1906, without congressional approval, in order to protect objects of historic, prehistoric, or scientific interest on federal land. Presidents of both parties have used the act extensively; Theodore Roosevelt proclaimed the first, Devils Tower in Wyoming, in 1906, and used the act to protect the Grand Canyon.

Differences from national parks

Unlike national parks, which Congress creates, national monuments are designated by the president (though Congress may also create them by statute) and can be administered by the National Park Service, the Bureau of Land Management, the U.S. Forest Service, or the Fish and Wildlife Service. Monuments generally permit a wider range of existing uses, such as grazing or hunting in some cases, whereas parks emphasize preservation of scenery and recreation. Several monuments, including Grand Canyon and Olympic, were later redesignated as national parks by Congress.

Controversy

Use of the Antiquities Act has sometimes been controversial, notably the large-scale designations in Utah — Grand Staircase–Escalante (1996) and Bears Ears (2016) — whose boundaries were substantially reduced by President Trump in 2017 and restored by President Biden in 2021, sparking litigation over presidential authority to shrink monuments.
