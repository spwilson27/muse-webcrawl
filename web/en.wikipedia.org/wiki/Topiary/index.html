Topiary

PracticeClipping and training perennial plants into defined shapes
Typical speciesBox, yew, privet, holly, myrtle
OriginsAncient Rome; revived in Renaissance gardens

Topiary is the horticultural practice of training perennial plants by clipping the foliage and twigs of trees, shrubs and subshrubs to develop and maintain clearly defined shapes, whether geometric or fanciful. The term also describes the plants themselves. It derives from the Latin topiarius, an ornamental gardener — Pliny the Younger describes elaborate clipped figures, animals and even fleets of ships in Tuscan gardens.

History

Roman topiary passed into the Renaissance garden, reaching exuberant heights in the knot gardens and parterres of Italy, France and the Netherlands; André Le Nôtre's gardens at Versailles made clipped cones, balls and spirals a signature of formal French design. English fashion oscillated: William and Mary's Dutch-style topiary at Hampton Court gave way to the naturalistic landscape movement of the 18th century, which swept much topiary away, before Victorian gardeners revived it as ornament for both great estates and cottage gardens. Celebrated surviving gardens include Levens Hall in Cumbria (1690s, among the world's oldest topiary gardens), Great Dixter, and Château de Villandry.

Technique

Slow-growing evergreens with small leaves — box (Buxus sempervirens) and yew (Taxus baccata) above all — tolerate repeated shearing and dense, fine-textured growth. Shapes may be formed freehand by skilled clipping or trained over wire frames ("stuffed" topiary, popular in American public gardens) or by grafting and pleaching. Maintenance demands regular clipping, feeding and vigilance against diseases such as box blight and pests such as the box tree moth, which have forced many gardens to adopt substitutes like Ilex crenata.
