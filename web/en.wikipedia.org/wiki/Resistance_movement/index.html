Resistance movement

A resistance movement is an organised effort by some portion of the civil population of a country to withstand or overthrow a legally established government or an occupying power and to disrupt civil order and stability. Resistance may be violent or nonviolent, and ranges from clandestine publishing and intelligence gathering to sabotage and full-scale guerrilla warfare. The term is most famously associated with the movements that opposed Nazi occupation in Europe during the Second World War.

Second World War

Occupied Europe saw extensive resistance: the French Resistance, the Yugoslav Partisans of Tito, the Polish Home Army, Soviet partisans behind the Eastern Front, the Italian resistance after 1943, and movements in Norway, Denmark, the Netherlands, Belgium, Greece and Czechoslovakia. They gathered intelligence for the Allies, rescued downed airmen and persecuted people, sabotaged railways and industry, published underground newspapers and, at moments such as the Warsaw Uprising of 1944, rose in open revolt. Reprisals against civilians were often brutal.

Other movements

The label has been applied to many later struggles, including anti-colonial movements, resistance to apartheid in South Africa, and opposition under military juntas and authoritarian regimes. Nonviolent resistance — strikes, boycotts, mass protest — underpinned movements from the Indian independence struggle led by Gandhi to the revolutions of 1989 in Eastern Europe. Whether a movement is called "resistance", "insurgency" or "terrorism" is often politically contested, since the terms carry different moral and legal implications.

Organisation

Resistance movements typically organise in small compartmentalised cells to survive infiltration, depend on sympathetic "auxiliaries" in the wider population for food, shelter and information, and may be supported by an external sponsor supplying money, weapons and radio links, as Britain's Special Operations Executive did in the Second World War.
