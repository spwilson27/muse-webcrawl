Singular measure

In mathematics, two positive (or signed or complex) measures μ and ν defined on a measurable space (Ω, Σ) are called singular if there exist two disjoint measurable sets A and B whose union is Ω such that μ is zero on all measurable subsets of B while ν is zero on all measurable subsets of A. This is denoted μ ⊥ ν.

A refined form of the Lebesgue decomposition theorem decomposes a singular measure into a singular continuous measure and a discrete measure. See singular distribution for examples of measures that are singular continuous.

Examples

A Dirac measure on the real line is singular with respect to Lebesgue measure. More interestingly, there exist singular continuous measures: the probability measure associated with the Cantor function (the "Devil's staircase") is concentrated on the Cantor set, which has Lebesgue measure zero, yet the measure has no atoms. Such measures arise naturally in the study of fractals and in spectral theory, where the Lebesgue decomposition of a spectral measure into absolutely continuous, singular continuous, and pure point parts has physical significance, for example in the theory of quasicrystals.

Properties

Mutual singularity is not symmetric for signed or complex measures in the same transparent way as for positive measures, but the standard definition still applies. By the Lebesgue decomposition theorem, any σ-finite measure ν can be uniquely decomposed with respect to another σ-finite measure μ into an absolutely continuous part and a part singular to μ.
