Saints of the Cristero War

The saints of the Cristero War are a group of Mexican Catholic martyrs killed during the Cristero War (1926–1929) and the religious persecution that preceded and followed it. The conflict arose when the government of President Plutarco Elías Calles enforced the anticlerical provisions of the Mexican Constitution of 1917 through the so-called Calles Law, restricting public worship, closing churches and penalising clergy. Catholic rebels known as Cristeros took up arms under the cry ¡Viva Cristo Rey! ("Long live Christ the King").

Canonisation

On 21 May 2000, Pope John Paul II canonised 25 martyrs of the persecution in Saint Peter's Square. The group is often called the "Saints Cristóbal Magallanes and Companions", after Cristóbal Magallanes Jara, a parish priest executed in 1927. Most of the 25 were diocesan priests; three were laymen, including David Galván Bermúdez's companions and the young Manuel Moralez. Further martyrs have since been beatified, among them the boy José Sánchez del Río, executed at fourteen in 1928, who was canonised by Pope Francis in 2016, and the Jesuit Miguel Pro, executed in 1927 and famous for photographs of his death before a firing squad with arms outstretched in the form of a cross.

Notable martyrs

Cristóbal Magallanes Jara (1869–1927), priest of Totatiche, Jalisco, was shot without trial after being falsely accused of supporting the rebellion. Miguel Pro (1891–1927) ministered clandestinely in Mexico City in disguise before his execution. Anacleto González Flores, a lay lawyer and organiser of Catholic resistance in Guadalajara, was tortured and executed in 1927; he was beatified in 2005. José Sánchez del Río was captured while serving as a flag-bearer for the Cristeros, had the soles of his feet cut, and was shot after refusing to renounce Christ the King.

Legacy

The martyrs remain powerful symbols in Mexican Catholicism, especially in the west-central states of Jalisco, Michoacán and Guanajuato where the rebellion was strongest. Their feasts are celebrated widely, and the episode was dramatised in the 2012 film For Greater Glory. A formal settlement, the Arreglos of 1929, ended open warfare, though persecution continued in some states into the 1930s.
