Arthropods (phylum Arthropoda, from Greek arthron, "joint", and pous, "foot") are invertebrate animals characterised by an exoskeleton of chitin, a segmented body and paired jointed appendages. They are by far the largest phylum in the animal kingdom: more than 1.2 million species have been described, and arthropods account for over 80 per cent of all known living animal species. The phylum includes insects, arachnids (spiders, scorpions, mites), crustaceans (crabs, shrimp, barnacles) and myriapods (centipedes and millipedes), as well as the extinct trilobites.

Characteristics

The arthropod body is built of repeated segments, typically grouped into functional regions such as the head, thorax and abdomen (or cephalothorax and abdomen). The cuticle forms a rigid exoskeleton that protects the animal and provides attachment for muscles, but must be shed periodically in a process called moulting (ecdysis) to allow growth. Appendages are jointed and often specialised as legs, antennae, mouthparts, claws, spinnerets or swimming limbs. Arthropods have an open circulatory system, a ventral nerve cord, and a variety of respiratory structures — gills, tracheae or book lungs.

Evolution

Arthropods appear in the fossil record of the Cambrian explosion, more than 520 million years ago. Trilobites dominated early marine faunas, and giant forms such as the eurypterids ("sea scorpions") and the millipede-like Arthropleura were among the largest invertebrates ever to live. Arthropods were among the first animals to colonise land, and insects later became the first animals to evolve flight.

Classification

The living arthropods are usually divided into four subphyla: Chelicerata (spiders, scorpions, horseshoe crabs, mites and ticks); Myriapoda (centipedes and millipedes); Crustacea (a paraphyletic grouping that includes crabs, lobsters, shrimp, krill and barnacles); and Hexapoda (insects and their six-legged relatives). Molecular studies indicate that insects are nested within the crustaceans, together forming the group Pancrustacea.

Ecology and importance

Arthropods occupy nearly every habitat on Earth and are essential as pollinators, decomposers, prey and predators. They are also of great economic significance: crustaceans and insects are food; bees produce honey and pollinate crops; silkworms produce silk. Against this, many arthropods are pests of crops and stored products, and ticks, mosquitoes and other blood-feeders transmit diseases including malaria, dengue and Lyme disease. The scientific study of arthropods is called arthropodology; entomology is the study of insects.
