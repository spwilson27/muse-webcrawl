The Companion cavalry (Greek: hetairoi) was the elite heavy cavalry force of the Macedonian army under Philip II and Alexander the Great. Recruited from the Macedonian nobility, the Companions formed the king's shock cavalry and personal following in battle.

Organisation

The Companions were organised into squadrons (ilai), each drawn from a region of Macedon, with the royal squadron (ile basilike) commanded by the king himself. Under Alexander the force numbered around 1,800 men. They fought in a wedge formation designed to punch through enemy lines.

Equipment and tactics

Companions carried the long xyston lance, a sword for close combat, and wore a cuirass and the distinctive Boeotian helmet, but carried no shield. Alexander typically led the Companions in a decisive charge against the enemy centre or commander, delivered in combination with the phalanx and light troops in the "hammer and anvil" tactics refined by Philip II.

Notable battles

The Companions delivered the decisive charge at the Granicus (334 BC), Issus (333 BC), and Gaugamela (331 BC), where Alexander's charge drove Darius III from the field. They also fought at the Hydaspes in India (326 BC).
