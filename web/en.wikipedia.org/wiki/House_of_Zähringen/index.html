The House of Zähringen (German: Zähringer) was a dynasty of Swabian nobility in the Holy Roman Empire, named after Zähringen Castle near Freiburg im Breisgau. During the 11th and 12th centuries the Zähringers rose to become one of the most powerful princely families in the south-west of the Empire, holding the Duchy of Swabia in rivalry with the Hohenstaufen and exercising authority over large parts of what is now south-western Germany and Switzerland.

History

The family's rise began with Berthold I ("the Bearded"), count in the Breisgau, who was invested with the Duchy of Carinthia and the March of Verona in 1061. His son Berthold II supported the papal cause during the Investiture Controversy, opposed Emperor Henry IV, and was elected anti-duke of Swabia; in 1098 a settlement gave him the title Duke of Zähringen, which replaced Swabia as the family's principal dignity. The Zähringer dukes became noted founders of towns and monasteries: members of the family founded or developed Freiburg im Breisgau, Villingen, Bern, Fribourg, Murten, Rheinfelden, and many other settlements, particularly in the Burgundian lands west of the Jura, where they served as imperial rectors.

Extinction and legacy

The ducal line ended with the death of Berthold V in 1218 without male heirs. His possessions were divided: the Burgundian lands largely reverted to the Empire or passed to the counts of Kyburg, while the family's Swabian allodial properties passed through his sisters to the counts of Urach and Teck. A collateral line, descended from Berthold I's son Hermann, continued as the Margraves of Baden, one branch of which ruled Baden into the 20th century and regarded itself as continuing the Zähringer heritage.
