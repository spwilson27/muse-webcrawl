Resolution (music)

In music theory, a resolution is the movement of a note or chord from dissonance (an unstable sound) to consonance (a stable, final sound). Dissonance creates tension and a sense of movement, and its resolution to consonance is one of the principal engines of Western tonal music.

Melodic resolution

Individual unstable tones have conventional resolutions: the leading note (scale degree 7) tends to rise to the tonic; the fourth scale degree falls to the third; and chordal sevenths resolve downwards by step. Non-chord tones are defined largely by how they resolve — a suspension prepares as a consonance, sounds as a dissonance against the new chord, and resolves down by step, while an appoggiatura leaps to a dissonant note that then resolves by step.

Harmonic resolution

Chords built on the dominant, especially the dominant seventh, resolve to the tonic; cadences — perfect, plagal, imperfect and interrupted (deceptive) — classify the standard ways phrases resolve. A deceptive cadence deliberately frustrates resolution (V–vi), prolonging the music. In jazz, ii–V–I progressions and tritone substitutions are built from chains of dominant resolutions.

Delayed and evaded resolution

Composers exploit the expectation of resolution by delaying it — through sequences, pedal points and evaded cadences — a technique central to the expressive power of composers from Bach to Wagner, whose Tristan und Isolde famously postpones full resolution for hours. Much twentieth-century music abandons the dissonance–consonance polarity altogether, "emancipating the dissonance" in Schoenberg's phrase.
