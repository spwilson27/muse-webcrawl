Compendium

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Reference volumes of the kind in which compendia are published

A compendium is a concise collection of information pertaining to a body of knowledge. A compendium may summarize a larger work, in which case it may also be called an epitome or abridgement. The term comes from the Latin word compendere, meaning "to weigh together" or "to shorten". Compendia often assemble facts, figures, and summaries on a single subject into a single volume so that a reader can consult the essentials without reading the full literature on the topic.

Characteristics

A compendium is distinguished from a general encyclopedia by its focus on a particular subject or field, and from a textbook by its emphasis on reference rather than instruction. Typical examples include medical compendia listing diseases, drugs and dosages; legal compendia summarizing statutes and case law; and statistical compendia published by governments, which gather annual data on population, trade, health, and the economy.

Because compendia compress information, they are usually organized for rapid consultation: entries are short, arranged alphabetically or by category, and supported by tables and indexes. New editions are frequently issued so that the contents can be revised as knowledge changes.

History

The practice of compiling compendia dates to antiquity. Pliny the Elder's Natural History (AD 77) is often described as an early compendium of Roman knowledge, gathering material on astronomy, geography, zoology, and medicine into one work. In the Middle Ages, scholars such as Vincent of Beauvais produced vast compendia, notably the Speculum Maius, intended to summarize all human knowledge. The printing press made compendia cheaper and more widely available, and by the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries subject-specific compendia were standard reference tools in professions such as medicine, law, and engineering.

Modern usage

Today the word is used for any curated summary collection, including digital databases and online resources. Video-game and tabletop-game publishers issue compendia of rules, creatures, or items; scientific societies publish compendia of nomenclature and standards. The underlying idea remains the same: a single authoritative source in which the important facts of a field are gathered for convenient reference.
