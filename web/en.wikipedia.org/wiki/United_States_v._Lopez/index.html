United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549 (1995), was a landmark case of the Supreme Court of the United States concerning the scope of Congress's power under the Commerce Clause of the United States Constitution. In a 5–4 decision, the Court held that the Gun-Free School Zones Act of 1990, which made it a federal offense to possess a firearm within 1,000 feet of a school, exceeded Congress's commerce power because the regulated activity was not economic in nature.

Background

Alfonso Lopez Jr., a senior at Edison High School in San Antonio, Texas, was arrested in 1992 for carrying a concealed .38-caliber handgun into school. After state charges were dropped, he was charged under the federal Act and convicted. The Fifth Circuit reversed, holding the statute unconstitutional, and the Supreme Court affirmed.

Decision

Chief Justice William Rehnquist's majority opinion identified three categories of activity Congress may regulate under the Commerce Clause: the channels of interstate commerce; the instrumentalities of (or persons and things in) interstate commerce; and activities that substantially affect interstate commerce. Possession of a gun in a local school zone, the Court held, was not economic activity substantially affecting interstate commerce, and the statute contained no jurisdictional element ensuring a case-by-case nexus to commerce. Justice Thomas concurred, questioning the "substantial effects" doctrine, while Justices Breyer and Stevens filed principal dissents emphasizing rational-basis review.

Significance

Lopez was the first decision in nearly sixty years — since the New Deal era — in which the Court struck down a federal statute as exceeding the commerce power, and it inaugurated the Rehnquist Court's "federalism revolution," continued in United States v. Morrison (2000). Congress subsequently amended the Act to add a jurisdictional element tying the firearm to interstate commerce.
