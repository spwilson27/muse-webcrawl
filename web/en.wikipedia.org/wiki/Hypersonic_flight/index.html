Hypersonic flight

Hypersonic flight is flight through the atmosphere at speeds above Mach 5 — more than five times the speed of sound, roughly 6,200 km/h (3,850 mph) at sea level. At such speeds, aerodynamic heating, thin shock layers, and chemical reactions in the air (dissociation and ionization) become dominant design problems. All crewed spacecraft re-enter the atmosphere at hypersonic speeds, and hypersonic vehicles are a focus of modern weapons development.

History

The first manufactured object to reach hypersonic speed was the German V-2 rocket in the 1940s. The rocket-powered North American X-15 research aircraft flew 199 flights between 1959 and 1968, reaching Mach 6.7 (7,274 km/h) with pilot William J. Knight — still the record for a crewed, powered aircraft. The Space Shuttle, Apollo capsules, and other re-entry vehicles routinely endured hypersonic flight during descent, protected by ablative or reusable thermal protection systems.

Vehicle classes

  • Ballistic re-entry vehicles — warheads and capsules on ballistic trajectories.
  • Hypersonic glide vehicles — boosted to altitude by rockets, then glide and maneuver at hypersonic speed in the upper atmosphere.
  • Air-breathing cruise vehicles — powered by scramjets, such as the experimental NASA X-43 (Mach 9.6 in 2004) and the Boeing X-51 Waverider (Mach 5.1 sustained in 2013).

Challenges

Key difficulties include surface temperatures exceeding 1,000 °C requiring exotic materials and active cooling; the "plasma blackout" of radio communications caused by ionized air during re-entry; propulsion that must operate across a huge speed range; and precise guidance and control in a regime where small shape changes produce large aerodynamic effects. Since the 2010s, the United States, China, and Russia have invested heavily in hypersonic glide vehicles and cruise missiles, citing their ability to evade existing missile defenses by maneuvering at relatively low altitude.
