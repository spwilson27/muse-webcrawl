Cow's milk is the usual cause of milk allergy, the most common food allergy in early childhood.

Milk allergy is an adverse immune reaction to one or more proteins in cow's milk. It is the most common food allergy in infants and young children, affecting roughly 2–3% of infants in developed countries, though most children outgrow it by school age. The principal allergenic proteins are casein and the whey proteins alpha-lactalbumin and beta-lactoglobulin.

Milk allergy is distinct from lactose intolerance, which is a non-immune inability to digest the milk sugar lactose and does not involve an immune response or the risk of anaphylaxis.

Signs and symptoms

Reactions may be IgE-mediated, occurring within minutes to two hours of ingestion, or non-IgE-mediated, with delayed symptoms developing over hours to days. Symptoms can include hives, eczema, swelling of the lips and face, vomiting, diarrhoea, bloody stools (particularly in infants), abdominal pain, coughing, and wheeze. In severe cases, milk allergy can cause anaphylaxis, a potentially life-threatening systemic reaction requiring immediate treatment with epinephrine (adrenaline).

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is based on clinical history supported by skin-prick testing, blood tests for milk-specific IgE, and, where needed, a supervised oral food challenge. Management consists primarily of strict avoidance of cow's milk and milk-containing foods, careful reading of food labels — milk is a declared major allergen on labels in many countries — and an emergency plan including an epinephrine auto-injector for those at risk of anaphylaxis. Infants who cannot be breastfed are usually given extensively hydrolysed or amino-acid-based formula. Many children tolerate milk baked into foods before they tolerate fresh milk, and tolerance is monitored with periodic re-challenge.

Prognosis

The majority of children outgrow milk allergy: studies suggest roughly half do so by age five and a large majority by adolescence, although the rate of resolution appears slower than once believed, and allergy to baked milk persisting into later childhood is associated with a more persistent course. Milk allergy persisting into adulthood is uncommon but well documented.
