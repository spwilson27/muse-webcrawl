Norm (philosophy)

Norm (philosophy)
FieldPhilosophy, ethics, logic

Overview

A norm, in philosophy, is a standard or rule that guides, prescribes, or evaluates conduct, reasoning, or belief. Norms differ from purely descriptive regularities: a norm states how something ought to be or how agents ought to act, rather than merely how things happen to be.

Normativity

The concept is central to ethics, where moral norms prescribe right action; to epistemology, where norms of rationality govern belief formation; and to logic and philosophy of language, where norms govern correct inference and meaningful usage. Philosophers debate whether normative facts can be reduced to natural facts — the so-called "is–ought problem" raised by David Hume — and whether norms derive their authority from reason, social practice, or divine command.

Some thinkers, such as Christine Korsgaard, argue that normativity arises from the structure of self-conscious agency, while others treat norms as expressions of attitudes or as products of social convention.

Types of norms

Philosophers commonly distinguish categorical norms, which bind unconditionally, from hypothetical norms, which bind only relative to an end the agent has. They also distinguish moral, epistemic, aesthetic, legal, and logical norms, and study how norms can conflict and how they guide reasoning about what to do and what to believe.
