Battle of Dettingen

The Battle of Dettingen was fought on 27 June 1743 during the War of the Austrian Succession, at Dettingen in Bavaria, between an allied army of British, Hanoverian and Austrian troops — the so-called Pragmatic Army — and a French army under the Duc de Noailles. It is famous as the last occasion on which a reigning British monarch, King George II, personally led his troops into battle.

Background

The Pragmatic Army, assembled to support Maria Theresa of Austria, had advanced into Germany but found its supply lines cut when Noailles seized the defiles along the River Main, trapping the allies near Aschaffenburg with starvation threatening. George II arrived to take personal command and resolved to fight his way out along the north bank of the Main.

The battle

Noailles planned to hold a strong position at Dettingen while his artillery across the Main bombarded the allied column as it marched into the trap. The plan unravelled when the French commander at Dettingen, the Duc de Gramont, impatiently abandoned his position and led his troops — including the elite Maison du Roi cavalry — across the stream into open ground. In the ensuing fight, British and Hanoverian infantry volleys shattered the French charges, and the French were driven back across the stream in disorder, many drowning. George II, sword in hand, rallied his infantry during a critical moment after his horse bolted toward the enemy lines.

Aftermath

French losses were roughly 4,000–5,000 men, against some 2,000 allied casualties. The victory reopened the allied supply lines and was celebrated in Britain; Handel composed the Dettingen Te Deum to mark the occasion. Strategically the campaign soon stalled, but Dettingen remained a cherished episode in British military tradition.
