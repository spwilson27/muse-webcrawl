Niklas Luhmann (8 December 1927 – 6 November 1998) was a German sociologist, philosopher and legal scholar, one of the most important social theorists of the 20th century and the founder of a comprehensive theory of social systems.

Niklas Luhmann

Niklas Luhmann (1927–1998) was a German sociologist whose theory of social systems — developed over a thirty-year professorship at Bielefeld University — is among the most ambitious attempts to describe modern society as a whole.

Life

Born in Lüneburg, Luhmann was conscripted as a Luftwaffe auxiliary at sixteen and briefly a prisoner of war in 1945. He studied law at Freiburg, practised as an administrator in Lower Saxony, and in 1960–61 studied under Talcott Parsons at Harvard. Taking his doctorate and habilitation at Münster in a famously short period, he became in 1968 the first professor of sociology at the newly founded Bielefeld University, where he remained until his retirement in 1993. On appointment he described his research project in one line: "Theory of society; duration: 30 years; costs: none." He completed it almost to schedule with Die Gesellschaft der Gesellschaft (1997; "The Society of Society").

Theory

Luhmann's systems theory holds that society consists not of people but of communications. Social systems are autopoietic — they produce and reproduce themselves from their own operations — and operationally closed, observing their environment only through their own distinctions. Modern society, he argued, is functionally differentiated into autonomous subsystems (economy, law, science, politics, art, religion, education) each operating with its own binary code (payment/non-payment, legal/illegal, true/false, government/opposition). His huge output — over 70 books and 400 articles — also rested on his famous Zettelkasten, a slip-box of some 90,000 index cards which he treated as a research partner. His 1971 public debate with Jürgen Habermas was a landmark of postwar German social theory.
