Repossession

Repossession is a legal process in which a lender or creditor retakes ownership of property that was used as collateral for a loan after the borrower defaults. Repossession is most commonly associated with automobiles bought on credit, but it also applies to other financed goods; for real estate, the analogous process is usually foreclosure.

Process

In most U.S. states, under Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code, a secured creditor may repossess collateral without a court order provided it does not "breach the peace" — for example, by breaking into a locked garage or using threats. Agents commonly tow vehicles from driveways or public streets. After repossession, the creditor must notify the borrower and sell the property in a "commercially reasonable" manner, typically at auction. The borrower may usually reinstate or redeem the loan before sale by paying arrears or the full balance.

Deficiency and consequences

If the sale proceeds do not cover the outstanding debt, the borrower may owe a deficiency balance, while any surplus must be returned to the borrower. Repossession severely damages credit history and remains on credit reports for up to seven years in the United States.

Other jurisdictions

In the United Kingdom, goods bought under hire purchase may be repossessed by court order once a threshold of payments has been made, and the Consumer Credit Act 1974 gives borrowers substantial protections, including default notices and a right to terminate the agreement and return the goods. Rules vary widely in other countries.
