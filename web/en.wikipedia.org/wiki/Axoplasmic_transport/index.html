Axoplasmic transport

Motors: Kinesin (anterograde), dynein (retrograde)
Fast transport: ~50–400 mm/day
Slow transport: ~0.2–8 mm/day

Axoplasmic transport, also called axonal transport, is the movement of organelles, proteins, vesicles and other cargo along the axon of a neuron, between the cell body and the synaptic terminals. It is essential because axons, which can be a metre or more long, cannot synthesise most proteins locally.

Mechanism

Cargo moves along microtubule tracks. Kinesin motors carry cargo toward the axon terminal (anterograde transport), while cytoplasmic dynein carries it back toward the cell body (retrograde transport). Fast transport moves membrane-bound organelles; slow transport moves cytoskeletal and cytosolic proteins.

Clinical significance

Disrupted transport is implicated in neurodegenerative diseases including Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Some viruses, including rabies and herpesviruses, exploit retrograde transport to reach the cell body.
