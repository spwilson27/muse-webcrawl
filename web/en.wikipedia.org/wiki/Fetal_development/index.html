Fetal development

Fetal development, also called prenatal development, is the process by which a human embryo and then fetus develops during gestation, from fertilisation until birth. The fetal period begins at the start of the ninth week after fertilisation (eleventh week of gestational age) and continues until birth; before that point the developing organism is called an embryo.

Terminology and timing

Development is usually described in weeks of gestational age counted from the first day of the mother's last menstrual period, so the fetal period runs from about week 11 to week 40 of pregnancy. During this time the structures formed in the embryonic period grow and mature; very little new structure appears, but the body grows enormously in size — from roughly 30 mm and a few grams at week 9 to about 50 cm and 3.4 kg at term.

Development by month

Third month: the face becomes recognisably human, the liver produces red blood cells, and external genitalia begin to differentiate. Reflex movements begin, though they cannot yet be felt. Fourth month: the skeleton ossifies, the nervous system begins coordinated activity, and the mother typically begins to feel movement ("quickening") around weeks 18–20. Fifth and sixth months: fine hair (lanugo) and vernix caseosa cover the skin; the lungs develop bronchioles and alveolar ducts, though gas exchange is not yet possible. The age of viability — when survival outside the uterus becomes possible with intensive care — is around 22–24 weeks.

Seventh month onward: the fetus gains weight rapidly as subcutaneous fat accumulates, the brain develops gyri and sulci, and the lungs produce surfactant, which allows the air sacs to inflate. By week 37 the fetus is considered early term; most organs are fully functional except the brain and lungs, which continue maturing after birth.

Circulation

Fetal circulation differs from adult circulation because the lungs are not used for gas exchange; the placenta serves that role. Oxygenated blood from the placenta travels through the umbilical vein and largely bypasses the liver via the ductus venosus. Two shunts — the foramen ovale between the atria and the ductus arteriosus between the pulmonary artery and aorta — divert blood away from the lungs. These shunts normally close shortly after birth.

Factors affecting development

Development can be influenced by maternal nutrition, infections (such as rubella and Zika virus), teratogens including alcohol (fetal alcohol spectrum disorders), medications, and environmental toxins. Adequate folate intake before and during early pregnancy greatly reduces the risk of neural tube defects. Placental insufficiency can cause intrauterine growth restriction.
