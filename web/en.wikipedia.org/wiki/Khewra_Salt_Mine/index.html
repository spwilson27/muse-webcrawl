Khewra Salt Mine

Khewra Salt Mine
Location: Khewra, Punjab, Pakistan
Annual production: about 400,000 tonnes of salt
Rank: second largest salt mine in the world

The Khewra Salt Mine (also called the Mayo Salt Mine) is a salt mine in Khewra, north of Pind Dadan Khan, in the Jhelum District of Punjab, Pakistan. It is Pakistan's largest and oldest salt mine and the second largest in the world, producing around 400,000 tonnes of rock salt per year, most of it the pink salt marketed internationally as Himalayan salt.

History

According to tradition, the salt deposits at Khewra were discovered when the army of Alexander the Great passed through the region in 326 BC and his horses were seen licking the salty rocks. Systematic mining began under the Mughal Empire, and the modern mine was developed after the British annexed Punjab in 1849; the chief engineer Dr H. Warth established the main tunnel at ground level in 1872. The mine is now operated by the Pakistan Mineral Development Corporation.

Description

The mine lies in the Salt Range, a fold belt of Precambrian and Cambrian rocks. Its workings extend on some nineteen levels, with around 40 kilometres of tunnels. Inside, visitors can see structures carved entirely from salt bricks, including a small mosque, a replica of the Great Wall of China, and a model of the Minar-e-Pakistan, as well as salt ponds and an "assembly hall" chamber about 75 metres high. The mine receives roughly a quarter of a million tourists a year.

A narrow-gauge railway built in the British era still carries visitors into the mine. The salt is estimated to contain reserves of some 82 million tonnes in the main seam, and total reserves in the Khewra deposit may exceed 600 million tonnes.
