Pergola

Pergola
TypeGarden structure
OriginItalian Renaissance gardens; Latin pergula
MaterialsWood, stone, brick, metal, vinyl

A pergola is an outdoor garden feature forming a shaded walkway, passageway, or sitting area of vertical posts or pillars that usually support cross-beams and a sturdy open lattice, often upon which woody vines are trained. The word derives from the Italian pergola, from Latin pergula, meaning a projecting eave. As a freestanding structure or attached to a building, the pergola provides partial shade without fully enclosing a space, and differs from a gazebo in having a flat, open roof rather than a domed or closed one, and from an arbor in being generally larger and more permanent.

History

Structures of this kind appear in ancient Egyptian gardens, where vines were trained over raised beams. Pergolas flourished in the Italian Renaissance garden, where — described by writers such as John Evelyn, who noted the vine-covered pergolas of the Mediterranean — they shaded walks between garden compartments. They fell out of fashion in the naturalistic English landscape garden of the 18th century but returned with the Arts and Crafts movement: Gertrude Jekyll and William Robinson championed sturdy brick-and-timber pergolas laden with roses and wisteria, a form that became a standard feature of suburban gardens in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Design and construction

Traditional pergolas use durable hardwoods such as cedar, teak or oak, or masonry pillars with timber beams; modern versions are also built in aluminium, fibreglass and vinyl. The open roof partially filters sunlight, and the structure is typically planted with climbers — grape vines, wisteria, jasmine, climbing roses or bougainvillea — that complete the shaded canopy in summer. Pergolas may define outdoor rooms on patios and decks, frame views, or support seating areas in public parks.
