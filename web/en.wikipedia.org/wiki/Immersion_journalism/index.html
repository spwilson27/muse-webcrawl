Immersion journalism

Immersion journalism is a style of journalism in which the reporter submerges himself or herself in a situation or community for an extended period in order to describe it from the inside. It overlaps with undercover reporting, participatory journalism and literary nonfiction.

Examples

Classic examples include Nellie Bly's 1887 undercover stay in a mental asylum for Ten Days in a Mad-House, Günter Wallraff's undercover work in West German industry, Barbara Ehrenreich's Nickel and Dimed (2001), about low-wage work in America, and Ted Conover's reporting written from inside the worlds of freight-riding hobos and trainee prison guards.

Criticism

Critics raise questions about deception of sources, the reporter's effect on the events described, and whether personal experience can stand in for systematic evidence.
