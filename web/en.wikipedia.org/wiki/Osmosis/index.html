Osmosis

Osmosis
Process typePassive transport (diffusion of solvent)
Key quantityOsmotic pressure, π = iMRT (van 't Hoff equation)
ApplicationsReverse osmosis desalination, dialysis, plant water uptake

Osmosis is the spontaneous net movement or diffusion of solvent molecules through a selectively permeable membrane from a region of high water potential (region of lower solute concentration) to a region of low water potential (region of higher solute concentration), in the direction that tends to equalize the solute concentrations on the two sides. It may also be used to describe a physical process in which any solvent moves across a selectively permeable membrane (permeable to the solvent, but not the solute) separating two solutions of different concentrations.

Mechanism and osmotic pressure

Osmosis is a form of passive transport driven by the difference in chemical potential of the solvent. The pressure that must be applied to the concentrated side to stop the net flow is the osmotic pressure, described for dilute solutions by the van 't Hoff equation π = iMRT. Water moves across cell membranes both directly through the lipid bilayer and through specialised channel proteins called aquaporins. The osmotic behaviour of a solution relative to a cell is described as hypotonic, isotonic, or hypertonic: animal cells swell (and may lyse) in hypotonic media and shrink (crenate) in hypertonic media, while plant cells become turgid in hypotonic media, the pressure against the cell wall that keeps non-woody tissues rigid.

Biological and industrial importance

Osmosis underlies water uptake by plant roots, reabsorption in the kidneys, and turgor-driven growth. Applied pressure exceeding osmotic pressure drives reverse osmosis, the basis of desalination and water purification systems used worldwide, as well as of haemodialysis (where diffusion and osmosis across a membrane clean the blood). The phenomenon was studied in the 18th–19th centuries by Nollet, Dutrochet, Pfeffer, and van 't Hoff, who showed its analogy to gas pressure.
