Oikos (Ancient Greek: οἶκος, plural οἶκοι) is the Greek word for a house, household, or family. In ancient Greek society the oikos was the basic unit of social and economic organisation: the household headed by the kyrios, comprising his wife, children, enslaved people, and the property — land, buildings and stores — needed to sustain them.

Structure and roles

The male head of household exercised authority over its members; the wife managed the interior of the house, textile production, and stores, in an ideal described in Xenophon's Oeconomicus. Physically, the Greek house was typically arranged around a central courtyard, with rooms including the andrōn (men's dining room), storerooms, and women's quarters. The oikos aimed at self-sufficiency (autarkeia) in food and goods.

Significance

Aristotle treats the oikos in the first book of the Politics as the natural foundation from which the village and then the polis develop, and analyses the household relations — master and slave, husband and wife, parent and child — as the elementary forms of rule. The word is the root of modern terms such as "economy" (from oikonomia, household management) and "ecology".
