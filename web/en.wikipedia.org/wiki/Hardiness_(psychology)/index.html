Hardiness (psychology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hardiness is a personality trait and style of coping associated with resilience to stress. It was introduced by the psychologist Suzanne Kobasa in 1979, in studies of executives who remained healthy despite high levels of stressful life events, and was further developed with Salvatore Maddi. Hardiness is conceptualised as a combination of three attitudes, known as the three Cs: commitment, control and challenge.

The three Cs

Commitment is the tendency to involve oneself actively in life's activities and relationships rather than retreating into alienation. Control is the belief that one can influence events through effort, rather than lapsing into helplessness. Challenge is the view that change is a normal and stimulating part of life and an opportunity for growth, rather than a threat to security.

Research

Kobasa's original study of business executives found that those high in hardiness had lower rates of illness for comparable stress levels. Subsequent research linked hardiness to better health and performance in groups including military personnel, athletes, teachers and emergency workers. Hardiness is thought to work by encouraging problem-focused coping and the reappraisal of stressors, buffering the physiological effects of stress, and it is measured by scales such as the Personal Views Survey. Training programmes, notably Maddi's "HardiTraining", aim to increase hardiness.

Criticism

Critics have questioned the construct's measurement, the stability of its three-factor structure, and the extent to which hardiness overlaps with low neuroticism or general optimism. Hardiness nonetheless remains an influential concept in health psychology and in the broader study of psychological resilience.
