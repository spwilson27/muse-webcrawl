Bronze Age Europe

The Bronze Age in Europe (c. 3200–600 BC) is the period of European prehistory characterised by the use of bronze — an alloy of copper and tin — for tools and weapons. It succeeded the Neolithic and Copper Ages and was followed by the Iron Age. Chronology varies by region: bronze metallurgy appeared earliest in the Aegean and southeast Europe and reached Scandinavia and Britain much later.

Periods and cultures

In the Aegean, the Bronze Age saw the palace civilisations of Minoan Crete and Mycenaean Greece, with writing systems (Linear A and Linear B) and long-distance trade. Central Europe's Early Bronze Age is represented by the Únětice culture, famous for the Nebra sky disc; the Middle Bronze Age by the Tumulus culture; and the Late Bronze Age by the widespread Urnfield culture, whose cremation cemeteries stretched from Hungary to Spain.

Northern Europe experienced a flourishing Nordic Bronze Age (c. 1700–500 BC), rich in rock art, lurs (bronze horns) and elaborate burials such as the Egtved Girl. In Britain, the Wessex culture and later hoards of metalwork mark the period; in Iberia, cultures such as El Argar developed early bronze metallurgy, partly driven by local copper and tin sources.

Trade and society

Because tin is scarce, bronze working depended on long-distance exchange networks moving Cornish and Erzgebirge tin, Baltic amber, salt and finished metalwork across the continent. Shipwrecks such as Uluburun (off Turkey) reveal cargoes of copper and tin ingots in roughly the 10:1 ratio needed for bronze. The period saw increasing social stratification, warrior elites, hillforts in the Late Bronze Age, and prestige goods deposited in hoards and graves.

End of the Bronze Age

Around 1200 BC the eastern Mediterranean palace systems collapsed (the "Late Bronze Age collapse"), while in most of Europe the transition to iron was gradual, occurring between about 1000 and 600 BC as ironworking spread from the Near East and the Hallstatt culture emerged.
