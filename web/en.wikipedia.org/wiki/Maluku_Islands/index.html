The Maluku Islands (Indonesian: Kepulauan Maluku), also known as the Moluccas, are an archipelago in eastern Indonesia, lying between Sulawesi and New Guinea. Historically famous as the original Spice Islands, they were for centuries the world's only source of nutmeg, mace, and cloves, which drew Portuguese, Spanish, Dutch, and English traders and colonists to the region.

Geography

The islands are divided administratively between the provinces of Maluku, whose capital is Ambon, and North Maluku, whose capital is Sofifi on Halmahera. The largest islands include Halmahera, Seram, and Buru; the small volcanic Banda Islands were the original source of nutmeg, and Ternate and Tidore were major clove-producing sultanates.

History

The spice trade made the islands a focus of European rivalry from the early 16th century. The Dutch East India Company (VOC) eventually monopolized the trade, brutally enforcing control of nutmeg production in the Banda Islands in 1621. The islands became part of the Dutch East Indies and then of independent Indonesia in the mid-20th century. Between 1999 and 2002 the region experienced severe sectarian violence between Muslim and Christian communities, ended by the Malino II Accord.

Economy and ecology

Agriculture, fishing, and forestry remain important, along with nickel mining, especially on Halmahera. The islands lie in the biodiverse transition zone known as Wallacea and feature extensive coral reefs and rainforests.
