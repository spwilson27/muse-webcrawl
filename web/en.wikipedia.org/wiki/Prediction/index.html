Prediction

A prediction (Latin præ-, "before", and dicere, "to say"), or forecast, is a statement about a future event or about future data. Predictions are often, but not always, based upon experience or knowledge of the forecaster. There is no universal agreement about the exact difference between the two terms; different authors and disciplines ascribe different connotations.

Although future events are necessarily uncertain, so guaranteed accurate information about the future is impossible, prediction can be useful to assist in making plans about possible developments. In the scientific context, a prediction is a rigorous, often quantitative, statement forecasting what would be observed under specific conditions — for example, the prediction, in general relativity, of the deflection of starlight by the Sun, later confirmed by observation.

Informal prediction in daily life

In everyday life, people predict the weather, the behaviour of others, and the outcomes of events ranging from elections to sporting contests. Weather forecasting applies science and technology to predict the conditions of the atmosphere for a given location and time, and has grown steadily more accurate with numerical models and satellite data, though chaotic dynamics limit long-range skill.

Prediction in science

In science, a prediction is a statement about what a theory implies will be observed. Predictions that are novel — that do not merely accommodate already-known data — are regarded as especially strong tests of a theory. Successful risky predictions, such as the discovery of Neptune from perturbations in the orbit of Uranus, or the bending of light during the 1919 solar eclipse, are landmarks in the history of science. Conversely, the philosopher Karl Popper held that a theory is scientific only if it forbids certain outcomes — that is, if it makes falsifiable predictions.

Statistics and forecasting

In statistics, predictions are one aspect of statistical inference; prediction intervals quantify the uncertainty of a forecast. Time-series methods, regression models, and machine-learning techniques are widely used for demand forecasting, financial prediction, and econometrics, though systematic biases — overconfidence, anchoring — are known to degrade human judgement of probabilities. Philip Tetlock's research on expert political judgement found that most experts performed only modestly better than chance, while "superforecasters" using probabilistic reasoning and frequent updating performed substantially better.

Prediction and prophecy

Throughout history, prediction has also taken religious and divinatory forms: oracle, prophecy, augury, and astrology. The Oracle at Delphi, the prophecies attributed to Nostradamus, and various forms of fortune-telling exemplify claims to foreknowledge grounded in supernatural rather than empirical authority. Such claims have generally not withstood controlled testing.

"Prediction is very difficult, especially if it's about the future." — attributed in various forms, often to Niels Bohr
