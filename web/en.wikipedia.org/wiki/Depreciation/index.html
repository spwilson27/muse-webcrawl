Depreciation

In accounting, depreciation is a method of allocating the cost of a tangible (physical) asset over its useful life, reflecting the gradual decrease in the asset's value through wear, age and obsolescence. Businesses depreciate long-term assets both for accounting purposes — matching the cost of an asset to the periods in which it generates revenue — and for tax purposes, where depreciation deductions reduce taxable income. The corresponding concept for intangible assets is amortisation, and for natural resources, depletion.

Methods

Common methods include straight-line depreciation, which charges an equal amount each year (cost minus salvage value, divided by useful life); the declining balance method, which applies a fixed percentage to the shrinking book value and so charges more early on; and units-of-production depreciation, based on actual use or output. In economics, depreciation (also called capital consumption) is the measure of value lost by the existing stock of capital goods in a period.

Effects

Depreciation is a non-cash expense: it reduces reported profit and the asset's carrying value on the balance sheet but does not itself involve cash outflow. Tax rules in most countries prescribe allowable lives and methods, which may differ from those used in financial reporting.
