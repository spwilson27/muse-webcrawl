Vacuum packing

Vacuum packing is a method of packaging that removes air from the package prior to sealing. This method involves (manually or automatically) placing items in a plastic film package, removing air from inside, and sealing the package. Vacuum packing reduces atmospheric oxygen, limiting the growth of aerobic bacteria and fungi and preventing the evaporation of volatile components, thereby extending shelf life.

Purposes

Vacuum packing is widely used to store dry foods over long periods, such as cereals, nuts, cured meats, cheese, smoked fish, coffee, and potato chips, and to store fresh foods such as vegetables and meat on a short-term basis, since it inhibits bacterial growth. It also reduces the bulk of non-food items; clothing and bedding can be compressed for storage or travel. In cooking, vacuum-sealed food is the basis of the sous vide method.

Equipment

External vacuum sealers draw air from a bag placed outside the machine and heat-seal it, while chamber vacuum machines place the entire package inside a chamber from which the air is evacuated; chamber machines can seal liquids and are standard in commercial food processing. Industrial systems may flush the package with inert gas after evacuation, a form of modified atmosphere packaging.

Safety

Vacuum-packed perishable foods must still be refrigerated or frozen, because the anaerobic environment can favour the growth of Clostridium botulinum and other pathogens if temperature control is neglected.
