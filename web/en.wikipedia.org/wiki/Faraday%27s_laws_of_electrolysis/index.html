Faraday's laws of electrolysis

Faraday's laws of electrolysis are quantitative relationships based on the electrochemical research published by Michael Faraday in 1833. They govern the amount of material deposited or dissolved at electrodes during electrolysis and laid the foundations of electrochemistry.

First law

The mass of a substance altered at an electrode during electrolysis is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity transferred at that electrode: m ∝ Q, where Q is the total electric charge passed.

Second law

For a given quantity of electricity, the mass of a substance altered is proportional to its equivalent weight — its molar mass divided by the number of electrons involved in the reaction. Combined, the laws give m = (Q · M) / (F · z), where F is the Faraday constant, approximately 96,485 coulombs per mole, and z is the valency of the ions.

Significance

The laws implied that electricity, like matter, might come in discrete units, an early hint of the electron, and they remain fundamental to electroplating, electrowinning, and coulometry.
