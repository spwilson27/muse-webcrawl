The Azuchi–Momoyama period is the final phase of the Sengoku period in Japanese history, from 1568 to 1600, during which Japan was reunified after a century of civil war. The name derives from the sites of the two great unifiers' castles: Azuchi Castle of Oda Nobunaga and Momoyama (Fushimi) Castle of Toyotomi Hideyoshi.

Castle building and lavish screen painting flourished under the unifiers.

Reunification

Nobunaga entered Kyoto in 1568 and, through campaigns against rival daimyō and the warrior monks of Enryaku-ji and the Ikkō-ikki, unified central Japan; he was killed in the Honnō-ji Incident of 1582. His retainer Hideyoshi completed the unification by 1590 with the fall of the Hōjō at Odawara. Hideyoshi carried out land surveys, the "sword hunt" separating warriors from peasants, and class reforms; he then launched invasions of Korea (1592–98) that failed after his death in 1598. Victory at the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600 made Tokugawa Ieyasu dominant, leading to the Tokugawa shogunate in 1603.

Culture and trade

The period saw the arrival of Europeans ("Nanban" trade), firearms, and Christianity, alongside the flowering of the tea ceremony under Sen no Rikyū, bold castle architecture (Azuchi, Himeji, Osaka, Fushimi), and the ornate gold-leaf screen paintings of the Kanō school.
