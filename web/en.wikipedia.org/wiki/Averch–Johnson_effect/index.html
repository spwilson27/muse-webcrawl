Averch–Johnson effect

The Averch–Johnson effect is the tendency of regulated companies to engage in excessive amounts of capital accumulation in order to expand the volume of their profits. If companies' profits to capital ratio is regulated at a certain percentage, then there is a strong incentive for companies to over-invest in order to increase profits overall. This investment goes beyond any optimal efficiency point for capital that the company may have calculated, as higher profit is almost always desired over and above efficiency.

The model

The effect was formalized by Harvey Averch and Leland L. Johnson in their 1962 paper in the American Economic Review, "Behavior of the Firm Under Regulatory Constraint". Under rate-of-return regulation, a monopolist's profit is constrained so that the rate of return on its capital base does not exceed a "fair" rate s, which exceeds the cost of capital r. Maximizing profit subject to this constraint, the firm chooses an input mix in which capital is substituted for labour beyond the cost-minimizing point: the marginal rate of technical substitution no longer equals the ratio of input prices, and the firm "gold-plates" its rate base.

Implications

The result motivated economists to look for incentive-compatible forms of regulation, such as price-cap (RPI–X) regulation, which avoids tying allowed profits directly to the capital stock. Empirical studies have searched for over-capitalization in regulated utilities, with mixed findings.
