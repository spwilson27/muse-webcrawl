Contempt of Congress is the act of obstructing the work of the United States Congress or one of its committees — most commonly by refusing to comply with a congressional subpoena, declining to testify, or withholding documents. The power to punish such contempt rests on Congress's implied constitutional authority to conduct investigations in aid of its legislative function, an authority the Supreme Court affirmed in McGrain v. Daugherty, 273 U.S. 135 (1927). Congress may proceed in three ways: through its dormant inherent contempt power, by criminal referral to the Department of Justice under statute, or by civil suit seeking a court order compelling compliance.

Historical background

The contempt power descends from the British Parliament's authority to punish breaches of privilege. The first American case arose in 1795, when the House arrested Robert Randall for attempting to bribe members. Through the nineteenth century Congress used its inherent power — trying and imprisoning contemnors itself through the Sergeant at Arms — most prominently in the 1927 case of Harry Sinclair's associates during the Teapot Dome investigations. In Jurney v. MacCracken, 294 U.S. 125 (1935), the Court upheld the Senate's power to punish a past contempt (the destruction of subpoenaed documents) even though compliance was no longer possible. Congress last exercised inherent contempt in 1934 and has since preferred statutory referral.

Mechanisms

Criminal contempt (statutory)

Under 2 U.S.C. § 192, enacted in 1857, wilful default or refusal to answer "any question pertinent to the question under inquiry" before Congress or a committee is a federal misdemeanour, punishable by a fine of $100 to $100,000 and imprisonment of one to twelve months. Section 194 requires the committee and the full chamber to certify the contempt to the United States Attorney, whose "duty" is to present it to a grand jury. In practice, prosecutors exercise discretion — a particular difficulty when the contemnor is an executive official asserting executive privilege, as in the cases of Harriet Miers (2008) and Eric Holder (2012), where the Justice Department declined to prosecute its own administration's position. Private citizens and former officials have been prosecuted, including Steve Bannon (convicted 2022) and Peter Navarro (convicted 2023) for defying the January 6 Committee.

Civil enforcement

Either chamber may authorise a civil action asking a federal court to declare that the subpoena must be obeyed — the route taken against Attorney General William Barr and Commerce Secretary Wilbur Ross in 2019, and in the House's litigation over Donald Trump's financial records, which produced the Supreme Court's balancing test in Trump v. Mazars USA, LLP (2020). Civil suits avoid executive-branch gatekeeping but can outlast the Congress that filed them.

Inherent contempt

Under its inherent power, Congress may itself arrest, try, and detain a contemnor, or — in modern proposals — impose monetary fines without imprisonment. Scholars and some members have periodically proposed reviving fines as a faster instrument of oversight, but the power has not been used since the 1930s.

Defences and limits

Witnesses may resist on grounds of executive privilege, the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination, attorney–client privilege, or lack of pertinency. The contempt power extends only to inquiries within Congress's legitimate legislative sphere, though courts construe that sphere broadly.
