Rare species

A rare species is a group of organisms that are very uncommon, scarce, or infrequently encountered. This designation may be applied to either a plant or animal taxon, and is distinct from the term endangered or threatened species. Designation of a rare species may be made by an official body, such as a national government, state, or province.

Rarity can result from a naturally small geographic range, strict habitat specialisation, or naturally low population densities. Species may also become rare through habitat loss, exploitation, or competition from introduced species. Land plants with very specific climate requirements may be naturally rare yet stable.

Rabinowitz's classification

Ecologist Deborah Rabinowitz proposed a classification of rarity based on three factors: geographic range (large or small), habitat specificity (broad or restricted), and local population size (large or small). Species that are small-ranged, habitat-restricted and sparsely distributed are considered the rarest.

Conservation

Rare species are not necessarily at immediate risk of extinction, but their small populations make them vulnerable to stochastic events such as disease, fire, or climate shifts. Conservation assessments such as the IUCN Red List take rarity into account when evaluating species' status.
