Subsidiarity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Subsidiarity is an organising principle that matters ought to be handled by the smallest, lowest or least centralised competent authority. Political decisions should be taken at a local level if possible, rather than by a central authority. The principle is found in Catholic social teaching, in the constitutional law of the European Union, and in theories of Federalism.

The classic formulation is in Pope Pius XI's encyclical Quadragesimo anno (1931): it is an injustice, a grave evil and a disturbance of right order to assign to a greater and higher association what lesser and subordinate organisations can do. In EU law, Article 5 of the Treaty on European Union provides that in areas outside its exclusive competence the Union shall act only if and in so far as the objectives cannot be sufficiently achieved by the member states and can be better achieved at Union level; national parliaments police the principle through an “early warning” procedure.

Applications

Subsidiarity informs Catholic social teaching alongside solidarity and the common good, and has been taken up in political economy — notably in arguments that fiscal and regulatory competence should rest with the lowest tier of government able to internalise a policy's effects. In Germany the principle shapes relations between the Federation and the Länder. Critics note that “competence” is itself contestable: almost any problem can be described as exceeding local capacity, so the principle guides argument more often than it decides cases.
