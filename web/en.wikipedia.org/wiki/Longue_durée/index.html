The longue durée (French for "long duration") is an approach to the study of history developed by the Annales school, most fully articulated by the French historian Fernand Braudel. It emphasises the slow-moving structural forces — geography, climate, demography, technology and mental frameworks — that shape societies over centuries, in contrast to the histoire événementielle, the history of short-lived political and military events.

Braudel's three levels of time

In the preface to his masterpiece The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II (1949), Braudel distinguished three levels of historical time: the almost imperceptible time of structures (the longue durée), the slower rhythm of economic and social conjunctures (cycles of roughly decades to half-centuries), and the rapid, "surface" time of individual events. His 1958 essay in the journal Annales argued that historians should privilege the longest level, since structures constrain and outlast the lives of individuals.

Influence

The concept reshaped twentieth-century historiography, informing world-systems analysis (Immanuel Wallerstein), environmental history, the history of mentalities, and later "deep history" and big history. Critics have objected that the approach can underplay human agency and contingency, but the longue durée remains a foundational concept in historical methodology.
