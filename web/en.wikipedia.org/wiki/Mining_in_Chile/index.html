Mining in Chile is a pillar of the Chilean economy and one of the country's best-known industries worldwide. Chile is the world's largest producer of copper and a leading producer of lithium, molybdenum, and other minerals. The bulk of large-scale mining is concentrated in the arid north of the country, in and around the Atacama Desert, where some of the largest open-pit and underground mines on Earth are located.

Copper

Chile typically accounts for roughly a quarter to a third of world copper mine production. The giant porphyry copper deposits of the Andes include Escondida, the world's largest copper mine, and Chuquicamata, Collahuasi, Los Pelambres, and El Teniente. The state-owned company Codelco, created in 1976 from nationalised copper assets, remains one of the world's largest copper producers, alongside private groups such as BHP, Antofagasta plc, Anglo American, and Freeport-McMoRan.

Lithium and other minerals

The salt flats (salares) of the Atacama contain some of the world's largest lithium brine resources, and Chile has been one of the top two lithium producers globally. Chile also produces gold, silver, iron ore, molybdenum as a by-product of copper mining, and historically was famous for its nitrate (saltpeter) deposits, which dominated world fertiliser markets in the late 19th and early 20th centuries before synthetic production displaced them.

History

Mining predates Spanish colonisation, and silver, gold, and coal mining expanded during the colonial and early republican periods. The nitrate boom enriched the country after the War of the Pacific (1879–1884) gave Chile control of the nitrate provinces. Copper mining industrialised in the early 20th century under American companies, and the industry was nationalised under President Salvador Allende in 1971; Codelco was retained after the 1973 coup while new private investment was later encouraged.
