Dynamic game difficulty balancing

Rubber-banding in racing games is a familiar form of dynamic difficulty adjustment.

Dynamic game difficulty balancing (also dynamic difficulty adjustment, or DDA) is the process of automatically changing parameters, scenarios and behaviour in a video game—as the game is played—in order to keep the level of challenge matched to the player's ability. The aim is to keep the player in a state of engagement described by psychologists as flow: neither bored by tasks that are too easy nor frustrated by ones that are too hard.

Methods

Common techniques include adjusting enemy health, accuracy and aggression; changing the availability of resources such as ammunition or health pickups; and altering the behaviour of opponents, as in the "rubber-banding" of racing games, where AI drivers speed up when behind and slow down when ahead. More sophisticated systems track a model of player performance. Valve's Left 4 Dead (2008) used an "AI Director" that varied the pacing and intensity of zombie attacks according to the survivors' estimated stress, while Resident Evil 4 quietly scaled enemy numbers and toughness behind its visible difficulty settings.

Reception

Designers must balance fairness against transparency: players who notice heavy-handed adjustment may feel their achievements are devalued or that success is punished, a criticism often levelled at racing games in which opponents inexplicably catch up. Some games expose the system openly, offering adaptive difficulty as an explicit option, and researchers have explored machine-learning approaches that adapt challenge, content and even level layout to individual players.
