The cinema of Australia began with the production of The Story of the Kelly Gang (1906), often identified as the world's first feature-length narrative film. Australia developed a significant early film industry, but domestic production declined under the pressure of foreign distribution monopolies before a government-supported revival in the 1970s — the Australian New Wave — brought Australian stories to international prominence.

Early years

Australia was among the earliest filmmaking nations; the Salvation Army's Limelight Department produced films from 1897. The 1906 Kelly Gang film ran over an hour and toured widely. The 1910s and 1920s saw a productive silent industry, including Raymond Longford and Lottie Lyell's collaborations such as The Sentimental Bloke (1919). Charles Chauvel's Forty Thousand Horsemen (1940) and Sons of Matthew (1949) sustained local production through the mid-century, though Hollywood and British imports dominated screens.

The New Wave

Federal and state support from around 1970 — film schools, the Australian Film Commission, and tax incentives — launched the Australian New Wave. Key works include Peter Weir's Picnic at Hanging Rock (1975) and The Last Wave (1977), Fred Schepisi's The Chant of Jimmie Blacksmith (1978), Gillian Armstrong's My Brilliant Career (1979), and George Miller's Mad Max (1979). In parallel, low-budget "Ozploitation" genre films found cult audiences abroad.

International success

The 1980s and 1990s produced global successes: "Crocodile" Dundee (1986), Strictly Ballroom (1992), Muriel's Wedding (1994), The Adventures of Priscilla, Queen of the Desert (1994), Babe (1995), and Shine (1996). Australian directors and actors became major figures in Hollywood, while the domestic industry continued to rely on a mix of public funding and international co-production. Later landmarks include Mad Max: Fury Road (2015) and a growing body of Indigenous Australian filmmaking led by directors such as Warwick Thornton.
