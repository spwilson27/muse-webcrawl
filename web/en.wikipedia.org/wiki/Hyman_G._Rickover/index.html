Hyman G. Rickover

Hyman George Rickover (January 27, 1900 – July 8, 1986) was an admiral in the United States Navy. He directed the original development of naval nuclear propulsion and controlled its operations for three decades as director of the U.S. Naval Reactors office. In addition, he oversaw the development of the Shippingport Atomic Power Station, the world's first commercial pressurized water reactor used for generating electricity. Rickover is known as the "Father of the Nuclear Navy."

Early life and career

Rickover was born Chaim Godalia Rickover to Jewish parents in Maków Mazowiecki, then part of the Russian Empire, and immigrated to the United States as a child, settling in Chicago. He graduated from the U.S. Naval Academy in 1922 and earned a master's degree in electrical engineering from Columbia University. He served on submarines and, during the Second World War, headed the electrical section of the Bureau of Ships, receiving the Legion of Merit.

Naval Reactors

In 1946 Rickover was assigned to the Manhattan Project's Oak Ridge laboratory, where he became convinced of the promise of nuclear propulsion for submarines. Appointed director of the Naval Reactors Branch in 1949, he drove the development of the pressurized water reactor that powered USS Nautilus, the world's first nuclear-powered vessel, launched in 1954. Rickover personally selected and trained the officers and crews of the nuclear fleet, famously interviewing every prospective nuclear officer himself, and enforced a culture of rigorous technical standards and accountability. His tenure was repeatedly extended with congressional support, and he served a record 64 years in uniform before his forced retirement in 1982. The first U.S. Navy submarine named for a living person, USS Hyman G. Rickover (SSN-709), honored him; a second Virginia-class submarine, SSN-795, later took the name as well.

Legacy and criticism

Rickover's program achieved a notable reactor safety record in the U.S. nuclear navy, and his management philosophy influenced engineering organizations worldwide. He was also criticized for his abrasive personality, autocratic management style, prolonged dominance over naval reactor policy, and his role in the dismissal of Captain William J. Ruhe and conflicts with senior officers. He received two Congressional Gold Medals and the Presidential Medal of Freedom.
