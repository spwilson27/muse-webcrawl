Nkrumaism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Kwame Nkrumah (1909–1972)

Nkrumaism is the political philosophy and ideology associated with Kwame Nkrumah, the leader of Ghana's independence movement and the country's first prime minister and president. Nkrumah set out the system most fully in works such as Consciencism: Philosophy and Ideology for Decolonization (1964), Neo-Colonialism, the Last Stage of Imperialism (1965), and the Handbook of Revolutionary Warfare (1968).

Principles

Nkrumaism rests on several connected ideas. Politically, it demands complete decolonization and the political unification of Africa — "Africa Must Unite" — arguing that the continent's balkanized states would remain weak and dependent without a continental union government. Economically, it prescribes an African form of socialism: state-led industrialization, public ownership of key sectors, and planned development, while insisting that African socialism grows from communalist traditions rather than simply copying Soviet or Western models. Philosophically, consciencism attempts to reconcile the three strands Nkrumah identified in the modern African conscience — indigenous tradition, Islam, and Euro-Christian influence — within a materialist framework affirming the equality and dignity of all people. Against the lingering economic domination that followed flag independence, Nkrumah coined the modern usage of neo-colonialism, holding that a state may be nominally independent while its economy is directed from outside.

Practice and legacy

In Ghana, Nkrumaism was the doctrine of the Convention People's Party, expressed in mass mobilization, "positive action", large state projects such as the Volta River (Akosombo) Dam, and support for liberation movements across Africa. Critics associated its later phase with one-party rule, preventive detention, and economic mismanagement; Nkrumah was overthrown by a military coup in 1966 and died in exile in 1972. The philosophy has been revived in Ghana by parties claiming his mantle, notably the modern CPP and the People's National Convention, and it remains a reference point in debates about Pan-Africanism, African socialism, and continental integration under the African Union.
