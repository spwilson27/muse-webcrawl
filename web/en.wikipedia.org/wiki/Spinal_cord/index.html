Spinal cord

The spinal cord is the long, thin bundle of nervous tissue extending from the brainstem down the vertebral canal. It carries signals between the Brain and the body and mediates reflexes; with the brain it forms the central nervous system.

Anatomy

In adults the cord is about 45 cm long, ending around the first lumbar vertebra in the conus medullaris. Thirty-one pairs of spinal nerves branch from its segments (8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, 1 coccygeal). Cross-sections show a butterfly-shaped core of grey matter surrounded by white-matter tracts: ascending tracts carry sensation (touch, proprioception, pain, temperature), descending tracts carry motor commands.

Reflexes and injury

Simple reflex arcs — such as the knee-jerk — run entirely within the cord. Spinal cord injury interrupts traffic below the lesion: damage at cervical levels can cause tetraplegia, at thoracic/lumbar levels paraplegia; roughly a quarter-million to a half-million people are injured worldwide each year, mostly in road accidents and falls.
