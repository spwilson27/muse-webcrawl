Matchmaking (video games)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In multiplayer video games, matchmaking is the process of connecting players together for online play sessions. A matchmaking system typically attempts to assemble players of comparable skill into balanced matches, replacing or supplementing older server-browser models in which players manually chose a server to join.

How it works

Players enter a queue, and the system forms matches according to criteria that may include a hidden or visible skill rating (matchmaking rating, or MMR, often derived from systems such as Elo or TrueSkill), geographic proximity and connection quality (ping), preferred game mode, language, and party size. To keep waiting times acceptable, matchmaking pools usually widen their acceptable skill range the longer a player waits.

Ranked and casual play

Many games separate matchmaking into casual (unranked) queues, which prioritise speed and relaxed play, and ranked (competitive) queues, in which outcomes adjust a visible rank or rating and matches are formed more strictly by skill. Skill-based matchmaking (SBMM) in casual modes is a persistent topic of debate among players: supporters argue it protects new and weaker players from one-sided games, while critics contend it makes every match feel highly competitive, penalises playing with friends of differing skill, and can increase queue times.

History

Online play in the 1990s relied mainly on server browsers and third-party services. Centralised matchmaking became prominent with console services such as Xbox Live in the early 2000s and the release of games like Halo 2 (2004), whose playlist system and skill ranks popularised the model. It is now standard in shooters, MOBAs, sports and battle royale games.

Related issues

Matchmaking quality is affected by smurfing (experienced players using new accounts), boosting (inflating another player's rank), cheating, and uneven regional player populations. Engagement-optimised matchmaking — the allegation that some systems manipulate matches to maximise player retention rather than fairness — is a recurring controversy; a widely discussed patent by Activision on steering players toward purchases through matchmaking attracted criticism although the company stated it was exploratory and unused.
