Stephen (Greek: Στέφανος, Stéphanos, "crown") is traditionally regarded as the protomartyr, the first martyr of Christianity. According to the Acts of the Apostles, he was a deacon in the early church at Jerusalem, appointed to distribute food to Greek-speaking widows, who was stoned to death after preaching before the Sanhedrin. His martyrdom, dated to about 34–36 AD, was witnessed by Saul of Tarsus, who later became the apostle Paul.

Account in Acts

Acts 6 describes Stephen as one of seven men chosen by the apostles to serve the Hellenistic widows in the daily distribution, "a man full of faith and of the Holy Spirit". Accused of blasphemy against Moses and God, he was brought before the Sanhedrin, where Acts 7 records his long speech reviewing Israel's history and accusing the council of resisting the Holy Spirit and murdering the Righteous One. Enraged, the crowd dragged him outside the city and stoned him. As he died, Stephen prayed, "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit", and asked forgiveness for his executioners — words deliberately echoing the death of Jesus. The witnesses laid their coats at the feet of Saul.

Veneration

Stephen's feast falls immediately after Christmas — 26 December in the West — reflecting his closeness to Christ in the calendar of saints. His relics were said to have been discovered in 415 at Caphar Gamala through a dream of the priest Lucian, and were translated to Jerusalem and later widely distributed; in Rome they are venerated at the basilica of Saint Lawrence outside the Walls, where tradition holds the bodies of the two deacon-martyrs lie together. Stephen is depicted in art as a young deacon in a dalmatic, often holding the stones of his martyrdom or a palm branch. He is the patron saint of deacons, stonemasons, and coffin makers, and the name saint of kings of Hungary, Serbia, and elsewhere.

Historicity and interpretation

Scholars debate how much of the Acts account is historical: the speech of Acts 7 is widely read as a Lukan composition summarising early Christian preaching, while a historical core — an early follower of Jesus executed in Jerusalem — is commonly accepted. The figure of Stephen embodies the Hellenist wing of the Jerusalem church and marks the narrative point at which the Christian message begins to spread beyond Jerusalem, partly through the dispersion that followed his death.
