The Battle of Zenta was fought on 11 September 1697 near Zenta (modern Senta, Serbia), on the river Tisza, between the forces of the Habsburg Monarchy under Prince Eugene of Savoy and the Ottoman Empire under Sultan Mustafa II. It was one of the most decisive defeats in Ottoman history and the culminating battle of the Great Turkish War.

Background

After the failed Ottoman siege of Vienna in 1683, the Holy League had reconquered most of Hungary. In 1697 the young Prince Eugene, newly appointed to command in Hungary, learned from a captured Ottoman officer that the Sultan's army was crossing the Tisza at Zenta on a pontoon bridge to campaign toward Transylvania. Eugene force-marched his army south and arrived on the afternoon of 11 September to find the Ottoman army half across the river, its cavalry already over and its infantry and artillery entrenched on the far (western) bank behind earthworks.

The battle

Against the expectations of his own council, Eugene attacked at once, before sunset. His dragoons dismounted to assault the entrenchments while Imperial infantry and artillery poured fire into the crowded bridgehead; Imperial cavalry swung wide to seal the crossing. The Ottoman position collapsed into a massacre: the pontoon bridge broke under the crush of fugitives, and thousands drowned in the Tisza. The Grand Vizier Elmas Mehmed Pasha was killed — reportedly by his own mutinous Janissaries — and the Sultan, watching from the far bank, abandoned his camp, treasury and harem and fled to Temesvár. Imperial losses were only about 2,000 men; the Ottomans lost perhaps 20,000–30,000 killed, along with their entire artillery train, the Sultan's seal, and an immense booty.

Consequences

Zenta destroyed the last Ottoman field army in Hungary and compelled the Porte to negotiate. The resulting Treaty of Karlowitz (1699) transferred Hungary, Transylvania, Croatia and Slavonia to the Habsburgs and confirmed the Ottoman retreat from Central Europe — a turning point in European history. The battle also made Eugene's reputation as the outstanding commander of his generation.
