Peter Albert David Singer (born 1946) is an Australian moral philosopher, emeritus professor at Princeton University, and one of the most influential — and contested — living philosophers. A preference utilitarian, he is best known for founding the modern animal liberation movement with Animal Liberation (1975), for his arguments on global poverty that helped inspire effective altruism, and for controversial positions in bioethics on abortion, infanticide and euthanasia.

Animal ethics

Animal Liberation argued that the capacity to suffer, not species membership, sets the boundary of moral concern: "speciesism" — discounting animals' interests because they are not human — parallels racism and sexism, and the equal consideration of interests condemns factory farming and most animal experimentation. The book, drawing on Jeremy Bentham's question "Can they suffer?", is credited with launching the modern animal rights and welfare movement; Singer himself prefers the language of liberation and interests to that of rights.

Poverty, altruism and bioethics

His 1972 essay "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" set out the drowning-child argument: if we can prevent something very bad without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to; applied to affluent life, it demands serious giving to relieve global poverty — the seed of The Life You Can Save (2009) and of the effective altruism movement. In bioethics, Singer's Practical Ethics (1979) defends abortion and, in strictly limited circumstances, the permissibility of ending the lives of severely disabled newborns and of voluntary euthanasia — positions that have made him a lightning rod: appointments in Germany and elsewhere were protested and cancelled, and his Princeton appointment drew demonstrations by disability advocates, whose criticisms (notably Harriet McBryde Johnson's) he engaged in print and in person.

Reception

Singer's plain-prose, argument-first style made him philosophy's most public practitioner: The New Yorker called him the most influential living philosopher (1999), and Time listed him among its 100 most influential people (2005). He has pledged and given a large share of his income to effective charities, co-founded The Life You Can Save organisation, and extended his arguments to climate, global governance (One World, 2002) and the far future; critics continue to dispute both his utilitarian premises and particular conclusions, while much of his animal and poverty argument has entered mainstream practice.
