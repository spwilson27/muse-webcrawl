Operational amplifier

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An operational amplifier (often op amp or opamp) is a DC-coupled electronic amplifier with a differential input, a (usually) single-ended output, and an extremely high gain. By using negative feedback, its overall characteristics — gain, bandwidth, input and output impedance — can be set almost entirely by external components, making the op amp one of the most versatile and widely used building blocks in analogue electronics.

Principles

The op amp has two inputs: the inverting input (−) and the non-inverting input (+). Ideally it amplifies only the difference between them with infinite gain, infinite bandwidth, infinite input impedance and zero output impedance; real devices approximate these ideals. Connected with a feedback network, op amps can add, subtract, integrate and differentiate signals, perform logarithmic and exponential operations, and serve as oscillators, filters, comparators, voltage regulators and analogue-to-digital front ends. In the classic inverting amplifier configuration the closed-loop gain is set simply by the ratio of two resistors, −Rf/Rin.

History

The operational amplifier originated in analogue computers of the 1940s, where it performed mathematical operations — hence the name. Early designs used vacuum tubes, such as the Loebe, Summing and GAP/R K2-W amplifiers. Transistorised modules followed in the 1960s, and the first widely used monolithic integrated-circuit op amp, the μA709, was designed by Bob Widlar at Fairchild in 1965. Its successor, the μA741 (1968), with internal frequency compensation, became an industry standard for decades; modern op amps offer vastly better precision, speed, rail-to-rail operation and low-power performance.

Applications

Op amps are found in nearly every branch of electronics: audio and instrumentation amplifiers, active filters, sensor signal conditioning, power supplies, control systems and communications circuits. Although digital processing has displaced analogue computation, the op amp remains indispensable wherever physical signals must be amplified, filtered or buffered before conversion to the digital domain.
