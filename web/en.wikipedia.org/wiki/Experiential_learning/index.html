Experiential learning

Experiential learning is the process of learning through experience — "learning by doing" — and, more specifically, by reflecting on that experience. The theory is most closely associated with David Kolb, whose Experiential Learning (1984) built on the work of John Dewey, Kurt Lewin and Jean Piaget to argue that knowledge is created through the transformation of experience.

Kolb's cycle

Kolb's model describes learning as a four-stage cycle:

  • Concrete experience — encountering a new experience or situation;
  • Reflective observation — reviewing and reflecting on the experience;
  • Abstract conceptualisation — forming or modifying concepts and generalisations;
  • Active experimentation — testing the concepts in new situations, generating further experience.

Learning can begin at any stage but is most effective when all four are traversed. Kolb also derived a theory of learning styles (diverging, assimilating, converging, accommodating) from the two dimensions of the cycle — grasping experience (concrete vs. abstract) and transforming it (reflection vs. action).

Applications and criticism

Experiential methods underpin internships, service learning, simulations, outdoor and adventure education, laboratory work, and much management training. Dewey warned, however, that not all experience is educative: experiences must be connected and reflected upon. Empirical support for matching teaching to Kolb learning styles is weak, and critics regard the cycle as a useful heuristic rather than a validated model of cognition; nonetheless the framework remains influential in professional education.
