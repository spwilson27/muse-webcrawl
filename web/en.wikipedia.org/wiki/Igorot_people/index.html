Igorot people

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Igorot people are an ethnolinguistic group inhabiting the mountains of Luzon in the Philippines. "Igorot" is an umbrella term for the Indigenous peoples of the Cordillera Administrative Region, including the Bontoc, Ibaloi, Ifugao, Isneg (Apayao), Kalinga and Kankanaey peoples. The name derives from the Austronesian root golot, "mountain", with the prefix i-, "people of".

Culture and livelihood

The Igorot are renowned for wet-rice agriculture on sculpted mountain terraces, most famously the Banaue Rice Terraces of Ifugao, built and maintained over centuries. Traditional society was organised in independent villages; ritual life centred on ancestor veneration, agricultural rites and, historically, feuds between settlements. Distinct crafts include wood carving (such as the Ifugao bulul rice deities), weaving, basketry and metalwork.

History

The mountain peoples largely resisted Spanish colonisation, unlike the Christianised lowland groups; punitive expeditions into the Cordillera met sustained resistance, partly motivated by Spanish attempts to monopolise the region's gold. Under American rule the Igorots were famously exhibited at the 1904 St. Louis World's Fair, a display now regarded as a dehumanising colonial spectacle. Igorot soldiers served with distinction in both world wars, and the Cordillera peoples have since campaigned for regional autonomy and protection of ancestral lands, notably in the successful opposition to the Chico River Dam Project in the 1970s, during which Kalinga leader Macli-ing Dulag was assassinated.
