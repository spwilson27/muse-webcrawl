Dogma

Dogma, in its broadest sense, is any belief held unquestioningly and with undefended certainty. It may be in the form of an official system of principles or doctrines of a religion, such as Roman Catholicism, Judaism, or Protestantism, as well as the positions of a philosopher or of a philosophical school, such as Stoicism.

It may also be found in political belief systems, such as Marxism, and is often used, in a pejorative sense, to describe beliefs accepted without question. In the pejorative sense, dogma refers to enforced decisions, such as those of aggressive political interests or authorities.

Etymology

The word dogma was adopted in the 17th century from Latin dogma, derived from the Greek δόγμα (dogma), meaning "that which one thinks is true" and the verb δοκέω (dokeo), "I think, I suppose, I imagine". In ancient Greek usage it referred to a decree or a philosophical tenet.

Religious dogma

In Roman Catholic theology, a dogma is a truth revealed by God and proposed by the Church for belief, such as the dogmas of the Immaculate Conception (1854) and the Assumption of Mary (1950), defined ex cathedra by the pope. Other Christian traditions use the term more loosely for core doctrines such as the Trinity and the Incarnation. Comparable concepts exist in other religions, including formalised creeds and articles of faith.
