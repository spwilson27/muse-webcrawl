The Immigration Act of 1924, or Johnson–Reed Act, was a United States federal law that set strict quotas on immigration according to national origin and effectively barred immigration from Asia. It capped annual immigration at about 164,000 and limited visas to 2 per cent of each nationality's population in the United States as recorded in the 1890 census — a formula deliberately chosen to favour northern and western Europeans over southern and eastern Europeans.

Provisions

The Act excluded all "aliens ineligible to citizenship", a category aimed at Japanese immigrants and completing the exclusion of Asians begun by the Chinese Exclusion Act and the "Asiatic Barred Zone" of 1917; this provoked protest in Japan, where 1 July 1924 was marked as a day of humiliation. It also created the consular visa system and funded the Border Patrol. Western Hemisphere immigration was exempted from quotas, facilitating Mexican labour migration.

Context and legacy

The law reflected nativism, eugenic thinking and post-war isolationism. The national-origins system survived, with modifications, until the Hart–Celler Act of 1965 abolished it. Historians note that the quotas also trapped refugees fleeing Nazi persecution in the 1930s.
