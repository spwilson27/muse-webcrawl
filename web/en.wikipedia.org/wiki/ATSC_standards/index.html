ATSC standards

Overview of the ATSC digital television system

ATSC standards are a set of standards developed by the Advanced Television Systems Committee (ATSC) for digital television transmission over terrestrial, cable and satellite networks. They are used principally in North America, succeeding the analog NTSC system, and were first adopted in the United States in 1996. The standards are defined in a suite of documents designated A/49 through A/385 and beyond, covering everything from the physical transmission layer to application-level interactivity.

Background

The ATSC was formed in 1982 by organizations including the Electronic Industries Alliance, the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers, the National Association of Broadcasters, the National Cable and Telecommunications Association, and the Society of Motion Picture and Television Engineers. In the early 1990s the committee evaluated competing digital high-definition television systems, and in 1993 the "Grand Alliance" of rival proponents merged their approaches into a single system, which became the basis of the ATSC standard. The United States Federal Communications Commission adopted the standard on December 24, 1996, and the first broadcasts began in 1998.

Technical overview

The original ATSC standard, now retroactively called ATSC 1.0, uses 8VSB (eight-level vestigial sideband) modulation for terrestrial broadcasting, carrying a payload of about 19.39 Mbit/s in a 6 MHz channel. Video is encoded with MPEG-2 (with H.264/MPEG-4 AVC permitted in later amendments), and audio uses the Dolby Digital AC-3 codec. The system supports a family of display formats including 1080i, 720p, and standard-definition 480i and 480p.

ATSC 2.0 and 3.0

ATSC 2.0 was a planned incremental revision adding interactive services and internet-protocol enhancements, but it was largely superseded. ATSC 3.0, marketed in the United States as "NextGen TV", is a major overhaul ratified beginning in 2016–2018. It abandons 8VSB in favour of COFDM, uses HEVC video coding, supports 4K resolution, high dynamic range, high frame rates, immersive audio, and IP-based delivery that allows integration with broadband services. South Korea adopted ATSC 3.0 for its terrestrial 4K broadcasts beginning in 2017, and Jamaica and Brazil have also moved toward the system. In the United States the transition is voluntary, with broadcasters simulcasting ATSC 1.0 "lighthouse" stations during the changeover.

Mobile and cable variants

The A/153 standard, ATSC-M/H, extended the original system for reception by mobile and handheld devices, while cable systems in North America generally use the related SCTE standards based on QAM modulation, often informally grouped with ATSC deployments.
