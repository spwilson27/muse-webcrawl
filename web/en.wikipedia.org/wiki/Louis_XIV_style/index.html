Louis XIV style

The Louis XIV style, sometimes called French classicism, is the decorative and architectural style that flourished in France during the reign of King Louis XIV (1643–1715), reaching its height between about 1660 and 1700. Created to glorify the monarchy, it combined classical order, symmetry and grandeur with the rich ornament of the Baroque, and found its supreme expression in the Palace of Versailles. The style was directed for the king by the painter Charles Le Brun, first director of the Gobelins manufactory, and influenced taste across Europe.

Characteristics

The style emphasises symmetry, axial planning, classical orders and allegorical programmes celebrating the king as Apollo, the sun god. Interiors used rich materials — marble, gilded bronze, mirrors, lacquer and Gobelins tapestries — arranged in enfilades of state rooms. Ornament drew on Greek and Roman mythology, military trophies and solar emblems.

Architecture and gardens

At Versailles, Jules Hardouin-Mansart and Le Brun created the Hall of Mirrors (1678–1684), lined with 357 mirrors facing the garden windows. André Le Nôtre's gardens extended the palace's axis to the horizon with parterres, bosquets, canals and fountains — the classic French formal garden, or jardin à la française. In Paris the style produced the Louvre Colonnade, the Invalides with its great dome, and the places royales.

Furniture

Louis XIV furniture is massive, symmetrical and sumptuous, built in oak and veneered with ebony, tortoiseshell and exotic woods. André-Charles Boulle perfected the marquetry of brass and tortoiseshell that bears his name ("Boulle work"). New forms included the commode, the bureau plat and the console table; seat furniture evolved from the high-backed fauteuil toward more comfortable forms toward the end of the reign. Silver furniture, famously made for Versailles, was later melted down to pay for the king's wars.

Influence

Through Versailles the Louis XIV style became the model for courts from Stockholm to St Petersburg. Its later phase (c. 1690–1715), with lighter, more curvilinear ornament pioneered by designers such as Jean Bérain, prepared the transition to the Régence and Rococo of the Louis XV style.
