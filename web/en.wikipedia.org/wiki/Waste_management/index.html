Waste management

FocusCollection, transport, treatment and disposal of waste materials
Key conceptWaste hierarchy: reduce, reuse, recycle, recover, dispose
Major streamsMunicipal solid waste, industrial waste, hazardous waste, electronic waste, biomedical waste

Waste management (or waste disposal) comprises the processes and actions required to manage waste from its inception to its final disposal. This includes the collection, transport, treatment and disposal of waste, together with monitoring and regulation of the waste management process and waste-related laws, technologies and economic mechanisms.

Waste hierarchy

The waste hierarchy is a framework that ranks waste management options according to their environmental impact. The most preferred option is waste prevention (reduction at source), followed by reuse, recycling, other recovery (including energy recovery through incineration), and finally disposal in landfill as the least preferred option. The hierarchy underpins waste policy in the European Union and many other jurisdictions.

Methods

Landfill

Landfilling is the oldest and still most common form of waste disposal worldwide. Modern sanitary landfills are engineered sites with liners, leachate collection and landfill-gas management; older practices such as open dumping remain widespread in many developing countries and are a major source of pollution.

Incineration

Incineration burns waste at high temperatures, substantially reducing its volume and destroying pathogens and hazardous compounds. Waste-to-energy plants recover electricity and heat from combustion, though the process produces ash and flue gases that require treatment.

Recycling and composting

Recycling converts waste materials such as glass, paper, metal and plastics into new products, conserving raw materials and energy. Biological treatment — composting and anaerobic digestion — handles organic wastes, producing soil improvers, biogas or both.

By waste type

Specialised regimes govern hazardous waste (chemical, toxic or radioactive materials), electronic waste (discarded devices containing valuable and toxic components), biomedical waste from healthcare, construction and demolition debris, and wastewater and sewage sludge. International movements of hazardous waste are controlled by the Basel Convention of 1989.

History

Organised waste collection dates to the nineteenth century, when public-health reformers in industrial cities linked refuse accumulation to disease. London's Metropolitan Board of Works, the introduction of the covered refuse bin, and New York's Department of Street Cleaning under George E. Waring in the 1890s were landmark developments. The later twentieth century added sanitary landfill standards, large-scale recycling programmes, and extended producer responsibility schemes.
