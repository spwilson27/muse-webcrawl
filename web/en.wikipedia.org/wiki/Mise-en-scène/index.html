Mise-en-scène

Mise-en-scène
FieldFilm studies, theatre
OriginFrench theatre, 19th century
Key elementsSetting, lighting, costume and makeup, staging and performance

Mise-en-scène (French: [miz ɑ̃ sɛn]; "placing on stage") is an expression used to describe the design aspects of a theatre or film production, which essentially means "visual theme" or "telling a story" — both in visually artful ways through storyboarding, cinematography and stage design, and in poetically artful ways through direction. It is also commonly used to refer to multiple single scenes within a film. The term was first popularised by film critics of the French journal Cahiers du Cinéma in the 1950s.

Mise-en-scène has been called film criticism's "grand undefined term". It encompasses everything that appears before the camera — sets, props, costumes, actors, and lighting — and their arrangement. Along with cinematography and editing, mise-en-scène is one of the three principal means by which a film expresses meaning to its viewers.

Definition

When applied to the cinema, mise-en-scène refers to everything that appears before the camera and its arrangement: composition, sets, props, actors, costumes, and lighting. It differs from cinematography (how the scene is filmed) and editing (how shots are joined). The term originates in French theatre, where it described the staging of a play: the arrangement of scenery and stage properties, and the positioning and movement of actors.

Key aspects

Set design

Set design (or production design) is the creation of the physical environment in which the action takes place. Sets may be built in a studio, adapted from existing locations, or created digitally. The design of a set contributes to mood, indicates period and place, and can symbolise characters' inner states.

Lighting

Lighting shapes how objects and actors appear on screen. Classical Hollywood used a three-point lighting system (key, fill, and back light). Low-key lighting, with strong contrasts and shadows, is characteristic of film noir, while high-key lighting produces a bright, even illumination typical of comedies and musicals.

Costume and makeup

Costume and makeup communicate a character's social position, personality, and historical period, and can signal transformations over the course of a story.

Staging and performance

Staging concerns the movement and placement of actors within the frame (blocking), while performance encompasses acting style — from the naturalism of Method acting to the stylised gestures of expressionist cinema.

History in film criticism

French critics writing for Cahiers du Cinéma in the 1950s, including André Bazin and the young critics who became the French New Wave directors, championed mise-en-scène as the essence of cinema and the primary evidence of a director's personal style (auteur theory). Bazin praised long takes and deep-focus cinematography, as in the films of Jean Renoir, Orson Welles and William Wyler, because they preserved the spatial unity of a scene and respected the ambiguity of reality. In the English-speaking world, the concept was taken up by journals such as Movie in Britain and by critics including V. F. Perkins.
