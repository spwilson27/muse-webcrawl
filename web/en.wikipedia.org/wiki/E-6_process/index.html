The E-6 process (often simply called E-6) is a chromogenic photographic process for developing Ektachrome, Fujichrome and other color reversal (also called slide or transparency) photographic film. It was introduced by Kodak in 1976, replacing the earlier E-4 process.

Unlike some color reversal processes, such as K-14 for Kodachrome, the E-6 process can be performed by individual users in a home darkroom or by professional laboratories with relatively modest equipment, which made it one of the most widely used slide-film processes in the world.

Process steps

The E-6 process consists of the following principal steps, carried out at a tightly controlled temperature of approximately 38 °C (100.4 °F):

  • First developer — a black-and-white developer that develops a negative silver image in all emulsion layers.
  • Wash
  • Reversal bath — prepares the remaining (undeveloped) silver halide for the color developer, traditionally by chemical fogging (older processes used a re-exposure to light).
  • Color developer — develops the remaining silver halide, forming a positive silver image; the oxidized developer reacts with dye couplers in each layer to form cyan, magenta and yellow dyes.
  • Pre-bleach (conditioner)
  • Bleach — converts metallic silver back to silver halide.
  • Fixer — removes the silver halide, leaving only the dye image.
  • Final wash
  • Final rinse / stabilizer — contains a wetting agent and hardener to protect the dyes.
  • Drying

Variations

The process is normally run in six main chemical steps plus washes, hence the name "E-6". A shortened three-bath variant, combining some steps, is offered for small-scale and home use. Because slide film has very narrow exposure latitude, temperature and timing control are critical; deviations produce color shifts and contrast changes, an effect sometimes exploited deliberately in cross processing, where E-6 film is developed in C-41 color negative chemistry or vice versa.
