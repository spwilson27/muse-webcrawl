First-wave feminism

First-wave feminism was a period of feminist activity in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, focused primarily on overturning legal inequalities and, above all, on gaining women's right to vote — women's suffrage. The term first wave was coined retrospectively, after the term second-wave feminism came into use in the late 1960s, and it groups movements in countries such as the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, the Netherlands, and New Zealand. Its landmark texts include Mary Wollstonecraft's A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792) and its landmark events the Seneca Falls Convention of 1848 and the suffrage victories of 1893 (New Zealand) through 1920 (United States) and 1928 (United Kingdom).

Origins

Enlightenment arguments for women's rational equality — Wollstonecraft's Vindication (1792), Olympe de Gouges's Declaration of the Rights of Woman (1791) — supplied the intellectual foundation, and abolitionism supplied much of the early organisation. In the United States, the Seneca Falls Convention of 1848, organised by Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott, issued a Declaration of Sentiments demanding, controversially even among reformers, the vote for women. Campaigns also targeted married women's property rights (secured in Britain by the Married Women's Property Acts of 1870–1882 and in U.S. states from 1839), access to higher education and the professions, and divorce and custody law.

The suffrage campaigns

In Britain, John Stuart Mill presented the first women's suffrage petition to Parliament in 1866 and published The Subjection of Women (1869). Constitutional campaigning by Millicent Fawcett's National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies was joined after 1903 by Emmeline Pankhurst's Women's Social and Political Union, whose "suffragettes" used civil disobedience, hunger strikes (met by forcible feeding), and arson; Emily Davison died under the king's horse at the 1913 Derby. In the United States, Susan B. Anthony and Stanton led the National Woman Suffrage Association, while Alice Paul later imported militant tactics. Victory came piecemeal: New Zealand (1893), Australia (1902), Finland (1906), Norway (1913), and, in the aftermath of the First World War, the United States (Nineteenth Amendment, 1920) and Britain (equal franchise, 1928).

Criticism of the "wave" model

Historians have criticised the first-wave label for centring white, middle-class, Anglophone movements and understating the activism of Black women (such as Sojourner Truth and Ida B. Wells), working-class women, and feminists outside the West, as well as the continuity of organising between the supposed waves.
