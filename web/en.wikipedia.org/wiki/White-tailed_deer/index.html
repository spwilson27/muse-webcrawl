White-tailed deer

The white-tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus), also known as the whitetail or Virginia deer, is a medium-sized deer native to North, Central and South America. It is the most widely distributed large wild mammal in the Americas and one of the most familiar game animals in the United States.

Description

Adults stand about 0.8–1.1 m (2.6–3.6 ft) at the shoulder and weigh 40–130 kg (90–290 lb), with males larger than females. The coat is reddish-brown in summer and greyer in winter. The species is named for the white underside of its tail, which it raises like a flag when alarmed. Males grow branching antlers each spring and shed them in winter; females normally lack antlers.

Behaviour and ecology

White-tailed deer are browsers and grazers, eating acorns, grasses, forbs, twigs and crops. They are strong swimmers and can run at up to 48 km/h (30 mph). Females give birth to one to three spotted fawns in late spring. Predators include wolves, coyotes, bears and mountain lions, but in much of the eastern United States the loss of large predators has allowed deer populations to grow to levels that damage forests and crops.

Relationship with humans

The white-tailed deer is the most popular big-game animal in North America, hunted by millions of sportsmen, and deer hunting is tightly regulated by state and provincial agencies. Vehicle collisions with deer are a significant cause of property damage and injury, and deer are hosts for the ticks that spread Lyme disease.
