Postcodes in the United Kingdom

The 124 postcode areas of the United Kingdom.

Postcodes in the United Kingdom, known as post codes, are alphanumeric codes of between five and seven characters used by Royal Mail to sort and route mail. The system was trialled in Norwich in 1959 and rolled out nationwide between 1966 and 1974. There are approximately 1.8 million postcodes covering some 29 million delivery addresses, and postcodes are widely used for purposes far beyond mail, including navigation, insurance, statistics and marketing.

Structure

A postcode consists of an outward code and an inward code separated by a space. The outward code identifies the postcode area (one or two letters, such as SW for south-west London or EH for Edinburgh) and the district (one or two digits, sometimes with a letter). The inward code begins with a digit identifying the sector and ends with two letters identifying the unit, which typically covers a small group of addresses — often around fifteen properties, a single street segment or a single large organisation.

History

London had been divided into postal districts since 1857 (giving rise to codes such as EC and WC), and other large cities adopted numbered districts in the early twentieth century. The modern alphanumeric system, designed to be read by automated sorting machinery, was devised under the direction of the Post Office in the 1960s; Norwich was the experimental pilot, and its original codes were later converted to the national format. Postcodes became a standard part of UK addresses, and the underlying address data is maintained in Royal Mail's Postcode Address File (PAF).

Non-geographic and special codes

Some postcodes are non-geographic, assigned to large users and PO boxes. Special codes include GIR 0AA for Girobank and SAN TA1 for letters to Father Christmas. Crown dependencies and British Overseas Territories, such as Gibraltar (GX11 1AA), use UK-style codes.
