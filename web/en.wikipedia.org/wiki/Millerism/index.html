Millerism was a nineteenth-century American religious movement based on the teachings of Baptist preacher William Miller, who concluded from his study of the Book of Daniel that Jesus Christ would return to earth — and the world would be cleansed by fire — around the year 1843, later refined to 22 October 1844. The movement attracted tens of thousands of followers across the United States during the Second Great Awakening.

Origins

Miller, a farmer and War of 1812 veteran from Low Hampton, New York, began in 1818 a close study of the Bible using a concordance. Interpreting the "two thousand and three hundred days" of Daniel 8:14 as years beginning in 457 BC, he concluded that the "cleansing of the sanctuary" — which he identified with Christ's Second Coming — would occur about 1843. He began preaching publicly in 1831; the movement grew rapidly after 1840 under the promotional energy of publisher Joshua Vaughan Himes, with camp meetings, newspapers such as the Signs of the Times, and striking prophetic charts.

The Great Disappointment

When Christ did not return in the spring of 1844, many followers accepted Samuel S. Snow's recalculation fixing the date to 22 October 1844, the tenth day of the seventh month of the Jewish Karaite calendar. Believers prepared for the end; some gave away property and possessions. The failure of the expectation on that date became known as the Great Disappointment. Most Millerites drifted back to their former churches or abandoned the faith, but a minority reinterpreted the prophecy.

Legacy

From the fragments of Millerism emerged the Adventist family of churches. One group concluded that in 1844 Christ had entered the Most Holy Place of the heavenly sanctuary and begun an "investigative judgment" — a doctrine central to the Seventh-day Adventist Church, organised in 1863 and today numbering more than twenty million members worldwide. Other offshoots include the Advent Christian Church and, indirectly, the Bible Student movement from which Jehovah's Witnesses later emerged. Miller himself never set the October date and died in 1849, still expecting Christ's soon return.
