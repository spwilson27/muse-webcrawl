Vertical farming

Vertical farming is the practice of growing crops in vertically stacked layers, usually in controlled indoor environments. It commonly employs controlled-environment agriculture (CEA), using soilless techniques such as hydroponics, aeroponics or aquaponics, with artificial lighting and climate control to optimise plant growth year-round.

History

Precedents include the hanging gardens of antiquity and early 20th-century proposals for "vertical" greenhouses. The modern concept was popularised by Columbia University ecologist Dickson Despommier, who developed the idea with his students from 1999 and published The Vertical Farm in 2010. Commercial ventures expanded rapidly in the 2010s, particularly in the United States, Japan, Singapore and the Netherlands, though the sector has also seen prominent bankruptcies driven by energy costs.

Techniques

Plants are grown on stacked shelves or towers under LED lighting tuned to photosynthetic spectra. Nutrient solution circulates through hydroponic trays or is misted onto roots in aeroponic systems; aquaponic systems combine fish raising with plant growing. Sensors and automation regulate temperature, humidity, CO2 and nutrient concentration, and multiple harvests per year are possible because seasons are irrelevant indoors.

Advantages and disadvantages

Claimed advantages include yields per unit land area many times those of field farming, water use reductions of up to 95 per cent, elimination of most pesticides, independence from weather, and production close to urban consumers, reducing transport. The main disadvantages are high capital costs and high electricity demand for lighting and cooling, which make vertical farming economical mainly for high-value, fast-growing, perishable crops rather than grains or root vegetables.
