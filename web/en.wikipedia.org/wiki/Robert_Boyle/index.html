Robert Boyle (1627–1691) was an Anglo-Irish natural philosopher regarded as one of the founders of modern chemistry. His book The Sceptical Chymist (1661) attacked the Aristotelian four elements and alchemical principles, arguing that matter consists of corpuscles and that elements are simple, unmingled bodies — a recognisably modern programme.

With his assistant Robert Hooke, Boyle built an improved air pump and discovered the inverse relation of pressure and volume for gases — Boyle's law (1662). He also pioneered acid–base indicators (violet syrup), flame tests and careful published experimental reporting, and was a founding figure of the Royal Society circle.

[Image: Boyle with his air pump apparatus in a 17th-century laboratory]

The mechanical philosophy

Boyle sought to explain chemical phenomena by the size, shape and motion of particles — the corpuscular or mechanical philosophy — against scholastic 'forms and qualities'. His wealth (as Earl of Cork's son) funded a private laboratory in Oxford and London.

Legacy

Boyle's insistence on public, replicable experiments and his definition-seeking about 'element' set chemistry's course toward Lavoisier; the Boyle Lectures he endowed still run.
