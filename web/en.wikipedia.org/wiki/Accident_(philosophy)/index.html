Accident (philosophy)

In metaphysics and philosophy, an accident is a property of a thing that is not part of its essence and can change without the thing ceasing to be what it is. The concept, systematized by Aristotle and central to scholastic philosophy, contrasts with substance and essence: a substance (say, a person) persists through changes in its accidents, such as height, hair color, location, or state of knowledge.

Aristotle and the categories

In Aristotle's Categories and Metaphysics, a substance is the primary kind of being, and the other categories — quantity, quality, relation, place, time, posture, having, action, and passion — are its accidents, existing in the substance rather than independently. "Accidental" predication also gives its name to the fallacy of accident, in which a general rule is applied to a case it does not cover.

Scholastic development

Medieval philosophers, especially Thomas Aquinas, refined the distinction: some accidents are proper (propria), necessarily flowing from a thing's essence without being part of its definition — as the capacity for laughter was said to be proper to humans — while others are separable accidents that may come and go. The doctrine became theologically significant in the Eucharist: in transubstantiation, the substance of bread and wine was held to change while their accidents (appearance, taste) remained.

Modern usage

Early modern philosophers such as Descartes and Locke reworked the distinction in terms of primary and secondary qualities and the contrast between substance and its modes. In contemporary analytic metaphysics the term survives mainly in discussions of essentialism — the question of which of a thing's properties it has essentially and which only accidentally.
