Leukodystrophy

Leukodystrophies are a group of usually inherited disorders characterized by degeneration of the white matter of the brain and spinal cord. The word derives from the Greek leuko (white), dys (abnormal), and trophe (growth). Each leukodystrophy is caused by a defect in a gene involved in the production or maintenance of myelin, the fatty sheath that insulates nerve fibres, or in the cells that make and maintain it. Damage to myelin disrupts nerve signal transmission and leads to progressive loss of motor and cognitive function.

Types

Dozens of distinct leukodystrophies have been identified. Well-known examples include:

  • Adrenoleukodystrophy (ALD), an X-linked disorder of very-long-chain fatty acid metabolism
  • Metachromatic leukodystrophy, caused by deficiency of the enzyme arylsulfatase A
  • Krabbe disease, caused by deficiency of galactocerebrosidase
  • Canavan disease, caused by deficiency of aspartoacylase
  • Pelizaeus–Merzbacher disease, an X-linked disorder of proteolipid protein
  • Alexander disease, associated with mutations in the GFAP gene
  • Leukoencephalopathy with vanishing white matter

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically begin in infancy or childhood, though adult-onset forms exist. They may include developmental regression, spasticity, weakness, loss of coordination (ataxia), seizures, vision and hearing loss, and cognitive decline. Course and severity vary widely by type.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is based on characteristic white-matter patterns on MRI, confirmed by biochemical assays and genetic testing. Most leukodystrophies have no cure, and care is supportive. For a few types, early haematopoietic stem cell transplantation can slow progression if performed before symptoms advance; gene therapy has been approved for early forms of metachromatic leukodystrophy, and the film Lorenzo's Oil popularized adrenoleukodystrophy and one family's search for a treatment.
