Cleomenes I (Greek: Κλεομένης; died c. 490 BC) was an Agiad king of Sparta who reigned from about 519 BC until his death around 490 BC. One of the most forceful and controversial Spartan kings, he intervened repeatedly in the politics of Athens, expelling the tyrant Hippias, backing Isagoras against Cleisthenes, and later campaigning against Argos. Herodotus portrays him as brilliant but unstable, a judgment echoed in the manner of his death.

Interventions in Athens

In 510 BC Cleomenes led the Spartan expedition that expelled the Peisistratid tyrant Hippias from Athens. In 508 he returned to support the oligarch Isagoras against the democratic reforms of Cleisthenes, occupying the Acropolis before an Athenian uprising besieged and expelled him. In 506 his grand invasion of Attica collapsed when the Corinthians withdrew and his Eurypontid co-king Demaratus abandoned the expedition, prompting a law that only one king could lead an army on campaign.

Persian threat and Argos

Cleomenes rejected the Ionian revolt's appeal for help against Persia and, according to Herodotus, was warned by Aristagoras's bronze map of the vastness of the Persian Empire. He expelled the Persian-leaning tyrant of Miletus's envoys and later drove Aeginetan hostages into Athenian custody over alleged collaboration with Persia. Around 494 BC he crushed Argos at the Battle of Sepeia, then notoriously burned a sacred grove containing Argive survivors, an act of impiety said to have haunted him.

Downfall and death

Cleomenes engineered the deposition of Demaratus by bribing the Delphic oracle to declare him illegitimate; when the bribery was exposed, he fled Sparta, raised an Arcadian revolt, and was forcibly restored but declared mad and imprisoned. He died in custody around 490 BC, officially by suicide — Herodotus records that he mutilated himself with a knife — though Greek opinion variously blamed divine punishment for his sacrileges or, in the Spartan account, the effects of drinking unmixed wine learned from Scythians. He was succeeded by his half-brother Leonidas I.
