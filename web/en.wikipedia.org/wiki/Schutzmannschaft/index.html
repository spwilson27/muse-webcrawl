Schutzmannschaft

The Schutzmannschaft ("protective teams"), abbreviated Schuma, were auxiliary police forces of native collaborators in German-occupied Eastern Europe during World War II, organized under the Ordnungspolizei from July 1941 on Heinrich Himmler's orders. By late 1942 they numbered roughly 300,000 men in the occupied Soviet territories and the Baltics.

Organization

Schutzmannschaften were organized into individual posts (Einzeldienst), guard units, and closed battalions (Schutzmannschafts-Bataillone) used for rear-area security, guarding POW camps and ghettos, and anti-partisan warfare. Latvian, Lithuanian, Estonian, Ukrainian, Belarusian, and Tatar formations were raised, each with distinctive collar insignia but under German police command.

Role in the Holocaust

Schuma units played a central auxiliary role in the "Holocaust by bullets," cordoning villages, guarding execution sites, and in many cases carrying out mass shootings of Jews alongside Einsatzgruppen and police battalions, as well as participating in brutal anti-partisan reprisals such as Operation Cottbus.

Aftermath

As the war turned, many Schuma battalions were absorbed into the Waffen-SS national legions; after 1945 some members were prosecuted, while others emigrated and were investigated decades later in Western countries.
