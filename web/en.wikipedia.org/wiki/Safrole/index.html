Safrole is the principal component of sassafras root bark oil.

Safrole is an organic compound of the phenylpropanoid family, a colourless or slightly yellow oily liquid with a characteristic sweet, warm-spicy odour. It is the main constituent of sassafras oil and occurs in camphor laurel, ocotea and trace amounts in nutmeg, black pepper and basil.

Uses

Safrole was historically used to flavour root beer and sassafras tea, and in perfumery and soap manufacture. Industrially it is converted to heliotropin (piperonal, a fragrance), piperonyl butoxide (an insecticide synergist) and, via isosafrole, other fine chemicals.

Toxicity and regulation

In 1960 the US Food and Drug Administration banned safrole as a food additive after studies showed it to be a weak hepatocarcinogen in rodents; its metabolite 1′-hydroxysafrole is considered the proximate carcinogen. Sassafras products sold for food use must now be safrole-free.

Because safrole can be isomerized and oxidized to MDP2P, a precursor in the synthesis of MDMA (ecstasy), it is listed as a Table I precursor under the 1988 UN Convention and as a DEA List I chemical in the United States. Large-scale harvesting of safrole-bearing trees such as Ocotea and the Mreah Prew Phnom tree in Cambodia for illicit production has caused significant environmental damage.
