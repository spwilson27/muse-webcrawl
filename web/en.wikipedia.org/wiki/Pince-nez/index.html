Pince-nez

Pince-nez (French pronunciation: [pɛ̃sˈne], plural form same as singular; also pincenez in historical use) is a style of eyeglasses, popular in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, that are supported without earpieces, by pinching the bridge of the nose. The name comes from the French pincer, "to pinch", and nez, "nose".

Design and use

Pince-nez were usually connected to the wearer's clothing by a cord, chain, or ribbon to prevent loss if they slipped. Variants included hard-bridge and spring-bridge ("C-bridge") designs, and the rimless Oxford style with a flexible nose guard. Although older types of nose-supported glasses existed from the fifteenth century, the refined pince-nez became fashionable from the 1840s and was widely worn until after the First World War.

Notable wearers

Pince-nez are associated with figures such as U.S. President Theodore Roosevelt, the Russian writer Anton Chekhov (whose portrait wearing them lent its name to the Chekhov pince-nez style), and the fictional professor in many caricatures of the era. Morpheus in The Matrix films wears a futuristic version. The style declined as temple-arm spectacles became standard, but survives as a period and costume accessory.
