Normal mode

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A normal mode of an oscillating system is a pattern of motion in which all parts of the system move sinusoidally with the same frequency and with a fixed phase relation. The free motion described by the normal modes takes place at fixed frequencies known as the natural frequencies or resonant frequencies of the system.

Normal modes are a central concept in wave theory, structural dynamics, molecular vibration, and quantum mechanics. Any general motion of a linear system can be expressed as a superposition of its normal modes, making them the natural basis for analysing coupled oscillators.

Coupled oscillators

For a system of two identical pendulums joined by a weak spring, the normal modes are the in-phase motion (both pendulums swinging together, spring unstretched) and the out-of-phase motion (pendulums swinging oppositely, spring maximally worked). Each mode oscillates at a single frequency; arbitrary motions — including the famous energy-exchange "beats" pattern — are combinations of these two modes.

Mathematical description

For a linear system described by a mass matrix M and stiffness matrix K, the normal modes are the solutions of the generalized eigenvalue problem:

K x = ω² M x

The eigenvalues give the squares of the natural angular frequencies and the eigenvectors give the mode shapes. For continuous systems such as strings and membranes, the modes become standing waves indexed by integers, with harmonic frequency spectra.

Applications

  • Structural engineering — modal analysis predicts how bridges and buildings respond to earthquakes and wind.
  • Molecular spectroscopy — the normal modes of a molecule determine its infrared and Raman spectra.
  • Geophysics — the Earth's free oscillations after great earthquakes are normal modes of the whole planet.
  • Quantum mechanics — phonons are the quanta of normal modes of a crystal lattice.
