4 Vesta

Vesta as seen by the Dawn spacecraft in 2011.

4 Vesta is one of the largest objects in the asteroid belt, with a mean diameter of about 525 kilometers. It was discovered by German astronomer Heinrich Wilhelm Olbers on 29 March 1807 and is named after Vesta, the virgin goddess of hearth and home in Roman mythology. Vesta is the second-most-massive and second-largest body in the belt after the dwarf planet Ceres, and accounts for roughly 9% of the belt's total mass.

Geology

Vesta is a differentiated protoplanet: density and gravity measurements by NASA's Dawn spacecraft, which orbited Vesta from July 2011 to September 2012, confirm an iron-nickel core, a rocky mantle, and a basaltic crust — a surviving remnant of the early Solar System's rocky building blocks. Its surface is covered largely by basaltic lava flows, linking it to the HED clan of meteorites (howardites, eucrites, and diogenites) found on Earth. The southern hemisphere is dominated by the Rheasilvia impact basin, about 505 km wide and among the largest craters in the Solar System, with a central mound some 20–25 km high; an older basin, Veneneia, lies partly beneath it. Material blasted from these impacts formed the Vesta family of asteroids and supplies many HED meteorites.

Observation

With an orbital period of 3.63 years at about 2.36 AU, Vesta is the brightest asteroid as seen from Earth and can occasionally reach magnitude 5.5, faintly visible to the naked eye under dark skies. Its rotation period is about 5.34 hours.
