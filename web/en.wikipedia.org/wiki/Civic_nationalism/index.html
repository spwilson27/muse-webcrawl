Civic nationalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Civic nationalism, also known as liberal or territorial nationalism, is a form of nationalism that defines the nation by shared citizenship, political values and institutions rather than by ethnicity or descent. Whoever accepts the nation's laws and political creed — and is accepted in return — can belong. The French revolutionary tradition, Ernest Renan's definition of the nation as a “daily plebiscite” (1882), and the American ideal of a nation “dedicated to a proposition” are its classic statements.

Civic nationalism corresponds to Jus soli (birth on the territory) in citizenship law and is inclusive in principle: immigrants can become full nationals by naturalisation and adherence to common values. It underlies official national ideologies in countries such as France, the United States and Canada.

Critiques

Critics note that real civic nations are never purely civic: language requirements, cultural expectations and historical ethnic majorities shape who counts as belonging, and civic rhetoric can mask majoritarian norms (French laïcité disputes are often read this way). Conversely, some theorists argue civic nationalism is too thin to sustain solidarity and sacrifice. The civic/ethnic distinction remains a standard analytical tool despite these complications.
