The Principality of Vladimir-Suzdal (Russian: Владимиро-Суздальское княжество), originally known as the Rostov-Suzdal principality, was one of the major principalities that succeeded Kievan Rus' in the late 12th century, centred on Vladimir on the Klyazma. It is traditionally regarded as the cradle of the modern Russian state and nation.

History

The territory, in the north-east of Rus' between the Volga and Oka rivers, was initially governed from Rostov and then Suzdal. Yuri Dolgorukiy ("Long-Armed"), a son of Vladimir Monomakh, ruled the land in the early 12th century and founded numerous towns, including, by tradition, Moscow in 1147. His son Andrey Bogolyubsky moved the capital to Vladimir in 1157, building the famous white-stone churches and the Golden Gate, and made the principality the dominant power in Rus'.

Andrey's brother Vsevolod the Big Nest (reigned 1176–1212) brought the principality to its zenith. His numerous sons founded appanage principalities across the north-east. The region flourished culturally, producing masterpieces of white-stone architecture such as the Cathedral of St. Demetrius and the Church of the Intercession on the Nerl.

Mongol invasion and decline

In 1238 the armies of Batu Khan devastated Vladimir and Suzdal during the Mongol invasion of Rus'. The principality fragmented into smaller appanages — Suzdal, Gorodets, Kostroma, Moscow and others — all subject to the Golden Horde. The title of Grand Prince of Vladimir remained prestigious, contested by the princes of Tver and Moscow; by the 14th century it had effectively merged with the rising Grand Duchy of Moscow.

Legacy

The white monuments of Vladimir and Suzdal are inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List. Historians regard the north-east as the region where the Great Russian ethnos and the centralised Muscovite state took shape.
