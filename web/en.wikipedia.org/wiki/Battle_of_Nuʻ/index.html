Battle of Nuʻuanu

The Battle of Nuʻuanu, fought in May 1795 on the island of Oahu, was the decisive battle in Kamehameha I's conquest of the Hawaiian Islands. Called Kalelekaʻanae ("the leaping fish") in Hawaiian, the battle completed Kamehameha's control of all the major islands except Kauai and Niʻihau, which joined his kingdom by agreement in 1810.

Background

After victories at Mokuʻōhai (1782) and Kepaniwai, and the conquest of Maui and Molokai in 1795, Kamehameha landed on Oahu with an armada of some 1,200 war canoes and about 10,000 soldiers, equipped with muskets and cannon advised by the British seamen John Young and Isaac Davis. The defending Oahu forces under Kalanikūpule took position in the Nuʻuanu Valley above Honolulu.

The battle

Kamehameha's artillery and musketeers drove the defenders steadily up the valley toward the Nuʻuanu Pali, a sheer cliff. Cornered at the precipice, hundreds of Oahu warriors were forced or fell over the edge to their deaths — tradition says some 400 to 800 men. Kalanikūpule escaped to the mountains but was later captured and sacrificed. In the 19th century, construction work uncovered hundreds of skulls below the Pali, attributed to the battle.

Legacy

The battle is commemorated at the Nuʻuanu Pali State Wayside, a popular lookout on the windward side of Oahu. It stands as the climactic event in the unification of the Kingdom of Hawaii.
