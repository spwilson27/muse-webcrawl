Adhesion is the tendency of dissimilar particles or surfaces to cling to one another, as opposed to cohesion, which refers to the tendency of similar particles or surfaces to cling to one another. The forces that cause adhesion and cohesion can be divided into several types: chemical bonds, intermolecular (van der Waals) forces, electrostatic forces, and mechanical interlocking.

Mechanisms

Five principal mechanisms have been proposed to explain adhesion. Mechanical interlocking occurs when an adhesive flows into pores and irregularities of a surface and hardens. Chemical adhesion involves the formation of covalent, ionic, or hydrogen bonds across the interface. Dispersive adhesion arises from van der Waals forces between molecules of the two materials. Electrostatic adhesion results from charge separation at the interface, and diffusive adhesion occurs when polymer chains from the two surfaces interdiffuse, as in welding of plastics.

Surface energy and wetting

The work required to separate two surfaces is related to their surface energies through the Dupré equation. Good adhesion generally requires the adhesive to wet the substrate — that is, to spread over it with a low contact angle — which is favoured when the surface energy of the substrate exceeds that of the adhesive.

Biological adhesion

Adhesion is widespread in nature. Geckos adhere to smooth surfaces using millions of microscopic hairs (setae) that exploit van der Waals forces. Mussels secrete adhesive proteins rich in the amino acid DOPA that cure underwater. Cell adhesion molecules mediate the binding of cells to one another and to the extracellular matrix, a process essential to development and wound healing; in medicine, "adhesions" also denotes fibrous bands that can form between tissues after surgery.

Applications

Adhesion underlies adhesives and glues, paints and coatings, printing, composite materials, and the behaviour of water transported through plant xylem, where the adhesion of water to vessel walls works together with cohesion to draw water upward.
