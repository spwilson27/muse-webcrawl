Knut Hamsun (born Knut Pedersen; August 4, 1859 – February 19, 1952) was a Norwegian writer who was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1920. He is widely regarded as one of the most influential and innovative literary stylists of the early 20th century, a pioneer of psychological literature and stream of consciousness. Isaac Bashevis Singer called him "the father of the modern school of literature".

Life and work

Born into poverty in the Gudbrandsdalen valley and raised in Nordland, Hamsun worked at a succession of manual jobs — including two periods in the United States as a tram conductor and farm labourer — before his breakthrough with the fragment "Først og sist" and the novel Hunger (Sult, 1890), a semi-autobiographical account of a starving young writer in Kristiania, celebrated for its exploration of the irrational workings of the mind. Mysteries (1892), Pan (1894), and Victoria (1898) consolidated his fame. His later, more epic mode culminated in Growth of the Soil (Markens Grøde, 1917), the monumental story of the settler Isak, which won him the Nobel Prize.

Politics and legacy

Hamsun's admiration for Germany, hostility to Britain and the United States, and support for the Nazi occupation of Norway — including a notorious obituary praising Hitler after his death in 1945 — destroyed his reputation at home. After the war he was detained, fined heavily for collaboration, and wrote his final book, On Overgrown Paths (1949), in self-defence. His literary reputation has since been substantially rehabilitated internationally, though debate over his politics continues in Norway; the Hamsun Centre opened in Hamarøy in 2009 amid ongoing controversy.
