Dogū

Dogū (土偶, "earthen figure") are small humanoid and animal figurines made during the later part of the Jōmon period (14,000–400 BC) of prehistoric Japan. Dogū come exclusively from the Jōmon period; by the following Yayoi period, they were no longer made.

Characteristics

Many dogū have distinctive features: large eyes, sometimes rendered with a single slit — giving the so-called "goggle-eyed" or shakōki type — small waists and wide hips, and elaborate patterns incised or applied to the surface. Some are hollow and made with considerable technical skill. Large numbers are found broken, leading to theories that they were deliberately broken in rituals of sympathetic healing, the figurine taking on an ailment or misfortune and being destroyed in its place.

Significance

Their purpose remains debated: suggestions include fertility symbols, toys, funerary items and effigies used in healing rituals. The most famous examples, such as the Hollow Dogū from Hokkaidō, are designated National Treasures of Japan.
