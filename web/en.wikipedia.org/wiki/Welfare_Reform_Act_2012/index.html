Welfare Reform Act 2012

The Welfare Reform Act 2012 is an Act of Parliament of the United Kingdom that introduced the most far-reaching changes to the British social security system in a generation. Championed by Iain Duncan Smith, the Secretary of State for Work and Pensions in the coalition government, it received Royal Assent on 8 March 2012.

Main provisions

  • Universal Credit – a single monthly payment replacing six means-tested benefits and tax credits (Income Support, income-based Jobseeker's Allowance, income-related Employment and Support Allowance, Housing Benefit, Child Tax Credit and Working Tax Credit), intended to simplify the system and smooth the transition into work.
  • Personal Independence Payment – replacing Disability Living Allowance for working-age claimants, with a new assessment.
  • Benefit cap – a limit on the total benefits a working-age household can receive.
  • Under-occupancy penalty (widely called the "bedroom tax") – a reduction in Housing Benefit for social housing tenants deemed to have spare bedrooms.
  • Changes to Child Benefit for higher-rate taxpayers, reforms to the Social Fund, and a ten-year limit effects through the introduction of new conditionality and sanctions regimes.

Implementation and reception

Universal Credit was rolled out gradually from 2013 and was repeatedly delayed, with full rollout extending well into the 2020s. The reforms were defended as promoting work incentives and fairness, and criticised by charities, parliamentary committees and the UN special rapporteur on extreme poverty for increasing hardship among low-income and disabled claimants. The Act remains the legislative foundation of the modern UK benefits system.
