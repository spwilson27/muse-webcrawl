Mutual organization

In a mutual, policyholders or customers are also the owners.

A mutual organisation (often simply a mutual) is an organisation — such as an insurer, bank or building society — that is owned by its customers or members rather than by outside shareholders. Because there are no external investors, any surplus is typically returned to members in the form of lower premiums, higher interest rates, dividends or reinvestment in services, rather than being paid out as shareholder profit.

Structure

In a mutual insurer, policyholders collectively own the company and elect its board. In a mutual savings bank or credit union, depositors or members hold the ownership rights. Voting is usually on a one-member-one-vote basis, regardless of the size of a member's holding. This contrasts with a stock company, in which voting power and dividends follow share ownership.

History and demutualization

Mutual forms were the dominant model for life insurance and savings institutions in the 19th and early 20th centuries: policyholders pooled risk without needing outside capital. From the 1980s onwards, many mutuals converted to shareholder ownership in a process called demutualization — notably British building societies such as Abbey National and Halifax, and large insurers in the United States, Britain and Australia. Supporters of conversion argued it gave access to capital markets; critics said it transferred collectively built wealth to a single generation of members ("carpetbagging" controversies surrounded windfall shares). Prominent remaining mutuals include Nationwide Building Society in the UK, many credit unions, and mutual insurers such as State Farm in the US.

The mutual should not be confused with mutual aid, a principle of reciprocal voluntary cooperation in social and political theory. See also cooperative and building society.
