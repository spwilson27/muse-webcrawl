Reader-response criticism

Reader-response criticism is a school of literary theory that focuses on the reader (or "audience") and their experience of a literary work, in contrast to theories that emphasize the author or the text itself. It holds that literature should be understood as a performing art in which each reader creates their own, possibly unique, text-related performance — meaning emerges from the transaction between text and reader rather than residing fixed within the text.

Key figures

Important theorists include Stanley Fish, who argued that meaning is produced by "interpretive communities" of readers sharing strategies; Wolfgang Iser, who described the "implied reader" and the gaps (Leerstellen) that readers fill in; Hans Robert Jauss, founder of reception theory, who introduced the "horizon of expectations"; Louise Rosenblatt, whose transactional theory distinguished efferent from aesthetic reading; and David Bleich, who advocated subjective criticism based on individual responses.

Varieties

Reader-response approaches range from transactional and affective stylistics to subjective and social reader-response theory. Although diverse, they share the view that a text is an event that happens in the reader's mind and that interpretation cannot be separated from the act of reading.

Influence

Emerging in the 1960s and 1970s in reaction against the New Criticism's focus on the autonomous text, reader-response criticism influenced pedagogy, reception studies, and later audience studies in film and media.
