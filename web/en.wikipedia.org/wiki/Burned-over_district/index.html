Burned-over district

The burned-over district refers to the western and central portions of the US state of New York in the early 19th century, where religious revivals and the formation of new religious movements of the Second Great Awakening took place, to such a great extent that spiritual fervor seemed to set the area on fire. The phrase was coined by the revivalist Charles Grandison Finney, who noted the area had been so thoroughly evangelised that there was no "fuel" (unconverted population) left for further revivals.

Background

The opening of the Erie Canal (1825) and rapid settlement transformed the region, loosening traditional church structures among a mobile, market-oriented population. Revival preachers — most famously Finney in Rochester in 1830–31 — used "new measures" such as the anxious bench and protracted meetings to win converts.

New movements

The district was the birthplace or early home of an extraordinary cluster of movements: the Latter Day Saint movement (Joseph Smith received his visions near Palmyra and published the Book of Mormon there in 1830); Millerism, which anticipated Christ's return in 1843–44; Spiritualism, beginning with the Fox sisters at Hydesville in 1848; the communal Oneida Community of John Humphrey Noyes; and the Shaker village at Sodus Bay. The region also incubated reform crusades — abolitionism, temperance and women's rights, whose first convention met at Seneca Falls in 1848.

Historiography

Whitney R. Cross's The Burned-over District (1950) is the classic study, linking religious enthusiasm to the social and economic dislocations of the market revolution. Later historians have qualified Finney's metaphor, noting conventional church growth continued alongside the new movements.
