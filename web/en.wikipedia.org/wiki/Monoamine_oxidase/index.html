Monoamine oxidase (MAO) is a family of enzymes that catalyse the oxidation of monoamines, employing oxygen to clip off their amine group. They are found bound to the outer membrane of mitochondria in most cell types of the body. In humans there are two types: MAO-A and MAO-B, both of which play important roles in the inactivation of monoamine neurotransmitters, though with differing substrate preferences.

Function

MAO-A preferentially deaminates serotonin, melatonin, norepinephrine, and epinephrine, while MAO-B mainly acts on phenethylamine and benzylamine; both act on dopamine and tyramine. By regulating neurotransmitter levels, MAO influences mood and behaviour, and its activity is implicated in depression and other neuropsychiatric conditions.

In the gut and liver, MAO also breaks down dietary tyramine. People taking MAO-inhibiting drugs who consume tyramine-rich foods (such as aged cheeses) can suffer dangerous hypertensive crises — the so-called 'cheese effect'.

Inhibitors and medicine

Monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) were among the first antidepressants discovered, in the 1950s, and remain in use for atypical and treatment-resistant depression. Selective MAO-B inhibitors such as selegiline and rasagiline are used in Parkinson's disease to slow dopamine breakdown.

The MAOA gene, encoding MAO-A, lies on the X chromosome; rare deficiency causes Brunner syndrome, and common variants have been studied (controversially) in relation to aggression.
