B Reactor

The B Reactor at the Hanford Site in Washington state was the world's first full-scale plutonium production nuclear reactor. Built during the Manhattan Project in 1943–44, it produced the plutonium used in the Trinity test device and in the "Fat Man" bomb detonated over Nagasaki. It was designated a U.S. National Historic Landmark in 2008 and in 2015 became part of the Manhattan Project National Historical Park, open to public tours.

Design and operation

The reactor consisted of a graphite block about 36 by 36 feet in cross-section, pierced by 2,004 horizontal aluminium process tubes loaded with slugs of natural uranium. Water from the Columbia River was pumped through the tubes for cooling. Irradiated fuel slugs were pushed out into a cooling pool and later sent to chemical separation plants, where plutonium was extracted. Construction began in October 1943 and the reactor went critical on 26 September 1944; an early shutdown caused by xenon-135 poisoning was overcome by loading extra fuel, a solution Enrico Fermi and John Archibald Wheeler's team identified in its first days of operation.

Legacy

B Reactor operated until 1968. Unlike Hanford's other wartime reactors, which were cocooned, B Reactor was preserved for its historic significance and is the only one of the site's nine production reactors accessible to the public.
