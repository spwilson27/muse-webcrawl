The Dungan Revolt (1862–1877), known in Chinese as the Tongzhi Hui Rebellion, was a large-scale uprising of Hui Muslims and other Muslim ethnic groups in the northwestern provinces of Qing China—Shaanxi, Gansu, Ningxia and Xinjiang. Coming on top of the Taiping Rebellion and other mid-century revolts, it devastated the northwest and caused enormous loss of life through fighting, famine and massacre.

Course of the revolt

Fighting began in Shaanxi in 1862 amid ethnic violence between Hui communities and Han militias. Rebel leaders such as Ma Hualong in Gansu built fortified strongholds, while in Xinjiang a series of local regimes rose as Qing authority collapsed; in 1867 the Kashgarian ruler Yakub Beg established the emirate of Yettishar centred on Kashgar. The Qing court eventually entrusted the reconquest to Zuo Zongtang, whose methodical campaign—supported by elaborate grain logistics and foreign loans—took Jinjibao in 1871, advanced across Gansu, and in 1876–77 recovered Xinjiang, defeating Yakub Beg's forces; Yakub Beg died in 1877 and Kashgar fell that December.

Consequences

Population losses in Shaanxi and Gansu were catastrophic, with some estimates of decline running into the millions, and many Hui were forcibly relocated. Large numbers of Hui refugees—the Dungans—fled across the Russian border into what is now Kyrgyzstan and Kazakhstan, where their descendants still live. Xinjiang was reorganised as a province of the empire in 1884.
