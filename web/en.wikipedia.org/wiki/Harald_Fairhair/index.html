Harald Fairhair (Old Norse: Haraldr Hárfagri; c. 850 – c. 932) is depicted by medieval Norwegian historians as the first King of Norway. According to traditions current in Norway and Iceland in the 12th and 13th centuries, he reigned from c. 872 to c. 930 and consolidated one of the largest of the Norwegian petty kingdoms into a single realm. Most of the available information about Harald Fairhair comes from later saga writing, and the historicity of details of his life and reign is debated by modern historians.

The sagas describe Harald as the son of Halfdan the Black, a petty king of Vestfold. According to Heimskringla, Harald vowed not to cut or comb his hair until he had united all of Norway, earning him first the byname "Shockhead" (lúfa) and, after ten years, "Fairhair" when he finally had his hair cut.

Unification of Norway

According to saga tradition, Harald's unification culminated in the Battle of Hafrsfjord, conventionally dated to 872, where he defeated an alliance of petty kings and earls from western Norway. After this victory, Harald claimed overlordship over the whole country. He imposed taxes on land and established a system of jarls (earls) to administer the districts of his realm, each supported by revenues from their territories.

The unification process, and the burdens that came with it, are said to have driven many Norwegians to emigrate, contributing to the Norse settlement of Iceland, the Faroe Islands, and other Atlantic colonies during the Viking Age.

Family and succession

The sagas credit Harald with many sons by different wives and concubines, including Eric Bloodaxe, whom he is said to have favoured as his successor, and Haakon the Good, who was raised at the court of King Æthelstan of England and later took the Norwegian throne. The proliferation of royal sons led to strife after Harald's death, and the unity of the kingdom was repeatedly contested in the following generations.

Historical assessment

Modern historians generally accept that a king named Harald exercised power in western Norway in the late 9th and early 10th centuries, but they treat many saga details — including the hair vow, the exact date of Hafrsfjord, and the extent of his realm — as literary traditions recorded centuries later. Contemporary skaldic poetry, such as the Haraldskvæði attributed to Þorbjörn Hornklofi, provides some of the earliest evidence connected to Harald and the battle of Hafrsfjord.

Legacy

Harald Fairhair occupies a central place in Norwegian national memory as the founder of the Norwegian kingdom. The Haraldshaugen monument near Haugesund, erected in 1872 for the millennium of the Battle of Hafrsfjord, marks the site traditionally identified as his burial place. He appears in numerous sagas, plays, and modern works of historical fiction.
