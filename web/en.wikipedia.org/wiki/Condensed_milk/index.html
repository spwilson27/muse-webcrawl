Condensed milk

Condensed milk is cow's milk from which most of the water has been removed. It is most often sold in cans as sweetened condensed milk, a very thick, sweet product made by evaporating roughly 60 per cent of the water from whole milk and adding a large proportion of sugar (around 40–45 per cent). The high sugar concentration and the heat treatment during manufacture give the product a shelf life of years without refrigeration before opening.

History

Methods of preserving milk by concentration were explored in the early 19th century; Nicolas Appert condensed milk in France in the 1820s. The American inventor Gail Borden patented a process in 1856 using a vacuum pan to evaporate water without scorching the milk, and founded the New York Condensed Milk Company. Condensed milk became an important staple during the American Civil War and later in regions lacking refrigeration, and Borden's Eagle Brand became a widely recognized product.

Production and properties

Fresh milk is pasteurized and evaporated under reduced pressure until about half or more of its water is removed. Sugar is added to the concentrated milk; the sugar binds water and makes the environment inhospitable to microorganisms, so sweetened condensed milk can be stored unopened for years, though it gradually darkens and thickens. Unsweetened condensed milk, sterilized more heavily, is sold as evaporated milk and is a distinct product with a shorter unopened shelf life and a slightly caramelized flavour.

Uses

Sweetened condensed milk is a key ingredient in many desserts, including key lime pie, tres leches cake, Brazilian brigadeiros, and dulce de leche (produced by slowly heating sweetened condensed milk). It is stirred into coffee and tea in many countries — for example in Vietnamese coffee — and used in fudge, ice cream, and confectionery. It should not be used as a direct substitute for fresh milk in infant feeding because of its very high sugar content and unbalanced nutrient profile.
