Coal mining disasters

Coal mining disasters are mining accidents that occur during the process of mining coal, most commonly underground. Thousands of miners die from mining accidents each year, especially in the processes of coal mining, although most deaths now occur in developing countries and in illegal rural mines. The most common causes of death in coal mines are methane and coal dust explosions, suffocation from poisonous gases such as carbon monoxide and hydrogen sulfide (afterdamp), roof falls, flooding, and vehicle collisions.

Causes

Underground coal seams release firedamp, a flammable mixture consisting mostly of methane. If methane accumulates to between roughly 5% and 15% in air and encounters an ignition source — a spark from equipment, a naked flame, or a shot fired to blast coal — a violent explosion results. Such an explosion can raise coal dust into the air, producing a far more powerful secondary coal dust explosion that propagates through the whole mine. After an explosion, the atmosphere is filled with afterdamp, rich in carbon monoxide, which historically killed more miners than the explosion itself. Other major hazards include inundations from old flooded workings, spontaneous combustion, and the collapse of roofs and coal faces.

The Benxihu (Honkeiko) colliery explosion of 26 April 1942 in Japanese-occupied Manchuria remains the worst mining disaster in history, with 1,549 deaths — about a third of the miners on shift. The Courrières disaster of 10 March 1906, which killed 1,099 miners including many children, remains the worst mining accident in European history and prompted reforms in mine safety across the continent.

Safety improvements

Disasters drove successive waves of safety legislation and technology: the Davy safety lamp (1815) reduced ignition risk; mechanical ventilation, stone-dusting to inert coal dust, methane monitoring, self-rescuers, and refuge chambers all followed major accidents. In the United States, the Monongah disaster led to the creation of the Bureau of Mines in 1910; in Britain, disasters such as Senghenydd led to the Coal Mines Act 1911. Despite improvements, coal mining remains one of the most dangerous occupations, with China, India, and other major producers still recording hundreds of deaths annually in recent decades.
