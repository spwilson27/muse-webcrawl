The Battle of Fort Oswego was a successful French siege and capture of the British fortifications at Oswego on the southeastern shore of Lake Ontario in August 1756, during the French and Indian War (the North American theatre of the Seven Years' War). The victory gave France control of the lake's south shore and eliminated the principal British position threatening Canada from the west.

Background

Oswego had grown from a fur-trading post into a cluster of three works: Fort Ontario on the east bank of the Oswego River, the older Fort Oswego (Fort Pepperrell) on the west bank, and the unfinished Fort George. The fortifications were poorly designed and dilapidated. In the summer of 1756 the new French commander in Canada, the Marquis de Montcalm, concentrated some 3,000 regulars, Canadian militia, and Native allies for a descent on Oswego.

The siege

Montcalm's forces landed unopposed on 10 August and opened siege approaches against Fort Ontario. The garrison of Fort Ontario, running short of ammunition, spiked its guns and withdrew across the river on 13 August. From the captured heights the French bombarded Fort Oswego; its commander, Colonel James Mercer, was killed by a cannon shot, and on 14 August Lieutenant-Colonel John Littlehales surrendered the remaining forts and the entire garrison — about 1,700 soldiers and civilians.

Aftermath

The French destroyed the forts and withdrew to Canada with their prisoners and captured stores, including the vessels of the small British lake flotilla. After the surrender, some wounded and sick prisoners were killed by Native allies of the French, an episode that foreshadowed the controversy after Fort William Henry the following year. Oswego's fall exposed the New York frontier, though the British rebuilt a fort there in 1759 during their advance on Canada.
