Periphrasis

In linguistics and grammar, periphrasis (from Greek periphrazō, "to speak around") is the use of multiple separate words to carry the meaning of prefixes, suffixes or other grammatical structures that could otherwise be expressed by a single inflected word. The resulting construction is called periphrastic.

Examples

English periphrastic forms include the comparative more happy (an alternative to the inflected happier), the future construction will go, and the perfect have gone, in which auxiliary verbs carry tense, aspect and mood instead of changes to the main verb. English also uses periphrasis in causatives such as have something done and in the periphrastic do of questions and negatives.

Periphrasis vs. inflection

Languages vary in how much of their grammar is expressed inflectionally (synthetically) versus periphrastically (analytically). Latin, for example, forms the perfect passive periphrastically with the verb "to be" plus a participle, and Romance future tenses partly developed from periphrastic constructions that later fused into single words. In rhetoric, periphrasis also denotes circumlocution — speaking in a roundabout way, such as describing "the father of his country" instead of naming George Washington.
