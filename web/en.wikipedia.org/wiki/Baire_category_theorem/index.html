Baire category theorem

Baire category theorem

FieldGeneral topology, functional analysis
Proved byRené-Louis Baire (1899, for ℝn); generalised by Hausdorff
ConsequencesUniform boundedness principle, open mapping theorem, closed graph theorem

The Baire category theorem is a fundamental result of general topology and functional analysis, proved by René-Louis Baire in his 1899 doctoral thesis. It states, roughly, that a complete metric space is "large" in a specific topological sense: it cannot be expressed as a countable union of negligible ("meagre") sets.

Definitions

A subset of a topological space is called nowhere dense if the interior of its closure is empty. A set is meagre (of the first category) if it is a countable union of nowhere dense sets; a set that is not meagre is of the second category. A set whose complement is meagre is called comeagre.

Statement

Baire category theorem. Every complete metric space is a Baire space: the intersection of any countable collection of open dense sets is dense. Equivalently, a non-empty complete metric space is not meagre in itself. The theorem also holds for locally compact Hausdorff spaces.

The proof proceeds by constructing, inside any given open set, a nested sequence of closed balls with radii tending to zero, each avoiding one of the given nowhere dense sets; completeness then guarantees a common point.

Examples and consequences

The theorem shows that the irrational numbers are comeagre in ℝ (the rationals being a countable union of points, hence meagre), and implies that any countable complete metric space without isolated points is impossible — applied to ℝ it also yields an existence proof that ℝ is uncountable in the style of Cantor. In functional analysis, the Baire category theorem is the standard tool behind three cornerstone results for Banach spaces:

  • the uniform boundedness principle (Banach–Steinhaus theorem);
  • the open mapping theorem;
  • the closed graph theorem.

It is also used to prove "generic" existence theorems — for instance, that a typical continuous function on [0, 1] is nowhere differentiable — and in descriptive set theory, where sets with the Baire property are studied.
