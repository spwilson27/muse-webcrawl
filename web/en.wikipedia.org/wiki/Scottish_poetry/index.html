Scottish poetry

Scottish poetry is poetry written by Scots, in any of the languages of Scotland — Scottish Gaelic, Scots, Scottish English, and Latin — or by poets of the Scottish diaspora. It has one of the longest continuous traditions in Europe, extending from the early medieval Brittonic and Gaelic verse of the first millennium to a thriving contemporary scene.

Early and medieval poetry

The earliest surviving Scottish poetry includes Brittonic praise-poems such as Y Gododdin, attributed to Aneirin, and Gaelic verse preserved in the Irish annals and in the Book of the Dean of Lismore (compiled c. 1512–1542), the earliest surviving anthology of Scottish Gaelic poetry. In Scots, John Barbour's The Brus (c. 1375) opened a tradition that flowered in the court of the Stewart kings: the Makars Robert Henryson, William Dunbar and Gavin Douglas produced some of the finest poetry in any British vernacular of their age.

From the Reformation to Burns

The Reformation and the departure of the court to London in 1603 reduced patronage, but ballad and song traditions flourished. The 18th-century vernacular revival — James Thomson writing in English, Allan Ramsay and Robert Fergusson in Scots — culminated in Robert Burns, Scotland's national poet, whose work combined song, satire, love lyric and vernacular narrative. James Macpherson's "Ossian" poems (1760s), presented as translations of ancient Gaelic epic, proved hugely influential in Europe despite controversy over their authenticity. Lady Anne Lindsay's "Auld Robin Gray" and the songs of Carolina Nairne kept women's voices central to the song tradition.

Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Nineteenth-century Scotland produced popular newspaper poets such as William Miller ("Wee Willie Winkie") and William McGonagall — celebrated, uniquely, for the badness of his verse — alongside the "Whistle-Binkie" anthologies. The 20th-century Scottish Renaissance, led by Hugh MacDiarmid, renewed poetry in Scots, Gaelic and English: Edwin Muir, Sorley MacLean (whose Gaelic Dàin do Eimhir is a landmark of European modernism), George Mackay Brown, Norman MacCaig, Edwin Morgan and Iain Crichton Smith reshaped the art. Glasgow poets such as Tom Leonard brought working-class vernacular into serious poetry.

Contemporary poetry

Modern Scottish poetry includes the work of Carol Ann Duffy (born in Glasgow, UK Poet Laureate 2009–2019), Kathleen Jamie, Don Paterson, John Burnside, Robert Crawford and Jackie Kay, who served as Makar (national poet) from 2016, followed by Kathleen Jamie in 2021. The office of Makar, revived in 2004 with Edwin Morgan as its first holder, symbolises poetry's continuing place in Scottish public life.
