Pescetarianism

Pescetarianism (also spelled pescatarianism) is the dietary practice of abstaining from meat (the flesh of land animals) while consuming fish and other seafood. Most pescetarians also eat dairy products and eggs, making the diet a form of semi-vegetarianism closely resembling a vegetarian diet supplemented with seafood. The word blends the Italian pesce ("fish") with "vegetarian"; it entered English dictionaries in the early 1990s.

Motivations

People adopt pescetarian diets for health reasons — fish provides omega-3 fatty acids and lean protein — for ethical concerns weighted toward land animals, for environmental reasons (seafood often has a lower carbon footprint than red meat, though this varies greatly by species and fishing method), or as a transitional step toward vegetarianism or veganism.

Health and nutrition

Observational studies associate pescetarian diets with lower risks of ischemic heart disease and colorectal cancer compared with meat-eating diets, though higher fish intake raises questions about mercury and other contaminants, particularly for pregnant women, and about overfishing and sustainability. Nutritionally the diet generally supplies ample vitamin B12 and protein, the nutrients most watched in plant-based diets.

Terminology debates

Some vegetarians object to describing pescetarians as vegetarians, since fish are animals; others regard the diet as part of the flexitarian spectrum. Related terms include "pesco-vegetarian" and, in Japanese dietary culture, the traditional Buddhist-influenced patterns that historically limited meat but permitted fish.
