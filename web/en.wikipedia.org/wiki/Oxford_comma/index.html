Oxford comma

The Oxford comma (also called the serial comma or Harvard comma) is a comma placed immediately after the penultimate term (i.e., before the coordinating conjunction) in a series of three or more terms. For example, a list of three countries might be punctuated either as "France, Spain, and Portugal" (with the Oxford comma) or as "France, Spain and Portugal" (without it).

The Oxford comma is named after its use by editors at Oxford University Press, and in the United States the convention is sometimes called the Harvard comma, after its use in Harvard University Press style guides.

Usage

Style guides are divided on the Oxford comma. Many American style guides, including The Chicago Manual of Style and the MLA and APA styles, recommend or require its use, while journalistic style guides such as the AP Stylebook generally recommend omitting it except where needed for clarity. British usage is more variable.

Arguments

Supporters argue that the Oxford comma removes ambiguity. A frequently cited example of ambiguity without it is the classic book dedication:

"To my parents, Ayn Rand and God."

Read literally, this could imply the writer's parents are Ayn Rand and God. With the Oxford comma — "To my parents, Ayn Rand, and God" — the list is clearly three separate dedications. Opponents argue that in most contexts the comma is unnecessary, that rewording is a better way to resolve ambiguity, and that omitting it saves space, a consideration historically important in newspaper printing.

Legal significance

The Oxford comma gained public attention in 2017 in the US case O'Connor v. Oakhurst Dairy, in which the United States Court of Appeals for the First Circuit found that the absence of a serial comma in a Maine overtime statute created ambiguity, ruling in favour of delivery drivers and contributing to a settlement of about five million dollars.
