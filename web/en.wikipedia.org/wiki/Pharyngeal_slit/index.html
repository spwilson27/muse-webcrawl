Pharyngeal slit

Pharyngeal slits are openings in the pharynx — the part of the digestive tract just behind the mouth — that connect the pharyngeal cavity to the outside of the body. They are one of the defining characteristics of the phylum Chordata: all chordates possess pharyngeal slits at some stage of their development, together with a notochord, a dorsal nerve cord and a post-anal tail.

Function

In the earliest chordates and in living filter-feeding forms such as lancelets (amphioxus) and tunicates, the slits serve feeding: water enters the mouth, passes out through the slits, and food particles are trapped on mucus. In fishes the walls between the slits bear gills, and the openings became the gill slits used for gas exchange; water pumped over the gills exits through them.

In vertebrate development

In tetrapods, including humans, true open slits never fully form in the embryo, but the corresponding structures — the pharyngeal (branchial) arches, pouches and clefts — are prominent in early development and give rise to important structures of the head and neck. Derivatives include the auditory tube and middle-ear cavity (first pouch), the palatine tonsils (second pouch), the parathyroid glands and thymus (third and fourth pouches), and the bones and muscles of the jaw, ear and throat from the intervening arches.

Pharyngeal slits also occur in hemichordates such as acorn worms, supporting the close relationship between hemichordates and chordates within the deuterostomes.
