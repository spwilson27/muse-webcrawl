The Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA) is a US law passed in December 2015 that governs the United States K–12 public education policy. It reauthorised the Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965 and replaced the No Child Left Behind Act (NCLB) of 2001. ESSA shifted significant authority over school accountability from the federal government back to the states, while retaining annual standardised testing requirements.

Background

By the 2010s, NCLB's requirement that all students reach proficiency by 2014, and its system of escalating sanctions for schools that missed "adequate yearly progress", had become widely criticised, and most states had received federal waivers from its provisions. ESSA was passed by Congress with bipartisan support and signed by President Barack Obama on 10 December 2015, taking full effect in the 2017–18 school year.

Provisions

ESSA requires states to test students in reading and mathematics annually in grades 3–8 and once in high school, and to break down results by student subgroups. However, each state designs its own goals and accountability system, which must include academic indicators and at least one measure of school quality or student success, and must identify and intervene in the lowest-performing 5% of schools and in schools where subgroups consistently underperform. The law eliminated the federal "highly qualified teacher" requirement, ended the federal teacher-evaluation mandates of the waiver era, and maintained funding formulas for disadvantaged students under Title I. It also explicitly prohibited the Secretary of Education from mandating or incentivising particular academic standards, a response to controversy over the Common Core.

Reception

The law was praised for reducing federal overreach and prescription, while critics expressed concern that weaker federal oversight might allow states to neglect disadvantaged student groups. Implementation and state accountability plans remained subjects of debate in subsequent years.
