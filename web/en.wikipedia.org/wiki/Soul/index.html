Soul

In many religious and philosophical traditions, the soul is the non-material essence of a person, which includes one's identity, personality, and memories, that is believed to be able to survive physical death. In theology, the soul is further thought to be immortal and to exist before and after the human body's existence.

Religious views

In the Abrahamic religions, only human beings have immortal souls. In Judaism, the soul is associated with concepts such as nephesh and neshamah. Christianity developed doctrines of the soul's immortality, judgment after death, heaven and hell. Islam teaches that the soul (ruh) is breathed into the body by God and returns to God after death. In Hinduism, the atman is the eternal self that undergoes reincarnation according to karma until liberation (moksha). Buddhism, by contrast, teaches anatta — the doctrine that no permanent, unchanging soul exists.

Philosophical views

For Plato, the soul is tripartite — rational, spirited, and appetitive — and immortal, as argued in the Phaedo. Aristotle defined the soul (psyche) as the form or first actuality of a living body, in his De Anima. Descartes identified the soul with the thinking mind, distinct from the extended body, giving rise to Cartesian dualism and the mind–body problem. Modern philosophy of mind generally explains mental phenomena without positing an immaterial soul.

Science

There is no scientific evidence for a soul separable from the brain; neuroscientific research correlates mental functions with brain activity. The concept remains central to religious belief, ethics, and cultural ideas about personal identity and the afterlife.
