Chorale prelude

In music, a chorale prelude (German: Choralvorspiel) is a short liturgical composition for organ based on a chorale (Lutheran hymn) melody. It was typically played before the singing of the chorale in a church service, to introduce the tune and set the mood, and sometimes alternated with sung stanzas.

History

The genre developed in 17th-century Germany, with early examples by composers such as Samuel Scheidt, Heinrich Scheidemann, and Dieterich Buxtehude. Techniques included presenting the melody in long notes (cantus firmus), decorating it with ornamentation, treating each phrase as a fugal exposition, or accompanying it with imitative counter-melodies derived from the tune's opening notes.

Bach

Johann Sebastian Bach brought the chorale prelude to its height. His Orgelbüchlein (Little Organ Book) contains 46 short preludes for the church year, and the "Great Eighteen" and Schübler Chorales explore the form on a larger scale, the latter being transcriptions of his own cantata movements.

Later composers

Johannes Brahms wrote eleven chorale preludes as his final works (Op. 122), and Max Reger composed dozens, including large chorale fantasias. The form continues in the work of modern organ composers.
