Fractional quantum Hall effect

The fractional quantum Hall effect (FQHE) is a physical phenomenon in which the Hall conductance of 2D non-interacting topological insulators shows precisely quantized plateaus at fractional values of e2/h. It is a property of a collective state in which electrons bind magnetic flux lines to form new quasiparticles, and the excitations have a fractional elementary charge and possibly also fractional statistics.

History and explanation

The effect was discovered in 1982 by Daniel Tsui, Horst Störmer, and Arthur Gossard, who observed a plateau at filling factor 1/3 in a high-mobility gallium arsenide heterostructure at low temperature and strong magnetic field. Robert Laughlin proposed an explanation the following year, introducing an exactly solvable incompressible quantum liquid wavefunction — the Laughlin wavefunction — whose quasiparticle excitations carry charge e/3. Tsui, Störmer, and Laughlin shared the 1998 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery and explanation of the effect.

The FQHE is a consequence of strong correlations between electrons in a partially filled Landau level. Unlike the integer quantum Hall effect, which is essentially a single-particle phenomenon, the fractional effect requires electron–electron interactions. In the composite fermion picture developed by Jainendra Jain, electrons capture an even number of magnetic flux quanta to form composite fermions, which then experience a reduced effective magnetic field and exhibit an integer quantum Hall effect that maps onto the fractional states of the original electrons.

Impact

The fractional quantum Hall effect demonstrated the existence of emergent quasiparticles with fractional charge and anyonic statistics in a condensed matter system. Certain states, notably the 5/2 state, are believed to host non-Abelian anyons, whose braiding properties have made them candidates for fault-tolerant topological quantum computation. The phenomenon remains a central example of topological order in physics.
