Algorithmic art

Algorithmic art, also known as computer-generated art, is art — mainly visual art — in which the design is generated by an algorithm. Artists who work in this way, sometimes called algorists, write rules or code that autonomously produce the artwork, often exploiting controlled randomness so that each run yields a distinct but related output.

History

The origins of algorithmic art lie in the geometric patterns of Islamic art, generated by rule-based constructions, and in procedural techniques such as the harmonograph. With computers, pioneers in the 1960s — including Frieder Nake, A. Michael Noll, and Georg Nees — used plotters to draw algorithmically composed images, staging some of the first exhibitions of computer art. Vera Molnár and Manfred Mohr developed distinctive procedural styles over decades.

Methods

Techniques include fractal art, L-systems for plant-like forms, genetic algorithms that evolve images under selection, cellular automata, and — since the 2010s — generative adversarial networks and other AI models. In so-called generative art the artist designs an autonomous system whose behaviour produces the final work, raising enduring questions about authorship and creativity.

Recognition

Algorithmic art is collected by major museums; the 2018 auction of the GAN-produced Portrait of Edmond de Belamy at Christie's drew wide attention to the field, and long-form generative platforms have since brought algorists a broad audience.
