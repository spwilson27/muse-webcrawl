Transformative use

Appropriation art and parody are classic arenas for the transformative-use doctrine.

In United States copyright law, transformative use is a doctrine that permits the use of copyrighted works to create new works that alter the original with "new expression, meaning, or message". Transformative use is an important consideration in fair use analysis: the more transformative a use, the more likely it is to be held fair.

Origin

The concept was articulated by Judge Pierre Leval in his 1990 article "Toward a Fair Use Standard" and adopted by the Supreme Court in Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc. (1994), which held that 2 Live Crew's parody of "Oh, Pretty Woman" could be fair use. The Court asked whether the new work "merely supersedes the objects of the original creation" or "instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character".

Applications

Courts have found transformative use in parody, thumbnail images in search engines (Kelly v. Arriba Soft), mass digitisation for full-text search (Authors Guild v. Google, 2015), and some forms of appropriation art — though Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith (2023) narrowed the doctrine where the secondary work shares substantially the same purpose and commercial character as the original. A transformative use is distinct from a derivative work, which recasts the original without adding the new meaning or message that transformation requires.

The related cultural concept of the transformative work is also central to fan communities: fan fiction, fan vids and other fanworks are defended by organisations such as the Organization for Transformative Works as creative transformations of source texts.

References: Leval, P., "Toward a Fair Use Standard", Harvard Law Review 103 (1990); Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, 510 U.S. 569 (1994).
