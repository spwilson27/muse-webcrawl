Pathological science

Pathological science is a term coined by the Nobel laureate chemist Irving Langmuir for the process in science in which researchers, without deliberate dishonesty, are led astray by self-deception and subjective effects into believing in phenomena that do not exist. Langmuir described it in a celebrated 1953 talk at the Knolls Atomic Power Laboratory; his examples included N rays, mitogenetic rays, and certain claims about extrasensory perception.

Langmuir's examples often involved barely detectable effects at the threshold of observation.

Langmuir's symptoms

Langmuir identified several characteristic symptoms of pathological science:

  • The maximum effect is produced by a causative agent of barely detectable intensity, and the effect is largely independent of the intensity of the cause.
  • The effect is of a magnitude that remains close to the limit of detectability.
  • Claims of great accuracy are made for the measurements.
  • Fantastic theories contrary to experience are suggested.
  • Criticisms are met by ad hoc excuses.
  • The ratio of supporters to critics rises to about 50 per cent and then falls gradually to oblivion.

Famous examples

The classic case is the N rays affair of 1903, in which the French physicist Prosper-René Blondlot claimed to discover a new form of radiation; dozens of laboratories reported confirming the effect before a visit by Robert W. Wood demonstrated that the readings depended on the observer's expectations. Other frequently cited episodes include polywater in the 1960s — an anomalous form of water that proved to be contaminated ordinary water — and, in the view of many commentators, the 1989 cold fusion announcement, though that case also involved questions of experimental error and replicability.

Distinction from fraud and pseudoscience

Pathological science differs from scientific misconduct because its practitioners genuinely believe their results; it differs from pseudoscience because it arises within the normal institutions of science and eventually collapses under scrutiny. The episode is often cited in discussions of confirmation bias, the experimenter's regress, and the importance of blinded measurement.
