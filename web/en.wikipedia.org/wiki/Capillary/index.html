Capillary

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Capillaries are the smallest blood vessels, forming vast networks (capillary beds) between arterioles and venules in almost every tissue. Their walls are a single layer of endothelial cells, sometimes with pores, across which oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients and waste products are exchanged between blood and tissue fluid.

Types and exchange

Continuous capillaries (in muscle, skin, brain — where they form part of the blood–brain barrier) have tight junctions; fenestrated capillaries (in kidneys, intestines, endocrine glands) have pores for rapid exchange; sinusoidal capillaries in the liver, spleen and bone marrow have large gaps letting cells and big molecules pass. Exchange occurs by diffusion, filtration driven by blood pressure, and reabsorption driven by plasma protein osmotic pressure.

Scale

A capillary is typically 5–10 micrometres wide — barely wider than a red blood cell passing through it. The total length of capillaries in a human body is often estimated in the tens of thousands of kilometres. Flow through beds is regulated by precapillary sphincters according to tissue needs.
