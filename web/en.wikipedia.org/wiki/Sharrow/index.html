Sharrow

Sharrow
TypePavement marking / traffic sign
PurposeIndicate a shared travel lane for bicycles and motor vehicles
Formal nameShared lane marking

A sharrow (a portmanteau of share and arrow) is a road marking consisting of a bicycle symbol with two chevrons or arrows above it, painted on a travel lane that is shared by bicycles and motor vehicles. Officially termed a shared lane marking, it indicates where cyclists should ride within the lane, alerts motorists to the likely presence of cyclists, and encourages safe passing, without creating an exclusive bicycle lane.

History

The marking originated in Denver, Colorado in the 1990s and was popularised by San Francisco beginning in 2004. It was incorporated into the United States Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD) in its 2009 edition, which standardised the symbol and placement guidance. Similar markings have since been adopted in other countries including Canada, Australia and Japan.

Usage and guidance

Guidance typically limits sharrows to streets with low speed limits (generally 35 mph / 56 km/h or less) where there is insufficient width for a dedicated bike lane. The marking is placed toward the centre of the lane, or at least 1.2 metres (4 feet) from the kerb, so that cyclists ride clear of the "door zone" of parked cars.

Effectiveness and criticism

Studies have found modest benefits: cyclists tend to ride slightly further from parked cars and some motorists give more passing space. Critics argue that sharrows — sometimes nicknamed "share the road in paint only" — do little to protect cyclists compared with physically separated infrastructure, and that they can create a false sense of safety on busy streets.
