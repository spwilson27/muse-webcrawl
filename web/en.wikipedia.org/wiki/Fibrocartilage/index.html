Fibrocartilage

Fibrocartilage is a tough, dense, fibrous material that helps fill in torn parts of cartilage, provides a strong, sturdy support and acts as a shock absorber in joints. It is a type of cartilage found in areas requiring both strength and elasticity. Fibrocartilage consists of a mixture of white fibrous tissue and cartilaginous tissue in various proportions; it owes its inflexibility and toughness to the former, and its elasticity to the latter.

Structure

Fibrocartilage contains rows of chondrocytes embedded in a matrix rich in type I collagen fibres, in contrast to hyaline cartilage, which is dominated by type II collagen. It has no distinct perichondrium. The dense, regularly arranged collagen bundles allow it to withstand high compressive and tensile loads, but it has limited capacity for self-repair because it is avascular.

Locations

Fibrocartilage is found in the intervertebral discs of the spine, the pubic symphysis, the menisci of the knee, the triangular fibrocartilage complex of the wrist, the labra of the shoulder and hip, and at the insertions of tendons and ligaments into bone (entheses). The callus formed during the repair of a bone fracture is also composed of fibrocartilage.

Clinical significance

Damage to fibrocartilaginous structures is common in sports and ageing: meniscal tears of the knee, degeneration of intervertebral discs contributing to back pain, and triangular fibrocartilage tears causing wrist pain are prominent examples. Surgical repair or partial removal is often required because the tissue heals poorly on its own.
