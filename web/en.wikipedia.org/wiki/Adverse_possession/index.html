Adverse possession

Adverse possession, sometimes colloquially described as "squatter's rights", is a legal principle in common law under which a person who does not have legal title to a piece of property — usually land — may acquire legal ownership based on continuous possession or occupation of the land without the permission of its legal owner. The property acquired in this way is also called an adverse possession title.

Requirements

Although the details vary by jurisdiction, a claim of adverse possession typically requires that the possession be:

  • Actual — the claimant exercises control over the property;
  • Open and notorious — visible enough that the true owner would notice;
  • Exclusive — not shared with the general public or the owner;
  • Hostile — without the owner's permission and inconsistent with the owner's rights;
  • Continuous — for the statutory period, which commonly ranges from 5 to 30 years depending on the jurisdiction.

Rationale

The doctrine is justified on several grounds: it penalizes owners who "sleep on their rights", rewards productive use of land, settles stale claims where evidence has been lost, and corrects minor boundary errors. Statutes of limitation on ejectment actions effectively bar the original owner from recovering the land once the period has run.

Variations

Some jurisdictions require the possessor to act in good faith; others require only that the possession be hostile. In civil law systems, the analogous doctrine is usually called acquisitive prescription (Latin: usucapio). Many jurisdictions restrict or bar adverse possession claims against government-owned land.
