Whale fall

A whale fall occurs when the carcass of a whale sinks to the ocean floor at depths greater than 1,000 m, in the bathyal or abyssal zone. On the sea floor, these carcasses create a complex, localised ecosystem that supplies sustenance to deep-sea organisms — equivalent to many years' worth of the ordinary organic "marine snow" falling from above — and can support communities for decades.

Stages of decomposition

Researchers recognise overlapping ecological stages: a mobile-scavenger stage, in which hagfish, sleeper sharks and amphipods strip the soft tissue; an enrichment-opportunist stage, with dense colonisation by polychaetes and crustaceans; a sulfophilic stage, in which bacteria break down lipids in the bones, releasing sulfides that support chemosynthetic fauna much like hydrothermal vents; and finally a reef stage. Whale falls host specialists found nowhere else, such as the bone-eating worm Osedax, and are thought to act as "stepping stones" allowing vent and seep fauna to disperse across the deep ocean.

Discovery

Whale falls were first observed in 1977 off California, and were systematically studied from 1987 when the submersible Alvin found a skeleton supporting a rich community in the Santa Catalina Basin.
