Wye Valley

The Wye Valley (Welsh: Dyffryn Gwy) is the valley of the River Wye along the southern part of the border between England and Wales. It is recognised as an Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty (AONB) in England and a National Landscape in Wales, covering some 326 square kilometres (126 sq mi) of limestone gorges, ancient broadleaved woodland and riverside settlements from just south of Hereford down to Chepstow, where the Wye joins the Severn Estuary.

Landscape and nature

The lower Wye flows through a deep incised meander valley cut into carboniferous limestone and old red sandstone. The woods of the valley are among the most important areas of native broadleaved woodland in Britain, supporting species such as lesser horseshoe bats, peregrine falcons, goshawks and otters. Landmarks include Symonds Yat Rock, the Lancaut peninsula and the limestone crags around Tintern.

History and tourism

The valley is celebrated as a birthplace of British tourism: in 1770 the Reverend William Gilpin published his Observations on the River Wye, popularising the idea of the picturesque, and boat tours from Ross-on-Wye to Chepstow became fashionable. The ruins of Tintern Abbey, founded in 1131, are the valley's best-known historic site and inspired Wordsworth's poem "Lines Composed a Few Miles Above Tintern Abbey" (1798) and paintings by J. M. W. Turner.
