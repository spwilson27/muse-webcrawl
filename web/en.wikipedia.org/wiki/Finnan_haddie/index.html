Finnan haddie

Finnan haddie (also known as Finnan haddock, Finnan, Finny haddock, or Findrum in Scotland) is cold-smoked haddock, representative of a regional method of smoking with green wood and peat in north-east Scotland. It is often served poached in milk for breakfast.

Origin

The origin of the name is a matter of debate. According to a story dating back to the eighteenth century, the name derives from the village of Findon (sometimes spelt Findhorn), Aberdeenshire, near Aberdeen in Scotland, where fishmongers originally salted, dried and smoked the fish. The name was originally Findrum or Finnan haddies (plural), and referred to the fish of Findon.

The dish is known to have been mentioned as early as the 1648 book The Compleat Angler by Izaak Walton under the name of "Finnock Haddocks". During the 18th and 19th centuries Finnan haddies were considered a delicacy.

Culinary uses

Finnan haddie is often served poached in milk, as part of a traditional Scottish breakfast, although it is also served at high tea and supper in Scotland. In the traditional kedgeree recipe, Finnan haddie is used as the fish. It is also a component of the Cullen skink soup and the Arnold Bennett omelette.
