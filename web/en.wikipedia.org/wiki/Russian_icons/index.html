Russian icons

Russian icons are sacred images painted in the Orthodox Christian tradition in Russia from the Christianisation of Kievan Rus in 988 until the present. Painted typically in egg tempera on wooden panels, icons were central to worship in churches — where they filled the iconostasis — and in homes, where the "beautiful corner" held the family icons.

History

Early Russian icon painting, centred on Novgorod and later Moscow, followed Byzantine models. The 12th-century Our Lady of Vladimir, a Byzantine work brought to Rus, became the most revered image of Russia. The art reached its classical height in the 15th century with Andrei Rublev, whose Trinity (c. 1411 or 1420s) is considered the masterpiece of Russian medieval painting, and Dionisius. Iconographers worked within strict canonical types (podlinniki pattern books), signing their work rarely, since the image was understood as a window to the divine rather than personal art.

Later developments

The 17th century brought Western influence under Simon Ushakov and the Armoury Chamber school; after the schism of 1666, the Old Believers preserved older styles. Many panels were covered with metal riza covers, and in the 19th century mass-produced and printed icons spread. Soviet persecution destroyed countless icons, though restoration work — cleaning off darkened varnish and overpainting — revealed the brilliant colours of medieval panels to modern viewers, transforming the reputation of icon painting as art. Major collections are held by the Tretyakov Gallery in Moscow and the Russian Museum in St. Petersburg.
