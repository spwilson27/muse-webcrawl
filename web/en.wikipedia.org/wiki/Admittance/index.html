Admittance

In electrical engineering, admittance is a measure of how easily a circuit or device will allow a current to flow. It is defined as the inverse of impedance, analogous to how conductance and resistance are inverses. The SI unit of admittance is the siemens (symbol S), and its symbol is Y. Oliver Heaviside coined the term admittance in December 1887.

Definition

Admittance is defined as

Y = 1/Z = I/V

where Z is impedance, I is the current phasor, and V is the voltage phasor. As a complex quantity it decomposes as

Y = G + jB

where G is conductance, measured in siemens, representing the real part associated with energy dissipation, and B is susceptance, also in siemens, representing the imaginary part associated with energy storage. The magnitude of admittance is |Y| = √(G² + B²).

Conversion from impedance

Given impedance Z = R + jX (resistance R and reactance X), the admittance is

Y = (R − jX) / (R² + X²),

so that conductance G = R / (R² + X²) and susceptance B = −X / (R² + X²). Note that conductance is not simply the reciprocal of resistance unless the reactance is zero.

Applications

Admittance is especially convenient for analyzing components in parallel, because the total admittance of parallel branches is the simple sum of their admittances, just as total resistance is the sum for series components. It is fundamental to the nodal analysis of AC networks, to Smith chart calculations (which plot normalized impedance or admittance for transmission-line problems), and to the bus admittance matrix (Y-bus) used in power-flow studies of electric grids.
