The Tujia (Chinese: 土家族; pinyin: Tǔjiāzú; endonym: Bizika, "native dwellers") are an ethnic group native to the Wuling Mountains of south-central China. Numbering about 8.35 million at the 2010 census, they are the eighth-largest of the 56 officially recognised ethnic groups of the People's Republic of China. Their heartland spans western Hunan (Xiangxi Tujia and Miao Autonomous Prefecture), western Hubei (Enshi Tujia and Miao Autonomous Prefecture), south-eastern Chongqing, and north-eastern Guizhou.

History

The Tujia trace their origins to the ancient Ba people and other groups of the middle Yangtze region. For centuries their mountain homeland was governed indirectly by hereditary native chieftains under the tusi system, until the Qing dynasty replaced them with regular officials in the eighteenth century (the gaitu guiliu reforms). The Tujia were formally recognised as a distinct ethnic minority only in 1957, after ethnologists — most prominently Pan Guangdan — documented their distinct history and surviving customs.

Language

The Tujia language belongs to the Tibeto-Burman branch of the Sino-Tibetan family, with Northern and Southern varieties that are not fully mutually intelligible. It has no traditional written form. Because of centuries of contact and schooling in Chinese, the language is now severely endangered: most ethnic Tujia speak Southwestern Mandarin, and fluent Tujia speakers number only in the tens of thousands, concentrated in a few counties of northern Xiangxi.

Culture

Signature Tujia customs include the kujia ("crying marriage"), in which a bride ritually weeps songs in the days before her wedding, and the Baishou dance, a large group dance in which hundreds of participants perform coordinated hand gestures telling stories of creation, migration and farming. Tujia brocade (Xilankapu), woven by women with geometric patterns in bright colours, is a celebrated craft listed in China's national intangible cultural heritage, as are Tujia New Year customs and the Maogusi ritual dance-drama honouring ancestral spirits. Traditional stilted houses (diaojiaolou) built on hillsides remain characteristic of Tujia villages.
