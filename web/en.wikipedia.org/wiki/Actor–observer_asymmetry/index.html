Actor–observer asymmetry

Actor–observer asymmetry (also actor–observer bias or actor–observer difference) is a bias in attribution in social psychology. It is the tendency to attribute one's own actions to situational causes while attributing other people's actions to their personality or disposition. The concept was proposed by Edward Jones and Richard Nisbett in 1971.

[Image: Actor and observer perspectives]

Explanations

Jones and Nisbett proposed two explanations. First, a perceptual one: an actor's attention is focused outward on the situation, while an observer's attention is focused on the actor, who is the salient figure. Second, an informational one: actors have access to knowledge about their own behaviour across many situations, making situational variability evident, whereas observers see only a single instance. A meta-analysis by Bertram Malle (2006), however, found the overall effect to be much smaller than traditionally assumed and dependent on how attributions are measured, sparking debate about the robustness of the asymmetry.

Relation to other biases

The asymmetry is related to, but distinct from, the fundamental attribution error (the general tendency to overemphasise dispositional explanations for others' behaviour) and the self-serving bias.
