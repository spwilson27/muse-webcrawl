Shampoo

Shampoo is a hair care product, typically a viscous liquid, used for cleaning hair. It works by trapping oils and dirt in micelles formed by surfactants, which are then rinsed away with water. Shampoo is generally scrubbed into wet hair, massaged into the scalp, and rinsed; a second application is common, and conditioner is often applied afterwards.

Etymology and history

The word derives from the Hindi champo (चाँपो), meaning "to press or knead", via the Anglo-Indian practice of hair-washing with massage, popularised in Britain in the early 19th century by the entrepreneur Sake Dean Mahomed, who opened shampooing baths in Brighton. Traditional cleansers included soap, reetha (soapnut) and shikakai in India. Modern liquid shampoos based on synthetic detergents appeared in the 1930s — Procter & Gamble's Drene was among the first — and displaced soap-based products because they lathered in hard water and left less residue.

Composition

Most shampoos consist mainly of water, with primary surfactants such as sodium laureth sulfate or sodium lauryl sulfate, co-surfactants such as cocamidopropyl betaine, viscosity modifiers, preservatives, fragrance and conditioning agents such as silicones. Specialised formulations include anti-dandruff shampoos (with agents such as zinc pyrithione, selenium sulfide or ketoconazole), "2-in-1" shampoo-conditioners, sulphate-free and clarifying shampoos, and dry shampoo powders or sprays.

Use and debate

Daily shampooing became the norm in the later 20th century, but dermatologists debate optimal frequency, which depends on hair type and sebum production. The "no poo" movement advocates washing without conventional shampoo, using water, baking soda or conditioner-only methods.
