Tetrahedron

In geometry, a tetrahedron (plural: tetrahedra or tetrahedrons) is a polyhedron composed of four triangular faces, six straight edges, and four vertices. It is the three-dimensional case of the simplex and the simplest of all ordinary convex polyhedra — the only one with fewer than five faces.

Regular tetrahedron

A regular tetrahedron has all four faces equilateral triangles and is one of the five Platonic solids. For edge length a, its surface area is √3·a² and its volume is a³/(6√2) ≈ 0.11785·a³. Its dihedral angle is arccos(1/3) ≈ 70.53°. Unlike other Platonic solids it is self-dual, and it does not tile space by itself, though Aristotle mistakenly claimed it did.

Formulas and properties

The volume of any tetrahedron is one third of the product of a base area and the corresponding height, and can be computed from vertex coordinates as one sixth of the absolute value of a scalar triple product. The Cayley–Menger determinant generalises Heron's formula to give volume from six edge lengths.

Applications

Tetrahedral geometry appears in chemistry, where molecules such as methane have tetrahedral arrangement of bonds, in finite-element mesh generation, in caltrops and four-sided dice, and in structural frameworks.
