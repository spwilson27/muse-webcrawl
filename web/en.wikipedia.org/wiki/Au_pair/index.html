Au pair

An au pair is a young person from abroad who lives with a host family and provides childcare (and sometimes light household help) in exchange for room, board, a small stipend or "pocket money", and the opportunity to learn the host country's language and culture. The term comes from the French au pair, meaning "on a par" or "equal to", reflecting the idea that the au pair is treated as a member of the family rather than as a domestic employee.

History

The au pair arrangement developed in the 19th century as a way for young women to learn languages abroad, and after the Second World War it expanded across Europe as a form of cultural exchange. In 1969 the Council of Europe adopted the European Agreement on Au Pair Placement, which set out basic conditions including maximum working hours, rest days and the requirement that the au pair be given time to attend language courses.

Arrangements by country

United States

In the United States, au pairs enter on the J-1 Exchange Visitor visa through designated sponsor organisations. Participants must be between 18 and 26, may stay for twelve months with the possibility of extension, and receive a weekly stipend; host families must provide a private bedroom, meals and an educational allowance toward coursework.

Europe

Many European countries operate national au pair schemes, generally requiring participants to be under 30, limiting weekly childcare hours (often to about 25–30), and requiring pocket money. Following debates about labour rights, some countries have tightened or closed their schemes.

Duties and conditions

Typical duties include taking children to and from school, helping with homework, preparing children's meals, playing with children and light housework connected with the children. Au pairs are generally not expected to perform heavy housework or to act as full-time professional nannies, and arrangements commonly include at least one full day off per week and paid time for language study.

Criticism

Au pair programmes have been criticised where blurred lines between "cultural exchange" and employment have led to excessive hours or below-minimum-wage effective pay, prompting court cases and regulatory reform in several countries.
