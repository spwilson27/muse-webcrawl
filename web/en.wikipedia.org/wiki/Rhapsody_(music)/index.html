Rhapsody (music)

A rhapsody in music is a one-movement work that is episodic yet integrated, free-flowing in structure, featuring a range of highly contrasted moods, colour, and tonality. It often has an air of spontaneous inspiration and improvisation. The term derives from the Greek rhapsode, a performer of epic poetry.

History

The rhapsody became popular in the 19th century, particularly through Franz Liszt's Hungarian Rhapsodies, which drew on Hungarian folk themes. Other notable examples include Brahms's two Rhapsodies for piano, Op. 79, Rachmaninoff's Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini, and Gershwin's Rhapsody in Blue (1924). Nationalist composers including Dvořák (Slavonic Rhapsodies) and Enescu (Romanian Rhapsodies) used the form to celebrate folk material.
