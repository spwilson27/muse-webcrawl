Stadtholder

In the Low Countries, a stadtholder (Dutch: stadhouder, meaning "place-holder" or "stead-holder") was a steward appointed to govern a province in the name of a feudal lord. In the Dutch Republic (1581–1795), the stadtholders of the provinces—servants, in theory, of the provincial States assemblies—became de facto heads of state, and the office, dominated by the House of Orange-Nassau, evolved toward a hereditary position rivalling monarchy.

Origins

Under Burgundian and Habsburg rule, the stadtholder was the sovereign's lieutenant in a province, presiding over its court and commanding its forces. When the northern provinces rebelled against Spain during the Eighty Years' War, they retained the office but now appointed the stadtholder themselves. William of Orange, stadtholder of Holland, Zeeland, and Utrecht under Philip II, became the leader of the revolt and the Republic's first great stadtholder.

The office in the Republic

Each of the seven provinces could appoint its own stadtholder, but in practice one man—usually the Prince of Orange—held most provinces at once, and a second stadtholder of the Nassau-Dietz line governed Friesland and Groningen. The stadtholder commanded the army and navy (as captain-general and admiral-general), appointed many magistrates, issued pardons, and exercised great informal influence, yet legally remained a servant of the sovereign provincial States. Tension between the stadtholder's "Orangist" supporters and the republican "States party" regents defined Dutch politics, and twice the office was left vacant: the First Stadtholderless Period (1650–1672) and the Second (1702–1747). In 1747, amid crisis, William IV was made stadtholder of all provinces and the office declared hereditary; his son William V was the last stadtholder, fleeing in 1795 before French revolutionary forces.

Legacy

The stadtholderate was the seedbed of the Dutch monarchy: William V's son returned in 1813 as sovereign prince and in 1815 became King William I of the Netherlands. The stadtholder's residence survives in The Hague's Binnenhof complex, and the title's ambiguity—servant yet quasi-sovereign—remains a classic case study in constitutional history.
