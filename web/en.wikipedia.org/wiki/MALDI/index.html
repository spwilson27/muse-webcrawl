Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI) is an ionization technique that uses a laser energy-absorbing matrix to create ions from large molecules with minimal fragmentation. It has been applied to the analysis of biomolecules (biopolymers such as DNA, proteins, peptides and carbohydrates) and various organic molecules (such as polymers, dendrimers and other macromolecules), which tend to be fragile and fragment when ionized by more conventional ionization methods. It is similar in character to electrospray ionization (ESI) in that both techniques are relatively soft ways of obtaining ions of large molecules in the gas phase, though MALDI typically produces far fewer multi-charged ions.

History

The MALDI methodology is rooted in work from the 1980s. In 1985, Franz Hillenkamp, Michael Karas and co-workers described using the amino acid tryptophan as a matrix that made it possible to ionize larger peptides. Independently, Koichi Tanaka demonstrated in 1987 that a mixture of ultra-fine metal powder and glycerol could ionize large biomolecules such as proteins; Tanaka shared the 2002 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the development of soft desorption ionization methods. Karas and Hillenkamp subsequently achieved ionization of the protein albumin using a nicotinic acid matrix and a 266 nm laser, establishing the matrix-assisted approach in close to its modern form.

Principle

The sample is mixed with a suitable matrix material — commonly a crystalline organic acid such as sinapinic acid, α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid, or 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid — and applied to a metal plate. On drying, the matrix and analyte co-crystallize. A pulsed laser, often a nitrogen laser at 337 nm or a frequency-tripled Nd:YAG laser at 355 nm, irradiates the spot. The matrix absorbs the laser energy and is desorbed and ionized; it transfers charge to the analyte molecules, which are released into the gas phase as ions.

MALDI is most often coupled with time-of-flight (TOF) mass analyzers, because the pulsed nature of the ionization matches the TOF measurement cycle. The resulting spectra typically consist predominantly of singly charged [M+H]+ ions, which simplifies interpretation: peaks correspond closely to molecular masses.

Applications

MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry is used for rapid identification of microorganisms in clinical microbiology, by matching the spectrum of ribosomal proteins against reference libraries. It is used in proteomics for peptide mass fingerprinting, in polymer chemistry for molecular weight distributions, in imaging mass spectrometry to map molecules across tissue sections, and in quality control of synthetic biomolecules such as therapeutic oligonucleotides.
