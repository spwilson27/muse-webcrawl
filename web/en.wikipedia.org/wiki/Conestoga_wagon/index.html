Conestoga wagon

The Conestoga wagon is a specific design of heavy covered wagon that was used extensively during the late eighteenth century and nineteenth century in the United States and Canada. It was large enough to transport loads up to six tons (5.4 metric tons), and was drawn by horses, mules or oxen. It was designed to resemble a boat, with both ends curving upward, to help keep its load from shifting while traversing hilly or rough terrain, and to help it float across rivers.

A Conestoga wagon, recognisable by its curved, boat-shaped bed and white canvas top.

History

The wagon took its name from the Conestoga River region of Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, where German settlers developed the design in the early 1700s. Pulled typically by a team of six horses — the lead pair controlled by a driver who rode the left rear ("wheel") horse, an origin of left-hand driving conventions — Conestogas carried freight between farms, towns and ports for over a century. They were freight wagons rather than emigrant vehicles; westward-moving families more commonly used the lighter prairie schooner.

Design

Distinctive features included a curved floor rising at both ends, iron-rimmed wooden wheels with broad tires for soft ground, a watertight bed caulked with tar, and a white hemp or linen canvas cover stretched over wooden hoops. The wagons were often painted with a light blue body and red wheels, a traditional colour scheme of the region.
