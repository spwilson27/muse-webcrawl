Games as a service

In the video game industry, games as a service (GaaS), also known as live service games, represents providing video games or game content on a continuing revenue model, similar to software as a service. Games as a service are ways to monetize video games either after their initial sale, or to support a free-to-play model. Games released under the GaaS model typically receive a long or indefinite stream of monetized new content over time to encourage players to continue paying for the game and its updates.

History

The idea of games as a service began with the introduction of massively multiplayer online games (MMOs) such as World of Warcraft in the early 2000s, which used subscription models to fund continuing development. In the late 2000s and early 2010s, mobile and social games popularized free-to-play models supported by microtransactions. By the mid-2010s, major publishers — including Electronic Arts, Activision Blizzard, and Ubisoft — had shifted major franchises toward live-service designs, with downloadable content, season passes, battle passes, and in-game stores generating recurring revenue. Fortnite (2017) became the emblematic example, earning billions through cosmetic sales tied to seasonal content.

Models

  • Subscriptions: players pay a recurring fee for access, as in classic MMOs.
  • Season passes and battle passes: paid tracks of unlockable content over a limited season.
  • Microtransactions: in-game purchases of items, currency, or cosmetic content.
  • Downloadable content (DLC) and expansions: periodic paid content drops.
  • Game subscription services: library access through services such as Xbox Game Pass.

Rationale

Publishers favor GaaS because a single successful title can produce a steady, predictable revenue stream for years, smoothing the boom-and-bust cycle of one-off releases and reducing reliance on expensive new game launches. Continued updates can also grow a game's player base over time, as happened with Destiny, Warframe, and Genshin Impact.

Criticism

The model has drawn criticism for aggressive monetization, including loot boxes — which regulators in several countries have compared to gambling — for content being withheld from the base game, and for games becoming unplayable when servers are shut down. High-profile failures of live-service launches, such as Anthem and Concord, have also prompted debate about market saturation and the risks of chasing the model. Preservationists argue that server-dependent games are especially vulnerable to being lost when support ends.
