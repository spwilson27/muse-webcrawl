Battle of Ap Bac

The Battle of Ap Bac was a small but significant engagement during the Vietnam War, fought on 2 January 1963 near the hamlet of Ap Bac in the Mekong Delta of South Vietnam. A reinforced battalion of the Viet Cong's 514th and 261st battalions, numbering roughly 350 men, defeated a much larger South Vietnamese (ARVN) force supported by artillery, M113 armored personnel carriers and American helicopters, inflicting a public relations as well as military defeat on the government of Ngo Dinh Diem and its American advisers.

Background

Acting on intelligence that a Viet Cong force with a radio transmitter was located near Ap Bac, the ARVN 7th Division planned a converging attack using infantry, a company of M113 armored personnel carriers, artillery and helicopter-borne reinforcements advised by Americans including Lt. Col. John Paul Vann. The Viet Cong, warned in advance, took up well-prepared positions along a canal lined with trees and dug in to await the attack.

Battle

The ARVN advance stalled almost immediately under accurate fire. The M113 company advanced into the killing zone and lost several vehicles when its gunners were killed at their exposed machine guns; repeated helicopter insertions into the landing zones around the hamlet resulted in five helicopters shot down or crash-landed. Requests for aggressive action were delayed or refused by cautious ARVN commanders, and paratroop reinforcements were dropped late in the day in the wrong location. Under cover of darkness the Viet Cong withdrew in good order, having won a striking victory.

Significance

Ap Bac became a symbol of the failures of the Diem regime's military and of the limits of American advice: despite overwhelming material superiority, the ARVN proved unwilling to fight aggressively. Vann's blunt criticisms, reported by journalists such as Neil Sheehan and David Halberstam, deepened American press skepticism about the war. The battle foreshadowed the far larger commitment to come.
