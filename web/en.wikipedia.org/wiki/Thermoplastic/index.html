Thermoplastic

A thermoplastic is a polymer material that becomes pliable or mouldable above a specific temperature and solidifies on cooling — a transition that is physically reversible and can be repeated many times. Most thermoplastics are high-molecular-weight polymers whose chains are held together by intermolecular forces rather than covalent cross-links; heating weakens those forces (or melts crystalline regions), allowing the material to flow. This contrasts with thermosetting polymers, which cure irreversibly into a cross-linked network and decompose rather than melt when reheated. Thermoplastics dominate the plastics industry and include polyethylene, polypropylene, polystyrene, polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polycarbonate, nylon and acrylic (PMMA).

Properties and structure

Thermoplastics may be amorphous (glassy or rubbery depending on the glass transition temperature, Tg) or semicrystalline, with a distinct melting temperature Tm. Their mechanical properties range from the soft, flexible polyethylenes to high-performance engineering polymers — polyetheretherketone (PEEK), polysulfones, polyimides — that withstand temperatures above 200 °C and aggressive chemicals. Repeated melt processing gradually degrades most thermoplastics through chain scission and oxidation, so recycling is practical but not indefinite.

Processing and applications

Because they melt, thermoplastics can be shaped by injection moulding, extrusion, blow moulding, thermoforming, rotational moulding, film blowing and additive manufacturing (fused filament fabrication uses thermoplastic filament such as PLA, ABS and PETG), and they can be joined by heat welding or solvent bonding. Applications span packaging (the largest single use), piping and cable insulation, automotive parts, medical devices, textiles (synthetic fibres), consumer goods and construction. Thermoplastic elastomers combine melt processability with rubber-like behaviour through block-copolymer or blend morphologies.

Recycling

The resin identification code system (numbers 1–7) labels the common thermoplastics for sorting; PET and HDPE are the most widely recycled. Mechanical recycling — sorting, washing, re-melting — is facilitated precisely by the reversible melting that defines the class, although contamination, additives and mixed streams limit output quality ("downcycling"). Chemical recycling routes that depolymerise some thermoplastics back to monomers are under development.
