The Rif War was an armed conflict fought from 1921 to 1926 between Spain (joined by France from 1925) and the Berber tribes of the Rif region of northern Morocco, led by Abd el-Krim. The Rifians proclaimed the Republic of the Rif and for several years resisted the colonial powers in one of the most significant anti-colonial wars of the interwar period.

Background

Under the Treaty of Fez (1912), Spain administered a protectorate over northern Morocco. Spanish efforts to extend control inland from Melilla into the rugged Rif mountains met fierce tribal resistance, organised increasingly under Abd el-Krim, a former qadi and journalist who unified the tribes.

Course of the war

In July–August 1921 Rifian forces destroyed a Spanish army at the Battle of Annual — the "Disaster of Annual" in Spanish memory — killing on the order of 8,000–13,000 Spanish soldiers and capturing large stocks of weapons. The Rifians advanced almost to Melilla and consolidated the Rif Republic. Spain responded with a brutal campaign, including the aerial use of chemical weapons (mustard gas), one of the earliest uses of chemical bombing against civilians and combatants. When Rifian forces attacked the French zone in 1925, France joined with a large army under Marshal Pétain. A joint Spanish amphibious landing at Alhucemas Bay (September 1925) — an early example of combined-arms amphibious warfare — broke Rifian resistance, and Abd el-Krim surrendered in May 1926. He was exiled to Réunion.

Legacy

The disaster at Annual destabilised Restoration Spain and contributed to Primo de Rivera's 1923 coup. The war's veterans (the Africanistas) played a central role in the Spanish Civil War, and the conflict became a symbol of anti-colonial resistance in Morocco.
