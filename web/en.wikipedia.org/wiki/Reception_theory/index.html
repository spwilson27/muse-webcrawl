Reception theory

Reception theory is a version of reader-response literary theory that emphasises each particular reader's reception or interpretation of a literary text. It arose from the work of the Constance School in West Germany in the late 1960s, principally Hans Robert Jauss and Wolfgang Iser.

Key concepts

Jauss proposed that literary history should be written as a history of the reception of texts, and introduced the concept of the "horizon of expectations" (Erwartungshorizont): the set of cultural, generic and personal expectations a reader brings to a work, against which the work is measured and which it may confirm or shock. Great works, Jauss argued, violate and thereby change the horizon of their first readers.

Iser, by contrast, focused on the act of reading itself. He argued that a text contains gaps or blanks (Leerstellen) that the reader must fill in, so that meaning is produced in the interaction between text and reader; the text has an "implied reader" built into its structure.

Influence

Reception theory shifted literary studies away from authorial intention and the autonomous text toward audiences, influencing reception studies in media theory, the British cultural studies work of Stuart Hall on encoding and decoding, and empirical studies of reading.
