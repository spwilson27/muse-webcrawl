Sanation

Sanation (Polish: Sanacja) was a political movement and ruling camp in the Second Polish Republic between 1926 and 1939. Its name derived from the Latin sanatio, "healing", and expressed the movement's pledge to restore moral "health" to Polish public life after the May 1926 coup d'état led by Marshal Józef Piłsudski.

Józef Piłsudski, whose 1926 coup brought the Sanation camp to power in Poland.

Origins

Piłsudski, the founding statesman of independent Poland, had retired from politics in 1923, frustrated with party infighting, unstable coalition governments, hyperinflation, and corruption scandals. On 12–14 May 1926 his loyal troops seized Warsaw in the May Coup, forcing the resignation of President Stanisław Wojciechowski and the government of Wincenty Witos. Piłsudski declined the presidency himself but remained the dominant figure of the state as Minister of Military Affairs and Inspector General of the Armed Forces until his death in 1935.

Programme and rule

The Sanation camp stood above party divisions, at least in its own rhetoric, emphasising the state, the army, clean administration, and moral renewal over ideology. Its electoral vehicle from 1928 was the Nonpartisan Bloc for Cooperation with the Government (BBWR). After disputed elections and the imprisonment of opposition leaders in the Brest trials of 1930–31, the regime became increasingly authoritarian; the April Constitution of 1935 strengthened the presidency and was tailored for Piłsudski, who died a month after its adoption.

The colonels' regime

After 1935 power passed to Piłsudski's associates, often called the "colonels' regime" — among them President Ignacy Mościcki, Marshal Edward Śmigły-Rydz, and foreign minister Józef Beck. Factional struggles between Mościcki's "castle" group and Śmigły-Rydz's camp weakened the movement, which dissolved its structures into the Camp of National Unity (OZN) in 1937. Sanation rule ended with the German and Soviet invasion of Poland in September 1939.
