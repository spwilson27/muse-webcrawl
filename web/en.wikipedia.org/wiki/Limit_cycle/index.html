Limit cycle

In mathematics, in the study of dynamical systems with two-dimensional phase space, a limit cycle is a closed trajectory in phase space having the property that at least one other trajectory spirals into it either as time approaches infinity or as time approaches minus infinity. Such behaviour is exhibited in some nonlinear systems. Limit cycles have been used to model the behaviour of many real-world oscillatory systems, such as the heartbeat and electronic oscillators.

Stability

A limit cycle is stable if nearby trajectories converge to it as time increases, unstable if they converge as time decreases, and semi-stable if it attracts on one side and repels on the other. Stable limit cycles give rise to self-sustained oscillations: a system started near the cycle settles onto periodic motion of definite amplitude and period, robust against small perturbations — behaviour impossible for linear systems, whose oscillations depend on initial conditions.

Existence

The Poincaré–Bendixson theorem states that a two-dimensional continuous dynamical system that has a nonempty compact limit set containing no fixed points must have a limit cycle; it provides a common route to proving the existence of limit cycles in planar systems. The classical example is the Van der Pol oscillator, a nonlinear oscillator originally developed to describe triode circuits, which possesses a unique stable limit cycle for each positive damping parameter.

Applications

Limit-cycle models are used in neuroscience (rhythmic firing of neurons), cardiology, chemical kinetics (the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction), ecology (predator–prey cycles), and aeronautics, where wing flutter can be described as a limit cycle oscillation.
