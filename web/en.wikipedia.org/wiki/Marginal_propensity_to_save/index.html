Marginal propensity to save

Illustration: The saving function; its slope is the MPS (placeholder image)

The marginal propensity to save (MPS) is the proportion of an additional unit of income that is saved rather than spent on consumption. It is a core concept in Keynesian economics, introduced by John Maynard Keynes in The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936).

Definition

Formally, MPS is the change in saving (ΔS) divided by the change in disposable income (ΔY) that produced it:

MPS = ΔS / ΔY

Because any additional income is either consumed or saved, the marginal propensity to save and the marginal propensity to consume (MPC) always sum to one:

MPC + MPS = 1

For example, if households save 20 cents of every extra dollar earned, the MPS is 0.2 and the MPC is 0.8.

Role in the multiplier

The MPS is the reciprocal of the spending multiplier: the Keynesian multiplier equals 1/MPS (equivalently, 1/(1 − MPC)). A smaller MPS (and hence a larger MPC) produces a larger multiplier effect from government spending or investment; a larger MPS means more "leakage" from the circular flow of income and a smaller multiplier. The MPS is generally observed to rise with income, since higher-income households save a larger fraction of additional earnings, and it figures in debates about fiscal policy, the paradox of thrift, and the effectiveness of tax cuts targeted at different income groups.
