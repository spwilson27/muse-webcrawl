The Battle of Sempach was fought on 9 July 1386 between the Old Swiss Confederacy and Duke Leopold III of Austria. The Swiss victory — in which Leopold and a large part of the Habsburg nobility of the Aargau and Swabia were killed — secured the Confederacy's survival and its control of Lucerne and the lake country. In Swiss national tradition the battle is inseparable from the legend of Arnold Winkelried, who is said to have gathered the Austrian lances into his own breast to open a gap for his comrades.

Background

Lucerne, a Confederation member since 1332, was expanding aggressively at Habsburg expense, granting citizenship to subjects of Austrian towns and seizing Sempach and other places. Leopold III marched in the summer of 1386 to crush the Confederates, his army heavy with the chivalry of Further Austria. The Confederates of the Forest Cantons hurried to Lucerne's aid and met the Austrians near Sempach on a hot July day.

The battle

The Austrian knights, confident and fearing the Swiss would escape, dismounted (cutting off the long points of their fashionable shoes to fight on foot, the chronicles say) and advanced in a dense phalanx with levelled lances. The Swiss vanguard was at first driven back, and their leader Gundoldingen killed; but the main Confederate body arriving from the woods fell on the Austrian flank. The long Swiss halberds and the press of the dismounted men-at-arms in heavy armour, suffocating in the heat, turned the fight: the Austrian formation broke, the squires fled with the horses, and Leopold and hundreds of nobles were cut down where they stood.

The Winkelried legend

Later chronicles relate that Arnold Winkelried of Unterwalden, seeing the Swiss could not break the hedge of lances, threw himself upon them crying "I will open a passage for you — care for my wife and children!", and dragged a sheaf of spears down with his body so the Confederates poured through the breach. The story is not in the earliest accounts and is generally treated as legend, though it became one of Switzerland's most potent founding myths.

Aftermath

The victory, followed by Näfels (1388), forced the Habsburgs into truces that left the Confederacy effectively independent in its homelands; the battlefield chapel at Sempach and the annual commemoration still mark the day.
