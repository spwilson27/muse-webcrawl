Profession

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The learned professions traditionally comprised law, medicine, and divinity.

A profession is a field of work that has been successfully professionalized. It can be defined as a disciplined group of individuals, professionals, who adhere to ethical standards and hold themselves out as, and are accepted by the public as, possessing special knowledge and skills in a widely recognised body of learning derived from research, education, and training at a high level, and who are prepared to apply this knowledge and exercise these skills in the interest of others.

Characteristics

Sociologists have long debated the defining traits of a profession. The classic "trait" model lists prolonged specialist training, a formal code of ethics, a professional association, licensing or certification that restricts practice to qualified members, a degree of autonomy in work, and a service ideal that places clients' interests first. The historic "learned professions" were law, medicine, and divinity; the term now extends to occupations such as architecture, engineering, accountancy, nursing, teaching, and social work.

Professionalization

Professionalization is the social process by which an occupation transforms itself into a profession of the highest integrity and competence—typically by establishing credential requirements, founding professional bodies, securing legal protection of title or scope of practice, and adopting enforceable ethical codes. Critics from a conflict perspective note that professionalization also functions as a form of social closure, restricting entry and enhancing members' pay and status.

Regulation

Professions are typically regulated by statute, with registration or licensing administered by professional bodies that can discipline or strike off members for misconduct. Many professionals owe fiduciary duties to clients and may be liable for malpractice when their work falls below the standard of a reasonably competent practitioner.
