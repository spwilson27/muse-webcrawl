ACE (computer)

The Pilot ACE, the scaled-down test version of Turing's design, first ran in May 1950.

The ACE (Automatic Computing Engine) was one of the earliest stored-program computer designs, prepared by Alan Turing in a detailed report written at the end of 1945 for the National Physical Laboratory (NPL) in Teddington, England. Turing's proposal was remarkably complete: it specified the machine's logic circuits, its mercury delay-line memory, and even example programs, and it envisaged a versatile general-purpose machine able to tackle problems from codebreaking to chess.

Design

Unlike most contemporary designs, the ACE was intended to rely on sophisticated programming rather than elaborate hardware. Turing planned a high clock speed of about 1 MHz and a memory of delay lines storing roughly 200 long words initially, expandable as funds allowed. Instructions were timed to the circulation of data in the delay lines — the so-called "optimum programming" technique — in which the programmer scheduled each instruction so its operands would arrive exactly when needed. The full ACE was expected to have extensive floating-point capability implemented largely in software.

Construction

Bureaucratic delays and Turing's departure for Cambridge in 1947 stalled the full machine. In his absence the NPL team, including Jim Wilkinson, Mike Woodger and Donald Davies, built a reduced test version, the Pilot ACE, which ran its first program on 10 May 1950 and was demonstrated to the press in November of that year — making it one of the first stored-program computers in regular use anywhere, and for a time among the fastest. The Pilot ACE used about 800 vacuum tubes.

The Pilot ACE design was commercialised by English Electric as the DEUCE (1955), of which more than thirty were sold, and it influenced Harry Huskey's Bendix G-15 in the United States. The full ACE was eventually completed at NPL in 1957, but by then it was obsolete; it was retired in 1967. The Pilot ACE survives in the collection of the Science Museum, London.

Legacy

The ACE report is now recognised as a foundational document of computer science. It shows Turing moving from the theoretical universal machine of 1936 to practical engineering, and it contains early discussion of subroutines, software libraries and even machine learning and computer chess. Historians regard the ACE, together with the Manchester Baby and EDSAC, as a cornerstone of the British contribution to the stored-program computer.
