Contract law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Contract law is the body of law governing legally enforceable agreements. A contract is a promise or set of promises whose breach the law recognises as giving a remedy, formed typically by offer and acceptance, supported by consideration (in common-law systems), with an intention to create legal relations, between parties with capacity, on terms that are sufficiently certain and lawful. Contract law is a foundation of commerce: sales, employment, loans, leases and services are all contractual.

Systems differ in structure. English and American common law developed contract through case law and equity, supplemented by statutes such as the Uniform Commercial Code's Article 2 for sales of goods in the United States. Civil-law systems codify contract law in civil codes (France's Code civil of 1804, Germany's BGB of 1900), with a general duty of good faith more prominent than in common law. International commerce is harmonised in part by the UN Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG, 1980), in force in most major trading states.

Remedies and doctrines

The normal remedy for breach is damages measured by the expectation interest — putting the claimant in the position performance would have produced — subject to remoteness (Hadley v Baxendale, 1854) and mitigation. Equitable remedies such as specific performance and injunctions are exceptional. Doctrines policing unfairness include misrepresentation, duress, undue influence, unconscionability and, in consumer law, statutory control of unfair terms. Interpretation disputes concern implied terms, standard-form contracts and exemption clauses.
