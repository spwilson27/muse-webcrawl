Solvable group

In mathematics, more specifically in group theory, a solvable group (or soluble group) is a group that can be constructed from abelian groups using extensions. Equivalently, a group is solvable if its derived series terminates in the trivial subgroup after finitely many steps. The concept arose from Galois theory: a polynomial equation is solvable by radicals precisely when its Galois group is a solvable group.

The derived series of a solvable group eventually reaches the trivial subgroup.

Definition

A group G is solvable if there exists a finite sequence of subgroups 1 = G0 ◃ G1 ◃ ... ◃ Gn = G such that each factor group Gk+1/Gk is abelian. Equivalently, defining the derived series by G(0) = G and G(i+1) = [G(i), G(i)], the commutator subgroup of the previous term, G is solvable if G(n) = {1} for some n. The smallest such n is the derived length of the group.

Examples

All abelian groups are solvable, as are all nilpotent groups and all p-groups for a prime p. The symmetric group Sn is solvable for n ≤ 4 but not for n ≥ 5, because A5 is a non-abelian simple group; this fact lies behind the Abel–Ruffini theorem that general quintic equations cannot be solved by radicals. The group of invertible upper-triangular matrices over a field is solvable.

Properties and theorems

Subgroups, quotients, and extensions of solvable groups are solvable. By the Feit–Thompson theorem (1963), every finite group of odd order is solvable, which implies that every finite simple non-abelian group has even order. Burnside's paqb theorem states that every group whose order has only two distinct prime factors is solvable. The class of solvable groups was central to nineteenth-century algebra and remains fundamental to Galois theory and the classification of finite simple groups.
