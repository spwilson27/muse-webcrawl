Oscillating reaction

The Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction oscillates between colour states.

An oscillating reaction is a complex mixture of chemical compounds in which the concentration of one or more components exhibits periodic changes. Such reactions are important examples of non-equilibrium thermodynamics and of self-organization in chemistry: order emerges spontaneously in a system held far from equilibrium. In many oscillating reactions the oscillation is visible, because a coloured intermediate, an indicator, or a gas evolved in pulses makes the periodicity obvious.

Examples

The Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction

The most famous example is the Belousov–Zhabotinsky (BZ) reaction, in which bromate oxidises an organic acid (typically malonic acid) in acidic solution in the presence of a metal-ion catalyst such as cerium or the iron–phenanthroline complex ferroin. The solution cycles repeatedly between colourless and yellow, or red and blue, and in an unstirred dish forms spreading target patterns and spiral waves. Boris Belousov discovered the reaction in the 1950s while seeking an inorganic analogue of the Krebs cycle; Anatol Zhabotinsky developed it in the 1960s.

Other systems

The Briggs–Rauscher reaction oscillates dramatically between clear, amber, and deep blue. The Bray–Liebhafsky reaction, the oldest known homogeneous oscillator (1921), cycles in the iodate-catalysed decomposition of hydrogen peroxide, evolving oxygen in pulses. Gas-phase oscillations occur in the cool-flame oxidation of hydrocarbons, and electrochemical systems can oscillate in current or potential.

Mechanism

Oscillation requires feedback: the Field–Noyes model of the BZ reaction showed that autocatalytic production of an activator (HBrO2), coupled with a delayed inhibitory process that consumes it, can drive the system around a limit cycle rather than to a steady state. The abstract two-variable "Brusselator" and "Oregonator" models capture this behaviour mathematically. Sustained oscillation in a closed vessel is always transient — it runs down as reactants are consumed — but in a continuously stirred flow reactor the oscillations can be maintained indefinitely.

Significance

Oscillating reactions underpin chemical clocks, excitable media, and models of biological rhythms such as glycolytic oscillations, calcium waves, and cardiac dynamics; they also helped earn Ilya Prigogine the 1977 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the theory of dissipative structures.
