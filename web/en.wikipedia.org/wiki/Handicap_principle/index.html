Handicap principle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The handicap principle is a hypothesis proposed by Israeli biologist Amotz Zahavi in 1975. It suggests that animal communication can be honest and reliable because signals are costly to produce: a signal that imposes a real handicap on the signaller can only be afforded by individuals of genuinely high quality, so receivers can trust it. Zahavi illustrated the idea with the peacock's train — a burdensome ornament whose very costliness advertises the male's fitness to prospective mates.

History and reception

The idea was initially rejected by many evolutionary biologists, who considered it counterintuitive, and Zahavi's original paper was turned down by several journals. In 1990, Alan Grafen published a formal game-theoretic model showing that costly signalling can be evolutionarily stable under defined conditions, which greatly increased acceptance of the principle. The idea parallels the economic theory of costly signalling and Thorstein Veblen's concept of conspicuous consumption.

Examples

Classic examples include the extravagant tail of the peacock, the risky alarm calls and predator-inspection behaviour of gazelles (stotting), bright coloration in guppies, large antlers in deer, and elaborate song repertoires in birds. In each case, the trait both attracts mates or deters rivals and predators, and exposes the bearer to real costs that only healthy individuals can bear.

Criticism and scope

Debate continues over how general the principle is: many honest signals may instead be indices (unfakeable for physical reasons) or conventional signals kept honest by social punishment, rather than handicaps. Nevertheless, costly signalling theory is now a central framework in behavioural ecology and has been extended to human behaviour, religion and economics.
