Stand-in

A stand-in, in film and television production, is a person who substitutes for an actor before filming, for technical purposes such as lighting, camera setup and rehearsal. Stand-ins are chosen for a close match to the principal actor in height, build, hair and skin tone, and they wear colours similar to the actor's costume so that lighting and focus can be set accurately. A stand-in does not normally appear on camera in the finished work — distinguishing the role from a body double, who substitutes for the actor within shots, and a stunt double, who performs dangerous action.

Work on set

While the principal actors are in make-up or rehearsing elsewhere, the stand-in stands on the actor's marks so that the director of photography and crew can compose, focus and light the shot. Stand-ins watch rehearsals closely, learn the blocking, and in "second team" are called to set while the actors ("first team") rest. Some stand-ins work with one star for years across many productions.

Use in television

Multi-camera sitcoms and long-running series employ regular stand-ins extensively because of the volume of set-ups. Stand-ins may also read lines off-camera for the actor being filmed in reverse angles, deliver dialogue for rehearsal, or appear in the background as extras — though union rules in many countries regulate how a stand-in may be used on camera.

History and notable examples

The role emerged with the studio system as productions became more technically complex. The 1937 film Stand-In, starring Leslie Howard and Joan Blondell, played on the term. In a widely cited case, Pluma Noisom stood in for Gloria Swanson; Alfred Hitchcock occasionally had stand-ins take brief innocuous shots. Modern productions also use digital stand-ins and previsualisation, but the human stand-in remains standard on most sets.
