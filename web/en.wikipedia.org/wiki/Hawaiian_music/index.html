Hawaiian music

The music of Hawaii encompasses a wide variety of traditional and popular styles, from native Hawaiian folk music to modern rock and hip hop. Hawaii's musical contributions to the music of the United States are out of all proportion to its small size: it gave the world the steel guitar and the slack-key guitar, popularised the ukulele, and in the early twentieth century Hawaiian records and touring troupes sparked a nationwide craze.

Traditional music

Pre-contact Hawaiian music centred on mele — chanted poetry, often performed with hula dance — accompanied by instruments such as the ipu (gourd drum), pahu (sharkskin drum), ʻulīʻulī (feathered rattles) and nose flute (ʻohe hano ihu). Chants preserved genealogy, mythology and history. After contact, missionaries introduced hymn singing (himeni), whose harmony singing blended with native chant; Hawaiian monarchs such as Kalākaua and Liliʻuokalani — composer of "Aloha ʻOe" — were notable patrons and songwriters.

Instruments and innovations

Mexican and Spanish cowboys (paniolo) brought the guitar in the nineteenth century; Hawaiians developed the open-tuned slack-key style (kī hōʻalu). Around 1889 Joseph Kekuku is credited with inventing the steel guitar, played with a bar along raised strings; Portuguese immigrants brought the braguinha that became the ukulele. The steel guitar's sound spread into American country music and beyond.

Popularity

From the 1910s to the 1950s, Hawaiian music was a mainstream American phenomenon: the 1915 Panama-Pacific Exposition boosted its fame, "On the Beach at Waikiki" and other hapa haole songs sold hugely, radio's Hawaii Calls broadcast island music worldwide, and steel guitarist Sol Hoʻopiʻi and falsetto singers became stars. Later figures include Gabby Pahinui, the Sons of Hawaii, Don Ho ("Tiny Bubbles"), and Israel Kamakawiwoʻole, whose medley "Somewhere Over the Rainbow/What a Wonderful World" became a global standard. A Hawaiian renaissance from the 1970s revived Hawaiian-language song and hula, and Hawaiian recordings are recognised by a dedicated Grammy category historically and by the Nā Hōkū Hanohano Awards.
