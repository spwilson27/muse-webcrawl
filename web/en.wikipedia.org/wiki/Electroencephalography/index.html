Electroencephalography

Electroencephalography (EEG)
MeSHD004569
InventorHans Berger (1924, first human EEG)

Electroencephalography (EEG) is a method to record an electrogram of the spontaneous electrical activity of the brain, measured with electrodes placed on the scalp. The signals reflect summed postsynaptic potentials of cortical neurons and are described by frequency bands — delta, theta, alpha, beta and gamma.

History

Richard Caton reported electrical activity from animal brains in 1875. German psychiatrist Hans Berger recorded the first human EEG in 1924 and published in 1929, discovering the alpha rhythm ("Berger rhythm").

Clinical use

EEG is central to diagnosing epilepsy, sleep disorders, encephalopathies, coma and brain death, and is used in anaesthesia monitoring and intensive care. Event-related potentials and quantitative EEG support research on cognition, and EEG feeds many brain–computer interfaces. Compared with MEG or fMRI, EEG offers excellent temporal but limited spatial resolution.

Method

Electrodes use the international 10–20 system. Artefacts from muscle, eye movement and mains noise must be controlled. Intracranial EEG (iEEG) is used in epilepsy surgery evaluation.
