Cellular respiration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cellular respiration is the set of metabolic processes by which cells break down organic molecules — above all glucose — to produce ATP, the cell's usable energy. In aerobic respiration the final electron acceptor is oxygen, and the overall equation mirrors photosynthesis in reverse: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy.

Stages

Glycolysis, in the cytoplasm, splits glucose into pyruvate, netting 2 ATP and requiring no oxygen. In the mitochondria, pyruvate feeds the citric-acid (Krebs) cycle, releasing CO₂ and loading electron carriers. Finally the electron transport chain passes those electrons to oxygen, pumping protons whose flow back through ATP synthase produces the bulk of the ATP — around 30 molecules per glucose in total, by modern estimates.

Anaerobic alternatives

Without oxygen, cells rely on fermentation — regenerating the carriers glycolysis needs while producing lactate (in muscles and many bacteria) or ethanol and CO₂ (in yeast). Respiration is the reverse complement of photosynthesis (see chloroplast), and its mitochondrial machinery descends from ancient bacteria, as explained by endosymbiosis theory.
