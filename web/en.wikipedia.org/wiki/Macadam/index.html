Macadam

Macadam is a type of road construction pioneered by the Scottish engineer John Loudon McAdam in the early 19th century. It consists of a single layer of crushed, angular stone of roughly uniform size, compacted so that the stones interlock, laid on a raised, cambered and well-drained formation.

McAdam's method

McAdam argued that a road's strength came from the native soil beneath, kept dry by drainage and protected by a relatively thin stone crust — rejecting the heavy, expensive stone foundations of earlier engineers such as Thomas Telford. Stones were broken small enough to pass a standard gauge, spread in layers, and consolidated by traffic. The resulting roads were cheaper and longer-lasting than their predecessors and spread rapidly through Britain, Europe, and North America in the 1820s and 1830s.

Later developments

Because macadam surfaces generated dust and broke down under motor traffic, binders were added: tar-bound macadam ("tarmac") was patented by E. Purnell Hooley in 1901, and asphalt-bound versions became the basis of modern road pavement. The word survives in "tarmac" and in the names of many streets.
