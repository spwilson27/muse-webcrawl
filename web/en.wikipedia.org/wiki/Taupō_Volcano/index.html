Taupō Volcano

LocationWaikato, North Island, New Zealand
TypeRhyolitic caldera (supervolcano)
Last eruptionc. 232 CE (Hatepe eruption)
Largest eruptionOruanui eruption, c. 25,400 years ago

The Taupō Volcano is a large rhyolitic supervolcano in the centre of New Zealand's North Island, underlying Lake Taupō in the Taupō Volcanic Zone. It is one of the most frequently active and productive silicic volcanoes on Earth, having produced two of the world's most violent eruptions of the last 30,000 years.

Eruptive history

Volcanism at Taupō began about 300,000 years ago. The Oruanui eruption, around 25,400 years ago, was the largest known eruption of the last 70,000 years, ejecting some 530 cubic kilometres of material and forming much of the modern caldera; it is rated VEI 8, in supervolcano class. The much smaller but still enormous Hatepe eruption, around 232 CE, ejected about 120 cubic kilometres and produced a pyroclastic flow that devastated roughly 20,000 square kilometres; its ash column is thought to have turned skies red as far away as Rome and China. The eruption completed the emptying of the magma chamber, after which the caldera filled to become Lake Taupō.

Geology and monitoring

The volcano sits in a rifting continental arc setting, where extension of the crust allows large volumes of rhyolitic magma to accumulate. GeoNet monitors the volcano with seismographs, GPS and lake-level gauges. Episodes of unrest with earthquake swarms and ground deformation, such as in 2022–2023, are common and do not necessarily lead to eruption, but the volcano is classified as active and potentially hazardous.
