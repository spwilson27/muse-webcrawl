Caning in Singapore

Caning is a form of corporal punishment that remains widely used in Singapore. It is practised in several contexts: as a judicial punishment, as a prison disciplinary measure, as a military punishment, and as a disciplinary method in schools and homes.

Judicial caning

Judicial caning, introduced during British colonial rule, is mandatory for more than 30 offences in Singapore — including robbery, drug trafficking, vandalism and certain immigration offences — and discretionary for over 90 others. It applies only to male offenders aged 18 to 50 who are certified medically fit. Sentences range from one to a maximum of 24 strokes of a rattan cane, administered on the bare buttocks in a single session. Women, men over 50, and those sentenced to death are exempt.

Michael Fay incident

The practice attracted international attention in 1994 when American teenager Michael Fay was caned for vandalism despite diplomatic appeals from the United States. The case sparked debate about cultural relativism, deterrence and human rights.

School and home caning

Corporal punishment of male students with a light cane on the hands or clothed buttocks remains lawful in Singapore schools under strict guidelines, and caning by parents is also legal. Human rights organisations criticise all forms of judicial corporal punishment as cruel and degrading, while the Singapore government defends caning as an effective deterrent that enjoys broad public support.
