Quasi-Monte Carlo method

In numerical analysis, the quasi-Monte Carlo method is a method for numerical integration and solving some other problems using low-discrepancy sequences (also called quasi-random sequences or subrandom sequences). This is in contrast to the regular Monte Carlo method, which uses sequences of pseudorandom numbers. Monte Carlo and quasi-Monte Carlo methods are applied in similar settings and estimate an integral by averaging function values at a set of points in the domain.

Convergence

For the Monte Carlo method, the error of the estimate typically decreases at a rate proportional to N−1/2, where N is the number of points. For quasi-Monte Carlo methods using low-discrepancy sequences, the Koksma–Hlawka inequality bounds the error by a term proportional to the discrepancy of the point set times the variation of the integrand; low-discrepancy sequences achieve discrepancy of order (log N)d / N in d dimensions, giving a convergence rate approaching N−1 — nearly twice the order of plain Monte Carlo, though the advantage degrades in very high dimensions unless the integrand has low effective dimension.

Low-discrepancy sequences

Popular constructions include the van der Corput sequence in one dimension and its multidimensional generalizations: Halton sequences, Sobol sequences, Faure sequences, and Niederreiter sequences. Lattice rules and digital nets are closely related point sets used in quasi-Monte Carlo integration.

Randomization

Randomized quasi-Monte Carlo methods — for example, randomly shifted lattice rules or scrambled digital nets — preserve the low-discrepancy structure while permitting unbiased error estimates from repeated randomized replicates. Quasi-Monte Carlo methods are widely used in computational finance for pricing derivative securities, where integrals in moderate dimension must be computed accurately, and in computer graphics for rendering.
