Fealty

Fealty (from Latin fidelitas, fidelity) was an oath of loyalty sworn by a vassal to his lord within the feudal system of medieval Europe. The vassal pledged fidelity and obedience in return for the lord's protection and, commonly, the grant of a fief, usually land.

The ceremony

The act of becoming a vassal was performed in the ceremony of homage and fealty. The vassal knelt before the lord, placed his joined hands between the lord's hands — the immixtio manuum — and swore an oath of fealty, often on a Bible or relic, promising to be faithful to the lord against all men. The oath was sealed with a kiss (the osculum) and followed by investiture, in which the lord handed over a symbolic object — a clod of earth, a branch or a banner — representing the fief.

Obligations

A vassal's duties typically included military service, attendance at the lord's court to give counsel, and certain financial aids, such as ransoming the lord if he were captured. Breaking the oath — a felony in the original sense of the word — made the vassal liable to forfeit his fief. Fealty differed from homage in that it centred on the oath itself; clergy, who were prohibited from swearing certain oaths, might do fealty without homage.

Later use

The word survives in modern usage as a general term for loyalty or allegiance, and in legal history in connection with tenures in England, where oaths of fealty were still recorded on manorial courts long after the feudal system had decayed.
