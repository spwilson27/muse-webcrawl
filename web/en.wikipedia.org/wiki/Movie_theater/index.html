Movie theater

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A modern multiplex auditorium.

A movie theater (American English), also called a cinema (British English) or picture house, is a venue, usually a building, that contains an auditorium for viewing films for entertainment. Most movie theaters are commercial operations catering to the general public, who attend by purchasing tickets.

History

The first permanent film venues were converted storefronts — the American nickelodeons of the 1900s, named for their five-cent admission. Purpose-built theatres followed, culminating in the lavish "movie palaces" of the 1920s, such as New York's Roxy and Los Angeles' Grauman's Chinese Theatre, seating thousands. Sound (from 1927), widescreen (1950s), and colour transformed presentation. From the 1960s, multi-screen multiplexes — pioneered by Stanley Durwood's AMC — spread from suburban America worldwide, and the 2000s–2010s brought wholesale conversion from 35 mm film to digital projection. Drive-in theatres, peaking in the 1950s United States, survive as a niche format.

Operation and economics

Theatres rent films from distributors, traditionally surrendering most of the opening weeks' box office, and earn much of their profit from concession stands selling popcorn, drinks, and candy. Premium formats — IMAX, Dolby Cinema, 3D, reclining seats, and dine-in service — command higher prices. The exhibition industry was reshaped in the 2010s–2020s by streaming competition and the COVID-19 pandemic, which closed cinemas worldwide and shortened theatrical release windows. Major chains include AMC, Cineworld (Regal), and Cinemark.
