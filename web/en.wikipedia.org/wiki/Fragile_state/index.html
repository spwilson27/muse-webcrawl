Fragile state

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A fragile state or weak state is a state whose central government is so weak or ineffective that it has little practical control over much of its territory, cannot provide public services reliably, and suffers from widespread corruption, institutional decay, or vulnerability to internal and external shocks. Such states may experience high levels of political instability, communal or insurgent violence, large displaced populations, and sharp economic decline. A fragile state at the extreme end of the spectrum is a failed state, where the government has effectively lost control of its territory and legitimacy.

Definition and measurement

There is no single agreed definition. International organisations approach fragility through different lenses: the World Bank classifies "fragile and conflict-affected situations" using governance scores and the presence of conflict; the OECD emphasises multidimensional fragility across economic, environmental, political, security, and societal dimensions; and the Fund for Peace publishes the Fragile States Index, ranking countries annually by twelve indicators of vulnerability. Common features include illegitimate or contested government, inability to maintain a monopoly on the legitimate use of force, porous borders, and dependence on external actors for basic functions.

Causes and consequences

Fragility is associated with legacies of colonial border-drawing, weak institutions, resource dependence, inequality, rapid population growth, neighbouring conflicts, and histories of authoritarian rule or civil war. Fragile states matter internationally because they are linked to humanitarian crises, refugee flows, transnational crime, and the harbouring of armed groups; they are also where extreme poverty is increasingly concentrated, which is why development agencies treat fragility as a central policy problem. Critics of the concept argue that "fragility" is a vague, donor-driven label that can pathologise diverse political situations and justify external intervention.
