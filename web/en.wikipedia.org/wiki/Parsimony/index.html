Parsimony

Parsimony is the principle of preferring the simplest explanation or model that adequately accounts for the evidence. In science and philosophy it is most famously expressed as Occam's razor, attributed to the 14th-century friar William of Ockham: entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity.

In science

In statistics and model selection, parsimonious models are preferred because overly complex models risk overfitting the data. Criteria such as the Akaike information criterion and the Bayesian information criterion formalize the trade-off between goodness of fit and model complexity.

In phylogenetics

In cladistics, maximum parsimony is a method of inferring evolutionary trees by minimizing the total number of evolutionary changes required to explain the observed character data. Though widely used, it can be statistically inconsistent under some conditions, a phenomenon known as long-branch attraction.

In economics and linguistics

The term also appears in the "parsimony principle" in economics and in historical linguistics, where the simplest sound changes are preferred. In everyday usage, "parsimony" can also mean extreme frugality or stinginess.
