Molecular chaperone

In molecular biology, molecular chaperones are proteins that assist the conformational folding of other proteins, the assembly of macromolecular complexes, and the prevention of unwanted aggregation, without becoming part of the final structure of their clients. The term was introduced by Ron Laskey in 1978 for nucleoplasmin's role in histone assembly, and the concept was generalized by John Ellis. Many chaperones are heat shock proteins, induced when cells are stressed by elevated temperature.

The bacterial chaperonin GroEL–GroES.

Functions

Newly synthesized polypeptides emerge from the ribosome with exposed hydrophobic surfaces that risk misfolding and aggregation. Chaperones bind these surfaces, shield them, and — in the case of the barrel-shaped chaperonins such as GroEL in bacteria and TRiC/CCT in eukaryotes — provide an isolated chamber in which a single protein can fold, powered by ATP hydrolysis. Chaperones also refold proteins after stress, escort clients across membranes, regulate signalling proteins such as steroid hormone receptors (with Hsp90), and target terminally misfolded proteins for degradation.

Classes

Major families are named by molecular weight: Hsp40 (co-chaperones that deliver clients and stimulate ATPase activity), Hsp60 (chaperonins), Hsp70, Hsp90, Hsp100, and the small heat shock proteins, which hold denatured proteins in a refoldable state. Chaperone dysfunction is implicated in neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's, where protein aggregation is central, and in cancer, where Hsp90 stabilizes mutated oncoproteins — making chaperones drug targets.
