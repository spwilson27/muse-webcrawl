Income tax in the United Kingdom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: HMRC headquarters, London]

Income tax in the United Kingdom is the largest single source of government revenue, administered by HM Revenue and Customs (HMRC). Most employees pay through the Pay As You Earn (PAYE) system, in which employers deduct tax and National Insurance contributions before paying wages; others file Self Assessment returns.

History

Income tax was first introduced by William Pitt the Younger in 1799 to finance the war against revolutionary France, and, after lapsing, was reintroduced by Robert Peel in 1842 as a temporary measure. It has remained in force ever since; rates peaked in the late 1970s at 83 per cent on earned income (plus a surcharge on investment income) and were cut sharply in the 1980s, the top rate falling to 40 per cent by 1988.

Current structure

For most of the UK the main rates on earned income (2024/25, England, Wales and Northern Ireland) are:

The personal allowance is withdrawn at £1 for every £2 of income above £100,000. Different rates apply to savings and dividend income. Since 2016 Scotland has set its own rates and bands on non-savings income, with more bands and a higher top rate than the rest of the UK; Wales has had a limited power to vary rates. Thresholds have been frozen in nominal terms since 2021, producing substantial "fiscal drag".
