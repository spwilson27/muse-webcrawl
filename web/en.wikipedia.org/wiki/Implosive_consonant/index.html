Implosive consonant

Implosive consonants are a group of stop consonants produced with a glottalic ingressive airstream mechanism: the glottis moves downward while air is drawn into the mouth, so that the sound is made on a lowered-larynx, partially ingressive airflow. They are transcribed in the International Phonetic Alphabet with a rightward hook on the corresponding voiced stop symbol, as in [ɓ], [ɗ], [ʄ], [ɠ] and [ʛ].

Distribution

Implosives occur in roughly 13% of the world's languages. They are especially common in sub-Saharan African languages such as Sindhi (spoken in Pakistan and India) is a notable non-African example with a full series of implosives, as are Vietnamese and several languages of Southeast Asia and the Americas, including Mayan languages and languages of the Amazon.

Types

The most common implosive is the voiced bilabial [ɓ]; alveolar [ɗ] is also frequent, while palatal, velar and uvular implosives are rarer. Voiceless implosives are exceedingly rare and their existence is debated. Implosives contrast in Sindhi with plain aspirated, unaspirated and breathy-voiced stops, giving that language an unusually rich stop inventory.
