Biodegradable plastic

Biodegradable plastics are plastics that can be decomposed by the action of living organisms, usually microorganisms, into water, carbon dioxide (or methane under anaerobic conditions), and biomass. They are distinct from bioplastics made from renewable feedstocks — a plastic can be bio-based without being biodegradable, and a few biodegradable plastics are made from fossil fuels. Biodegradation claims are environment-specific: many materials certified as industrially compostable will not break down appreciably in soil, in home compost, or in the ocean.

Packaging marketed as biodegradable and compostable.

Types

The principal families include polylactic acid (PLA), fermented from plant starches and the most widely used compostable plastic, which typically requires industrial composting temperatures around 60 °C; polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs), produced directly by bacteria and among the few plastics that biodegrade in marine conditions; starch blends; and fossil-based but biodegradable polyesters such as PBAT and polycaprolactone. So-called "oxo-degradable" plastics, which fragment under light and oxygen, are increasingly restricted because they mainly produce microplastics rather than fully mineralizing.

Applications and standards

Typical uses are compost caddy liners, disposable cutlery and cups, food packaging, mulch films, and medical items such as absorbable sutures. Standards including EN 13432 in Europe and ASTM D6400 in the United States specify test conditions and time limits for certification, and labeling schemes attempt to distinguish "compostable" products from merely "biodegradable" ones — a term regulators in some jurisdictions restrict as unqualified marketing.

Environmental debate

Advocates argue biodegradable plastics reduce persistent plastic pollution and suit food-contaminated waste streams that cannot be recycled. Critics note that in landfills they may generate methane, that they can contaminate conventional plastic recycling, that littered items often persist far longer than consumers expect, and that land use for feedstock crops raises its own concerns. Their benefits therefore depend heavily on matching material, product, and local waste infrastructure.
