Anarchism and religion

Related topicsAnarchism, atheism, Christian anarchism, Jewish anarchism, religious socialism
Key figuresLeo Tolstoy, Peter Chelčický, Dorothy Day, Jacques Ellul

Anarchism and religion have had a complex and often fraught relationship throughout the history of the anarchist movement. Although many classical anarchists were strongly anti-clerical and opposed organised religion as a hierarchical institution allied with the state and capital, others have drawn on religious traditions — including Christianity, Judaism, Buddhism, and Islam — as sources of anarchist inspiration.

Anarchist views on religion

Mikhail Bakunin's posthumously published work God and the State (1882) argued that the idea of God was incompatible with human freedom, famously inverting Voltaire's aphorism: "If God really existed, it would be necessary to abolish him." Pierre-Joseph Proudhon, who coined the slogan "Property is theft!", regarded the Church as a pillar of the authority he opposed. Spanish anarchists of the early twentieth century were notably militant in their anticlericalism, and during the Spanish Civil War churches were burned and clergy attacked by some anarchist militias.

Religious anarchism

Despite this anti-religious strain, a religious current has existed within anarchism since its beginnings. Christian anarchism holds that anarchism is inherent in Christianity because only God, not earthly rulers, is owed ultimate allegiance. Peter Chelčický in the fifteenth century argued that the true Christian could not participate in government or violence. Leo Tolstoy's later writings, especially The Kingdom of God Is Within You (1894), deeply influenced Mahatma Gandhi and the Catholic Worker movement founded by Dorothy Day and Peter Maurin.

Jewish anarchism flourished among Yiddish-speaking immigrants in London and New York, with figures such as Rudolf Rocker (a non-Jewish organiser of Jewish anarchist unions) and later secular and religious currents, including the thought of Martin Buber and Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook's mystical anarchist tendencies. Buddhist anarchism has been articulated by figures including Gary Snyder, and Muslim anarchist currents have been discussed by writers such as Peter Lamborn Wilson (Hakim Bey).
