Hernia

A hernia is the abnormal exit of tissue or an organ, such as the bowel, through the wall of the cavity in which it normally resides. Various types of hernias can occur, most commonly involving the abdomen, and specifically the groin. Groin hernias are most commonly inguinal hernias but may also be femoral hernias. Other types include hiatus hernias, incisional hernias and umbilical hernias.

Signs and symptoms

Hernias often present as a visible or palpable bulge that may enlarge with coughing or straining. Symptoms, which occur in about two-thirds of people, include pain or discomfort — particularly during bending, lifting or coughing — and a sensation of heaviness or dragging. A hernia whose contents cannot be pushed back is termed irreducible or incarcerated; if the blood supply to the trapped tissue is cut off, the hernia is strangulated, a surgical emergency that can lead to gangrene of the bowel.

Causes and types

Risk factors include male sex, advancing age, family history, chronic cough, constipation, heavy lifting, smoking and connective-tissue disorders. Inguinal hernias account for roughly three quarters of abdominal-wall hernias and are far more common in men; femoral hernias, though rarer, are more likely to strangulate and occur more often in women.

Treatment

Definitive treatment is surgical repair (herniorrhaphy), either by open surgery or laparoscopically, frequently using a synthetic mesh to reinforce the wall. Small, asymptomatic hernias in men may be managed by watchful waiting, but most eventually come to repair. About 27% of men and 3% of women will undergo inguinal hernia repair at some point in their lives.
