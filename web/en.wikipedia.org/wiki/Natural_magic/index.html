Natural magic
EraRenaissance
Key figuresMarsilio Ficino, Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa, Giambattista della Porta

Natural magic (Latin: magia naturalis) was a philosophical and proto-scientific tradition of the Renaissance that sought to understand and manipulate the hidden or "occult" powers of nature. Its practitioners distinguished natural magic, which worked through the inherent virtues of natural things such as herbs, stones, and celestial influences, from demonic magic, which depended on the invocation of spirits and was condemned by the Church.

History

The concept drew on ancient sources including Pliny the Elder's Natural History and medieval works on the properties of things. Marsilio Ficino's De vita libri tres (1489) presented a system of astral medicine and talismanic magic grounded in Neoplatonism. Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa's Three Books of Occult Philosophy (1533) synthesised natural, celestial, and ceremonial magic, while Giambattista della Porta's Magia Naturalis (1558, expanded 1589) presented experiments in optics, magnetism, distillation, and other phenomena in terms accessible to a broad readership.

Relation to early science

Historians of science have debated the role of natural magic in the Scientific Revolution. Its emphasis on experiment, manipulation, and the search for hidden causes influenced figures such as Francis Bacon, who retained the term "natural magic" for the practical application of natural knowledge. As mechanical philosophy displaced theories of occult qualities during the seventeenth century, natural magic gradually dissolved into experimental science, technology, and entertainment.
