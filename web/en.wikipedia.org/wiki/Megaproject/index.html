Megaproject

A megaproject is an extremely large-scale, complex and transformative investment project, typically costing US$1 billion or more, taking many years to develop and build, involving multiple public and private stakeholders, and having a major impact on communities, economies and the environment. Examples include large dams, bridges and tunnels, airports, seaports, motorway and rail networks, oil and gas extraction complexes, and major urban developments.

Characteristics

According to researcher Bent Flyvbjerg, megaprojects are distinguished not only by their size but by their complexity, long planning horizons, contested politics, and their tendency to be "monuments" promoted for reasons of prestige and symbolism as much as economic return. They are often delivered by temporary multi-organisational ventures and financed through complex arrangements such as public–private partnerships.

The "iron law" of megaprojects

Empirical studies of large project datasets have found that megaprojects are systematically prone to cost overruns, schedule delays and benefit shortfalls — a pattern Flyvbjerg summarises as the "iron law of megaprojects: over budget, over time, under benefits, over and over again". Common explanations include technical difficulties, changes in scope, optimism bias, and strategic misrepresentation by promoters competing for funding. A proposed remedy is reference class forecasting, which anchors estimates in the outcomes of comparable completed projects.

Examples

Famous megaprojects include the Channel Tunnel, China's Three Gorges Dam and high-speed rail network, the Big Dig in Boston, the Panama and Suez canal expansions, London's Crossrail, and large airport projects such as Beijing Daxing International Airport. Planned or proposed megaprojects include high-speed rail in California, the NEOM development in Saudi Arabia, and various transcontinental tunnel and bridge schemes.

Criticism

Critics argue that megaprojects frequently displace communities, damage ecosystems, and divert resources from smaller, higher-return investments; supporters counter that they can deliver lasting productivity gains, national integration and technological capability.
