Lyricist

A lyricist or lyrist is a songwriter who writes lyrics — words for songs — as opposed to a composer or a singer-songwriter who usually composes the song's melody as well. A lyricist collaborates with regular commercial songwriters, pop stars, and producers. There have been many famed lyricist–composer partnerships in musical theatre and popular music, such as Rodgers and Hammerstein, Lerner and Loewe, George and Ira Gershwin, Gilbert and Sullivan, and Elton John and Bernie Taupin.

Roles and distinctions

A lyricist's contribution is the sung text: its rhyme, metre, imagery, and fit to the melody's phrasing. In musical theatre, the lyricist may also write the libretto (book) or share the role with the composer; in opera, the corresponding writer is the librettist. Singer-songwriters who write their own words are typically not described as lyricists alone. In hip hop, lyricism is a term of art for verbal skill in writing and performing rhymes (the MC as lyricist), judged for wordplay, flow, and storytelling.

Craft

Lyric writing balances singability (open vowels on sustained notes, natural stress patterns) with narrative and emotional content. Lyricists adapt techniques from poetry — internal rhyme, refrain, verse–chorus structure — but the words must work when heard once, in real time, and in harmony with melody and arrangement. Many lyricists, such as Stephen Sondheim, have written extensively about the discipline's constraints ("content dictates form").

Royalties

In the music industry, lyricists share in publishing royalties with composers, traditionally on a 50/50 split between words and music on a song's copyright, administered through performing rights organisations such as ASCAP, BMI, and PRS for Music.
