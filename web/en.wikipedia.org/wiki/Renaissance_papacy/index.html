The Renaissance papacy is a period of papal history between the Western Schism and the Reformation, roughly from the election of Pope Martin V in 1417 to the Sack of Rome in 1527 and the death of Clement VII in 1534. During this time the popes re-established Rome as their capital, rebuilt the Papal States as an Italian territorial power, and became the greatest patrons of Renaissance art and architecture — while also acquiring a reputation for nepotism, simony, political intrigue and moral corruption that helped provoke the Reformation.

Restoration of Rome

After the Avignon Papacy and the Western Schism, Martin V (r. 1417–1431) returned the papacy to Rome and began its physical restoration. Nicholas V (r. 1447–1455), a humanist scholar, refounded the Vatican Library, planned the rebuilding of St Peter's, and made Rome a centre of Renaissance learning, effectively founding the Renaissance papal court.

The "warrior popes" and dynastic politics

From the later 15th century, popes increasingly behaved as Italian princes. Sixtus IV (r. 1471–1484), who built the Sistine Chapel, enriched his della Rovere nephews and was implicated in the Pazzi conspiracy against the Medici. Alexander VI (r. 1492–1503), the Borgia pope, used the office to advance his children, including Cesare and Lucrezia Borgia, and became the era's byword for corruption. Julius II (r. 1503–1513), the "warrior pope," personally led armies to recover the Papal States, commissioned Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel ceiling and Raphael's Stanze, and laid the foundation stone of the new St Peter's in 1506. Leo X (r. 1513–1521), a Medici, made Rome the glittering capital of the High Renaissance while depleting the treasury; the sale of indulgences to finance St Peter's helped trigger Martin Luther's protest in 1517.

Collapse and aftermath

The Sack of Rome by imperial troops in 1527 shattered the Renaissance papal court. Clement VII (r. 1523–1534) was unable to check either the spread of Protestantism or Henry VIII's break with Rome. The papacy that emerged under Paul III, who convoked the Council of Trent, belonged to the different world of the Catholic Reformation.

Patronage

For all their scandals, the Renaissance popes commissioned much of the era's defining art: Botticelli, Perugino and Ghirlandaio in the Sistine Chapel; Bramante's Tempietto and the plan of St Peter's; Michelangelo's ceiling, Pietà and (later) Last Judgment; Raphael's frescoes in the Vatican apartments; and the humanist scholarship of the Vatican Library. Historians continue to debate how to weigh this cultural legacy against the fiscalism and nepotism that financed it.
