Multisensory integration

Multisensory integration, also known as multimodal integration, is the process by which information from different sensory modalities — such as sight, sound, touch, smell, and the vestibular sense — is combined by the nervous system to form a coherent perception of the world and to guide behavior.

Principles

Classic studies, many conducted on neurons in the cat and primate superior colliculus, identified several principles governing multisensory integration. The spatial principle states that signals arising from the same location are more likely to be integrated; the temporal principle holds that signals occurring close together in time are more likely to be integrated; and the principle of inverse effectiveness holds that the proportional benefit of combining signals is greatest when the individual signals are weakest.

Behavioral effects

Multisensory integration underlies well-known phenomena including the McGurk effect, in which visual speech information alters what a listener hears; the ventriloquism effect, in which sound is mislocalized toward a visual stimulus; and the rubber hand illusion, in which synchronized visual and tactile input alters the sense of body ownership. Combining redundant sensory cues can speed reaction times and improve detection and localization beyond what either sense achieves alone.

Neural basis

Multisensory neurons have been found in structures including the superior colliculus, superior temporal sulcus, intraparietal cortex, and many other areas once thought to be purely unimodal. Development of normal multisensory integration depends on experience during early life.
