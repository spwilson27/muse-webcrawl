The Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS) is a flight control software system developed by Boeing for the Boeing 737 MAX airliner. Its purpose was to make the MAX handle like earlier 737 models: because the MAX's larger, more forward-mounted engines could produce an increased nose-up pitch tendency at high angles of attack, MCAS automatically commands nose-down horizontal stabiliser trim when it senses an abnormally high angle of attack, enhancing pitch stability characteristics to comply with certification requirements.

Background

The 737 MAX mounted larger CFM LEAP-1B engines further forward and higher than on the previous 737NG. At high angles of attack the engine nacelles generate lift ahead of the centre of gravity, lightening stick forces in a way that did not meet certification standards for stick force gradient. Rather than redesign the airframe, Boeing introduced MCAS, a function within the speed trim system, so the MAX would feel and behave like the NG — preserving a common type rating that was a major selling point to airlines.

Design (original)

As originally certified, MCAS read data from a single angle-of-attack (AoA) sensor at a time. If that sensor reported a high angle of attack with flaps up and autopilot off, MCAS would trim the stabiliser nose-down for up to about 9.26 seconds, pause, and — if the condition persisted — trim again, potentially repeatedly and with authority of up to 2.5 degrees. Crucially, MCAS was not described in the flight crew operations manual, and most pilots were unaware of its existence before the first crash.

Crashes and grounding

On 29 October 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea after erroneous data from a faulty AoA sensor repeatedly triggered MCAS, killing all 189 people aboard. On 10 March 2019, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 crashed in similar circumstances, killing all 157 aboard. The worldwide 737 MAX fleet was grounded from March 2019; investigations criticised Boeing's design assumptions, its failure to disclose MCAS, and the FAA's delegated certification process. Boeing agreed to pay billions in compensation and penalties, and its CEO was forced out.

Redesign and return to service

Boeing redesigned MCAS to compare inputs from both AoA sensors, activate only once per high-AoA event, limit its stabiliser authority so pilots could always override it with elevator control, and disagree-alert crews to sensor faults. Pilot training was also overhauled to include simulator sessions. The FAA rescinded the grounding in November 2020, and other regulators followed; MCAS documentation is now included in crew manuals.
