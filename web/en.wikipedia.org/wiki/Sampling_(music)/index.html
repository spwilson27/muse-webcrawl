Sampling (music)

Sampling in music is the technique of reusing a portion (a "sample") of a sound recording in another song or piece. Samples may include rhythm, melody, speech or sound effects, and are often manipulated with samplers, turntables and digital audio workstations. Sampling is foundational to hip hop and electronic music.

History

Precursors include musique concrète in the 1940s, tape loops and the Mellotron. In 1970s–80s New York, hip hop DJs such as Kool Herc and Grandmaster Flash looped breakbeats from funk records; samplers such as the Fairlight CMI, E-mu SP-1200 and Akai MPC made studio sampling accessible. Landmark sample-based works include Public Enemy's dense collages, De La Soul's 3 Feet High and Rising, DJ Shadow's Endtroducing..... (1996) and the Avalanches' Since I Left You. The "Amen break" from the Winstons' "Amen, Brother" (1969) became the most sampled drum break in history.

Legal issues

Uncleared sampling generated landmark litigation: Grand Upright Music v. Warner Bros. (1991, Biz Markie) ended the era of casual uncleared sampling, and Bridgeport Music v. Dimension Films (2005) held for sound recordings that one must "get a license or do not sample". A 2016 Ninth Circuit decision in the Madonna "Vogue" case allowed minimal de minimis copying, creating a circuit split.

Practice

Producers "chop", pitch-shift, time-stretch and filter samples, clear them through publishers and labels, or use royalty-free sample packs and interpolation (re-recording a passage) to reduce costs.
