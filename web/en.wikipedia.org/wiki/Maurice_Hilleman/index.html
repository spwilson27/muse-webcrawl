Maurice Ralph Hilleman (30 August 1919 – 11 April 2005) was an American microbiologist who developed more than 40 vaccines, an unparalleled record of productivity. Of the 14 vaccines routinely recommended in American vaccine schedules, he developed eight, including those for measles, mumps, hepatitis A, hepatitis B, meningitis, pneumonia, and Haemophilus influenzae bacteria. He is often described as the most successful vaccinologist in history, credited with saving more lives than any other medical scientist of the 20th century.

Career

Born in Miles City, Montana, Hilleman earned his doctorate in microbiology from the University of Chicago in 1944. At the Walter Reed Army Institute of Research he identified the role of antigenic shift and drift in influenza pandemics and, in 1957, recognised the approaching Asian flu pandemic early, organising production of 40 million vaccine doses. Joining Merck in 1957, he developed the measles vaccine from a strain isolated from David Edmonston and the mumps vaccine (1967) from virus taken from his daughter Jeryl Lynn, whose name the strain still bears. He combined measles, mumps, and rubella vaccines into the MMR shot (1971).

Later achievements and recognition

Hilleman's team created the first licensed hepatitis B vaccine (1981), initially derived from human blood plasma and later the first recombinant vaccine made with genetically engineered yeast. Despite his contributions, he remained relatively unknown to the public; he received the National Medal of Science in 1988 and a special lifetime achievement award from the World Health Organization.
