Farmer field school

Farmers learn through field observation and experimentation in a farmer field school.

A farmer field school (FFS) is a group-based adult learning approach that teaches farmers agroecological and pest-management skills through hands-on, season-long experimentation in the field. The approach was developed by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) of the United Nations in Indonesia in 1989, initially to promote integrated pest management (IPM) in rice production.

Method

A typical field school brings together about 25 farmers who meet weekly throughout a growing season in a shared learning plot. Participants observe the crop ecosystem, count pests and beneficial insects, run simple experiments — such as comparing plots with and without insecticide — and draw conclusions collectively, guided by a trained facilitator rather than a lecturer. The method rests on four principles: grow a healthy crop, observe the field regularly, conserve natural enemies of pests, and become experts in one's own field.

Spread and impact

From Indonesia, farmer field schools spread across Asia, Africa, Latin America, and Eastern Europe, and the curriculum expanded beyond rice IPM to livestock, soil health, water management, climate adaptation, forestry, and even health and literacy topics. Evaluations have found that graduates often reduce pesticide use while maintaining or improving yields, though the wider diffusion of knowledge to non-participants has been debated.

Variants

Related models include junior farmer field and life schools for young people, pastoralist field schools, and climate field schools that incorporate weather and climate information into farm decision-making.
