Walter Jackson Freeman II

Walter Jackson Freeman II (November 14, 1895 – May 31, 1972) was an American physician who specialized in lobotomy. He was a member of the American Psychiatric Association and was the chairperson of neurology at George Washington University. Freeman is credited with popularizing the lobotomy in the United States and performing it on thousands of patients, including, in some cases, mentally ill children as young as four.

Lobotomy career

Inspired by the work of Portuguese neurologist Egas Moniz, Freeman performed the first prefrontal lobotomy in the United States in 1936 with neurosurgeon James Watts. He later developed the transorbital lobotomy, an outpatient procedure performed through the eye socket with an instrument nicknamed the "ice pick". Freeman personally performed roughly 3,500 lobotomies and promoted the procedure across the country until his operating privileges were revoked in 1967 after a patient's death.

Legacy

Freeman's zeal and disregard for patient outcomes have made him a controversial figure; the lobotomy fell into disrepute with the advent of antipsychotic medication and growing ethical criticism. His patients included Rosemary Kennedy, sister of President John F. Kennedy, whose unsuccessful lobotomy left her permanently incapacitated.
