Social medicine

Social medicine studies how social and economic conditions shape health, disease, and health care.

Social medicine is a field of medicine and social science that examines the interaction between society and health: how social and economic conditions — poverty, working conditions, housing, education, inequality, and political structures — determine the distribution of disease and access to care, and how health interventions must address those conditions to be effective. Its central dictum, attributed to Rudolf Virchow, is that "medicine is a social science, and politics nothing but medicine at a larger scale."

History

The field coalesced in 19th-century Europe. In 1848 the German pathologist Rudolf Virchow investigated a typhus epidemic in Upper Silesia and concluded that its causes were social and economic — malnutrition, poor housing, and political disenfranchisement — prescribing democracy, education, and employment rather than purely medical measures. In France, Jules Guérin coined the term médecine sociale in 1848, and in England reformers such as Edwin Chadwick linked disease to sanitation and urban poverty.

In the 20th century, social medicine developed distinct traditions: academic departments in Europe; in Latin America, a politically engaged medicina social associated with figures such as Salvador Allende in Chile; and in North America, community-oriented primary care pioneered by Sidney Kark in South Africa and at Pholela. Later figures include Paul Farmer, whose organization Partners In Health applied social-medicine principles to the treatment of tuberculosis and HIV in impoverished settings, insisting that effective care requires addressing material deprivation.

Key concepts

Social medicine overlaps with, and helped generate, the study of the social determinants of health, health equity, structural violence, and biosocial analysis. It differs from public health in its explicit focus on the political economy of health and from medical sociology in its orientation toward clinical practice and intervention. Contemporary social medicine informs medical education, global health, and advocacy for universal health coverage.
