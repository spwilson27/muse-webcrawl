The Extended Phenotype: The Gene as the Unit of Selection is a 1982 book by evolutionary biologist Richard Dawkins, presenting a refinement and extension of the gene-centered view of evolution introduced in his earlier work The Selfish Gene (1976). Dawkins has said he considers it his principal contribution to evolutionary theory.

The concept

The conventional phenotype comprises the observable traits an organism's genes produce within its own body. Dawkins argued that the phenotypic effects of a gene need not be confined to the organism's body: they can extend into the environment and into other organisms. Classic examples include the dam built by a beaver (an effect of beaver genes on the world), the webs and cases built by spiders and caddisfly larvae, and the manipulated behavior of hosts by parasites — such as flukes that make snails expose themselves to predators so the parasite can complete its life cycle.

Argument

The book develops the concept of the replicator — entities, such as genes, that make copies of themselves — and argues that natural selection acts on replicators through their phenotypic effects, wherever those effects are manifest. Dawkins challenged the privileging of the individual organism as the unit of selection, suggesting instead a "necker's cube" shift in perspective in which the organism is itself a "survival machine" built by genes, and its artifacts and influences are equally legitimate adaptations. He also introduced formal notions such as the "arms race" between lineages and extended phenotype effects at a distance ("action at a distance" through nervous systems, as in human artifacts).

Reception

Aimed at a professional rather than popular audience, the book influenced research programs in behavioral ecology, parasitology, and evolutionary theory. Its ideas underlie later concepts such as niche construction and the study of host manipulation by parasites. A revised edition with an afterword by Daniel Dennett appeared in 1999.
