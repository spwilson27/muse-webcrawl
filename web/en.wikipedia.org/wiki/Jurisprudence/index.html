Jurisprudence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jurisprudence is the theory and philosophy of law — the study of what law is, what it ought to be, and how it relates to morality, politics and society. Legal scholars explore the nature of law and legal reasoning, the concepts of rights, duties, justice and responsibility, and the practices of legal institutions. The terms jurisprudence and philosophy of law are largely synonymous in contemporary usage.

Schools

Natural law theory (Aquinas, Finnis) holds that law's validity depends in part on its moral content — an unjust law is a defective law. Legal positivism (Bentham, Austin, Hart, Kelsen, Raz) separates law's existence from its merits: law is law because it is posited by recognised social sources (Hart's "rule of recognition"); Hart's The Concept of Law (1961) anchors modern debate, with Ronald Dworkin's interpretivism attacking positivism from within liberal legalism. Legal realism (Holmes, Llewellyn) insisted law is what officials do, shifting attention to decision-making; critical legal studies, feminist jurisprudence, law and economics, and comparative and sociological approaches extended the field in the 20th century. Applied jurisprudence informs judging itself — originalism and proportionality are jurisprudential positions in action.
