Ptolemaic Kingdom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Pharos lighthouse and harbour of Alexandria as reconstructed]

The Ptolemaic Kingdom (305–30 BC) was a Hellenistic state based in Egypt, founded by Ptolemy I Soter, one of Alexander the Great's generals and companions, and ruled by his dynasty until the death of Cleopatra VII. From Alexandria — the greatest city of the Hellenistic world — the Ptolemies governed Egypt as pharaohs to their Egyptian subjects and as Greek kings to their Macedonian and Greek ruling class, exploiting the Nile valley's grain wealth to sustain a major Mediterranean power, a famous library and court, and a navy that long contested the eastern Mediterranean.

Rule and culture

Ptolemy I secured Alexander's body for burial in Alexandria and made the city a centre of learning: the Museum and Great Library, the Pharos lighthouse, and scholars such as Euclid and Eratosthenes. The dynasty ruled through a centralised Greek-language bureaucracy, state monopolies and temple patronage (building the great temples of Edfu, Dendera and Philae), while the kings married their sisters in pharaonic fashion. The Rosetta Stone — a priestly decree of Ptolemy V in three scripts — later unlocked the decipherment of hieroglyphs.

Decline and fall

Later Ptolemies were weakened by dynastic feuds, native revolts and growing Roman pressure; Egypt became a Roman client in all but name. Cleopatra VII, the dynasty's last and ablest ruler, allied with Julius Caesar and then Mark Antony; defeated by Octavian at Actium (31 BC), she and Antony died in 30 BC and Egypt became a Roman province — the last Hellenistic kingdom to fall.
