Cray-2

Cray-2
Manufacturer: Cray Research
Released: 1985
Processors: up to 4
Peak performance: approx. 1.9 GFLOPS
Memory: up to 256 megawords (2 GB)

The Cray-2 was a supercomputer manufactured by Cray Research beginning in 1985, and the successor to the company's celebrated Cray-1 and Cray X-MP. Designed by Seymour Cray, it was the fastest computer in the world on its release, with a peak performance of about 1.9 gigaFLOPS, a record it held until 1990.

Design

To shorten signal paths and increase speed, the Cray-2 packed its circuits far more densely than earlier machines, using three-dimensional module stacking. The resulting heat could not be removed by air cooling, so the computer was immersed in a bath of Fluorinert, an inert fluorocarbon liquid, which was circulated through the cabinet; on some installations the warmed liquid was displayed in a small fountain, nicknamed "Bubbles". The machine had a compact cylindrical cabinet about 45 inches (1.1 m) in diameter and height, up to four processors, and what was then an exceptionally large main memory — up to 256 megawords (2 gigabytes) — far exceeding that of its predecessors.

Use

Cray-2 systems were installed at United States government laboratories and agencies, including NASA Ames Research Center, the Lawrence Livermore and Los Alamos national laboratories, and defence organisations, as well as at a small number of universities and industrial sites. They were applied to fluid dynamics, weather and climate modelling, structural analysis, and classified defence work. About two dozen systems were built.

Legacy

Although its sales were limited by its cost and by the long development period that allowed competing vector machines to close the performance gap, the Cray-2's liquid-immersion cooling and its enormous memory influenced later supercomputer design. The line was succeeded by the Cray Y-MP and Cray C90 series.
