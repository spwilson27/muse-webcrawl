Movie camera

A movie camera (also known as a film camera or cine-camera) is a type of photographic camera that quickly takes a sequence of photographs on an image sensor or onto film stock. In contrast to a still camera, which captures a single snapshot at a time, a movie camera repeatedly captures images at a fixed rate — traditionally 24 frames per second for sound film — which, when projected or played back in sequence, create the illusion of motion.

History

Experiments in chronophotography by Eadweard Muybridge and Étienne-Jules Marey in the 1870s and 1880s laid the groundwork for motion picture cameras. Thomas Edison's laboratory, principally William Dickson, developed the Kinetograph in the early 1890s, while the Lumière brothers' Cinématographe (1895) combined camera, printer and projector in one portable device. Through the 20th century, manufacturers such as Bell & Howell, Mitchell, Panavision, Arriflex and Moviecam produced the professional 35 mm and 16 mm cameras used by the film industry.

Mechanism

A traditional film camera advances the film intermittently through the gate: a claw engages the film's perforations and pulls it down one frame while the shutter is closed, then the film is held still by registration pins while a rotating shutter exposes it. Film is supplied from a magazine, and the standard sound speed is 24 frames per second, although cameras can be "overcranked" or "undercranked" for slow or fast motion. A reflex viewfinder, using a mirrored shutter, allows the operator to see the exact image through the taking lens.

Digital cinema cameras

Since the early 2000s, digital movie cameras using large CMOS sensors — such as those from Arri (Alexa), Red Digital Cinema and Sony — have largely replaced film cameras in professional production, recording to solid-state media at resolutions of 4K and above. Film cameras nevertheless remain in use by some directors and for archival and aesthetic reasons.
