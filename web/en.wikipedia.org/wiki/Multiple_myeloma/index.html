Multiple myeloma (MM), also known as plasma cell myeloma and simply myeloma, is a cancer of plasma cells, a type of white blood cell that normally produces antibodies. Often, no symptoms are noticed initially. As it progresses, bone pain, anemia, kidney dysfunction, and infections may occur. Complications may include amyloidosis.

Signs and symptoms

The classic features of multiple myeloma are often summarised by the mnemonic CRAB: hyperCalcemia, Renal insufficiency, Anemia, and Bone lesions. Clonal plasma cells proliferate in the bone marrow, producing large quantities of a single type of immunoglobulin (the monoclonal or M protein) and suppressing normal blood cell production. Bone lesions arise because myeloma cells stimulate osteoclasts and suppress osteoblasts, causing "punched-out" lytic lesions, bone pain, and pathological fractures, particularly in the spine, skull, ribs, and pelvis.

Causes and pathophysiology

The cause of multiple myeloma is unknown. Risk factors include obesity, radiation exposure, family history, and certain infections. The underlying mechanism involves the abnormal proliferation of plasma cells in the bone marrow. Multiple myeloma almost always evolves from an asymptomatic premalignant stage termed monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), which progresses to myeloma at a rate of about 1% per year. Genetic abnormalities such as translocations involving the immunoglobulin heavy chain locus and hyperdiploidy are common early events.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is typically made by blood or urine tests finding abnormal antibodies (serum protein electrophoresis and free light chain assay), bone marrow biopsy finding cancerous plasma cells (≥10% clonal plasma cells), and medical imaging finding bone lesions. Serum calcium and creatinine are measured to assess end-organ damage. The International Myeloma Working Group criteria distinguish symptomatic myeloma from smoldering (asymptomatic) myeloma and MGUS.

Treatment

Multiple myeloma is considered treatable but generally incurable. Treatment for eligible patients often begins with combination therapy including a proteasome inhibitor (such as bortezomib), an immunomodulatory drug (such as lenalidomide), and dexamethasone, frequently combined with the anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody daratumumab, followed by high-dose chemotherapy with autologous stem cell transplantation in suitable patients. Bisphosphonates are given to reduce skeletal events, and supportive care addresses anemia, infection risk, and renal impairment. Newer options include CAR T-cell therapies directed at BCMA.

Prognosis and epidemiology

In 2020 there were an estimated 176,000 new cases globally. Multiple myeloma affects slightly more men than women and is roughly twice as common in people of African ancestry as in those of European ancestry. The median age at diagnosis is around 69 years. Five-year survival has improved substantially with modern therapies, exceeding 55% in the United States, though outcomes vary widely by stage and cytogenetic risk.
