Han Yu

Born768, Nanyang, Tang China
Died824 (aged 55–56), Chang'an
OccupationEssayist, poet, philosopher, official
Known forLeader of the Classical Prose Movement; Confucian revival

Han Yu (Chinese: 韓愈; 768–824), courtesy name Tuizhi (退之), was a Chinese essayist, poet, and philosopher of the Tang dynasty. He is regarded as one of the greatest prose writers in Chinese history and was the leading figure of the Classical Prose Movement (guwen), which sought to restore the direct, unadorned style of the ancients against the ornate parallel prose then fashionable. In the Song dynasty he came to be venerated as a forerunner of Neo-Confucianism.

Life

Han Yu was born in Nanyang in present-day Henan and was orphaned young, being raised by an elder brother and sister-in-law. After repeated failures he passed the jinshi examination in 792 and eventually obtained posts in the capital, Chang'an. His career was repeatedly interrupted by demotions provoked by his outspoken memorials.

His most famous act of defiance came in 819, when he submitted a memorial protesting the emperor Xianzong's veneration of a relic said to be the Buddha's finger bone, denouncing Buddhism as a foreign cult harmful to the state. He was exiled to Chaozhou in the far south, an episode remembered in Chinese culture as the archetype of the loyal official punished for honest remonstrance. He was later recalled and died in the capital in 824.

Thought

Han Yu argued for a revival of Confucianism at a time when Buddhism and Daoism dominated intellectual life. In essays such as "On the Origin of the Way" (Yuandao), he traced a transmission of the Confucian Way from the sage kings through Confucius and Mencius and argued that it had been lost after Mencius — a lineage claim later adopted by Neo-Confucian thinkers. He attacked Buddhism and Daoism as socially and economically destructive, while insisting that Confucian ethics, not metaphysical speculation, were the heart of the teaching.

Literary work

Han Yu was canonised as the first of the "Eight Masters of the Tang and Song" prose tradition. His essays range from philosophical treatises to the celebrated "Memorial on the Buddha Bone", biographical epitaphs, and the moving lament for his twelve-year-old daughter. His poetry, written in deliberate defiance of smooth convention, influenced later poets including Su Shi.

Legacy

The river and bridges of Chaozhou, where he spent his exile, were renamed in his honour — the Han River (Han Jiang) still bears his surname. Han Yu remains a central figure in the Chinese literary canon, and his dictum that prose should "reject old phrases" became a motto for stylistic reformers.
