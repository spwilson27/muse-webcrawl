Compromise of 1790

The Compromise of 1790 was a compromise between Alexander Hamilton, Secretary of the Treasury, and Thomas Jefferson, Secretary of State, and James Madison, in which Hamilton won the decision for the federal government to take over and pay the state debts (assumption), and Jefferson and Madison obtained the national capital, called the District of Columbia, located in the South along the Potomac River. The compromise made possible passage of the Residence Act and Funding Act of 1790.

Background

Hamilton's financial programme proposed that the federal government fund the national debt and assume the remaining war debts of the states, consolidating them into federal obligations. Southern states, several of which had already paid much of their debt, objected that assumption would penalise them and strengthen federal power; the assumption bill was defeated in the House of Representatives in the spring of 1790. At the same time, Congress was deadlocked over the location of the permanent national capital, with Northern, Southern, and middle-state candidates blocking each other.

The bargain

According to Jefferson's later account, he encountered a distressed Hamilton outside President Washington's office in New York in June 1790 and hosted a dinner at his lodgings at which Hamilton and Madison reached an understanding: Madison would not actively oppose assumption and would help secure enough votes (particularly from Virginia and Maryland members, with adjustments for states' accounts), while Hamilton would support placing the permanent capital on the Potomac, with Philadelphia serving as temporary capital for ten years. Historians note the deal was implemented through several congressional votes rather than a single formal agreement.

Legislation

The Residence Act, signed on 16 July 1790, authorised a district not more than ten miles square on the Potomac as the permanent seat of government from 1800, with Philadelphia the temporary capital. The Funding Act of August 1790 included the assumption of state debts, subject to a ceiling, and provided for settling accounts between the states and the Union.

Significance

The compromise settled two of the most divisive questions facing the new republic, stabilised federal credit, and fixed the capital's location in the South — a decision whose consequences (including the eventual site of Washington, D.C.) endure. It also exemplified the sectional bargaining that characterised early American politics and the growing rivalry between Hamilton's and Jefferson's visions of the federal government.
