Mach bands

Mach bands is an optical illusion named after the physicist Ernst Mach. It exaggerates the contrast between edges of slightly differing shades of gray, as soon as they come in contact with one another, by triggering edge-detection mechanisms in the human visual system. Along a boundary between a uniformly dark area and a uniformly light area, observers perceive a particularly dark band on the dark side and a particularly bright band on the light side, although both areas are in fact uniform.

Explanation

The effect is explained by lateral inhibition in the retina: photoreceptors and retinal ganglion cells respond not only to their own stimulation but are inhibited by their neighbours. At a luminance edge, cells on the bright side near the edge are less inhibited than cells deep within the bright region, so their response overshoots; the reverse happens on the dark side. The result is an apparent bright "overshoot" band and dark "undershoot" band flanking the edge — contrast enhancement performed by the visual system itself, not present in the physical stimulus.

Mach described the phenomenon in 1865. It is related to, but distinct from, the Cornsweet illusion and simultaneous contrast effects.

Applications

The Mach band effect matters in radiology, where it can create apparent lines along the edges of teeth and bones, potentially mimicking fractures or caries; radiologists are trained to discount it. It also illustrates why uniform gradients in computer graphics can appear banded, and why edge-enhancement (unsharp masking) sharpens images.
