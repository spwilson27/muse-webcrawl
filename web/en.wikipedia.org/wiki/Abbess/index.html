Abbess

An abbess (Latin: abbatissa) is the female superior of a community of Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican or Lutheran nuns, corresponding to the male title of abbot. To hold the office, an abbess is generally required to be at least 40 years old, to have been professed for at least ten years, and to be elected by the secret ballot of her community; she is solemnly blessed by the diocesan bishop or another abbot.

Role and authority

The abbess governs her abbey as its spiritual mother and temporal administrator, overseeing the community's prayer, work, property and discipline. Historically her insignia are the ring and the crosier (pastoral staff). In the Middle Ages some abbesses held extraordinary authority: the abbesses of great imperial abbeys such as Quedlinburg, Gandersheim and Essen in Germany sat in the Imperial Diet as princesses of the Empire, while the Abbess of Las Huelgas in Spain and the Abbess of Conversano in Italy exercised near-episcopal jurisdiction (nullius dioecesis) over surrounding parishes.

History

The office dates to the earliest organised female monasticism of the 4th–6th centuries; St Benedict's sister Scholastica is regarded as the first Benedictine abbess. In Anglo-Saxon England, royal abbesses such as Hild of Whitby presided over double monasteries of men and women and hosted the Synod of Whitby (664). Bridget of Kildare in Ireland and Radegund of Poitiers in Gaul are other prominent early abbesses. In the Eastern Orthodox Church the equivalent title is hegumenia (abbess of a monastery) or the superior styled gerontissa (eldress).

Since the Second Vatican Council the authority of abbesses in the Catholic Church has been clarified as domestic and maternal rather than sacramental: an abbess cannot ordain or exercise the power of orders, but within her monastery she holds a governance analogous to that of an abbot over monks.
