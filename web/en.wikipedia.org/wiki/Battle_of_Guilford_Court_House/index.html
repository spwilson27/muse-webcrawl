The Battle of Guilford Court House was fought on 15 March 1781 in present-day Greensboro, North Carolina, during the American Revolutionary War. Major General Nathanael Greene's American army met the smaller British force of Lieutenant General Charles Cornwallis after the exhausting "Race to the Dan." The British held the field but suffered losses they could not afford, and the battle is regarded as a decisive strategic blow to the British southern campaign.

Background

After the American victory at Cowpens destroyed Tarleton's command, Cornwallis burned his baggage train and pursued Greene across North Carolina. Greene escaped across the Dan River into Virginia, received reinforcements, and recrossed into North Carolina to offer battle near Guilford Court House with roughly 4,400 men, many of them militia, against about 2,100 British and German regulars.

The battle

Greene deployed in three lines modelled on Morgan's arrangement at Cowpens: North Carolina militia behind a rail fence, Virginia militia in the woods, and his Continental regulars on high ground at the rear. The British fought their way through each line in succession in broken, wooded terrain. In the climactic fighting around the third line, Cornwallis controversially ordered his artillery to fire grapeshot into a confused melee of British and American troops. The Americans withdrew in good order, leaving the British in possession of the field.

Outcome

British casualties were about a quarter of Cornwallis's force — over 500 killed and wounded — while American losses were lighter in proportion and included many militia who simply went home. Cornwallis, unable to pursue or to supply his army in the interior, fell back to Wilmington and then marched into Virginia, where his campaign ended at Yorktown. In London, Charles James Fox remarked of Guilford that "another such victory would ruin the British army."
