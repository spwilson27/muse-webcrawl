The Millennium Challenge Corporation (MCC) is a bilateral United States foreign aid agency established by Congress in 2004. Its mission is to reduce global poverty through economic growth. MCC provides time-limited grants — called compacts — to developing countries that meet independently assessed standards for good governance, economic freedom, and investment in their citizens.

Model

MCC's approach differs from traditional aid in several respects. Countries are selected competitively using published policy indicators covering ruling justly, investing in people, and encouraging economic freedom. Selected countries design their own programmes, which must target constraints to economic growth, and partner governments are responsible for implementation through accountable local entities. Compacts are typically five years in length and fund sectors such as transport infrastructure, energy, agriculture, water, health, and education. Smaller "threshold programmes" assist countries that are close to meeting the selection criteria.

History

President George W. Bush announced the Millennium Challenge Account in March 2002, ahead of the Monterrey Conference on Financing for Development, promising to increase U.S. development assistance for countries that "govern justly, invest in their people, and encourage economic freedom." Congress created the MCC in January 2004. The agency signed its first compact, with Madagascar, in 2005, and has since entered into compacts with dozens of countries across Africa, Latin America, Asia, and Eastern Europe, investing billions of dollars. MCC also suspends or terminates partnerships when countries experience coups or significant democratic backsliding, as occurred in several cases.
