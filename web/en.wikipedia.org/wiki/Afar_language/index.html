Afar (Afar: Qafar af) is a Lowland East Cushitic language of the Afroasiatic family, spoken by the Afar people in the Afar Region of Ethiopia, in southern Eritrea and in Djibouti — together the "Afar Triangle" around the Danakil Depression and the southern Red Sea coast. Speaker numbers are estimated at between one and a half and two and a half million. Afar is closely related to the Saho language of Eritrea, the two forming the Saho–Afar dialect cluster.

Status and writing

Afar is a working language of Ethiopia's Afar Region and is used in regional media and primary education; in Djibouti it is one of the two principal national languages alongside Somali, though French and Arabic hold official status; in Eritrea it is used in broadcasting and schooling. Historically written in the Arabic script and in Ge'ez characters, Afar is now officially written in a Latin-based alphabet (Qafar Feera) developed in the 1970s by Afar intellectuals, with conventions including doubled vowels for length and c and x for the pharyngeal consonants characteristic of Cushitic languages.

Linguistic features

Afar is an agglutinative, subject–object–verb language with a rich system of verbal derivation. Like other Cushitic languages it has pharyngeal consonants and a pitch-accent system, and it marks focus and case on nouns. Much vocabulary relating to administration and technology is borrowed from Arabic, Amharic, French and Italian. A substantial oral literature of poetry, proverbs and genealogical recitation exists, and the language has a growing printed and broadcast presence, including Bible translations and radio services.
