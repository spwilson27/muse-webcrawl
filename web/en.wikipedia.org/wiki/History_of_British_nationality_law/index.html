History of British nationality law

The history of British nationality law concerns the development of the legal status of British subjects and citizens from the feudal doctrine of allegiance to the modern framework of British citizenship. For centuries there was a single status — that of British subject — shared across the United Kingdom and its empire; only in the twentieth century did distinct citizenships emerge.

Early law: allegiance and subject status

English law derived subject status from allegiance owed to the Crown, crystallised in Calvin's Case (1608), which held that a person born within the King's dominions owed allegiance and was a subject, whatever their parents' status. This jus soli principle meant virtually everyone born in British territory was a British subject. Naturalisation required a private Act of Parliament until general procedures were introduced in the 19th century; the British Nationality and Status of Aliens Act 1914 codified the common code of British subject status across the Empire.

Citizenship of the United Kingdom and Colonies

The British Nationality Act 1948 created the citizenship of the United Kingdom and Colonies (CUKC), while retaining British subject as an overarching status shared with the self-governing Dominions, which created their own citizenships. CUKCs initially retained free entry to the UK, but the Commonwealth Immigrants Acts of 1962 and 1968 and the Immigration Act 1971 progressively restricted settlement, introducing the concept of "patriality" — the right of abode — largely along lines of UK ancestry.

The British Nationality Act 1981

The British Nationality Act 1981, in force from 1 January 1983, replaced CUKC with three categories: British citizens (with the right of abode), British Dependent Territories citizens (renamed British Overseas Territories citizens in 2002) and British Overseas citizens. Pure jus soli was abolished: a child born in the UK is British only if a parent is British or "settled". Later statutes addressed Hong Kong (whose British National (Overseas) status was created in 1985 and a settlement route opened in 2021), statelessness, and the removal of historical gender discrimination in nationality rules.

Legacy

The layered history of the law left complex legacies, including the Windrush scandal, in which long-resident Commonwealth citizens were wrongly treated as immigration offenders, prompting reforms and compensation schemes in the late 2010s.
