Lucayan people

The Lucayan people were the original inhabitants of the Bahamas and the Turks and Caicos Islands before the European arrival of Christopher Columbus in 1492. They were a branch of the Taíno who inhabited most of the Caribbean islands at the time. The Lucayans were the first indigenous Americans encountered by Columbus.

Origins and culture

The Lucayans descended from the Taíno peoples who migrated from the Orinoco River delta region of South America, island-hopping through the Lesser Antilles. They reached the Bahamian archipelago (which they called Lucayoneque, "islands of the people of the waters") probably around 800 AD. They lived in small villages ruled by a chief (cacique), farmed cassava, corn, squash and sweet potatoes, fished, and made pottery in the Palmetto ware style. They called their home islands by names such as Bimini and Guanahani.

Contact and extermination

On 12 October 1492, Columbus landed on an island the Lucayans called Guanahani, renamed San Salvador. Within two decades, Spanish slave raids, disease and forced labour in the mines of Hispaniola virtually exterminated the Lucayans; by about 1520 the Bahamas were almost entirely depopulated, and the islands remained largely empty until English settlement in the seventeenth century.
