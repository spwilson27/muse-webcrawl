Batson v. Kentucky

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Batson v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 79 (1986), is a landmark decision of the US Supreme Court that held that a prosecutor's use of peremptory challenges in a criminal trial to exclude jurors on the basis of race violates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. Overruling the evidentiary regime of Swain v. Alabama (1965), the Court established a three-step framework: the defendant makes a prima facie showing that a peremptory strike was exercised on racial grounds; the burden shifts to the prosecution to give a race-neutral explanation; and the trial court decides whether purposeful discrimination has been shown.

The Batson framework was extended to defence strikes (Georgia v. McCollum, 1992), civil cases (Edmonson, 1991) and gender (J.E.B. v. Alabama, 1994). Its enforcement is widely regarded as weak — race-neutral pretexts are easy to offer and hard to falsify (the comparative-juror method used in Foster v. Chatman, 2016, and Flowers v. Mississippi, 2019, where enforcement succeeded, required extraordinary records) — prompting reform jurisdictions to adopt objective-observer standards (Washington's GR 37, 2018; California's statute, 2020) and Arizona to abolish peremptory challenges altogether (2022, the first state to do so). Batson remains the canonical intersection of jury law, equality doctrine and the realities of trial practice.
