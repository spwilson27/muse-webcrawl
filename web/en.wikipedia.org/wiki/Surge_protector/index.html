Surge protector

A surge protector (or surge suppressor, surge diverter, or transient voltage surge suppressor) is an appliance or device intended to protect electrical equipment from voltage spikes in alternating current (AC) circuits. When the voltage exceeds a safe threshold, the device either short-circuits the excess current to ground or blocks it, limiting the voltage delivered to connected equipment to a brief pulse typically under a few hundred volts above normal.

How it works

Most consumer surge protectors use metal oxide varistors (MOVs), which behave as insulators at normal voltages but conduct strongly when voltage rises, shunting the surge to the neutral or ground wire. Better designs combine MOVs for line-to-neutral, line-to-ground, and neutral-to-ground modes, and may add gas discharge tubes, transient-voltage-suppression diodes, thermal fuses, and series inductors. MOVs degrade with each absorbed surge, so many protectors include an indicator light showing when protection has been exhausted.

Specifications

Key ratings include the clamping (let-through) voltage — lower is better; the joule rating, indicating total energy the device can absorb over its life; response time; and the maximum surge current. Standards such as UL 1449 in North America define test procedures and voltage protection ratings. Whole-house surge protective devices are installed at the service panel to guard everything downstream, complementing point-of-use strips.

Causes of surges

The most destructive surges come from lightning, but most surges are smaller and internally generated: utility grid switching, power restoration after outages, and cycling of large motors and air conditioners all produce transients that gradually damage sensitive electronics. A surge protector cannot, however, protect against a direct lightning strike's full energy, nor against sustained overvoltage, brownouts, or outages — those require other equipment such as uninterruptible power supplies or lightning rods.
