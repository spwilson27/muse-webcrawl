Hamiltonian economic program

The Hamiltonian economic program was a series of measures proposed by Alexander Hamilton, the first Secretary of the Treasury of the United States, in reports to Congress in the early 1790s. The program aimed to establish the credit of the new federal government, stabilize its finances, and encourage economic growth, particularly in manufacturing and commerce.

Components

Hamilton submitted three major reports to Congress: the First Report on the Public Credit (1790), the Report on a National Bank (1790), and the Report on Manufactures (1791). The program's main elements were:

  • Funding the national debt at par and having the federal government assume the remaining war debts of the states, thereby tying creditors' interests to the federal government.
  • A national bank — the First Bank of the United States, chartered in 1791 for twenty years — to hold federal funds, issue paper currency, and regulate credit.
  • An excise tax on whiskey and tariffs on imported goods to provide revenue for servicing the debt.
  • Support for manufactures, including proposed bounties and protective tariffs, set out in the Report on Manufactures.

Opposition and legacy

The program was opposed by Thomas Jefferson and James Madison, who feared it concentrated power in the federal government and favoured commercial and financial interests over agrarian ones. The conflict between Hamilton's Federalists and the Jeffersonian Republicans over these measures helped give rise to the First Party System in American politics. The whiskey excise provoked the Whiskey Rebellion of 1794.
