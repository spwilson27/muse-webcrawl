Alfried Felix Alwyn Krupp von Bohlen und Halbach (13 August 1907 – 30 July 1967) was a German industrialist, the last sole proprietor of the Krupp steel and armaments conglomerate of Essen. He directed the firm through the Second World War, was convicted of war crimes at Nuremberg for the use of forced labour, and later rebuilt the company in post-war West Germany.

Early life

Alfried was the eldest son of Bertha Krupp, heiress of the Krupp works, and the diplomat Gustav von Bohlen und Halbach. Trained in metallurgy at Munich, Berlin and Aachen, he joined the family firm in 1935 and its board in 1938. A keen yachtsman, he won a bronze medal in sailing at the 1936 Berlin Olympics.

Head of Krupp

Under the Lex Krupp decree of 1943, Alfried became sole owner of the firm. Krupp works were central to German armaments production — artillery, armour plate and the steel of the war economy — and employed vast numbers of concentration-camp inmates, prisoners of war and deported workers under brutal conditions.

Krupp Trial and release

In the Krupp Trial at Nuremberg (1947–48), one of the subsequent Nuremberg proceedings, Alfried Krupp was convicted of crimes against humanity and plunder, and sentenced to twelve years' imprisonment plus forfeiture of all property. In 1951, amid Cold War pressure to rebuild West German industry, US High Commissioner John J. McCloy commuted the sentences and restored most of his property.

Post-war rebuilding

Under the 1953 Mehlem Agreement, Krupp was formally required to divest his coal and steel interests, but enforcement lagged and he rebuilt a diversified industrial empire. At his death in 1967 the firm employed over 100,000 people. Because his son Arndt renounced the inheritance, the enterprise was converted into a foundation, the Alfried Krupp von Bohlen und Halbach Foundation, and the operating company became Fried. Krupp GmbH in 1968.
