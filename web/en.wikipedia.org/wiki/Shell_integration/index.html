Shell integration

Shell integration (the shell method in integral calculus) is a method for calculating the volume of a solid of revolution, when integrating along an axis perpendicular to the axis of revolution. It is a counterpart of disk integration, which integrates along an axis parallel to the axis of revolution.

The method

The method works as follows: consider a volume obtained by rotating a plane figure about an external axis. The figure is sliced into thin vertical strips parallel to the axis of rotation. Each strip, when rotated, sweeps out a thin cylindrical shell. The volume of such a shell is approximated by the volume of a hollow cylinder: its circumference times its height times its thickness.

If the shell has radius x, height f(x), and thickness dx, its volume is approximately

dV = 2π x f(x) dx

Summing over all shells from a to b gives the integral

V = ∫ab 2π x f(x) dx

Comparison with disk integration

In disk integration the region is sliced perpendicular to the axis of revolution, producing disks or washers whose volumes are integrated. Which method is preferable depends on the geometry of the region and the ease of expressing the bounding curve as a function of the chosen variable. For regions described by functions that are awkward to invert, the shell method often avoids solving for the inverse function entirely.

Example

The volume enclosed by the surfaces y = x and y = x2 rotated about the y-axis can be computed with shells of radius x and height x − x2, integrated from x = 0 to x = 1, giving V = π/6.
