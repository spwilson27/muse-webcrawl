Eblaite is an extinct East Semitic language once spoken in the ancient city of Ebla, in present-day northern Syria. It is known from the Ebla tablets — an archive of roughly 17,000–20,000 clay tablets and fragments in cuneiform script discovered in the 1970s at Tell Mardikh by an Italian archaeological mission led by Paolo Matthiae, with the decipherment pioneered by Giovanni Pettinato. The tablets date to roughly 2500–2250 BC, making Eblaite, together with Akkadian, one of the earliest attested Semitic languages.

Classification and features

Eblaite is usually classified as an East Semitic language, closely related to Akkadian, though it also shows features that link it to the West Semitic languages of the Levant — a combination reflecting Ebla's position between Mesopotamia and Syria. It was written in a cuneiform adapted from the Sumerian tradition, using many Sumerian logograms (Sumerograms) alongside syllabic spellings; bilingual Sumerian–Eblaite vocabularies found in the archive are among the oldest known dictionaries and were essential to reading the texts.

The tablets

The palace archive documents the administration of a major Bronze Age kingdom: treaties (including one with the city of Abarsal), trade records for textiles, metals and timber, accounts of deliveries, rituals, incantations, literary texts and lists of place names and deities. The texts transformed scholarly knowledge of third-millennium Syria, showing a sophisticated state trading across the ancient Near East. Eblaite died out when Ebla was destroyed, probably by Naram-Sin of Akkad, around 2250 BC; the city was rebuilt but its language was replaced by Amorite and other tongues.
