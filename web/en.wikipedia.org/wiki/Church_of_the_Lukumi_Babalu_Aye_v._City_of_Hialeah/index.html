Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah

Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah
508 U.S. 520 (1993)
Decided: 11 June 1993
Decision: 9–0, ordinances unconstitutional
Opinion by: Justice Anthony Kennedy

Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah, 508 U.S. 520 (1993), was a case in which the Supreme Court of the United States held that ordinances enacted by the city of Hialeah, Florida, prohibiting ritual animal sacrifice, violated the Free Exercise Clause of the First Amendment because they were designed to suppress the religious practice of Santería.

Background

The Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, a Santería congregation led by Ernesto Pichardo, planned to open a church and religious school in Hialeah. In response, the city council passed resolutions and ordinances that criminalized ritual animal sacrifice while exempting most other killings of animals, including kosher slaughter, hunting, and ordinary food preparation. Santería adherents sacrifice animals in rituals such as healings, births, and ordinations of priests; the animals are often then eaten.

Decision

Writing for a unanimous Court, Justice Anthony Kennedy held that the ordinances were neither neutral nor generally applicable: their text, effect, and the statements of city officials showed they targeted the Santería faith specifically. Because the laws were not neutral laws of general applicability, they were subject to strict scrutiny, and the city could not show a compelling interest pursued by the least restrictive means — the ordinances were both overbroad and underinclusive relative to their stated public-health and animal-cruelty goals.

Significance

Along with Employment Division v. Smith (1990), the case defines modern Free Exercise doctrine: laws that target religious practice, or that contain systems of individualized exemptions, remain subject to strict scrutiny even after Smith. The decision is a landmark in the protection of minority religions in the United States.
