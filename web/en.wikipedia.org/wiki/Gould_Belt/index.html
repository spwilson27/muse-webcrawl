Gould Belt

The Gould Belt is a local, partial ring of stars in the Milky Way, about 3,000 light-years across, tilted about 16 to 20 degrees to the galactic plane. It contains many hot, blue O- and B-type stars, many of them bright and nearby, in constellations such as Scorpius, Centaurus, Orion, Perseus, and Cygnus. It is named after the American astronomer Benjamin Gould, who identified it in 1879.

Overview

The belt contains many of the nearest star-forming regions and young stellar associations, including the Orion, Scorpius-Centaurus, and Perseus OB associations. Its stars are young, with ages of roughly 30 to 50 million years. The Sun lies close to the belt's inner region, roughly at its centre by some definitions.

Origin theories

The origin of the Gould Belt is debated. Proposed explanations include the impact of a high-velocity cloud or a dark matter clump on a molecular cloud, triggering star formation in an expanding ring, and supernova-driven expansion of a superbubble. Recent work with Gaia data has suggested that parts of the structure form a segment of the larger Radcliffe wave, a long filament of gas and young stars in the local arm.
