Knaster–Tarski theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Knaster–Tarski theorem, named after Bronisław Knaster and Alfred Tarski, is a fundamental fixed-point theorem in order theory and lattice theory. It states that any monotone function on a complete lattice has fixed points — in fact, its set of fixed points is itself a complete lattice.

Statement

Let (L, ≤) be a complete lattice and let f : L → L be an order-preserving (monotone) function. Then the set of fixed points of f is non-empty and forms a complete lattice under the induced order. Moreover, the least fixed point of f is the meet of all elements x with f(x) ≤ x, and the greatest fixed point is the join of all elements x with x ≤ f(x).

History

A special case for functions on power sets appeared in Knaster's work of 1928; Tarski published the general lattice-theoretic version in 1955 and recognised its wide applicability. A constructive variant using transfinite iteration from the bottom element (the Knaster–Tarski iteration, related to the Bourbaki–Witt theorem) is standard in computer science.

Applications

The theorem underlies the semantics of logic programming (the least Herbrand model of a definite program is the least fixed point of the immediate-consequence operator), denotational semantics of recursive definitions, abstract interpretation in static program analysis, and the modal μ-calculus. It is a classic example of a non-constructive existence proof that has constructive counterparts when the lattice has suitable chain conditions.
