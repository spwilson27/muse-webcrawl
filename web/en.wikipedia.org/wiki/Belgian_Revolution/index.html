The Belgian Revolution (French: Révolution belge, Dutch: Belgische Revolutie/opstand) was the conflict which led to the secession of the southern provinces (mainly the former Austrian Netherlands) from the United Kingdom of the Netherlands and the establishment of an independent Kingdom of Belgium.

Background

The Congress of Vienna (1815) had united the northern and southern Netherlands under King William I. The largely Catholic, partly French-speaking south resented Dutch Protestant dominance, language policy, under-representation in government, press restrictions and economic grievances. Inspired by the July Revolution in France, discontent boiled over in the summer of 1830.

Course of events

On 25 August 1830, rioting broke out in Brussels after a performance of the nationalist opera La muette de Portici. The unrest spread, volunteers seized control of the capital, and Dutch troops' attempt to retake Brussels in the "September Days" (23–26 September) failed. A provisional government proclaimed Belgian independence on 4 October 1830. A National Congress adopted a liberal constitution and, after rejecting French candidates, elected Prince Leopold of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha as king; he took the oath on 21 July 1831, now Belgium's National Day.

William I's Ten Days' Campaign of August 1831 briefly defeated Belgian forces before French intervention forced a Dutch withdrawal. The great powers had recognised Belgian independence at the London Conference of 1830–31, but final settlement — including the division of Luxembourg and Limburg — came only with the Treaty of London (1839), which guaranteed Belgium's perpetual neutrality.

Consequences

Belgium became a constitutional monarchy with one of Europe's most liberal constitutions. Its guaranteed neutrality held until Germany's invasion in 1914. The revolution also fixed the political union of Catholic and Liberal elites (unionism) that governed early Belgium and set the stage for its rapid industrialisation.
