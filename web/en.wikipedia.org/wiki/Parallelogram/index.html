In Euclidean geometry, a parallelogram is a simple (non-self-intersecting) quadrilateral with two pairs of parallel sides. The opposite sides of a parallelogram are of equal length, and its opposite angles are of equal measure. The congruences of opposite sides and angles follow directly from Euclid's parallel postulate and cannot be proved without it or one of its equivalent formulations.

By contrast, a quadrilateral with only one pair of parallel sides is called a trapezoid (or trapezium). The three-dimensional counterpart of a parallelogram is a parallelepiped.

Characterizations

A convex quadrilateral is a parallelogram if and only if any one of the following statements holds:

  • Both pairs of opposite sides are parallel.
  • Both pairs of opposite sides are equal in length.
  • Both pairs of opposite angles are equal.
  • The diagonals bisect each other.
  • One pair of opposite sides is both parallel and equal in length.
  • Each diagonal divides the quadrilateral into two congruent triangles.

Area

The area of a parallelogram equals the product of its base and the corresponding height: A = b · h. Equivalently, if two adjacent sides have lengths a and b with included angle θ, then A = a·b·sin θ. The area can also be computed as the magnitude of the cross product of two adjacent side vectors, a fact used in the geometric definition of the cross product in physics and engineering.

Special cases

  • A rectangle is a parallelogram with four right angles.
  • A rhombus is a parallelogram with four equal sides.
  • A square is a parallelogram with four equal sides and four right angles — both a rectangle and a rhombus.
  • A rhomboid is a parallelogram whose adjacent sides are unequal and whose angles are oblique.

Parallelogram law

The parallelogram law states that the sum of the squares of the lengths of the four sides of a parallelogram equals the sum of the squares of the lengths of the two diagonals. It generalizes the Pythagorean theorem and plays a role in the theory of inner product spaces, where it characterizes norms arising from an inner product.
