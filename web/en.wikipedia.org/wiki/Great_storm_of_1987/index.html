Great storm of 1987

The great storm of 1987 was a violent extratropical cyclone that struck southern England, northern France, and the Channel Islands on the night of 15–16 October 1987. With hurricane-force gusts, it was the most severe storm to affect England since the Great Storm of 1703, causing 18 deaths in England, extensive damage estimated at over £1 billion, and the felling of some 15 million trees.

The storm

A depression that formed in the Bay of Biscay deepened explosively as it crossed the English Channel, developing features later associated with a "sting jet". Gusts exceeded 100 mph (160 km/h) across much of south-eastern England, reaching 115 mph (185 km/h) at Shoreham; a gust of 136 mph (219 km/h) was recorded in Brittany. Widespread power cuts affected more than a million people, roads and railways were blocked by fallen trees, and several ships were driven aground.

The forecast

The storm became famous in Britain for the lunchtime weather forecast given by BBC weatherman Michael Fish, who assured viewers that a woman who had telephoned about a hurricane approaching from the west need not worry — "there isn't one". Although Fish was referring to a separate Florida hurricane and did warn of strong winds, the clip has become a byword for failed forecasting. An internal Met Office enquiry followed, and the event prompted lasting improvements in forecasting, observation over the Atlantic, and severe-weather warning procedures.

Impact

Famous losses included a quarter of the trees at the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, the devastation of the pinetum at Bedgebury, and the fall of six of the seven oak trees that gave Sevenoaks its name. In France 23 people died. The insured loss made the storm, at the time, one of the costliest weather events in British history.
