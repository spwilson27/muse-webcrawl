Messianism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Messianism is the belief in the advent of a messiah who acts as the saviour of a group of people. Messianism originated as a Zoroastrian religious belief and followed into the Abrahamic religions, but other religions have messianism-related concepts too. religions with a messiah concept include Judaism (the Mashiach), Christianity (Christ), Islam (the Mahdi and Isa/Jesus), Zoroastrianism (Saoshyant), and in more diffuse forms Buddhism (Maitreya) and Hinduism (Kalki).

Origins

The word derives from the Hebrew mashiach, "anointed one". Scholars generally trace the fully developed doctrine of a future saviour partly to Zoroastrian eschatology — with its expected Saoshyant, resurrection of the dead and final renovation of the world (Frashokereti) — which influenced Second Temple Judaism during and after the Babylonian exile. In the Hebrew Bible, "messiah" initially denoted an anointed king or priest, such as David or even the Persian Cyrus; the expectation of a future Davidic redeemer grew stronger in the centuries before the Common Era, amid apocalyptic literature such as Daniel and the Dead Sea Scrolls.

In the Abrahamic religions

  • Judaism: the Messiah is a future Jewish king from the Davidic line, who will rule in an age of peace, rebuild the Temple in Jerusalem and gather the exiles. Messianic movements within Judaism include those around Simon bar Kokhba and, in the 17th century, Sabbatai Zevi.
  • Christianity: Jesus of Nazareth is confessed as the Christ (Greek for "anointed"), whose life, death and resurrection redeem humanity and who will return in glory — the Second Coming.
  • Islam: the Mahdi is a prophesied redeemer who will rule before the Day of Judgment, appearing together with Isa (Jesus), who in Islamic belief returns to defeat the false messiah (al-Dajjal).

Secular messianism

The term is also applied metaphorically to secular ideologies and political movements that promise a radical redemption of society through a leader, class or nation, and scholars speak of "messianic" expectations in movements from cargo cults to some forms of revolutionary politics.
