The January Uprising (Polish: powstanie styczniowe; Lithuanian: 1863 metų sukilimas) was an insurrection against the Russian Empire in the former territories of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth. It began on 22 January 1863 and lasted until the last insurgents were captured in 1864. It remains the longest-lasting Polish uprising of the partition era.

Causes

After the defeat of the November Uprising (1830–31), Congress Poland's autonomy had been steadily eroded. In the early 1860s Polish society was divided between the aristocratic "Whites" and the democratic "Reds", who organised mass patriotic demonstrations in Warsaw that Russian troops repeatedly fired upon. The immediate spark was the announcement in January 1863 of a forced conscription (branka) of young Polish men into the Imperial Russian Army, ordered by Aleksander Wielopolski in an attempt to decapitate the conspiratorial movement.

Course of the uprising

The Central National Committee proclaimed a provisional national government and promised the enfranchisement of the peasantry with the land they worked. Lacking a regular army, the Poles waged guerrilla warfare in some 1,200 battles and skirmishes. Key leaders included Ludwik Mierosławski, Marian Langiewicz, and Romuald Traugutt, who became dictator of the uprising in October 1863 and reorganised the underground state. Insurgents included Polish, Lithuanian, Belarusian and Ukrainian volunteers, as well as foreign sympathisers such as the Italian Francesco Nullo. Some 200,000 people passed through the insurgent ranks over the course of the war, facing a Russian army that eventually numbered several hundred thousand.

Aftermath

The uprising was crushed with mass executions, the hanging of Traugutt and four associates in August 1864, deportations of tens of thousands to Siberia, confiscation of estates, and the abolition of the remaining separate institutions of Congress Poland, which was reduced to the "Vistula Land". In the former Grand Duchy of Lithuania, Governor Mikhail Muravyov's repression was especially severe. Paradoxically, the Tsar's government implemented the insurgents' own peasant reform in 1864 on relatively generous terms, partly to detach the peasantry from the national cause. The failure of the uprising discredited armed insurrection for a generation and encouraged the "organic work" movement of economic and educational self-improvement.
