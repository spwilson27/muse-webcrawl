Generic drug

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A generic drug is a pharmaceutical product that is equivalent to a brand-name drug in dosage, strength, route of administration, quality and intended use, but sold under its chemical (generic) name, usually much more cheaply. Generics become available after the originator's patents and exclusivity expire, and they account for the great majority of prescriptions in many countries.

Approval and equivalence

Regulators such as the US FDA and European Medicines Agency approve generics on proof of pharmaceutical equivalence and bioequivalence — the same active ingredient absorbed at the same rate and extent (conventionally with blood-level measures within an 80–125% confidence range of the brand). Inactive ingredients and appearance may differ. For most drugs generics perform identically, though narrow-therapeutic-index drugs (such as some epilepsy and transplant medicines) prompt extra caution about switching brands.

Economics

Because generic manufacturers do not repeat the cost of discovering and trialling the drug, prices can fall by 80% or more once several competitors enter. Generic competition and production — much of it in India and China — underpin affordable treatment of HIV, tuberculosis, hypertension and many other conditions worldwide, and are central to essential-medicines policy.
