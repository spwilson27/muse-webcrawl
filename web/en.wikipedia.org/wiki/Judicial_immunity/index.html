Judicial immunity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Judicial immunity protects judges from personal liability for acts performed in their official capacity.

Judicial immunity is a form of legal immunity that protects judges and other adjudicators from civil liability for acts performed in their judicial capacity. Its purpose is to preserve judicial independence: a judge who could be sued by every losing litigant might be pressured in deciding cases, and finality of judgments would be undermined.

United States

In United States law, judges enjoy absolute immunity from damages for judicial acts, even if committed in error, with malice, or in excess of jurisdiction, so long as the act is not taken in the complete absence of jurisdiction and is within the judge's general judicial function. The doctrine was established in Bradley v. Fisher (1872) and reaffirmed in Stump v. Sparkman (1978), where a judge who had ordered a young woman's sterilization was held immune, and Mireles v. Waco (1991). Immunity generally does not extend to non-judicial acts, such as employment decisions about court staff (Forrester v. White, 1988), and 1996 legislation largely barred injunctive relief against judges as well.

Other jurisdictions

English common law developed a similar privilege, historically extending absolute protection to superior court judges and more limited protection to inferior courts acting within jurisdiction. Many civil-law systems likewise shield judicial officers from personal damages suits, channelling complaints instead to appeals, judicial-conduct bodies, or claims against the state. Critics in several countries argue that broad immunity leaves victims of judicial misconduct without a remedy, while defenders reply that discipline and appeal, not private damages, are the proper corrective.
