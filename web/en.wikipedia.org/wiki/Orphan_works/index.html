Orphan works

Orphan works
FieldCopyright law
DefinitionCopyrighted works whose owners cannot be identified or located after a diligent search

Archives hold vast numbers of orphan works whose creators are unknown.

An orphan work is a copyright-protected work — such as a book, photograph, film, or sound recording — for which the rights holder cannot be identified or located by someone who wishes to use the work, despite a reasonably diligent search. Because copyright usually subsists automatically and for decades after the author's death, libraries, archives, museums, and filmmakers may be unable to lawfully reuse such works, and much cultural material risks decaying unused.

Scope of the problem

Studies by the British Library and others have suggested that a large share of in-copyright collection items — sometimes estimated at 40 percent or more — may be orphaned. The problem is acute for photographs, ephemera, unpublished manuscripts, and early audiovisual material, which often lack rights metadata.

Legal responses

In the United States, the U.S. Copyright Office reported on the problem in 2006 and 2015; the Orphan Works Act of 2008 passed the Senate but not the House, and no comprehensive federal statute has been enacted, though the Music Modernization Act of 2018 addressed a related problem for recordings. Canada operates a licensing scheme through the Copyright Board for unlocatable owners. The European Union adopted the Orphan Works Directive (2012/28/EU), allowing cultural heritage institutions to digitize and make available orphan works after a diligent search. The United Kingdom introduced an orphan works licensing scheme in 2014; an exception for cultural institutions also followed from the EU directive.

Mass digitization

Orphan works are central to debates over mass digitization projects such as Google Books and HathiTrust, and over "extended collective licensing" models used in Scandinavia.
