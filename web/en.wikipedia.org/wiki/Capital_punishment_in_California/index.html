Capital punishment in California

Capital punishment is a legal penalty in the U.S. state of California, but no execution has taken place since 2006, and in March 2019 Governor Gavin Newsom imposed a moratorium, granting reprieves to all condemned inmates and ordering the execution chamber at San Quentin State Prison closed. California maintains one of the largest death rows in the United States — over 700 inmates at its peak, housed at San Quentin (men) and Chowchilla (women) — yet has carried out only 13 executions since the penalty was reinstated in 1978. The last person executed was Clarence Ray Allen, by lethal injection on 17 January 2006.

History

California executed prisoners by hanging from statehood until adopting the gas chamber in 1937; the first gas-chamber execution was in 1938 at San Quentin, whose chamber was built in 1938. Between 1893 and 1967 the state carried out several hundred executions. The last pre-modern execution was Aaron Mitchell in 1967. In 1972, the California Supreme Court in People v. Anderson held the death penalty violated the state constitution's ban on cruel or unusual punishment, and the U.S. Supreme Court's Furman v. Georgia decision the same year halted executions nationally. Voters responded by passing Proposition 17 in November 1972, amending the state constitution to authorise capital punishment; new statutes followed in 1977 and the expanded 1978 law enacted by Proposition 7 (the Briggs Initiative).

The first execution under the modern regime was Robert Alton Harris in the gas chamber in 1992. Lethal injection became the default method in the 1990s. A 2006 federal court ruling (in the Morales litigation) halted executions over concerns that the three-drug protocol could cause an unconstitutionally painful death, and the state has not executed anyone since. Litigation over the lethal-injection protocol, a 2014 federal district ruling (later vacated on procedural grounds) finding the system's delay unconstitutional, and Proposition 62 (2016, repeal — failed) and Proposition 66 (2016, expedited procedures — passed) kept the issue before the courts and voters.

Capital offences and process

First-degree murder with one or more "special circumstances" (such as felony-murder during robbery, rape, or arson; multiple murders; murder of a police officer; or lying in wait) is punishable by death or life imprisonment without parole. Capital trials are bifurcated into guilt and penalty phases; the jury must unanimously recommend death. All death sentences receive automatic review by the California Supreme Court, and condemned inmates also pursue federal habeas petitions, producing waits that commonly exceed two decades. More death-row inmates have died of natural causes or suicide than by execution since 1978.

Moratorium and recent developments

On 13 March 2019, Governor Gavin Newsom signed an executive order imposing a moratorium on executions, reprieving all 737 condemned inmates (repealable reprieves lasting his governorship), withdrawing the lethal-injection protocol, and closing the San Quentin death chamber. Beginning in 2022–2024, the state began transferring condemned men out of San Quentin to other prisons under a programme aligned with Proposition 66's work requirements, effectively dismantling death row as a separate unit.
