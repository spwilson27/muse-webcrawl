The Duchy of Prussia (German: Herzogtum Preußen; Polish: Prusy Książęce) was a duchy established in 1525 in the eastern part of Prussia, the first Protestant state in Europe. Its capital was Königsberg (modern Kaliningrad). The duchy was created from the secularised lands of the Teutonic Order and was a fief of the Crown of the Kingdom of Poland for its first 132 years.

Establishment

After the Polish–Teutonic War of 1519–1521 ended inconclusively, the Teutonic Grand Master Albert of Hohenzollern, advised by Martin Luther, secularised the monastic state and converted to Lutheranism. By the Treaty of Kraków of 1525, Albert became the first Duke of Prussia, and on 10 April 1525 he paid the Prussian Homage to his uncle, King Sigismund I the Old of Poland, receiving the duchy as a Polish fief.

Brandenburg inheritance

Albert was succeeded by his son Albert Frederick in 1568. When Albert Frederick became mentally ill, administration passed to regents from the Brandenburg line of the Hohenzollerns. In 1618 the duchy was inherited by John Sigismund, Elector of Brandenburg, creating the personal union known as Brandenburg-Prussia — an awkward composite, since Brandenburg lay within the Holy Roman Empire while Ducal Prussia lay outside it, under Polish suzerainty.

Full sovereignty and the kingdom

During the Northern Wars, Frederick William, the "Great Elector", exploited Poland's weakness: by the Treaty of Wehlau (1657), confirmed at Oliva (1660), Poland renounced suzerainty and the duchy became fully sovereign. In 1701 the Elector Frederick III crowned himself at Königsberg as Frederick I, "King in Prussia" — the duchy thereby became the nucleus of the Kingdom of Prussia, the title deliberately using "in" because Royal (western) Prussia remained Polish territory.

Culture

The duchy was an early centre of Lutheran printing and scholarship. The University of Königsberg (Albertina) was founded in 1544, and the region later produced the philosopher Immanuel Kant. The Old Prussian language, spoken by the indigenous Baltic Prussians, died out during the 17th–18th centuries, and the duchy's population included German, Polish (Masurian) and Lithuanian speakers.
