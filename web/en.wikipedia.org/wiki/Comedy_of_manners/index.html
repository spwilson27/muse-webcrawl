Comedy of manners

Also called: Anti-sentimental comedy
Notable writers: Congreve, Wycherley, Wilde, Coward

A comedy of manners (from French comédie de mœurs) is a form of comedy that satirises the manners and affectations of contemporary society and social class, often represented by stock characters. Its dialogue is typically witty and verbal, and its plots often revolve around love intrigues and social reputation, with little emphasis on physical action.

History

The form has roots in Roman comedy and Molière in France. It flourished in England during the Restoration period (1660s–1700s) in plays by William Wycherley (The Country Wife), William Congreve (The Way of the World), George Etherege and Aphra Behn, which mocked the sexual and social codes of the court aristocracy. In the 18th century it was softened by sentimental comedy, then revived in altered form by Oscar Wilde (The Importance of Being Earnest, 1895) and later by Noël Coward and in drawing-room and screwball traditions on stage and screen.

Characteristics

Stock figures include the fop, the rake, the coquette, the gossip and the would-be wit. The humour arises from the gap between social pretence and real motive.
