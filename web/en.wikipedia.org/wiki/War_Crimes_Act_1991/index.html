War Crimes Act 1991

The War Crimes Act 1991 is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that gave British courts jurisdiction over acts of homicide committed during the Second World War in German-held territory, where the accused had subsequently become a British citizen or resident. It is one of the very few Acts passed using the Parliament Acts procedure after being rejected twice by the House of Lords. The Act produced only one successful prosecution.

Background

In the 1980s, investigative reporting alleged that Nazi collaborators and war criminals had settled in Britain after the war, some having entered as displaced persons or former members of formations such as the Waffen-SS Galicia Division. In 1988 the government appointed the Hetherington–Chalmers inquiry, which reviewed allegations against 301 individuals and recommended that British law be changed to permit trials for war crimes committed abroad, since extradition to the Soviet Union or Yugoslavia raised fair-trial concerns.

Passage

The bill was strongly opposed in the House of Lords, where peers including former Lord Chancellor Lord Hailsham objected to retrospective legislation and the practical fairness of trials half a century after the events. The Lords rejected the bill in 1990 and again in 1991; the Commons invoked the Parliament Acts, and the bill received royal assent on 9 May 1991 without Lords' consent — only the fourth use of the procedure, after the Welsh Church and Irish Home Rule bills of 1914 and before the Hunting Act 2004.

Application

A dedicated Metropolitan Police War Crimes Unit investigated hundreds of cases, but proceedings proved difficult: witnesses were elderly or dead and documentary evidence was scattered. Szymon Serafinowicz was charged in 1997 but found unfit to stand trial. In 1999 Anthony Sawoniuk, a former Belarusian auxiliary policeman living in London, was convicted at the Old Bailey of murdering Jews in Domachevo, Belarus, in 1942, and sentenced to life imprisonment; he died in prison in 2005. It remains the only conviction under the Act, which is still in force.
