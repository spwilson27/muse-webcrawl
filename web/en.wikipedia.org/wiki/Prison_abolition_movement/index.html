Prison abolition movement

The prison abolition movement is a network of activists, scholars and organisations seeking to eliminate prisons and the wider prison-industrial complex, and to build alternative systems of accountability and public safety. Unlike prison reform, which seeks to improve conditions within the existing system, abolitionists argue that incarceration is inherently harmful and should be drastically reduced and ultimately replaced.

History and ideas

Modern abolitionism grew out of Quaker activism, the prison unrest of the early 1970s, and works such as IFCO's Instead of Prisons (1976). Angela Y. Davis's Are Prisons Obsolete? (2003) became a foundational text, and organisations such as Critical Resistance (founded 1997) and writers such as Ruth Wilson Gilmore and Mariame Kaba have developed abolitionist theory and practice, linking prisons to racial capitalism and state violence. Michel Foucault's Discipline and Punish (1975) is a major scholarly influence.

Proposals

Abolitionists typically advocate decarceration, decriminalisation of offences such as drug possession and sex work, restorative and transformative justice processes, moratoria on new prison construction, and investment in housing, healthcare, education and employment as the real foundations of safety. They emphasise "non-reformist reforms" that shrink rather than strengthen the system.

Criticism

Critics argue that abolition underestimates the need to incapacitate dangerous offenders and protect victims, and question whether alternative processes can handle serious violent crime. Abolitionists respond that prisons themselves fail at deterrence and rehabilitation, and that abolition is a long-term horizon guiding practical steps.
