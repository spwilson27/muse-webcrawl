Hemodialysis

Hemodialysis, also spelled haemodialysis, or simply dialysis, is a process of purifying the blood of a person whose kidneys are not working normally. This type of dialysis achieves the extracorporeal removal of waste products such as creatinine and urea and of free water from the blood when the kidneys are in a state of kidney failure. Hemodialysis is one of three renal replacement therapies (the other two being kidney transplant and peritoneal dialysis).

A patient undergoing hemodialysis

Principle

Blood is pumped from the patient through a dialyzer ("artificial kidney") containing thousands of hollow fibres made of a semipermeable membrane. A sterile dialysis solution (dialysate) flows around the fibres in the opposite direction; small solutes such as urea, potassium and phosphate diffuse across the membrane down their concentration gradients, while excess fluid is removed by ultrafiltration under a pressure gradient. The cleansed blood is returned to the patient. A typical in-centre session lasts about four hours and is performed three times a week; home and nocturnal schedules are also used.

Vascular access

Repeated access to the circulation is achieved through a surgically created arteriovenous fistula, a synthetic graft, or a central venous catheter. The fistula is the preferred long-term access because of lower infection and clotting rates.

History and complications

The first successful chronic hemodialysis was made possible in the 1960s by Willem Kolff's rotating-drum kidney and by the Teflon shunt developed by Belding Scribner in Seattle. Complications include hypotension, cramps, infection, access thrombosis, and long-term problems such as anemia, bone disease and dialysis-related amyloidosis.
