The United States Copyright Office is a government body within the Library of Congress that maintains records of copyright registration in the United States, administers the statutory provisions of US copyright law, and advises Congress, federal agencies and the courts on copyright policy. It is headed by the Register of Copyrights, appointed by the Librarian of Congress.

Functions

The Office examines and registers claims to copyright — processing roughly half a million claims a year covering millions of works — records transfers and other documents pertaining to copyrights, and maintains the public catalogue of registrations, searchable online for records from 1978 onward. It also administers certain statutory licences (for example for cable retransmission and digital audio recording), designates agents for online service providers under the DMCA safe harbours, conducts periodic rulemakings for exemptions to the prohibition on circumventing technological protection measures (section 1201 of the Copyright Act), and receives mandatory deposit copies of published works for the Library of Congress.

History

Federal copyright registration was centralised in the Library of Congress in 1870 under Librarian Ainsworth Rand Spofford, and the Copyright Office was established as a separate department of the Library in 1897, with Thorvald Solberg as the first Register. Copyright in the US is automatic on fixation, so registration is not required for protection, but it confers important benefits in litigation, including eligibility for statutory damages and attorney's fees; the Supreme Court held in Fourth Estate v. Wall-Street.com (2019) that a claimant must generally have a granted registration before filing suit.
