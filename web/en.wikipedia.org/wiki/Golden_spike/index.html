Golden spike

The golden spike (also called "the Last Spike") is the ceremonial final spike driven by Leland Stanford to join the rails of the first transcontinental railroad across the United States, connecting the Central Pacific Railroad from Sacramento, California, with the Union Pacific Railroad from Omaha, Nebraska, at Promontory Summit, Utah Territory, on May 10, 1869.

The ceremony

The spike, made of 17.6-karat gold and engraved with the names of the railroad officers and directors, was presented by David Hewes, a San Francisco contractor. At the ceremony, Stanford swung a silver mallet at the spike but famously missed, striking the tie instead; a telegraph operator wired the word "DONE" to signal the completion to a waiting nation. The golden spike was removed immediately afterwards and replaced with an ordinary iron spike, and the last tie of polished California laurel was likewise swapped out.

Aftermath and legacy

The completion reduced cross-country travel from months to about a week and transformed the settlement and economy of the American West. The golden spike is now displayed at the Cantor Arts Center at Stanford University. The site is preserved as Golden Spike National Historical Park, where re-enactments are held each May 10. The phrase "golden spike" is also used in geology for stratigraphic reference points (formally, Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point, GSSP).
