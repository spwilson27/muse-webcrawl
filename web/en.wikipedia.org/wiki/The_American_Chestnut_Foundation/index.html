The American Chestnut Foundation (TACF) is a nonprofit organisation founded in 1983 whose mission is to restore the American chestnut tree (Castanea dentata) to the forests of eastern North America. The American chestnut, once one of the most abundant and valuable trees in the eastern United States, was devastated in the first half of the 20th century by the chestnut blight, an introduced fungal disease that killed an estimated four billion trees.

A chestnut sapling at one of the Foundation's breeding orchards

Breeding program

TACF's central strategy is backcross breeding. American chestnuts are crossed with blight-resistant Chinese chestnuts, and the offspring are repeatedly backcrossed to American trees, selecting at each generation for blight resistance. The goal is a tree that is fifteen-sixteenths American in character but carries the Chinese parent's resistance. The Foundation runs research farms, notably at Meadowview, Virginia, and works through state chapters from Maine to Alabama that maintain breeding orchards using local surviving "mother trees".

Other approaches

TACF also supports research into hypovirulence (viruses that weaken the blight fungus) and cooperates with university partners developing transgenic blight-tolerant chestnuts, such as the Darling line of trees engineered with a wheat gene to detoxify the fungus's oxalic acid.
