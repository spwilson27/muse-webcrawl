Gideon v. Wainwright

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), is a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court holding that the Sixth Amendment's guarantee of counsel is a fundamental right obligatory on the states through the Fourteenth Amendment: indigent defendants in felony prosecutions must be provided a lawyer. The unanimous opinion by Justice Hugo Black overruled Betts v. Brady (1942).

The case

Clarence Earl Gideon, charged with felony breaking and entering in Florida, was refused appointed counsel, defended himself, and was convicted; his handwritten in forma pauperis petition to the Supreme Court was answered by appointment of Abe Fortas (a future justice) to argue for him. Retried with a court-appointed lawyer, Gideon was acquitted. The right was later extended to any prosecution involving potential imprisonment (Argersinger, 1972; Scott, 1979) and to juveniles (In re Gault, 1967). Anthony Lewis's book Gideon's Trumpet (1964) made it one of the best-known cases in American law. Its promise is chronically strained by underfunded public-defender systems and crushing caseloads — a gap between right and delivery that reformers and courts still contest.
