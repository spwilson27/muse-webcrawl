Royal Prussia (Polish: Prusy Królewskie; German: Königlich-Preußen) was a province of the Crown of the Kingdom of Poland from 1466 to 1772. It was created from the western territories of the State of the Teutonic Order after the Thirteen Years' War by the Second Peace of Thorn (1466), in contrast to the remaining eastern part, "Ducal Prussia", which remained with the Order (and later became a Polish fief, then the core of the Kingdom of Prussia).

History

In 1454 the Prussian Confederation of cities and nobles, rebelling against the Teutonic Order, asked King Casimir IV of Poland for incorporation, triggering the Thirteen Years' War. The peace of 1466 confirmed the transfer of Pomerelia, the Chełmno (Kulm) land, Malbork (Marienburg), Elbląg (Elbing) and the prince-bishopric of Warmia (Ermland) to Poland. Royal Prussia enjoyed considerable autonomy: it had its own estates' assembly, treasury and coinage, and its great cities — above all Gdańsk — were prosperous centres of the Baltic grain trade conducted largely in German and Dutch.

The Union of Lublin (1569) tightened ties with the Commonwealth while the province retained distinctive privileges. Its wealth made it a prize in the Polish–Swedish wars of the 17th century. In the First Partition of Poland (1772), Frederick the Great annexed most of Royal Prussia to his kingdom as the province of West Prussia, linking Brandenburg with East Prussia by land; Gdańsk and Toruń followed in the Second Partition (1793).

Legacy

The distinction between Royal and Ducal Prussia shaped centuries of Polish–German territorial dispute, culminating in the interwar "Polish Corridor" controversy, since West Prussia's territory largely corresponded to the corridor granted to Poland after the First World War.
