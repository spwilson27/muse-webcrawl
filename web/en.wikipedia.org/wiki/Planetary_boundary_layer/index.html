Planetary boundary layer

In meteorology, the planetary boundary layer (PBL), also known as the atmospheric boundary layer (ABL), is the lowest part of the atmosphere, whose behaviour is directly influenced by contact with a planetary surface. On Earth it is the layer in which friction, turbulence, and exchanges of heat, moisture, and momentum with the surface shape the flow, and it responds to surface forcing on a timescale of about an hour or less.

Structure and diurnal cycle

Over land on a sunny day, solar heating generates a turbulent convective mixed layer that can grow to 1–3 km deep, capped by an entrainment zone. At night the surface cools, turbulence decays, and a shallow stable boundary layer forms near the ground beneath a residual layer. Over the oceans the boundary layer varies much less through the day.

Significance

The boundary layer controls the dispersion of air pollution, the formation of fog and low cloud, near-surface winds relevant to aviation and wind energy, and the fluxes that couple the surface to the free troposphere in weather and climate models, where its turbulence must be parameterized rather than resolved.
