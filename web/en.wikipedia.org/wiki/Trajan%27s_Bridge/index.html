Trajan's Bridge (Romanian: Podul lui Traian; Serbian: Трајанов мост) was a Roman segmental arch bridge, the first bridge to be built over the lower Danube and one of the greatest achievements in Roman architecture. For more than a thousand years it was the longest arch bridge in the world, both in terms of overall and span length.

Construction

The bridge was constructed between 103 and 105 AD by the architect Apollodorus of Damascus, on the orders of Emperor Trajan, to supply Roman troops during the conquest of Dacia. It stood east of the Iron Gates, near the present-day cities of Kladovo in Serbia and Drobeta-Turnu Severin in Romania. The wooden superstructure rested on twenty masonry pillars of brick, mortar and pozzolana cement, which rose some 45 metres above the river; the arches spanning between them were built of wooden segmental arches.

History

The superstructure is believed to have been dismantled in the mid-3rd century, possibly by Emperor Aurelian as the Romans withdrew from Dacia, though other accounts attribute its destruction to Hadrian. The pillars remined visible for centuries; twenty were still recorded in 1856, but by the early 20th century only a few survived. Remains of the bridge, together with the Roman camp of Drobeta on the northern bank, are depicted on Trajan's Column in Rome.
