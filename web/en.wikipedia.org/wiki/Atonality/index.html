Atonality
Stylistic originsLate Romanticism, Expressionism
Emergencec. 1908, Vienna
Key figuresArnold Schoenberg, Alban Berg, Anton Webern

Atonality in its broadest sense describes music that lacks a tonal center, or key. In this sense it usually refers to music composed from about 1908 to the present day, particularly the works of the Second Viennese School, in which traditional functional harmony is avoided. The term was coined in 1911, apparently pejoratively, and was resisted by Arnold Schoenberg, who preferred to think of his music as pantonal or as the "emancipation of the dissonance."

History

Late Romantic composers such as Wagner, Liszt, and Mahler had already stretched tonality to its limits through extreme chromaticism — the opening of Wagner's Tristan und Isolde is a famous precursor. Around 1908 Schoenberg abandoned key signatures entirely in works such as the String Quartet No. 2 (whose finale sets a Stefan George poem proclaiming "I feel air from another planet") and the Three Piano Pieces, Op. 11 (1909). His monodrama Erwartung (1909) and the song cycle Pierrot Lunaire (1912) are landmarks of free atonality.

In the early 1920s Schoenberg systematised his practice into the twelve-tone technique, ordering all twelve pitch classes in a row to guarantee the avoidance of a tonal hierarchy. His pupils Alban Berg and Anton Webern developed the method in different directions; Berg's opera Wozzeck (1925), though largely freely atonal, became one of the most successful modernist operas.

Characteristics and criticism

Atonal music typically features wide melodic leaps, dense chromatic pitch content, avoidance of triadic harmony and cadential formulas, and motivic organisation based on small pitch-class sets. Critics have debated whether listeners can perceive coherence in atonal music; some theorists argue the term is misleading because such music often contains local centricity and referential sonorities even without functional tonality.
