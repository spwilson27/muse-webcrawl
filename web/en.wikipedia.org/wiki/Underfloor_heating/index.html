Underfloor heating

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Underfloor heating is a form of central heating that warms a building by heating the floor itself, which then radiates heat into the room. Two main technologies are used: hydronic systems, which circulate warm water through pipes embedded in or beneath the floor, and electric systems, which use resistance heating cables or mats. Because the whole floor acts as a large, low-temperature radiator, the system produces even warmth and eliminates radiators and cold spots.

History

Heating floors is ancient: the Korean ondol has been used for over a thousand years, and the Romans circulated hot air from fires through cavities under raised floors in the hypocaust system. Modern hydronic underfloor heating was developed in the 20th century, notably by Frank Lloyd Wright in buildings such as the Johnson Wax headquarters, and spread widely in northern Europe and Korea, where it is now the dominant form of residential heating.

Operation and efficiency

Hydronic systems typically run water at 30–45 °C, far cooler than radiator circuits, which pairs well with condensing boilers, heat pumps, and solar thermal sources and can cut energy use for the same comfort. The warm floor heats occupants directly by radiation and creates an ideal temperature profile — warmest at ankle level, cooler at head height. Disadvantages include slow response time because of the floor's thermal mass, higher installation cost, difficulty of retrofit, and the need to choose floor finishes with low thermal resistance; thick carpets reduce output. Electric systems are cheaper to install and popular for small areas such as bathrooms, but running costs are usually higher. The same hydronic loops can be used in reverse for underfloor cooling in summer.
