Glenn Theodore Seaborg (1912–1999) was an American chemist who co-discovered ten transuranium elements, including plutonium (1940–41), americium, curium, berkelium and californium, and proposed the actinide concept that reshaped the periodic table's bottom rows.

Seaborg shared the 1951 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Edwin McMillan, chaired the US Atomic Energy Commission (1961–71), and is the only person to have had an element named after him in his lifetime — seaborgium (Sg, 106) — famously letting him write his address in elements: Sg, Lr, Bk, Cf, Am…

[Image: Seaborg pointing at the periodic table's actinide row]

Plutonium

The plutonium work, secret during the Second World War, scaled microchemistry on tracer amounts into industrial production for the Manhattan Project; Seaborg devised the bismuth phosphate process used at Hanford.

Actinides

Placing the heavy elements in a second f-block series (like the lanthanides) predicted the chemistry of elements not yet made and guided their synthesis — a rare case of the table being redesigned by an element-maker.
