The Time Machine

The Time Machine is a post-apocalyptic science fiction novella by English author H. G. Wells, published in 1895. Generally credited with popularising the concept of time travel by means of a vehicle or device, it coined the term "time machine" and is a foundational work of science fiction.

Plot

A Victorian scientist known only as the Time Traveller demonstrates a model time machine to his dinner guests, then departs in the full-sized machine. He travels to the year AD 802,701, where humanity has diverged into two species: the gentle, childlike, surface-dwelling Eloi, who live in idle comfort among the ruins of civilisation, and the brutish, subterranean Morlocks, who tend machinery underground and emerge at night to prey upon the Eloi. The Time Traveller befriends an Eloi woman, Weena, recovers his stolen machine from the Morlocks, and flees further into the future, witnessing the dying Earth under an enormous red sun before returning to his own time. Three years after recounting his story, he departs again and never returns.

Themes and influence

Wells, a socialist, used the Eloi–Morlock divide as a satirical extrapolation of the class divisions of Victorian Britain — the effete descendants of the rich above, the degraded descendants of labourers below. The novella established tropes of the genre, including the far-future dying Earth, and has been adapted repeatedly, notably in George Pal's 1960 film and the 2002 film directed by Wells's great-grandson Simon Wells.
