Lord George Gordon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lord George Gordon Born: 26 December 1751, London, England
Died: 1 November 1793 (aged 41), Newgate Prison, London
Known for: Gordon Riots of 1780
Occupation: Politician, agitator

Lord George Gordon (26 December 1751 – 1 November 1793) was a British politician and agitator best remembered as the instigator of the Gordon Riots of 1780, the most destructive riots in the history of London. A younger son of Cosmo Gordon, 3rd Duke of Gordon, he was an eccentric and unpredictable figure whose fervent anti-Catholic campaigning brought him national notoriety.

Early life and career

Gordon was educated at Eton and briefly served in the Royal Navy before entering Parliament in 1774 as member for Ludgershall. In the House of Commons he quickly gained a reputation for erratic, attention-seeking interventions, attacking both government and opposition with equal vigour.

The Protestant Association and the Gordon Riots

In 1779 Gordon became president of the Protestant Association, which campaigned for the repeal of the Papists Act 1778, a measure that had relaxed some of the legal restrictions on Roman Catholics. On 2 June 1780 he led a vast crowd, estimated at between 40,000 and 60,000 people, to Westminster to present a petition against the Act. The demonstration degenerated into several days of rioting: Catholic chapels and homes were burned, prisons including Newgate were broken open, and the Bank of England was attacked. The disturbances were suppressed by troops, with several hundred people killed.

Trial and later years

Gordon was arrested and tried for high treason in 1781, but was acquitted, largely thanks to the defence of his cousin, Thomas Erskine. In later years he converted to Judaism, taking the name Yisrael bar Avraham Gordon, and grew a beard in accordance with Jewish custom. In 1788 he was convicted of libelling the French ambassador and Marie Antoinette and imprisoned in Newgate, where he died of typhoid fever in 1793, still a prisoner because he could not find sureties for his good behaviour.

Legacy

The Gordon Riots remain a byword for mob violence in British history, and Charles Dickens dramatised them in his novel Barnaby Rudge (1841), in which Gordon appears as a deluded but charismatic figure.
