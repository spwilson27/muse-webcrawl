Helmholtz free energy

In thermodynamics, the Helmholtz free energy (or Helmholtz energy) is a thermodynamic potential that measures the useful work obtainable from a closed thermodynamic system at a constant temperature. It is defined as

A = U − TS

where A is the Helmholtz free energy, U is the internal energy of the system, T is the absolute temperature, and S is the entropy. The concept was developed by the German physicist Hermann von Helmholtz in the 1880s; the symbol A derives from the German word Arbeit (work).

Interpretation

The change in Helmholtz free energy during a process equals the maximum amount of work the system can perform at constant temperature: ΔA = Wmax. For a spontaneous process at constant temperature and volume, A decreases, reaching a minimum at equilibrium. The Helmholtz energy is the natural potential for systems held at constant temperature and volume, just as the Gibbs free energy — defined as G = H − TS — is natural at constant temperature and pressure, which is why Gibbs energy is more often used in chemistry.

Statistical mechanics

In statistical mechanics the Helmholtz free energy connects the macroscopic system to its microscopic partition function Z through A = −kBT ln Z, from which all thermodynamic properties of the system can be derived. It also appears in the thermodynamic integration methods used to compute free-energy differences in molecular simulation.
