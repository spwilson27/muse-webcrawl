Jan Hus (c. 1369 – 6 July 1415), often called John Hus, was a Czech theologian, priest, and rector of Charles University in Prague who became a church reformer and the inspiration of the Hussite movement. His teachings, strongly influenced by John Wycliffe, anticipated the Protestant Reformation by a century.

Reform preaching

Ordained around 1400, Hus preached in Czech at Prague's Bethlehem Chapel, denouncing clerical corruption, simony, and the sale of indulgences, and arguing that the Church should be reformed according to Scripture. His attacks on the hierarchy and support for communion in both kinds for the laity made him popular with Czech audiences and alarming to Rome; he was excommunicated and in 1412 left Prague under pressure.

Council of Constance

In 1414 Hus travelled to the Council of Constance under a safe-conduct from Emperor Sigismund, expecting to defend his views. He was instead arrested, tried for heresy, and — refusing to recant opinions he said he had never held — burned at the stake on 6 July 1415.

Aftermath

His execution provoked outrage in Bohemia and launched the Hussite Wars (1419–1434), in which Hussite armies repeatedly defeated crusading forces. Hus is honoured as a martyr and national hero in the Czech lands; 6 July is a public holiday in the Czech Republic, and Pope John Paul II in 1999 expressed "deep regret" for his death.
