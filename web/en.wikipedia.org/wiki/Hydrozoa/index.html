Hydrozoa

Hydrozoa (hydrozoans; from Ancient Greek hydōr "water" and zōia "animals") is a taxonomic class of individually very small, predatory animals, some solitary and some colonial, most of which live in salt water. The colonies of the colonial species can be large, and in some cases the specialized individual animals cannot survive outside the colony. A few genera within this class live in freshwater habitats. Hydrozoans are related to jellyfish and corals and belong to the phylum Cnidaria. The class includes about 3,800 recognized species.

Anatomy and life cycle

Most hydrozoans have both a polyp stage and a medusa stage in their life cycle, though some lack one or the other. Polyps are typically attached, tubular animals with a mouth surrounded by tentacles bearing stinging cells (cnidocytes); colonies share a common gastrovascular system. Medusae, when present, are small, bell-shaped, and usually possess a velum — a shelf of tissue projecting inward from the bell margin — which distinguishes hydrozoan medusae from those of scyphozoans. Fertilization produces a free-swimming planula larva that settles to found a new colony.

Notable examples

Well-known hydrozoans include the freshwater genus Hydra, which has no medusa stage and is a classic laboratory organism; the colonial siphonophores such as the Portuguese man o' war (Physalia physalis) and Praya dubia, among the longest animals known; the by-the-wind sailor (Velella velella); fire corals (Millepora), which secrete a calcareous skeleton; and the "immortal jellyfish" Turritopsis dohrnii, whose medusa can revert to the polyp stage through transdifferentiation.

Ecology

Hydrozoans are predators of small planktonic crustaceans and fish larvae and are themselves eaten by sea slugs, turtles, and other animals. Colonial forms commonly grow on seaweed, shells, rocks, and other organisms; some species are symbionts, and a few are parasites. Their stinging cells can deliver painful stings to humans, most notoriously in the Portuguese man o' war.
