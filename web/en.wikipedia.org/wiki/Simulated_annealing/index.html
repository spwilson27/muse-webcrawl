Simulated annealing

Simulated annealing
Type: Metaheuristic / optimisation algorithm
Introduced: Kirkpatrick, Gelatt & Vecchi (1983)
Inspired by: Annealing in metallurgy

Simulated annealing (SA) is a probabilistic technique for approximating the global optimum of a given function. It is a metaheuristic widely used for discrete optimisation problems — such as the travelling salesman problem and VLSI placement — where the search space is large and exact methods are impractical.

Overview

The method is inspired by annealing in metallurgy, in which a material is heated and slowly cooled so its atoms settle into a low-energy crystalline state. The algorithm maintains a current candidate solution and a "temperature" parameter that decreases over time. At each step it proposes a neighbouring solution; better solutions are always accepted, while worse ones are accepted with a probability that depends on how much worse they are and on the temperature — following the Metropolis criterion, exp(−ΔE/T). Early on, high temperature lets the search wander widely and escape local optima; as it cools, the search settles.

Cooling schedule

Performance depends heavily on the cooling schedule: geometric schedules (multiplying T by a factor such as 0.95–0.99 each step) are common. Cooling too fast ("quenching") freezes the search in poor local optima; sufficiently slow cooling guarantees convergence to a global optimum in theory, though at impractical cost.

Applications

Simulated annealing has been applied to circuit design, scheduling, protein folding, image processing, and machine-learning hyperparameter search, and it remains a benchmark against which newer metaheuristics are compared.
