Gonbad-e Qabus

LocationGonbad-e Kavus, Golestan Province, Iran
Built1006–1007 AD
HeightAbout 53 metres (72 m including the hill)
Commissioned byQabus ibn Wushmagir, Ziyarid emir
StatusUNESCO World Heritage Site (2012)

Gonbad-e Qabus is a monumental tower tomb in the city of Gonbad-e Kavus in north-eastern Iran, built in 1006–07 for Qabus ibn Wushmagir, the Ziyarid emir of Gorgan, a ruler renowned as a scholar and patron of letters at whose court the physician-philosopher Avicenna worked and for whom the scholar al-Biruni dedicated his chronology of ancient nations. It is among the earliest surviving dated Islamic monuments in Iran and gives its name to the modern city.

Architecture

The tower is built entirely of unglazed baked brick. Its body is a ten-pointed star in plan: a cylinder articulated by ten triangular flanges, rising about 37 metres and capped by a conical roof, the whole structure standing on an artificial hill for an overall height of some 72 metres. The interior is a plain hollow cylinder lit only by the single eastern doorway. Two bands of Kufic inscription in brick, just below the roof and above the door, record the founder's name and the date of construction in both solar and lunar years. Legend long held that the emir's coffin of glass was suspended within the dome, though excavations found no burial chamber in the tower itself.

Significance

Gonbad-e Qabus is the prototype and finest example of the tomb-tower tradition that spread across Iran, Anatolia and Central Asia. It was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2012, a thousand years after its construction, in recognition of its outstanding testimony to early Islamic architecture and the cultural exchange between the nomads of Central Asia and the civilisation of ancient Iran.
