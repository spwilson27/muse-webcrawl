Dom Paul Bellot (7 June 1876 – 5 November 1944) was a French Benedictine monk and architect, one of the most influential designers of Catholic churches in the first half of the twentieth century. Working in brick and reinforced concrete, he developed a modern ecclesiastical architecture of parabolic arches and patterned brick vaulting that spread from France to the Netherlands and French Canada, where his influence on Quebec church building was so deep that the style is still called bellotisme.

Life

Bellot studied architecture at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris, graduating in 1900, and in 1901 entered the Benedictine abbey of Solesmes, then in exile. He pronounced his vows at Quarr Abbey on the Isle of Wight, England, and soon became the Solesmes congregation's architect. His first major work was the priory church of Saint-Léger at Oosterhout in the Netherlands (begun 1906), followed by Quarr Abbey church itself (1911–1912), built in early Christian and Byzantine-inspired brick forms that caused a sensation in English architectural circles.

Style and major works

Bellot rejected both Gothic revival copying and bare functionalism. Drawing on Byzantine, Romanesque and Moorish sources and on the structural rationalism of Viollet-le-Duc, he built churches whose parabolic concrete arches and polychrome brick vaults were honestly expressed. Major works include the church of Notre-Dame at Audincourt (1932), the abbey church of Saint-Joseph de Clairval at Solignac, and numerous parish churches in France and the Netherlands.

Invited to Canada in 1937 to advise on the completion of Saint Joseph's Oratory in Montreal, Bellot settled in Quebec during the Second World War and designed or advised on dozens of churches across the province — including the abbey church of Saint-Benoît-du-Lac — training a generation of Quebec architects. He died in Montreal in 1944.
