Futurology

Futurology, also called futures studies or foresight, is the systematic and interdisciplinary study of possible, probable, and preferable futures, and of the worldviews and assumptions underlying them. Futurologists explore how present-day trends, technologies, and choices might develop over years to decades, aiming less to predict a single future than to map a space of alternatives that can inform planning and policy.

History

Although speculation about the future is ancient — from utopian literature to the forecasts of Jules Verne and H. G. Wells — futurology emerged as a formal field after the Second World War. The German jurist Ossip K. Flechtheim coined the term "futurology" in 1943. In the United States, the RAND Corporation developed the Delphi method of structured expert forecasting in the 1950s for military planning, and Herman Kahn popularised scenario thinking with studies of nuclear war and works such as The Year 2000 (1967). The Club of Rome's The Limits to Growth (1972) used computer modelling to examine global resource futures, while Shell's use of scenario planning in the 1970s, associated with Pierre Wack, established the discipline's credibility in corporate strategy.

Methods

Futurologists typically distinguish possible, plausible, probable, and preferable futures (the "futures cone"). Core techniques include environmental and horizon scanning for weak signals, trend analysis and extrapolation, expert elicitation via the Delphi method, cross-impact analysis, backcasting from desired futures, and the construction of multiple contrasting scenarios. Wild cards — low-probability, high-impact events — are treated explicitly to guard against overconfident extrapolation.

Criticism

Critics charge that futurology is not a science, that its long-range forecasts often fail (flying cars and moon bases by 2000 being stock examples), and that trend projection misses discontinuity and human agency. Practitioners respond that the field's purpose is preparedness rather than prophecy: a scenario need not come true to be useful in stress-testing decisions.
