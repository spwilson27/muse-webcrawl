Charles Maurice de Talleyrand-Perigord, 1st Prince of Benevento (1754–1838), was a French diplomat and statesman who served under successive regimes — the Ancien Régime, the French Revolution, Napoleon Bonaparte, and the Bourbon Restoration — earning a reputation as one of the most skillful and cynical diplomats in European history. He is often regarded as the archetype of the pragmatic survivor in politics.

Talleyrand, Prince of Benevento

Early life and the Church

Born into an old aristocratic family, Talleyrand was prevented by a childhood injury (often attributed to a fall) that left him with a club foot from pursuing the military career expected of the eldest son. He was instead directed toward the Church, studying theology at the Sorbonne and being ordained a priest in 1779. In 1789 he became Bishop of Autun, despite a famously worldly lifestyle that sat awkwardly with his clerical office.

Revolution and exile

As a deputy of the clergy to the Estates-General of 1789, Talleyrand supported the Revolution's early reforms, proposing the nationalization of Church lands and helping draft the Declaration of the Rights of Man. Excommunicated by the Pope for his role in the Civil Constitution of the Clergy, he resigned his bishopric. During the Reign of Terror he lived in exile in England and the United States, returning to France after Robespierre's fall.

Foreign minister under Napoleon

Talleyrand became foreign minister in 1797 and played a key role in bringing Napoleon to power in the coup of 18 Brumaire. As Napoleon's chief diplomat he negotiated treaties across Europe, though he increasingly opposed the Emperor's ambitions, secretly advising Austria and Russia. He resigned in 1807 and was later implicated in intrigues against Napoleon. Napoleon famously called him "a silk stocking filled with mud." The XYZ Affair of 1797–98, involving French agents demanding bribes from American envoys, occurred under his ministry and poisoned Franco-American relations.

Congress of Vienna and later career

After Napoleon's fall in 1814, Talleyrand headed the provisional government and represented defeated France at the Congress of Vienna, where by exploiting divisions among the victorious powers he secured remarkably lenient terms for France. He briefly served as prime minister under Louis XVIII in 1815. In 1830 he supported Louis-Philippe and served as ambassador to London, helping secure the independence of Belgium. He died in Paris in 1838, reconciling with the Church on his deathbed.

Legacy

Talleyrand's name became synonymous with diplomatic cunning and political flexibility. His memoirs, published posthumously, remain a classic of diplomatic literature. Critics have called him treacherous and corrupt — he amassed a huge fortune from bribes — while admirers credit him with consistently serving France's long-term interests above any regime.
