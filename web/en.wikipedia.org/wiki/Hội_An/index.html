Hội An (Vietnamese: [hôjˀ aːn]), formerly known in the Western world as Faifo or Faifoo, is a city of approximately 120,000 people in Vietnam's Quảng Nam Province, on the coast of the South China Sea. It is recognised as a well-preserved example of a Southeast Asian trading port dating from the 15th to the 19th century, and its Old Town was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1999.

History

Early history

The area around Hội An was settled by the Cham people, and the nearby port of the Champa kingdom was known as Lâm Ấp Phố. Excavations have also revealed traces of the earlier Sa Huỳnh culture. From the 7th to the 10th centuries, the Cham controlled the strategic spice trade in the region.

Trading port

By the late 16th century, under the Nguyễn lords, Hội An became one of the busiest international ports in Southeast Asia. Merchants from China, Japan, the Netherlands, and India traded in silk, fabrics, paper, porcelain, tea, and sugar. A Japanese quarter (Nihonmachi) developed in the early 17th century; the famous Japanese Covered Bridge (Chùa Cầu), built around 1593–1595, is its most enduring symbol. Christian missionaries, including Alexandre de Rhodes, were active in the port in this period.

Decline and preservation

In the late 18th century the Tây Sơn rebellion and the silting of the Thu Bồn River estuary undermined the port's importance, and trade shifted to Đà Nẵng. Paradoxically, this decline preserved Hội An's Old Town: unlike other Vietnamese cities, it escaped both heavy colonial redevelopment and destruction during the wars of the 20th century.

Modern era

In 1999 the Old Town was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The city has since become one of Vietnam's most popular tourist destinations, renowned for its monthly lantern festival, tailor shops, and cuisine, including cao lầu noodles and bánh mì. In 2023 Hội An joined the UNESCO Creative Cities Network for crafts and folk art.

Geography and climate

Hội An lies on the banks of the Thu Bồn River, about 30 km south of Đà Nẵng. The city includes the Chàm Islands (Cù Lao Chàm), a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve offshore. It has a tropical monsoon climate, with a rainy season from September to January that brings periodic flooding to the Old Town.

Landmarks

  • Japanese Covered Bridge (Chùa Cầu) – a covered wooden bridge built by the Japanese community in the 1590s, featured on the 20,000-đồng banknote.
  • Tan Ky House – a well-preserved merchant's house from the 18th century.
  • Cantonese Assembly Hall (Quảng Triệu) – built in 1885 by overseas Chinese merchants.
  • Phung Hung House and Quan Thang House – notable historic residences.
  • An Bàng Beach and Cửa Đại Beach – popular coastal areas within the city.

Culture

The Full Moon Lantern Festival is held on the 14th day of each lunar month, when electric lights in the Old Town are switched off and the river is filled with floating lanterns. The city is also famous for its tailors, who can produce custom garments within a day or two, and for lantern-making workshops.
