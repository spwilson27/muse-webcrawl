Atmosphere of Earth
Composition: 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, 0.9% argon
Surface pressure: 101.325 kPa
Kármán line: 100 km

The atmosphere of Earth is the layer of gases, commonly known as air, retained by Earth's gravity that surrounds the planet. It protects life by absorbing ultraviolet radiation, warming the surface through the greenhouse effect, reducing temperature extremes, and supplying oxygen for respiration and carbon dioxide for photosynthesis.

Composition

Dry air consists of about 78.09% nitrogen, 20.95% oxygen, 0.93% argon and small amounts of carbon dioxide (about 0.04%) and other trace gases, plus variable water vapour averaging around 1%.

Structure

The atmosphere is divided by temperature into the troposphere (where weather occurs, up to about 12 km), stratosphere (containing the ozone layer), mesosphere, thermosphere and exosphere. There is no sharp upper boundary; the Kármán line at 100 km is commonly used as the start of space, and air density falls so that 99.99997% of the atmosphere lies below 100 km.

Evolution

Earth's early atmosphere, produced by volcanic outgassing, contained little free oxygen. Photosynthetic cyanobacteria oxygenated the air in the Great Oxidation Event about 2.4 billion years ago, enabling aerobic life and the formation of the ozone layer.
