Mechanised agriculture

Mechanised agriculture is the process of using agricultural machinery to mechanise the work of agriculture, greatly increasing farm worker productivity. In modern times, powered machinery has replaced many farm jobs formerly carried out by manual labour or by working animals such as oxen, horses and mules.

The entire history of agriculture contains many examples of the use of tools, such as the hoe and the plough. The ongoing integration of machines since the Industrial Revolution, however, has allowed farming to become much less labour-intensive.

History

Mechanisation began with devices such as the seed drill, invented by Jethro Tull in 1701, which planted seeds in neat rows. The threshing machine, reaper and steel plough followed in the 19th century. Steam-powered traction engines gave way to the internal-combustion tractor in the early 20th century, and the combine harvester merged reaping and threshing into a single operation.

Applications

Modern mechanised farming uses tractors, combine harvesters, planters, sprayers, balers and irrigation systems. Precision agriculture adds GPS guidance, yield mapping and variable-rate application of seed and fertiliser. Mechanisation is credited with enabling a small fraction of the population to feed the rest, though it also raises concerns about soil compaction, fuel use, rural employment and the consolidation of farms.
