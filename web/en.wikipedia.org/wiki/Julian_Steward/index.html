Julian Steward

Julian Haynes Steward
Born: 31 January 1902, Washington, D.C.
Died: 6 February 1972, Urbana, Illinois
Known for: Cultural ecology; multilinear evolution
Fields: Anthropology

Julian Haynes Steward (January 31, 1902 – February 6, 1972) was an American anthropologist best known for developing the theory and method of cultural ecology and for his theory of multilinear evolution. A student of Alfred Kroeber and Robert Lowie at the University of California, Berkeley, he carried out fieldwork among the Shoshone and Paiute peoples of the Great Basin.

Work

In Basin-Plateau Aboriginal Sociopolitical Groups (1938) Steward showed how the arid environment and the technology available to Great Basin peoples shaped their small, mobile family-level social organisation. At the Smithsonian Institution he edited the monumental seven-volume Handbook of South American Indians (1946–1959). His book Theory of Culture Change (1955) set out cultural ecology — the study of how a society's subsistence technology interacts with its environment — and argued that cultures evolve along multiple, divergent lines rather than through a single universal sequence. He later directed the Puerto Rico Project, published as The People of Puerto Rico (1956), a pioneering study of a whole modern society.

Legacy

Teaching at Columbia University and the University of Illinois, Steward trained a generation of influential anthropologists, including Eric Wolf, Sidney Mintz and Marvin Harris, and his work helped shift American anthropology toward ecological and materialist explanation.
