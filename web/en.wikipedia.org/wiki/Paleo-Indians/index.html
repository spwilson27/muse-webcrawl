Paleo-Indians (also spelled Paleoindians) were the first peoples who entered and subsequently inhabited the Americas near the end of the last glacial period. The prefix paleo- comes from the Greek palaios, "old". Archaeologists apply the term to the earliest well-attested human cultures of the New World, characterised in North America by large, fluted stone projectile points used to hunt Ice Age megafauna.

Peopling of the Americas

Genetic and archaeological evidence indicates that the ancestors of Native Americans diverged from Siberian populations and crossed into Alaska via the Bering land bridge (Beringia), with widely discussed dates clustering between about 20,000 and 14,000 years ago. Movement south was possible along an ice-free corridor between the Cordilleran and Laurentide ice sheets and/or along the Pacific coast by boat — the "kelp highway" hypothesis. Sites such as Monte Verde in Chile (c. 14,500 years old) demonstrate human presence far to the south by the close of the Pleistocene; claims for much older sites, such as the human footprints at White Sands, New Mexico, continue to be debated.

Cultures and technology

The Clovis culture, identified by its distinctive fluted lanceolate points, spread across much of North America between roughly 13,200 and 12,800 years ago and was long treated as the first American culture. "Pre-Clovis" evidence has since moved the initial settlement earlier, though Clovis remains a pivotal horizon. Following the extinction of most North American megafauna around the end of the Younger Dryas, the Folsom culture specialised in hunting the large bison Bison antiquus. Contemporary traditions included the eastern fluted-point cultures, the Western Stemmed tradition, and in South America the fishtail-point cultures of the southern cone.

Lifestyle

Paleo-Indians were highly mobile hunter-gatherers living in small bands. Their toolkit centred on bifacial stone points and knives, endscrapers, gravers, and tools of bone and ivory; they used atlatls (spear-throwers) and constructed seasonal camps near water and game. Their prey included mammoth, mastodon, giant bison, camels, and horses. The extent to which human hunting ("overkill", in Paul Martin's hypothesis) versus climate change caused the late-Pleistocene extinctions remains contested. Burials are rare but informative, notably the Anzick child burial in Montana, the only known Clovis burial, and the Upward Sun River infants in Alaska.
