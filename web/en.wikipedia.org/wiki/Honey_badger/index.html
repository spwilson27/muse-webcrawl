Honey badger

Honey badger
Mellivora capensis
Family: Mustelidae
Conservation status: Least Concern

The honey badger (Mellivora capensis), also known as the ratel, is a mammal widely distributed in Africa, Southwest Asia, and the Indian subcontinent. Despite its name, it resembles other mustelids (weasels, otters and wolverines) more than true badgers.

Description

Honey badgers are stocky, 23–28 cm at the shoulder and 9–16 kg in weight, with loose, remarkably thick skin, small eyes, and powerful foreclaws for digging. Adults have a broad grey or white mantle over black underparts. The skin's looseness lets the animal twist within it to face attackers that seize it by the scruff.

Behaviour and diet

Largely nocturnal and solitary, the ratel is an opportunistic carnivore famed for fearlessness and tenacity: it eats snakes (showing considerable resistance to venom), rodents, birds, reptiles, insects and carrion, digs up burrowing prey, and raids beehives for honey and bee larvae — a habit from which its name derives. The supposed mutualism with the greater honeyguide bird, long repeated, has little direct evidence.

Reputation

The honey badger has been celebrated in folklore and popular culture for aggression disproportionate to its size; Guinness World Records once named it the world's most fearless creature.
