Seed money

Seed money provides the earliest external capital for a new venture.

Seed money, also known as seed funding or seed capital, is a form of securities offering in which an investor invests capital in a startup company in exchange for an equity stake or a convertible note in the company. The term seed suggests that this is a very early investment, meant to support the business until it can generate cash of its own, or until it is ready for further investments.

Seed money can be used to pay for preliminary operations such as market research and product development. Investors in seed capital are often the company's founders themselves, their family and friends, angel investors, and occasionally venture capital firms operating dedicated seed funds.

Overview

A seed round is typically the first official equity funding stage for a new company, preceding the Series A round. At the seed stage a company may have little more than a founding team, a prototype, and early evidence of demand. Because the risk of failure is extremely high, seed investments are usually small relative to later rounds, historically ranging from tens of thousands of dollars to a few million dollars, though pre-seed and seed rounds have grown considerably in size since the 2010s.

Seed funding is often raised through instruments designed to defer valuation, such as convertible notes and simple agreements for future equity (SAFEs), popularised by the accelerator Y Combinator. These instruments convert into shares at a later priced round, usually at a discount or subject to a valuation cap.

Sources of seed capital

Common sources of seed money include:

  • Founders, family and friends — informal capital raised from the entrepreneur's personal network, sometimes called a "friends and family round".
  • Angel investors — wealthy individuals who invest their own money in early-stage companies, often providing mentorship as well as capital.
  • Seed-stage venture funds — professional funds specialising in very early investments.
  • Accelerators and incubators — programmes that provide a small amount of seed capital in exchange for equity, alongside mentoring and workspace.
  • Crowdfunding — raising small amounts from a large number of backers, including reward-based and equity crowdfunding platforms.
  • Government grants and loans — public programmes supporting innovation and small business formation.

Use of funds

Seed capital is generally used to reach the milestones needed to raise a larger round or reach profitability: building a minimum viable product, hiring initial employees, validating the business model, and acquiring early customers. Because seed money is the riskiest capital a company raises, investors typically expect the potential for very high returns to compensate for the large proportion of seed-stage companies that fail.
