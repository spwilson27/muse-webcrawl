Iambic tetrameter

Iambic tetrameter is a metre of poetry consisting of four iambic feet per line. An iamb is a metrical foot of two syllables, the first unstressed and the second stressed (da-DUM), so a line of iambic tetrameter follows the pattern da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM, totalling eight syllables in its regular form.

Examples

The metre is common in hymns, ballads, and shorter lyric poems. Famous examples include Andrew Marvell's "To His Coy Mistress" ("Had we but world enough and time"), and it is used extensively by Lord Byron in poems such as "She Walks in Beauty". John Milton's companion poems "L'Allegro" and "Il Penseroso" are written largely in iambic tetrameter couplets.

Character

Because it is shorter than iambic pentameter, iambic tetrameter tends to feel lighter, faster, and more songlike; it often appears in rhyming couplets or quatrains. In classical Greek and Latin poetry, the iambic tetrameter (often in catalectic form) was used in dramatic and satirical verse.

Related forms

Iambic tetrameter alternates with iambic trimeter in the common ballad stanza known as common metre, used in countless hymns and folk songs. Samuel Taylor Coleridge's "The Rime of the Ancient Mariner" adapts this ballad form.
