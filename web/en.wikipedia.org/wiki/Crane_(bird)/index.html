Crane (bird)

Cranes are large, long-legged and long-necked birds of wetlands and grasslands.

Cranes are a family, the Gruidae, of large, long-legged, and long-necked birds in the group Gruiformes. The 15 extant species are placed in four genera: Antigone, Balearica, Leucogeranus and Grus. They are found on every continent except Antarctica and South America, typically in shallow wetlands and open grasslands.

Description

Cranes range in height from the demoiselle crane, at about 90 cm (35 in), to the sarus crane, which can reach 176 cm (69 in) and is the tallest flying bird. Their plumage is generally grey or white, often with bare red skin on the face. Unlike herons, cranes fly with their necks extended straight, and their loud, trumpeting calls are produced by a long, coiled trachea. Many species perform elaborate dancing displays involving leaping, bowing and wing-flapping, used in courtship and social bonding.

Behaviour and ecology

Cranes are opportunistic feeders, eating plant material, grains, insects, small vertebrates and invertebrates. Most northern species are migratory, travelling long distances in V-shaped flocks between breeding grounds in northern wetlands and wintering areas further south. Cranes are monogamous and generally mate for life; both parents share in incubating one or two eggs and rearing the young, which stay with their parents until the following breeding season.

Conservation and culture

Habitat loss and hunting have made many crane species threatened; the whooping crane of North America and the Siberian crane are critically endangered, and intensive conservation programmes, including captive breeding and reintroduction, have been undertaken for them. Cranes hold a prominent place in the folklore and art of many cultures, symbolising longevity and good fortune in East Asia, and the red-crowned crane is a famous motif in Japanese art.
