Ileostomy

An ileostomy is a surgical opening (stoma) created by bringing the end or a loop of the small intestine — the ileum — out onto the surface of the abdomen. Intestinal waste passes out of the ileostomy and is collected in an external ostomy pouching system attached to the skin. The procedure diverts the fecal stream away from the colon, rectum, and anus, either temporarily while downstream bowel heals, or permanently when those organs have been removed or cannot function.

An ileostomy is usually sited on the lower right side of the abdomen.

Indications

Ileostomies are performed in the management of several conditions, including:

  • Ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease that do not respond to medical therapy;
  • Colorectal cancer requiring removal of the rectum or colon;
  • Familial adenomatous polyposis, an inherited condition producing hundreds of colonic polyps;
  • Bowel obstruction, perforation, trauma, or congenital defects of the intestine.

Types

An end ileostomy is created when the ileum is divided and its end brought through the abdominal wall; it is often permanent, for example after total proctocolectomy. A loop ileostomy brings a loop of ileum to the surface and opens it without fully dividing the bowel; it is commonly used as a temporary diversion to protect a healing anastomosis, such as an ileal pouch–anal anastomosis (J-pouch), and can later be reversed in a smaller operation.

Output and care

Because the colon — which normally absorbs water — is bypassed, ileostomy output is liquid to porridge-like, typically 600–1,200 mL per day once adapted, and contains digestive enzymes that irritate the skin. Care involves fitting an adhesive skin barrier and pouch, emptying the pouch several times a day, and monitoring fluid and electrolyte balance, particularly sodium and water, to avoid dehydration when output is high.

Complications

Possible complications include skin irritation, stoma retraction or prolapse, parastomal hernia, bowel obstruction, and high-output dehydration leading to acute kidney injury. Most are manageable with specialist stoma nursing care.
