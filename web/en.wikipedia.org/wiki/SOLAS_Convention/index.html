SOLAS Convention

The International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea (SOLAS) is an international maritime treaty that sets minimum safety standards in the construction, equipment and operation of merchant ships. The convention requires signatory flag states to ensure that ships flying their flag comply with those standards. The current version, adopted in 1974 and in force since 1980, is regularly updated through amendments adopted by the International Maritime Organization (IMO), using a "tacit acceptance" procedure that allows amendments to enter into force quickly.

History

The first SOLAS convention was adopted in 1914 in response to the sinking of the RMS Titanic in 1912, addressing lifeboat capacity, watertight subdivision and radio watchkeeping; it never fully entered into force because of the First World War. Successive versions followed in 1929, 1948 and 1960. The 1974 convention, now administered by the IMO, introduced tacit acceptance so that technical amendments no longer waited years for ratification.

Contents

SOLAS is divided into chapters covering, among other topics: survey and certification; construction, subdivision and fire protection; life-saving appliances (Chapter III); radiocommunications and the Global Maritime Distress and Safety System (Chapter IV); safety of navigation (Chapter V), which also binds ships of states not party to other chapters; carriage of cargoes and dangerous goods; nuclear ships; the International Safety Management (ISM) Code (Chapter IX); high-speed craft; maritime security measures (Chapter XI-2, which introduced the ISPS Code after 2001); and safe container handling, including the 2016 requirement for verified gross mass of packed containers.

Significance

SOLAS is generally regarded as the most important of all international treaties concerning the safety of merchant ships. Compliance is monitored through flag-state surveys and port state control inspections.
