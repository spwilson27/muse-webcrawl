Calydonian Boar

The Calydonian boar hunt, depicted on Greek pottery.

The Calydonian Boar is a monster in Greek mythology sent by the goddess Artemis to ravage the region of Calydon in Aetolia, because King Oeneus had neglected to honour her in his sacrifices. The hunt for the boar assembled many of the great heroes of the age and is one of the central episodes of Greek heroic legend.

The hunt

Oeneus appealed for help, and hunters gathered from across Greece, including Meleager, the king's son; the huntress Atalanta; Jason; Theseus; Peleus; Telamon; Castor and Pollux; and Amphiaraus. The boar was enormous, with tusks like scimitars, and it killed several hunters before Atalanta drew first blood with an arrow. Meleager finished the kill and awarded the hide to Atalanta, judging that she deserved it.

Aftermath

Meleager's uncles, resenting that a woman should receive the prize, seized the hide, and Meleager killed them in anger. His mother Althaea, grieving her brothers, burned the brand on which Meleager's life depended — a log that the Fates had declared would measure out his lifespan — and he died. The story is told by Homer in the Iliad, by Apollodorus, and at length in Ovid's Metamorphoses.

In art

The hunt was a favourite subject in ancient art, appearing on the François Vase and in a celebrated sculptural group, and was later treated by Peter Paul Rubens. The boar's tusks were said in antiquity to have been preserved in a temple at Tegea.
