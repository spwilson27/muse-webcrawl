Deinstitutionalisation

Deinstitutionalisation

FieldPsychiatry, public policy, disability rights
Periodc. 1955–present
Key legislationCommunity Mental Health Act (US, 1963); Mental Health Act (Italy, 1978)

Deinstitutionalisation (or deinstitutionalization) is the process of replacing long-stay psychiatric hospitals and other large residential institutions with less isolated community mental health services for those diagnosed with a mental disorder or developmental disability. In the twentieth century it led to the closure or downsizing of most of the world's large psychiatric asylums and institutions for people with intellectual disabilities.

History

The movement gathered pace in the 1950s and 1960s, driven by several converging factors: the introduction of antipsychotic medication such as chlorpromazine; exposés of inhumane conditions inside asylums; sociological critiques such as Erving Goffman's Asylums (1961), which described institutions as "total institutions"; civil rights activism; and governments' interest in reducing the cost of large hospital systems. In the United States, the state psychiatric hospital population peaked at about 559,000 in 1955 and fell dramatically over the following decades. President John F. Kennedy's Community Mental Health Act of 1963 envisaged a network of community mental health centers to replace custodial hospitals.

Italy went furthest: the 1978 "Basaglia Law", named after psychiatrist Franco Basaglia, ordered the closure of all psychiatric hospitals and their replacement with community-based services. The United Kingdom pursued a similar policy of "care in the community" from the 1960s onward, accelerating under the NHS and Community Care Act 1990.

Outcomes and criticism

Supporters argue that community care is more humane, less stigmatising and more respectful of patients' rights than confinement in large institutions. Outcomes for many former residents improved when adequate housing, treatment and social support were provided. However, critics contend that in many countries deinstitutionalisation outpaced the funding of community services, contributing to homelessness, untreated illness and "transinstitutionalisation" — the movement of people with mental illness into prisons and jails. In the United States, people with serious mental illness became heavily over-represented in the criminal justice system, a problem sometimes called the "criminalisation" of mental illness.

Disability deinstitutionalisation

A parallel movement closed large institutions for people with intellectual and developmental disabilities, influenced by the normalisation principle articulated by Bengt Nirje and Wolf Wolfensberger, and by litigation over abusive conditions such as those at Willowbrook State School in New York. The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006) affirms the right of disabled people to live independently and be included in the community.
