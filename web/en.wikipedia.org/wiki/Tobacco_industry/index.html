Tobacco industry

The tobacco industry comprises the companies and factories involved worldwide in the growth, preparation for sale, shipment, advertising, and distribution of tobacco and tobacco-related products. It is a global multibillion-dollar industry, dominated in the early twenty-first century by a small number of large transnational corporations.

Tobacco is an agricultural commodity product, similar in economic terms to agricultural foodstuffs: the price is in part determined by crop yields, which vary depending on local weather conditions, and in part by market speculation. Tobacco has become increasingly concentrated among fewer companies, as is common in mature global agricultural industries.

History

Europeans cultivated tobacco first in the Americas, in the present-day Caribbean, from where the smoking of tobacco spread to Europe and Asia. By the nineteenth century, cigars and cigarettes had become the dominant products. James Bonsack's cigarette-making machine, patented in 1881, dramatically increased production and led to the formation of large tobacco trusts such as the American Tobacco Company.

"Big Tobacco"

The term Big Tobacco is often used to describe the largest manufacturers, including Philip Morris International, British American Tobacco, Imperial Brands, Altria, and Japan Tobacco International. Together these companies control a large share of the world cigarette market, alongside the state-owned China National Tobacco Corporation, the largest producer of cigarettes in the world.

Regulation and litigation

Since the mid-twentieth century, scientific evidence linking smoking to lung cancer and other diseases has prompted advertising restrictions, health warnings, taxation, and litigation. In 1998, the major United States manufacturers signed the Master Settlement Agreement with 46 state attorneys general. The World Health Organization Framework Convention on Tobacco Control, adopted in 2003, set global standards for tobacco control.
