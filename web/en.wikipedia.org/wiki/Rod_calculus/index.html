Rod calculus

Rod calculus or rod calculation (Chinese: 筹算) was the mechanical method of doing arithmetic in ancient China using counting rods — small bamboo, ivory or iron sticks — arranged on a checkered board. It was China's main method of computation from the Warring States period through the Yuan dynasty, when it was gradually replaced by the faster abacus.

Counting rod numerals in their vertical and horizontal forms.

Counting rods

Rod numerals are a positional decimal system: digits 1–9 are formed from arrangements of rods, with vertical forms used for units, hundreds and ten-thousands places and horizontal forms for tens and thousands places, alternating to avoid ambiguity. Red rods represented positive numbers and black rods negative numbers — among the earliest uses of signed numbers in mathematics. A blank space served for zero before a written symbol appeared.

Operations

By moving rods on the board, calculators could perform addition, subtraction, multiplication and division, extract square and cube roots, and solve systems of simultaneous linear equations using procedures equivalent to Gaussian elimination, as recorded in the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art. Fraction arithmetic and polynomial equations were handled with great sophistication, culminating in works such as Zhu Shijie's Jade Mirror of the Four Unknowns (1303).

Spread and decline

Rod calculus spread to Korea and Japan, where counting-rod traditions (such as Japanese sangi) persisted into the Edo period. In China the abacus supplanted rods during the Ming dynasty, and the board method was largely forgotten, though its notation survived in Suzhou numerals.
