Whole grain

A whole grain is a grain of any cereal and pseudocereal that contains the endosperm, germ, and bran, in contrast to refined grains, which retain only the endosperm. As part of a general healthy diet, consumption of whole grains is associated with lower risk of several diseases.

Assorted whole grains: wheat, oats, barley, and brown rice.

Structure

The three parts of a grain kernel are:

  • Bran — the multi-layered outer skin, rich in fibre, B vitamins, and minerals.
  • Germ — the embryo, which if fertilised will sprout into a new plant; it contains B vitamins, some protein, minerals, and healthy fats.
  • Endosperm — the largest portion, providing energy in the form of starchy carbohydrate, plus proteins and small amounts of vitamins and minerals.

Refined grains are milled to remove the bran and germ, leaving only the endosperm. Enriched refined grains have some nutrients added back, but not the fibre or full nutrient profile of the whole grain.

Examples

Common whole grains include wheat, oats, barley, rye, corn, brown rice, wild rice, sorghum, quinoa, amaranth, buckwheat, and millet. Products labelled "whole wheat", "wholemeal", or "100% whole grain" must contain all three parts of the kernel in their original proportions.

Health effects

Whole grains are a source of dietary fibre, magnesium, and other nutrients. Diets rich in whole grains are associated with reduced risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and colorectal cancer, and with better weight management. Public health guidance in many countries recommends that at least half of daily grain intake come from whole grains.
