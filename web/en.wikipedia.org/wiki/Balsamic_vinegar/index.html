Balsamic vinegar

Balsamic vinegar (Italian: aceto balsamico) is a dark, concentrated, and intensely flavoured vinegar originating in Italy, made wholly or partially from grape must—freshly crushed grape juice including skins, seeds, and stems. The word balsamic refers to its original use as a soothing tonic or "balm".

Categories

Three tiers are recognised in European law. Traditional balsamic vinegar (Aceto Balsamico Tradizionale), granted DOP status for Modena and Reggio Emilia, is made only from cooked Trebbiano and Lambrusco grape must and aged at least 12 years (gold-label versions 25 years or more) in a succession of barrels of different woods—oak, chestnut, cherry, juniper, and others—in producers' attics. It is thick, glossy, and sweet-sour, used by the drop over Parmesan, strawberries, or finished dishes. Balsamic Vinegar of Modena IGP blends grape must with wine vinegar and may add caramel colour, aged a minimum of 60 days (or over three years for "invecchiato"). Commercial "balsamic" vinegars elsewhere imitate the style with sweetened wine vinegar.

Production

For traditional vinegar, must is simmered for many hours until concentrated, then fermented and acetified, and annually topped up through the barrel sequence (rincalzo), gradually concentrating as water evaporates. A consorzio of producers and official tasters grades each vintage before bottling in the distinctive 100 ml bottles.

History and use

References to grape-must vinegars near Modena date to the 11th century—one was famously given to Emperor Henry III in 1046—and the Este family prized it at court. It remained a local specialty until international demand surged in the 1980s and 1990s, when chefs popularised balsamic glazes and dressings worldwide.
