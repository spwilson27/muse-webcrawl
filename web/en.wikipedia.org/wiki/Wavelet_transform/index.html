Wavelet transform

In mathematics, a wavelet transform is the representation of a function by wavelets: wave-like oscillations that are localised in both time and frequency. Unlike the Fourier transform, which decomposes a signal into infinitely extended sinusoids, the wavelet transform uses scaled and translated copies of a finite-length "mother wavelet", making it well suited to analysing signals with transients or discontinuities.

Types

The continuous wavelet transform (CWT) computes coefficients as inner products of the signal with wavelets at all scales and positions, producing a time–scale representation. The discrete wavelet transform (DWT) samples scales and positions, typically on a dyadic grid, and can be implemented efficiently with filter banks; it is widely used in signal compression, including the JPEG 2000 image standard and the FBI's fingerprint compression standard.

History

The concept developed from early 20th-century work by Alfréd Haar (whose Haar wavelet of 1909 is the simplest wavelet) through the work of Jean Morlet and Alex Grossmann in the 1980s, and the multiresolution analysis theory of Stéphane Mallat and Yves Meyer. Ingrid Daubechies constructed compactly supported orthonormal wavelets (the Daubechies wavelets) in 1988, enabling practical digital applications.

Applications

Applications include data compression, denoising, feature detection, seismology, medical imaging, solving differential equations, and the analysis of financial and physiological time series.
