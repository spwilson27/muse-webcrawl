George Stevens

Born George Cooper Stevens, 18 December 1904, Oakland, California, U.S.
Died 8 March 1975 (aged 70), Lancaster, California, U.S.
Occupation Film director, producer, screenwriter, cinematographer

George Cooper Stevens (December 18, 1904 – March 8, 1975) was an American film director, producer, screenwriter and cinematographer. He is known for the films Swing Time (1936), The More the Merrier (1943), A Place in the Sun (1951), Shane (1953), Giant (1956), The Diary of Anne Frank (1959), and won the Academy Award for Best Director twice, for A Place in the Sun and Giant.

Early life and career

Stevens was born in Oakland, California, the son of actors Landers Stevens and Georgie Cooper; his uncle was the theatre critic Ashton Stevens. He learned about the theatre from his parents and worked on the stage as a child. At 17 he joined a touring company in a production of Jane Eyre. He broke into the film business as a cameraman, working for Hal Roach Studios on Laurel and Hardy shorts, and graduated to directing comedy shorts in the late 1920s and early 1930s.

His first feature was The Cohens and Kellys in Trouble (1933). He gained prominence at RKO with a run of popular films including Alice Adams (1935), Annie Oakley (1935), Swing Time (1936) with Fred Astaire and Ginger Rogers, Quality Street (1937), A Damsel in Distress (1937), Vigil in the Night (1940), Penny Serenade (1941), Woman of the Year (1942), and The Talk of the Town (1942).

War service

In 1943 Stevens joined the U.S. Army Signal Corps, heading a film unit that documented the war in Europe, including the Normandy landings, the liberation of Paris, and the meeting of American and Soviet forces on the Elbe. His unit filmed the Dachau concentration camp after its liberation; the footage was later used as evidence at the Nuremberg trials. The experience profoundly affected his subsequent work, which became more serious in tone.

Post-war career

After the war Stevens formed Liberty Films with Frank Capra and William Wyler. His post-war films, characterized by meticulous craftsmanship and long shooting schedules, included I Remember Mama (1948), A Place in the Sun (1951), an adaptation of Theodore Dreiser's An American Tragedy that won six Academy Awards, the classic Western Shane (1953), the epic Giant (1956) with Elizabeth Taylor, Rock Hudson, and James Dean, and The Diary of Anne Frank (1959). His final film was The Only Game in Town (1970). His large-scale biblical epic The Greatest Story Ever Told (1965) was a commercial disappointment.

Stevens served as president of the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences from 1958 to 1959 and of the Screen Directors Guild. He died of a heart attack in 1975. His son, George Stevens Jr., founded the American Film Institute.
