Ajax (play)

Ajax (Ancient Greek: Αἴας) is a tragedy by the Greek playwright Sophocles, one of the seven of his plays to survive complete. Written in the 5th century BCE — it is generally considered one of his earlier works, possibly from the 440s BCE — it dramatises the disgrace and suicide of the hero Ajax after the Trojan War.

Synopsis

After the death of Achilles, his armour is awarded to Odysseus rather than to Ajax, the mightiest remaining Greek warrior. Humiliated and enraged, Ajax plans to murder the Greek commanders, but the goddess Athena drives him mad and he slaughters the army's livestock instead, believing them to be his enemies. Recovering his senses and overwhelmed by shame, Ajax ignores the pleas of his captive wife Tecmessa and, in a famous deception speech, pretends to accept his lot before going off alone and falling on the sword given him by Hector. The second half of the play concerns the dispute over his burial: Menelaus and Agamemnon refuse it, but Odysseus magnanimously persuades them to allow the rites, and Ajax's half-brother Teucer buries him.

Themes

The play explores heroic honour (timē), the clash between the individualistic heroic code and the needs of the community, madness, and the proper treatment of the dead. Ajax's unchanging nature — noble but rigid in a world that has moved on — is a classic study of the tragic hero, while Odysseus embodies flexibility and empathy.

Reception

Ajax has been continuously read and adapted since antiquity; its portrayal of a traumatised warrior has made it a touchstone in modern discussions of combat trauma and veterans, including performances for military audiences.
