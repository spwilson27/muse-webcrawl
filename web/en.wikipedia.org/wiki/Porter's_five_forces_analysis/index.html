Porter's five forces analysis

Porter's five forces analysis is a framework for analyzing the level of competition within an industry and business strategy development. It was created by Michael E. Porter of Harvard Business School and published in his 1979 Harvard Business Review article "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy", followed by his 1980 book Competitive Strategy. The framework draws upon industrial organization economics to derive five forces that determine the competitive intensity and therefore the attractiveness (profit potential) of an industry.

The five forces

Threat of new entrants

Profitable markets attract new entrants, which erodes profitability unless incumbents are protected by barriers to entry such as economies of scale, capital requirements, brand loyalty, access to distribution channels, government regulation and proprietary technology.

Threat of substitute products or services

Substitutes that perform the same function by different means place a ceiling on the prices an industry can charge. The threat is high when substitutes offer an attractive price-performance trade-off and buyers face low switching costs.

Bargaining power of buyers

Powerful customers can capture more value by forcing down prices, demanding better quality or more service. Buyer power is high when buyers are concentrated, purchase in large volumes, face low switching costs, or can credibly threaten backward integration.

Bargaining power of suppliers

Powerful suppliers can charge higher prices or limit quality and availability, squeezing industry profitability. Supplier power is high when suppliers are more concentrated than the industry they sell to, offer differentiated inputs, or when the industry is not an important customer group.

Competitive rivalry among existing competitors

Rivalry — through price discounting, new product introductions, advertising campaigns and service improvements — limits profitability. It is most intense when there are numerous or equally balanced competitors, slow industry growth, high fixed costs, or high exit barriers.

Use and criticism

The framework is widely used in strategy consulting and business education to assess industry structure before formulating strategy. Critics argue it assumes relatively static market structures, gives little weight to cooperation and complementors (leading to proposals of a "sixth force"), and can oversimplify fast-moving, platform-based markets. Porter responded that the framework is a tool for understanding the underlying drivers of profitability rather than a checklist, and updated the approach in his 2008 article "The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy".
