Global governance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Global governance encompasses the totality of institutions, policies, norms, procedures and initiatives through which states and other actors coordinate and regulate transnational issues — peace and security, trade, finance, health, environment and digital activity — without a world government. The term rose to prominence after the Cold War (the Commission on Global Governance's 1995 report Our Global Neighbourhood) to describe a polycentric order: formal international organisations, treaty regimes, informal groupings, public–private partnerships, expert networks and private standard-setters interacting in "regime complexes".

Issues

Scholarship examines global governance's effectiveness (climate and pandemic governance as stress tests), its authority and accountability deficits (who governs the governors — chains of delegation to distant technocracies), and power asymmetries in agenda-setting between the West, emerging powers and the Global South. Contemporary debates include reform of the Security Council and Bretton Woods quotas, the fragmentation of internet governance, private actors (platforms, rating agencies) exercising public functions, and whether the system is converging, fragmenting into blocs, or hollowing out under great-power rivalry.
