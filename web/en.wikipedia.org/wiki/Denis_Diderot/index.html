Denis Diderot (5 October 1713 – 31 July 1784) was a French philosopher, art critic, and writer, best known for serving as co-founder, chief editor, and contributor to the Encyclopédie along with Jean le Rond d'Alembert. He was a prominent figure during the Age of Enlightenment.

Portrait of Denis Diderot by Louis-Michel van Loo, 1767.

Early life

Diderot was born in Langres, Champagne, the son of a master cutler. He was educated by the Jesuits and at the University of Paris, initially intended for the clergy or law, but instead devoted himself to writing and translation, living precariously in Paris.

The Encyclopédie

In 1747 Diderot was engaged to edit a French encyclopedia, which grew into the vast Encyclopédie, ou dictionnaire raisonné des sciences, des arts et des métiers, published between 1751 and 1772. Its 28 volumes (including plates) aimed to gather all human knowledge and to change the common way of thinking; it became the flagship publication of the Enlightenment, despite censorship, bans, and the withdrawal of its royal privilege in 1759, after which Diderot continued it semi-clandestinely.

Other works

Diderot's wide-ranging writings include the materialist dialogues D'Alembert's Dream and Jacques the Fatalist, the novel The Nun (La Religieuse), the philosophical tale Rameau's Nephew, pioneering art criticism in his Salons, and early statements of ideas resembling evolution in his Letter on the Blind (1749), for which he was briefly imprisoned at Vincennes.

Later life and legacy

In 1773–74 Diderot visited Catherine the Great in St. Petersburg, having sold her his library to relieve his finances. He died in Paris in 1784. Long overshadowed by Voltaire and Rousseau, he is now recognised as one of the most original and wide-ranging thinkers of the Enlightenment.
