Cult of saints


Votive offerings and candles at the shrine of a saint

The cult of saints is the body of devotional practices directed toward saints — holy men and women, especially martyrs — within Roman Catholicism, Eastern Orthodoxy, Oriental Orthodoxy, and some other Christian traditions. Devotion typically centres on veneration of the saint's memory, imitation of their virtues, prayers asking for their intercession, pilgrimage to their shrines, and the keeping of their feast days and relics. Theologians distinguish this veneration (dulia) from the worship (latria) due to God alone.

Origins

The cult began in the early centuries of Christianity with the commemoration of martyrs at their tombs on the anniversary of their death, understood as their "birthday" into heaven. By the fourth century, the cult had extended to confessors, monks, bishops and virgins, and basilicas were built over martyrs' graves. The circulation of collections of miracles and lives of saints helped spread local cults across Christendom.

Practices

  • Feast days in the liturgical calendar, with local patron saints receiving special solemnity.
  • Pilgrimage to shrines and tombs, such as those of St Peter in Rome, St James at Santiago de Compostela, and St Thomas Becket at Canterbury.
  • Relics — bodily remains and contact objects — preserved in reliquaries and carried in procession.
  • Intercessory prayer, novenas, votive offerings and ex-votos in thanks for favours received.
  • Patronage: saints act as patrons of places, trades, and causes, and against particular illnesses.

Criticism and reform

Humanist critics such as Erasmus mocked abuses surrounding relics and pilgrimage, and the Protestant Reformers of the sixteenth century largely rejected invocation of saints and pilgrimage, closing shrines and destroying images in many regions. The Council of Trent reaffirmed the Catholic practice while ordering abuses corrected. Comparable devotions exist in other religions, such as the veneration of Sufi saints in Islam and of bodhisattvas in Mahayana Buddhism.
