Edmund Barton

Sir Edmund Barton (18 January 1849 – 7 January 1920) was an Australian politician and judge who served as the first prime minister of Australia from 1901 to 1903. He held office as the leader of the Protectionist Party, before resigning in 1903 to become a founding justice of the High Court of Australia, on which he served until his death.

Early life

Barton was born in Glebe, Sydney, the ninth child of William Barton, a stockbroker, and Mary Louise Barton. He was educated at Fort Street High School and Sydney Grammar School, then studied at the University of Sydney, graduating with a Bachelor of Arts in 1868 and a Master of Arts in 1870. He was called to the bar in 1871 and built a successful legal practice.

Colonial politics

Barton entered the New South Wales Legislative Assembly in 1879 and later served in the Legislative Council. He was Speaker of the Legislative Assembly from 1883 to 1887 and Attorney-General of New South Wales on several occasions. During the 1890s he emerged as the leading campaigner for the federation of the Australian colonies, speaking at the Corowa Conference of 1893 and leading the New South Wales campaign in the federation referendums of 1898 and 1899. His slogan was "a nation for a continent and a continent for a nation."

Prime Minister (1901–1903)

After some controversy over the so-called "Hopetoun Blunder," in which the Governor-General, Lord Hopetoun, first commissioned William Lyne to form a government, Barton was appointed prime minister on 1 January 1901, the day the Commonwealth of Australia came into being. His government passed foundational legislation including the Immigration Restriction Act 1901 (the basis of the White Australia policy), the Pacific Island Labourers Act, the Commonwealth Franchise Act 1902 (which gave women the vote in federal elections and the right to stand for parliament — a world first in combination), and established the Commonwealth Public Service and the federal judiciary.

High Court and death

Barton resigned as prime minister in September 1903 to accept appointment as one of the three founding justices of the High Court, alongside Chief Justice Samuel Griffith and Richard O'Connor. He served on the court for over sixteen years, frequently acting as chief justice, and died of heart failure at Medlow Bath in the Blue Mountains on 7 January 1920.
