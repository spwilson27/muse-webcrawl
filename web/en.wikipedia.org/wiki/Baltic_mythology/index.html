Baltic mythology

Baltic mythology is the body of myths and religious beliefs of the Baltic peoples — the Lithuanians, Latvians and the now-extinct Old Prussians and related tribes — descending from Proto-Indo-European religion. The Baltic region was the last part of Europe to be Christianised (Lithuania formally adopted Christianity only in 1387), so Baltic paganism preserved unusually archaic features and was recorded comparatively late, partly by hostile chroniclers.

Deities

  • Dievas (Lithuanian) / Dievs (Latvian) — the sky god, from Proto-Indo-European *Dyēus;
  • Perkūnas / Pērkons — the thunder god, among the most important deities;
  • Žemyna / Zemes māte — the earth goddess;
  • Saulė / Saule — the sun goddess;
  • Mėnuo / Mēness — the moon god;
  • Velnias / Velns — a chthonic figure later identified with the Devil;
  • Laima — goddess of fate and childbirth.

Practices

Worship centred on sacred groves and hilltop altars, with offerings, sacred fires and — in the earliest accounts — occasional human sacrifice. The Old Prussians maintained a central sanctuary at Romuva with an ever-burning fire and a high priest, the Krivių Krivaitis. A rich oral tradition of songs (Lithuanian dainos, Latvian dainas) preserved mythological material into the modern era.

Revival

Since the 20th century, reconstructed Baltic pagan movements — Romuva in Lithuania and Dievturība in Latvia — have revived the old religion, gaining official recognition as traditional faith communities.
