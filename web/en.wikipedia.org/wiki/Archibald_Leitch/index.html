Archibald Leitch (27 April 1865 – 25 April 1939) was a Scottish architect and engineer, best known for designing football stadiums throughout the United Kingdom and Ireland in the early 20th century. His distinctive double-decker stands — with their criss-cross balcony trussing and ornate red-brick façades — became a hallmark of the British game.

Career

Leitch began as an industrial architect in Glasgow, designing factories and warehouses. A supporter of Rangers, he was commissioned to design the new Ibrox Park in 1899. Early in his career, tragedy struck when part of a wooden terrace at Ibrox collapsed during the Scotland–England match of 1902, killing 25 people. Leitch, who was present, was distraught but continued working; he redesigned Ibrox and patented a safer form of terracing barrier.

Stadium designs

Over the following decades Leitch's practice designed or remodelled many of Britain's most famous grounds, including:

  • Old Trafford (Manchester United)
  • Anfield (Liverpool) — the original Kop and Main Stand
  • Highbury (Arsenal)
  • Goodison Park (Everton)
  • Stamford Bridge (Chelsea)
  • White Hart Lane (Tottenham Hotspur)
  • Villa Park (Aston Villa)
  • Hampden Park (Glasgow)
  • Windsor Park (Belfast)
  • Craven Cottage (Fulham), including the listed Johnny Haynes Stand and cottage pavilion

His signature feature, the lattice-work balcony truss on double-decker stands, survives at a handful of grounds, most notably Goodison Park and Ibrox's Main Stand, which is now a listed structure.

Legacy

Although trained as an industrial engineer rather than a fine architect, Leitch effectively created the template for the British football stadium. Many of his stands were demolished in the all-seater redevelopments that followed the Taylor Report, but his surviving work is increasingly protected and celebrated.
