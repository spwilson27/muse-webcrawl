Tarot card reading


Cards laid out for a tarot reading

Tarot card reading is a form of cartomancy whereby practitioners use tarot cards to gain insight into the past, present or future. They formulate a question, then draw cards to interpret for that purpose. A standard tarot deck used for divination typically contains 78 cards: 22 major arcana, which depict archetypal figures and events, and 56 minor arcana in four suits.

History

Tarot cards originated in fifteenth-century Italy as a card game (tarocchini and French tarot remain popular games in Europe). Their occult use developed in late eighteenth-century France: Jean-Baptiste Alliette, writing as Etteilla, published the first guide to divinatory tarot in 1785 and produced the first deck designed expressly for occult purposes. Antoine Court de Gébelin had earlier claimed, without evidence, that the cards preserved ancient Egyptian wisdom. Later occultists such as Éliphas Lévi and Arthur Edward Waite integrated tarot into Western esotericism; Waite's Rider–Waite deck of 1909, illustrated by Pamela Colman Smith, became the most influential modern deck.

Practice

Readings commonly employ layouts ("spreads") in which each position has a meaning, such as the three-card past–present–future spread or the ten-card Celtic Cross. Cards drawn reversed (upside down) are often given modified or opposite interpretations. Readers vary in approach from fortune-telling claims to reflective, counselling-style use in which the imagery is a prompt for the client's own reflection, sometimes compared to projective psychological techniques.

Skepticism

Scientific skeptics regard tarot reading as a pseudoscience, attributing its apparent accuracy to cold reading, confirmation bias, and the Barnum effect. Controlled studies have found no evidence that card draws convey information beyond chance or the reader's social skill. Nevertheless tarot remains a widespread cultural practice, commercial service, and artistic medium.
