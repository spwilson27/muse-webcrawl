Block grant

Block grant
TypeIntergovernmental grant-in-aid
Key featureBroad discretion for the recipient government
US examplesTANF, Community Development Block Grant

A block grant is a large sum of money granted by a higher level of government to a lower level (such as a national government to states or localities) for a general purpose, with only broad conditions attached. Unlike categorical grants, which must be spent on narrowly defined programmes, block grants give recipients substantial discretion over how the funds are used.

United States

Block grants became a major tool of American federalism from the 1960s and 1970s, promoted as a way to devolve decision-making to states. Major examples include the Community Development Block Grant (1974), the Social Services Block Grant, and the Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF) block grant created by the 1996 welfare reform, which replaced the open-ended AFDC entitlement with fixed state grants.

Debate

Supporters argue block grants encourage efficient, locally tailored spending and predictable federal costs. Critics argue that fixed funding erodes with inflation and fails to expand during recessions when need rises, and that reduced federal conditions can weaken national standards for civil rights and programme quality.

United Kingdom usage

In British local government finance, the "block grant" historically referred to central government funding distributed to councils by formula, largely superseded by later funding systems.
