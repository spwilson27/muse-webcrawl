Private sponsorship of refugees (Canada)

The Private Sponsorship of Refugees Program is a Canadian government program, established under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act, that allows private citizens and organizations to sponsor individual refugees and refugee families for resettlement in Canada, undertaking to provide financial and settlement support for a defined period, normally one year. Canada was the first country to establish such a program, and it has since been studied and adapted by other states.

History

Private sponsorship was formalized in the Immigration Act of 1976, which came into force in 1978. Its first major use came during the Indochina refugee crisis of 1979–80, when thousands of Canadian groups sponsored Vietnamese, Cambodian, and Laotian refugees; roughly half of the approximately 60,000 Indochinese refugees admitted in that period were privately sponsored. The program again expanded dramatically in 2015–16 with the resettlement of Syrian refugees, when private sponsors supplemented a large government-assisted commitment. In 1979, Canadians' response to the crisis contributed to the Nansen Refugee Award being given to the people of Canada in 1986.

How it works

Refugees may be sponsored by a Sponsorship Agreement Holder (an incorporated organization with an agreement with the government), by a constituent group, by a group of five or more Canadian citizens or permanent residents, or by a community organization. Sponsors commit to providing income support and settlement assistance — housing, food, orientation, and help accessing services — for the sponsorship period. Most privately sponsored refugees must be recognized as refugees under the UN Convention or as members of a country-of-asylum class; the Blended Visa Office-Referred program matches government-referred refugees with private sponsors and shares costs.

Outcomes

Studies of the program generally find that privately sponsored refugees achieve faster labour-market integration and language acquisition than government-assisted refugees in their first years, an effect often attributed to the social networks sponsors provide, though comparisons are affected by differences in the populations each stream serves.
