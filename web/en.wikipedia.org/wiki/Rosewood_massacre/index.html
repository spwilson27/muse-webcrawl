Rosewood massacre

The Rosewood massacre was a racially motivated massacre of Black residents and the destruction of the predominantly African American town of Rosewood, Florida, by white mobs during the first week of January 1923. The town was burned to the ground and never rebuilt; its surviving residents fled and did not return. The official death toll is usually given as eight — six Black and two white people — though survivors and later researchers have suggested the true number of Black victims was higher.

Background

Rosewood, in Levy County on Florida's Gulf coast, was a small, mostly self-sufficient Black community of a few hundred people, with churches, a school, a Masonic lodge, stores, and a baseball team; most residents worked in the timber and turpentine industries. The neighbouring town of Sumner was predominantly white. Tensions between Black communities and white mobs were high across the United States in this era of lynching and Jim Crow segregation.

The violence

On January 1, 1923, Fannie Taylor, a white woman in Sumner, claimed she had been beaten by a Black man. Although her story was doubted by the Black domestic worker who knew of Taylor's white lover, a manhunt began for Jesse Hunter, an escaped convict presumed to be the assailant. Posses with bloodhounds searched the area, lynched Sam Carter, a Black resident, and converged on Rosewood.

On the night of January 4, white men attacked the home of Sarah Carrier, suspecting she had sheltered Hunter. In the ensuing gun battle, Carrier and her son Sylvester were killed, as were two white attackers. News of Black residents killing white men triggered an influx of armed whites, possibly numbering in the hundreds or more, including Ku Klux Klan members who had held a rally in Gainesville days earlier. Over the following days the mob burned nearly every structure in Rosewood. Residents hid in the surrounding swamps in winter cold; some were evacuated by train to Gainesville through the efforts of white railroad conductors and the Wright family, who sheltered many survivors. By January 7 the town was abandoned and destroyed.

Aftermath

No one was ever arrested or prosecuted for the violence, and a special grand jury in February 1923 found insufficient evidence to indict. The survivors, having lost their homes and land, scattered and largely kept silent about the events for decades, fearing further violence.

The story returned to public attention in 1982 through investigative reporting, and survivors and descendants pressed Florida for redress. In 1994 Florida passed a claims bill paying compensation to survivors — then numbering nine — along with a scholarship fund for descendants; it was one of the first instances of a U.S. state compensating victims of racial violence. The events were dramatised in John Singleton's 1997 film Rosewood. In 2004 Florida designated the site a Florida Heritage Landmark. Rosewood is frequently taught alongside the Tulsa race massacre as an example of the destruction of prosperous Black communities during the Jim Crow era.
