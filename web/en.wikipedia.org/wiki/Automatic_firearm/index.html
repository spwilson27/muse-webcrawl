Automatic Firearm

An automatic firearm or fully automatic firearm (to distinguish it from a semi-automatic or self-loading weapon) is a self-loading firearm that continuously chambers and fires rounds when the trigger is held down until the trigger is released or the ammunition runs out. By contrast, a semi-automatic firearm fires one round per trigger pull and uses part of the fired cartridge's energy to cycle the action and load the next round.

Mechanisms

Automatic firearms operate using one of several principles to harness the energy of the fired cartridge. In blowback operation, the pressure in the chamber drives the bolt backward directly, a simple system used mostly in low-powered weapons such as submachine guns. Gas-operated systems divert propellant gas through a port in the barrel to drive a piston or the bolt carrier, as in most modern rifles. Recoil-operated designs use the recoil of the barrel and bolt moving together, common in machine guns such as the Browning M2.

History

The first successful automatic firearm is generally considered to be the Maxim gun, developed by Hiram Maxim in 1884, which used recoil energy to eject the spent case, load a fresh round and fire again. Automatic weapons transformed warfare in the First World War. In the interwar period, designers such as John Browning and John Garand refined self-loading actions, and the Second World War saw widespread issue of submachine guns and the first assault rifles, such as the German Sturmgewehr 44, which combined automatic fire with an intermediate cartridge.

Terminology and regulation

Selective-fire weapons can switch between semi-automatic and fully automatic modes, sometimes with a burst-fire option. Civilian ownership of automatic firearms is heavily restricted in most jurisdictions; in the United States, for example, they are regulated under the National Firearms Act of 1934, and the registry for transferable machine guns was closed to new civilian entries in 1986.
