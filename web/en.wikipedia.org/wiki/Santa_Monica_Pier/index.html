The Santa Monica Pier is a large double-jointed pier at the foot of Colorado Avenue in Santa Monica, California, extending into Santa Monica Bay. Opened in 1909, it is one of the most famous landmarks in the Los Angeles area and one of the most photographed locations in the world, attracting millions of visitors each year. It is popularly regarded as the western terminus of historic U.S. Route 66.

History

The municipal pier opened on 9 September 1909, initially to carry sewer lines beyond the breakers; the adjoining pleasure pier, built by amusement entrepreneur Charles I. D. Looff in 1916, added the Looff Hippodrome (1916, now a National Historic Landmark) housing a carousel whose 1922 Philadelphia Toboggan Company machine still operates, and the La Monica Ballroom (1924), once among the largest dance floors in America. A roller coaster, the Blue Streak Racer, operated in the pier's amusement era.

By the 1970s the storm-damaged pier was threatened with demolition; a citizens' campaign saved it, and in 1983 winter storms destroyed roughly a third of the structure, prompting a major rebuild. In 1996 the pier's amusement zone reopened as Pacific Park, the first amusement park on a West Coast pier since the 1930s, featuring the West Coaster roller coaster and the Pacific Wheel — a Ferris wheel that in 2008 was replaced with the world's first solar-powered wheel.

Attractions

Today the pier includes Pacific Park's rides and midway games, the Santa Monica Pier Aquarium (operated by Heal the Bay), restaurants and shops, a trapeze school, fishing decks (no licence required), and street performers. The "End of the Trail" sign commemorates Route 66, whose official end stood at Santa Monica Boulevard and Ocean Avenue. The pier has appeared in countless films and television programmes, including The Sting, Forrest Gump — whose studio inspired the Bubba Gump Shrimp Company restaurant on the pier — Iron Man and Hannah Montana: The Movie.
