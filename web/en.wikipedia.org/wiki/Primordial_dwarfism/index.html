Primordial dwarfism

Primordial dwarfism (PD) is a form of dwarfism that results in a smaller body size in all stages of life beginning from before birth. More specifically, primordial dwarfism is a diagnostic category including specific types of profoundly proportionate dwarfism, in which individuals are extremely small for their age, even as a fetus.

Most individuals with primordial dwarfism are not diagnosed until they are about 3–5 years of age. Medical professionals typically diagnose the fetus as having intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) when an ultrasound during pregnancy indicates poor growth. Unlike some other forms of dwarfism, hormone therapy is generally ineffective, because the growth restriction is intrinsic to the cells of the body rather than caused by hormone deficiency.

Types

Recognised forms of primordial dwarfism include:

  • Seckel syndrome — characterised by growth retardation, microcephaly, and a distinctive "bird-headed" facial profile.
  • Microcephalic osteodysplastic primordial dwarfism (MOPD types I, II, and III) — type II, caused by mutations in the pericentrin (PCNT) gene, is among the most common forms.
  • Meier-Gorlin syndrome — distinguished by small ears and absent or small kneecaps.
  • Russell–Silver syndrome — involving prenatal and postnatal growth restriction and body asymmetry.

Causes

Primordial dwarfism is genetic and usually inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. Mutations affect genes involved in cell division, DNA damage response, and centrosome function, such as PCNT, ATR, and genes encoding components of the pre-replication complex. Associated health issues can include vascular problems such as aneurysms and moyamoya disease, scoliosis, and insulin resistance.
