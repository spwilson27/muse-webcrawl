Varlık Vergisi

CountryTurkey
Enacted11 November 1942
Repealed15 March 1944
TypeCapital levy

Varlık Vergisi ("Wealth Tax" or "Capital Tax") was a Turkish tax levied in 1942, officially to raise revenue for defence during the Second World War and to curb wartime profiteering. In practice it fell overwhelmingly on Turkey's non-Muslim minorities — Greeks, Armenians, and Jews — who were assessed at rates many times higher than Muslim citizens.

Implementation

The law of 11 November 1942 gave local commissions wide discretion to assess wealthy residents, with no right of appeal and payment due within about two weeks. Non-Muslims were taxed at dramatically higher rates — in many cases beyond their total capital — forcing mass sales of businesses and property, largely to Muslim buyers, in what historians describe as an engineered transfer of wealth and a step in the "Turkification" of the economy.

Labour camps

Those who could not pay were sent to labour camps at Aşkale in eastern Turkey to work off their debts; 21 deportees died there. Of roughly 315 million lira assessed, about 221 million was collected, most of it from non-Muslims.

Repeal and legacy

Under domestic and international criticism, the remaining debts were cancelled and the tax repealed on 15 March 1944. The Varlık Vergisi remains a byword in Turkey for discriminatory state policy against minorities and is frequently cited alongside the 1955 Istanbul pogrom in accounts of the decline of Turkey's Greek, Armenian, and Jewish communities.
