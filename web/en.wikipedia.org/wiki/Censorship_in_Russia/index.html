Censorship in Russia

Censorship has a long history in imperial, Soviet, and post-Soviet Russia.

Censorship in Russia encompasses state control of media and information from the Russian Empire through the Soviet Union to the present-day Russian Federation. Although the 1993 Russian constitution formally guarantees freedom of speech and prohibits censorship, in practice the state exercises extensive control over television, print, and online media.

Imperial and Soviet eras

The Russian Empire operated formal censorship from the early 19th century, with censors approving publications and suppressing material deemed seditious or blasphemous. After the 1917 revolution, the Bolsheviks closed opposition newspapers and in 1922 created Glavlit, the Soviet censorship agency, which controlled virtually all printed matter, broadcasting, and cultural production for nearly seven decades. Dissident literature circulated illegally as samizdat.

Post-Soviet period

The 1990s saw comparatively pluralistic media, but from the 2000s onward the state and state-aligned owners consolidated control of major television networks, the principal source of news for most Russians. Roskomnadzor, the federal media regulator, maintains registers of banned content and websites.

Internet and wartime controls

Laws adopted from 2012 onward expanded blocking of websites, required data localisation, and created the "sovereign internet" framework. After Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, legislation criminalising "false information" about the armed forces—punishable by up to 15 years in prison—led to the closure or exile of nearly all independent Russian media, and major Western social platforms were blocked.
