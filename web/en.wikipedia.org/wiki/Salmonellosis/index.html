Salmonellosis is a symptomatic infection caused by bacteria of the genus Salmonella. It is one of the most common causes of foodborne illness worldwide. In humans the most common symptoms are diarrhoea, fever, abdominal cramps and vomiting, typically beginning between six hours and six days after infection and lasting four to seven days.

Signs and symptoms

Most people with salmonellosis develop diarrhoea, fever and stomach cramps within 6 to 72 hours of exposure. The illness usually resolves without treatment, though dehydration can be dangerous in infants, older adults and people with weakened immune systems. In a small proportion of cases the infection spreads from the intestines to the bloodstream, which can be life-threatening.

Cause and transmission

The disease is caused by Salmonella bacteria, most often Salmonella enterica serotypes Enteritidis and Typhimurium. Transmission is usually through contaminated food — particularly undercooked eggs, poultry and meat — unpasteurised milk, or contact with infected animals such as reptiles and poultry. Outbreaks have also been traced to contaminated produce and peanut butter.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by detection of the bacterium in a stool sample. Treatment is mainly supportive, replacing fluids and electrolytes lost through diarrhoea. Antibiotics are generally reserved for severe cases or patients at risk of invasive disease, partly because of rising antimicrobial resistance.

Epidemiology

Salmonellosis is estimated to cause tens of millions of cases of gastroenteritis globally each year. In the United States, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates about 1.35 million infections, 26,500 hospitalisations and 420 deaths annually. The related but distinct disease typhoid fever is caused by Salmonella Typhi.

Prevention

Prevention centres on food safety: cooking poultry and eggs thoroughly, avoiding cross-contamination in the kitchen, hand washing, and avoiding unpasteurised dairy products.
