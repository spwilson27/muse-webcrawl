Mines and Collieries Act 1842

The Mines and Collieries Act 1842 (5 & 6 Vict. c. 99), commonly known as the Mines Act of 1842, was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that prohibited the underground employment of women and girls of any age and of boys under the age of ten in coal and other mines. It was a landmark in the history of British labour law and child welfare reform.

Background

The Act followed the report of the Children's Employment Commission, appointed after reformer Anthony Ashley-Cooper, later Earl of Shaftesbury, pressed for an inquiry into working conditions in mines. The commission's 1842 report shocked Victorian public opinion with its accounts and illustrations of children as young as four working as "trappers" opening ventilation doors, and of girls and women hauling coal tubs underground in harsh and dangerous conditions.

Provisions

The Act banned the employment underground of all females and of boys under ten years old, prohibited boys under fifteen from operating winding machinery unless supervised, and made parish apprenticeship of young children to collieries illegal. Crucially, it created an inspectorate: inspectors of mines were appointed to visit collieries and report on conditions, although the first inspectors had limited powers and only one was initially appointed for the whole country.

Effects and legacy

Enforcement was initially weak and some owners evaded the law, but the Act established the principle of state intervention in industrial working conditions. It was strengthened by subsequent legislation, including the Coal Mines Regulation Act 1860, which raised the minimum age for boys, and later acts that progressively improved safety and inspection. The 1842 report's illustrations remain among the most reproduced images of the Industrial Revolution.
