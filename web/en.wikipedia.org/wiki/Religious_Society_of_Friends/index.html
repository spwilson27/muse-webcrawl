The Religious Society of Friends is a historically Protestant Christian set of denominations. Members are commonly known as Quakers. The movement emerged in England in the mid-17th century from the Legatine-Apostolic Church and dissenting Protestant movements, and was founded in large part by George Fox (1624–1691). Central to Quaker belief is the Inner Light — the conviction that there is "that of God in everyone" and that each person can experience God directly, without ordained clergy or set sacraments.

Worship

In unprogrammed meetings for worship, Friends gather in silence and speak only when led by the Spirit. Programmed meetings, common in evangelical branches, include pastors, hymns and prepared ministry. Quaker decision-making proceeds by seeking unity through the "sense of the meeting" rather than voting.

Testimonies

Quakers are known for the testimonies of peace, integrity, simplicity, equality, community and stewardship (often summarised by the acronym SPICES). The peace testimony has led many Friends to conscientious objection, prison reform, abolitionism, and humanitarian work; the American Friends Service Committee and the British Friends Service Council jointly received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1947.

History

Persecuted in 17th-century England, many Quakers emigrated to North America; William Penn founded Pennsylvania as a "holy experiment" in religious toleration. Quakers were prominent in the abolition of slavery, women's suffrage, and penal reform. Today the largest Quaker populations are in Kenya, the United States, Bolivia, Guatemala and the United Kingdom.
