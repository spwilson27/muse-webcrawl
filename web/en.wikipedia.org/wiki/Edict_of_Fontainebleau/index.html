The Edict of Fontainebleau, issued by King Louis XIV of France on 18 October 1685, revoked the Edict of Nantes of 1598, by which Henry IV had granted substantial rights and religious toleration to the Protestant Huguenots. The revocation declared Protestantism illegal in France and is known in French as the Révocation de l'Édit de Nantes.

Background

From the 1660s Louis XIV pursued a policy of pressuring Huguenots to convert to Catholicism: temples were demolished on legal pretexts, professions were progressively closed to Protestants, and from 1681 the notorious dragonnades billeted dragoons in Protestant households to intimidate families into conversion. The Edict of Fontainebleau claimed, disingenuously, that almost all Huguenots had already converted and that the Edict of Nantes was therefore unnecessary.

Provisions

The edict ordered the destruction of the remaining Huguenot churches, closed Protestant schools, required children to be baptised and raised as Catholics, exiled pastors who refused to convert within fifteen days — and, contradictorily, forbade lay Protestants from leaving the kingdom on pain of the galleys for men and imprisonment for women. Protestant worship and emigration were both criminalised.

Consequences

Despite the ban, an estimated 200,000 or more Huguenots fled France — the Refuge — to England, the Dutch Republic, Brandenburg-Prussia (whose Elector issued the Edict of Potsdam inviting them), Switzerland, Ireland, Scandinavia, and the colonies in North America and South Africa. The exiles, disproportionately skilled in textiles, watchmaking, finance and the professions, enriched their host countries while France lost taxpayers, craftsmen and soldiers. Within France, a clandestine Protestant "Church of the Desert" survived in remote regions such as the Cévennes, where persecution sparked the Camisard revolt (1702–1704).

The revocation was applauded by much of Catholic France but damaged Louis XIV's standing in Protestant Europe and stiffened the coalitions ranged against him. Legal toleration was only restored by the Edict of Versailles of 1787, and full religious freedom came with the French Revolution. In 1985 President François Mitterrand publicly apologised for the revocation on its tercentenary.
