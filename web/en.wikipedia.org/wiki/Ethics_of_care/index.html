Ethics of care

The ethics of care (alternatively care ethics or EoC) is a normative ethical theory that holds that moral action centres on interpersonal relationships and care or benevolence as a virtue. EoC is one of a cluster of normative ethical theories that were developed by some feminists in the second half of the twentieth century. While consequentialist and deontological ethical theories emphasise generalisable standards and impartiality, ethics of care emphasise the importance of relationships and context.

Origins

The theory is generally credited to psychologist Carol Gilligan's book In a Different Voice (1982), which criticised Lawrence Kohlberg's stages of moral development for being built on studies of boys and privileging an ethic of justice and abstract rights. Gilligan identified a different, equally mature moral orientation organised around responsibilities, relationships and care. Philosopher Nel Noddings developed the theory further in Caring: A Feminine Approach to Ethics and Moral Education (1984), grounding it in the relation between the "one-caring" and the "cared-for".

Themes

Care ethicists such as Joan Tronto analyse care as a practice with phases—caring about, taking care of, care-giving, care-receiving—and corresponding moral elements of attentiveness, responsibility, competence and responsiveness. The theory stresses human interdependence and vulnerability, and has been applied in nursing and medical ethics, education, political theory and international relations.

Criticism

Critics argue that care ethics risks reinforcing traditional gender roles by associating women with caring, may slide into partiality or relativism, and lacks clear principles for resolving conflicts between competing care claims. Supporters reply that mature care ethics integrates justice and extends concern beyond immediate relationships.
