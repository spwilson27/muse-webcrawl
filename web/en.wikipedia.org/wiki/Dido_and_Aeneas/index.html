Dido and Aeneas is an opera in a prologue and three acts by the English Baroque composer Henry Purcell, to a libretto by Nahum Tate based on Book IV of Virgil's Aeneid. Composed before 1689, it is Purcell's only true opera and arguably the first great English opera, famed above all for Dido's closing lament, "When I am laid in earth", built over a descending chromatic ground bass.

Synopsis

Dido, widowed queen of Carthage, falls in love with the Trojan hero Aeneas, who has been shipwrecked on her shore. In Tate's version — altered from Virgil — a Sorceress and her witches, delighting in ruin, conspire to destroy the love: a spirit disguised as Mercury reminds Aeneas of his duty to sail for Italy and found Rome. Aeneas obeys. Dido, betrayed and dishonoured, dismisses him and dies of grief as the chorus reflects that "great minds against themselves conspire".

History and music

The opera was performed in 1689 at Josias Priest's boarding school for young gentlewomen in Chelsea; an earlier court performance around 1687 has been proposed, and the work may derive from John Blow's Venus and Adonis. No autograph score survives, and the music for the prologue and some dances is lost. Though barely an hour long, the score packs arias, recitatives, choruses, and dances of great expressive economy — the witches' choruses, the sailors' dance, and the final lament, a passacaglia on a five-bar ground that has become one of the most famous laments in all opera.
