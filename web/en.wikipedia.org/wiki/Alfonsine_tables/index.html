Alfonsine tables

The Alfonsine tables (Spanish: Tablas alfonsíes, Latin: Tabulae Alphonsinae) are astronomical tables providing data for computing the positions of the Sun, Moon, and planets relative to the fixed stars. They were sponsored by King Alfonso X of Castile and produced in Toledo, Spain, in the late 13th century, and they remained the standard astronomical tables in Europe for more than three centuries.

Production

Alfonso X assembled a team of scholars, including Jewish astronomers such as Isaac ibn Sid and Yehuda ben Moshe, at Toledo. The tables, completed around 1272 and based on observations begun in 1263, built upon the earlier Toledan Tables and the work of the Andalusian astronomer al-Zarqali. The epoch of the tables was 1 January 1252, the year of Alfonso's coronation. They were computed for the meridian of Toledo within the Ptolemaic geocentric system.

Influence

The Alfonsine tables circulated widely in manuscript and were revised in Paris in the 1320s (the Parisian Alfonsine tables, associated with John of Murs, John of Lignères, and John of Saxony), which became the dominant version. They were among the first astronomical works printed, with an edition published in Venice in 1483, and further editions followed. Nicolaus Copernicus used them, and they were only superseded in the 16th–17th centuries by Erasmus Reinhold's Prutenic Tables (1551, based on Copernicus) and ultimately Kepler's Rudolphine Tables (1627).

The tables also contain instructions for computing eclipses and for astrological purposes, reflecting the close link between astronomy and astrology in medieval science.
