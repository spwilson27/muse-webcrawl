Catalytic converter

A catalytic converter is an exhaust emission control device that converts toxic gases and pollutants in exhaust gas from an internal combustion engine into less-toxic pollutants by catalyzing a redox reaction. Catalytic converters are usually used with internal combustion engines fueled by gasoline or diesel, including lean-burn engines, and sometimes on kerosene heaters and stoves.

The first widespread introduction of catalytic converters was in the United States automobile market. To comply with the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's stricter regulation of exhaust emissions, most gasoline-powered vehicles starting with the 1975 model year are equipped with catalytic converters. These "two-way" converters combined oxygen with carbon monoxide (CO) and unburned hydrocarbons (HC) to produce carbon dioxide (CO2) and water (H2O).

Structure

The catalytic converter consists of several components. The core or substrate is usually a ceramic monolith with a honeycomb structure, which provides a large surface area. A washcoat — a mixture of silica and alumina — is applied to the core, and the catalytic materials themselves, most commonly platinum, palladium, and rhodium, are suspended in the washcoat. The entire assembly is contained within a stainless steel housing.

Three-way converters

"Three-way" converters, introduced in the early 1980s, have the additional advantage of controlling the emission of nitrogen oxides (NOx), converting them into nitrogen (N2) and oxygen (O2). Three-way converters require the engine to operate near the stoichiometric point, at an air-to-fuel ratio of approximately 14.7:1 for gasoline. The main reactions in a three-way converter include the reduction of nitrogen oxides, the oxidation of carbon monoxide to carbon dioxide, and the oxidation of unburned hydrocarbons to carbon dioxide and water.

Environmental and health issues

Although catalytic converters greatly reduce harmful emissions, they are not without drawbacks. They require the use of unleaded fuel, since lead poisons the catalyst. Converters also produce some carbon dioxide and can emit nitrous oxide, a potent greenhouse gas. In recent years, theft of catalytic converters has become a significant problem in many countries because of the high value of the precious metals they contain, particularly platinum, palladium, and rhodium.

History

The catalytic converter was invented by French mechanical engineer Eugène Houdry, who developed an early version in the 1950s for use on smoke stacks. In the mid-1950s he began research to develop catalytic converters for gasoline engines used in cars. Later work by John J. Mooney and Carl D. Keith at the Engelhard Corporation led to the first production catalytic converter in 1973, in time for the 1975 model year vehicles mandated by clean air legislation.
