Agribusiness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Large-scale mechanized farming is characteristic of agribusiness

Agribusiness is the business sector encompassing farming and farming-related commercial activities. The term was coined in 1957 by John H. Davis and Ray A. Goldberg of Harvard Business School in their book A Concept of Agribusiness. Agribusiness involves the production, processing, and distribution of agricultural products: it includes the manufacture and supply of farm inputs such as seed, fertilizer, pesticides, and machinery; farming itself; and the storage, processing, transport, marketing, and retailing of food and fibre products.

Scope

Modern agribusiness is organized along value chains that link input suppliers, producers, processors, and distributors. Major agribusiness companies include grain traders and processors such as Cargill and Archer Daniels Midland, seed and agrochemical producers such as Bayer (which acquired Monsanto) and Corteva, and meat processors such as JBS and Tyson Foods. A characteristic feature is vertical integration — a single firm controlling several stages of production — and contract farming, in which processors supply inputs and specify methods in return for the grower's output.

Criticism

Agribusiness has been criticized for its environmental footprint (greenhouse gas emissions, water use, monoculture, and pesticide dependence), for market concentration that squeezes small farmers, and for effects on animal welfare, rural communities, and public health. Supporters point to the sector's role in raising yields and keeping food prices low. Debates over agribusiness connect to wider discussions of food security, globalization, and sustainable agriculture.
