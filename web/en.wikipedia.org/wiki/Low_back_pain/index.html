Low back pain

Low back pain (LBP) is a common disorder involving the muscles, nerves, and bones of the back, in between the lower edge of the ribs and the lower fold of the buttocks. Pain can vary from a dull constant ache to a sudden sharp feeling. Low back pain may be classified by duration as acute (pain lasting less than 6 weeks), sub-chronic (6 to 12 weeks), or chronic (more than 12 weeks). It is one of the most common reasons people visit a doctor and the leading cause of years lived with disability worldwide; most adults experience it at some point in their lives.

Causes and classification

In the great majority of cases (around 90 percent), no specific structural cause can be identified, and the pain is termed non-specific or mechanical, arising from muscles, ligaments, facet joints, or discs. Specific causes include intervertebral disc herniation with radicular pain (sciatica), spinal stenosis, vertebral compression fractures, and — rarely — serious disease such as infection, malignancy, or cauda equina syndrome. Clinicians screen for "red flag" symptoms (such as bladder or bowel dysfunction, fever, major trauma, or a history of cancer) that suggest a serious underlying cause requiring urgent investigation.

Management

Most acute episodes improve within a few weeks with reassurance, remaining active, and simple analgesia; routine imaging is not recommended without red flags. Guidelines advise against bed rest and against opioids for most cases. For chronic low back pain, exercise therapy, physical therapy, psychological approaches such as cognitive behavioural therapy, and multidisciplinary rehabilitation are recommended; medications include NSAIDs and, in selected cases, duloxetine or tramadol. Surgery is reserved for specific indications such as persistent radicular pain from disc herniation or symptomatic spinal stenosis.

Epidemiology and society

Low back pain affects hundreds of millions of people at any given time — global prevalence estimates suggest about 7.5 percent of the world's population — and it is a major cause of lost workdays and healthcare expenditure, costing tens of billions of dollars annually in the United States alone. Risk factors include obesity, sedentary work, heavy physical labour, smoking, and psychological distress.
