Bishop–Phelps theorem

FieldFunctional analysis

In mathematics, the Bishop–Phelps theorem is a result in functional analysis, proved by Errett Bishop and Robert R. Phelps in 1961, that has become a fundamental tool in the geometry of Banach spaces.

Statement

Let B be a real Banach space and let C be a nonempty closed bounded convex subset of B. A continuous linear functional on B is called a support functional of C if it attains its supremum on C. The theorem states that the set of support functionals of C is dense in the dual space B* with respect to the norm topology. Equivalently, a generic continuous linear functional on a Banach space attains its supremum on a given bounded closed convex set, and functionals attaining their maximum on the unit ball are dense in the dual.

Importance

The theorem answered a question about the approximability of functionals and underlies much of modern convex analysis in infinite dimensions. It is closely related to the Brøndsted–Rockafellar theorem, to subdifferentiability of convex functions, and to results such as the Bishop–Phelps property studied in the theory of norm-attaining operators. A standard corollary is that every Banach space is subreflexive.
