Level of detail (computer graphics)

In computer graphics, level of detail (LOD) refers to techniques that manage the complexity of a 3D model representation as it moves away from the viewer or according to other metrics such as object importance or speed. By rendering distant or less important objects with fewer polygons, LOD reduces the workload on the graphics pipeline while keeping visual degradation acceptable.

Discrete and continuous LOD

Discrete LOD stores several pre-generated versions of a model at decreasing resolutions and switches between them based on distance or screen-space size; mipmapping is the analogous idea for textures. Switching can cause visible "popping," which techniques such as alpha fading or geomorphing attempt to hide. Continuous LOD generates a spectrum of resolutions on the fly from a data structure such as a progressive mesh, allowing smooth transitions.

Techniques

Mesh simplification algorithms include vertex decimation, edge collapse (as in the quadric error metric method of Garland and Heckbert), and vertex clustering. Terrain rendering uses specialised schemes — quadtrees, geometry clipmaps, and chunked LOD — to handle very large landscapes. Modern engines combine LOD with impostors and billboards for very distant objects, and systems such as Unreal Engine's Nanite automate much of the process through clustered geometry streaming.

Applications

LOD is essential in flight simulators, open-world games, geographic information systems, and any real-time application where scene complexity exceeds the rendering budget. The concept dates to a 1976 paper by James Clark on hierarchical geometric models.
