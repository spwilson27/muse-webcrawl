Honiton lace

Honiton lace is a type of bobbin lace made in the town of Honiton and surrounding villages in East Devon, England. It is famed for its delicate floral designs — roses, thistles, shamrocks, ferns and butterflies — worked as separate motifs ("sprigs") that are later joined, allowing many lace makers to contribute to a single large piece.

History

Lace making reached Devon in the late 16th century, possibly with Flemish refugees. By the 17th century Honiton lace was highly prized at court. The industry declined with the introduction of machine-made net in the early 19th century but survived by applying handmade motifs to machine net.

Royal patronage

Queen Victoria ordered her wedding dress lace from Honiton in 1840; the flounce was made by some 200 village workers under the pattern of Reginald Easton. Victoria subsequently had christening robes trimmed with it, and a statue of her in Exeter is said to wear Honiton lace in its carving. The royal connection revived the trade.

Technique and legacy

Traditional Honiton is worked with very fine linen thread on a pillow with bobbins, using guipure techniques without a net ground in older pieces. The Allhallows Museum in Honiton holds a major collection, and small numbers of makers continue the craft today.
