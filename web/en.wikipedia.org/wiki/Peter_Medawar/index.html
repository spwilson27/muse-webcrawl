Peter Medawar

Born: Peter Brian Medawar, 28 February 1915, Petrópolis, Brazil

Died: 2 October 1987 (aged 72), London, England

Field: Immunology, zoology

Award: Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (1960)

Sir Peter Brian Medawar (28 February 1915 – 2 October 1987) was a British biologist whose work on graft rejection and acquired immune tolerance was foundational to organ transplantation. He shared the 1960 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Frank Macfarlane Burnet, and has been called the "father of transplantation".

Work

Born in Brazil to a Lebanese father and English mother, Medawar was educated at Marlborough College and Magdalen College, Oxford. During the Second World War he investigated skin grafting for burned airmen, which led him to the immunology of tissue rejection. With Rupert Billingham and Leslie Brent he demonstrated actively acquired tolerance (published 1953): mice exposed to foreign cells in fetal life accepted skin grafts from the same donor strain as adults — experimental confirmation of Burnet's theory, and a conceptual cornerstone of transplant biology.

Medawar also proposed an influential evolutionary theory of ageing — that ageing results from the declining force of natural selection late in life, allowing late-acting harmful mutations to accumulate (mutation accumulation theory).

Writing and legacy

Director of the National Institute for Medical Research from 1962, Medawar was also celebrated as a lucid writer on science, in books such as The Art of the Soluble (1967) and Advice to a Young Scientist (1979), defending a Popperian, hypothesis-driven view of scientific method. A series of strokes from 1969 curtailed his laboratory work; he died in 1987.
