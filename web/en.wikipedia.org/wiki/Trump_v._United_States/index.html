Trump v. United States, 600 U.S. ___ (2024), is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States holding that a president of the United States enjoys broad criminal immunity for official acts committed while in office. The case arose from the federal prosecution of former president Donald Trump over his conduct surrounding attempts to overturn the 2020 presidential election and the January 6 Capitol attack.

Background

In August 2023, a federal grand jury indicted Trump on four counts related to his efforts to remain in power after losing the 2020 election. Trump moved to dismiss the indictment, arguing that a president is absolutely immune from criminal prosecution for official acts. The district court and the D.C. Circuit rejected that claim; the Supreme Court granted certiorari and heard argument in April 2024.

Decision

In a 6–3 decision authored by Chief Justice John Roberts, the Court held that:

  • A president has absolute immunity from criminal prosecution for actions within his conclusive and preclusive constitutional authority — his "core constitutional powers".
  • A president has at least presumptive immunity for all other acts within the outer perimeter of his official responsibilities.
  • A president has no immunity for unofficial acts.

The Court also held that prosecutors may not use evidence of official acts to prove charges based on unofficial conduct. The case was remanded to the district court to classify the conduct alleged in the indictment.

Reactions and consequences

The ruling was praised by Trump and his supporters as a vindication of presidential authority and criticised by the dissent — led by Justice Sonia Sotomayor, who warned it made the president "a king above the law" — and by many legal scholars. The decision effectively ended any prospect of a pre-election trial in the federal election-interference case, and after Trump's victory in the 2024 election the prosecution was dismissed in line with the Justice Department policy against prosecuting a sitting president.
