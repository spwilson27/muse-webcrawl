Bicycle chain

A bicycle roller chain running over a chainring

A bicycle chain is a roller chain that transfers power from the pedals of a bicycle to the drive wheel. It consists of a series of short links — pairs of outer and inner steel plates joined by pins and rollers — that mesh with the teeth of the chainring and the rear sprocket. Chain drive, combined with the safety bicycle layout of the 1880s, made the modern bicycle practical and remains its dominant drive system.

Design and dimensions

Standard bicycle chains have a pitch of 1/2 inch (12.7 mm). The internal width between the inner plates varies with the drivetrain: older single-speed and hub-gear chains are 1/8 inch wide internally, while derailleur chains are nominally 3/32 inch, with narrower widths for 9-, 10-, 11- and 12-speed systems so the chain can fit between closely spaced sprockets and shift under the guidance of ramps and pins on the sprockets. Chains are sized by number of links and are joined with a split link, quick link, or a special connecting pin. Efficiency of a clean, lubricated chain drive is typically around 98%.

Wear and maintenance

Chains elongate with use as the pins and bushings wear — commonly called "chain stretch" — and a worn chain accelerates wear of sprockets and chainrings, so chains are treated as consumables and replaced when wear gauges indicate about 0.5–0.75% elongation. Regular cleaning and lubrication reduce wear. Alternatives such as belt drives and shaft drives exist but are heavier or less efficient, though belts are popular on utility and folding bicycles for their cleanliness.
