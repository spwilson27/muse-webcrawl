Dekulakization

Date1929–1933 (peak 1930–1931)
LocationSoviet Union
Target"Kulaks" — peasants classified as wealthy
OutcomeMillions deported, expropriated or killed; forced collectivisation of agriculture

Dekulakization (Russian: raskulachivanie) was the Soviet campaign of political repressions, including arrests, deportations and executions, directed against kulaks — the wealthier peasants — and their families between 1929 and 1933. It formed a central part of Joseph Stalin's policy of forced collectivisation of agriculture, announced as the "liquidation of the kulaks as a class".

Background

Under the New Economic Policy, a stratum of relatively prosperous peasants had emerged in the Soviet countryside. The grain procurement crises of 1927–1928 convinced Stalin that peasant smallholding was incompatible with rapid industrialisation. On 27 December 1929 he announced the liquidation of the kulaks as a class, and a secret Politburo decree of 30 January 1930 divided kulaks into three categories: those to be shot or imprisoned in concentration camps; those to be deported to remote regions of the USSR; and those to be expropriated and resettled on poor land within their own districts.

The campaign

Local commissions, often working from arbitrary quotas, classified households as kulak — a designation that could extend to any peasant who owned a mill, hired labour, sold produce, or resisted collectivisation. Property was confiscated, and families were deported in cattle trucks to "special settlements" in Siberia, Kazakhstan, the Urals and the far north, where many were put to forced labour in logging and mining. Between 1930 and 1931 alone, over 1.8 million people were deported. Peasant resistance — including uprisings, arson and the mass slaughter of livestock — was met with armed suppression by the OGPU secret police and regular troops.

Death toll and consequences

Historians estimate that several hundred thousand deportees died from hunger, cold and disease in exile by the mid-1930s; archival-based estimates of deaths directly attributable to dekulakization in 1930–1937 range from roughly 390,000 to over 600,000, with total excess deaths, including those in the ensuing famines, considerably higher. The destruction of the most productive farmers, combined with collectivisation, contributed to the devastating Soviet famine of 1932–1933, including the Holodomor in Ukraine and famine in Kazakhstan. Dekulakization shattered the social structure of the countryside and largely destroyed organised peasant resistance to Soviet power.

After Stalin's death, surviving special settlers were gradually released, and restrictions were lifted under Nikita Khrushchev, though confiscated property was not returned. In 1991 Russia's Law on the Rehabilitation of Victims of Political Repression formally recognised dekulakized peasants as victims.
