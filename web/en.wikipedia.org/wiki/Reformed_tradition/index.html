Reformed tradition


Calvin's Institutes, a defining text of the Reformed tradition.

The Reformed tradition is the body of doctrine, worship, and church life that flows from the Swiss and South German Reformation of the 16th century and is embodied today in the Reformed churches. Whereas Reformed Christianity (Calvinism) names the theological system, "the Reformed tradition" is used by historians and theologians to denote the tradition in its full historical development, including its diversity.

Origins and development

The tradition began with Huldrych Zwingli in Zurich and Heinrich Bullinger, his successor, and was given enduring form by John Calvin in Geneva. Through confessional documents—the First and Second Helvetic Confessions, the Heidelberg Catechism (1563), the Belgic Confession (1561), the Canons of Dort (1619), and the Westminster Standards (1640s)—the tradition acquired a shared doctrinal grammar while tolerating notable variety between national churches.

Distinctive emphases

  • Covenant theology — reading the whole Bible as structured by God's covenants.
  • The regulative principle — worship ordered by what Scripture commands.
  • Confessional identity — churches defined by written confessions rather than episcopal succession.
  • Cultural engagement — schools, universities, and a worldview extending Reformed thought to politics, economics, and the arts, later developed by figures such as Abraham Kuyper.

Later movements

Within the tradition arose Reformed scholasticism, Puritanism, pietist awakenings, the neo-Calvinism of Kuyper and Herman Bavinck, and the dialectical theology of Karl Barth. Today the tradition spans both ecumenical mainline churches and strict confessional bodies.
