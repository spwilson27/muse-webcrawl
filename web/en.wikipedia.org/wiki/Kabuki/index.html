Kabuki

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A kabuki actor in full kumadori makeup freezing in a mie pose, eyes crossed, gorgeous brocade arrested mid-whirl]

Kabuki (歌舞伎) is a classical form of Japanese theatre, mixing dramatic performance with traditional dance, renowned for its spectacular staging, elaborate costumes and make-up (kumadori), and the stylised mie poses in which actors freeze at climaxes. It developed from the dances of Izumo no Okuni (c. 1603) in Kyoto; after women were banned from the stage (1629), male actors specialised — the onnagata who play female roles remain among its highest arts.

Kabuki's repertoire (over 400 plays survive) draws on history plays (jidaimono), domestic tragedies (sewamono) and dance pieces, by playwrights including Chikamatsu Monzaemon and Tsuruya Nanboku IV; theatres like Tokyo's Kabuki-za run monthly programmes. UNESCO inscribed kabuki as Intangible Cultural Heritage (2005 proclamation, 2008 list).

The art of the moment

Kabuki is a theatre of virtuoso surfaces: the hanamichi runway through the audience, trap lifts and revolving stages (invented here in the 18th century), quick costume changes performed in view, the actor's mie held while wooden clappers (tsuke) beat the freeze. Its acting dynasties (the Ichikawa Danjūrō line above all) carry stage names as hereditary treasures; a name-taking ceremony (shūmei) is itself a theatrical event.

Survival

Suppressed at times in the Edo order it mirrored, nearly killed by war and by cinema, kabuki survived through its star system and state recognition (leading actors are Living National Treasures). Contemporary stars cross into film and television; 'Super Kabuki' productions and anime adaptations recruit new audiences. It remains the grandest living theatre of pre-modern Japan — three hours of dance, blood and brocade that still plays to full houses.
