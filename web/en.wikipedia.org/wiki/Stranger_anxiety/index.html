Stranger anxiety

Stranger anxiety is a form of distress that children experience when exposed to people unfamiliar to them. Symptoms may include getting quiet and staring at the stranger, verbally protesting by cries or other vocalisations, and hiding behind a familiar person. Stranger anxiety is a normal part of the developmental sequence that most children experience; it arises when infants first develop a sense of object permanence and form specific attachments to familiar caregivers.

Development

Stranger anxiety typically begins at around 6 to 8 months of age, peaks between 12 and 15 months, and then gradually declines through the second year of life. It is closely related to separation anxiety, which concerns distress at being parted from attachment figures. Developmental psychologists regard stranger wariness as a sign that the infant can distinguish familiar from unfamiliar faces and has formed a preferential attachment to caregivers.

Course and variation

The intensity of stranger anxiety varies considerably between children depending on temperament, the context of the encounter, and prior social experience. Children typically react less negatively when the stranger approaches slowly, when the encounter occurs in a familiar setting, and when the caregiver responds calmly and positively. Stranger anxiety normally fades as the child's social world widens; extreme or persistent social fear in older children may instead indicate social anxiety disorder, a distinct condition.
