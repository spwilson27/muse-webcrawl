The GNU Free Documentation License (GNU FDL or simply GFDL) is a copyleft license for free documentation, designed by the Free Software Foundation (FSF) for the GNU Project. It is similar to the GNU General Public License, giving readers the rights to copy, redistribute, and modify (except for "invariant sections") a work and requires all copies and derivatives to be available under the same license. Copies may also be sold commercially, but, if produced in larger quantities (greater than 100), the original document or source code must be made available to the work's recipient.

History

The FDL was released in draft form for feedback in 1999 and published as version 1.1 in March 2000, followed by version 1.2 in 2002 and version 1.3 in November 2008. It was intended to complement the GPL by covering manuals and textbooks accompanying free software, embodying Richard Stallman's view that documentation should be free in the same sense as software.

Terms

Works licensed under the GFDL may designate "Invariant Sections" that cannot be modified or removed, and "Cover Texts" that must be reproduced on covers. Critics, including the Debian project, have argued that these provisions — along with anti-DRM clauses and burdensome requirements for mass printing — make some GFDL-licensed works non-free in practice.

Use and relicensing

Wikipedia originally used the GFDL for its text. Version 1.3 of the license contained a time-limited provision allowing mass collaborative websites to relicense their content under the Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike license (CC BY-SA), a provision drafted largely at Wikipedia's request; in 2009 the Wikimedia community voted to migrate to CC BY-SA as its primary license.
