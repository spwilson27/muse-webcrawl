Centrifugal compressor

A centrifugal compressor is a type of dynamic gas compressor, also called a radial compressor, that raises the pressure of a gas by adding kinetic energy with a rotating impeller and then converting that energy to pressure in a diffuser. Gas enters axially near the hub, is thrown outward by centrifugal force through the impeller passages, and leaves at high velocity into a volute or diffuser.

Operation

Single-stage centrifugal compressors typically achieve pressure ratios of about 3:1 to 4:1. Their performance is limited by surge (unstable flow reversal at low flow rates) and choke (sonic flow at high rates). Unlike reciprocating compressors, they deliver smooth, oil-free flow and suit continuous large-volume service.

Applications

Centrifugal compressors are used in turbochargers and small gas turbines (including early jet engines and many modern auxiliary power units), in refrigeration and air-conditioning chillers, in pipeline natural-gas transport, and in process industries such as refining and petrochemicals.

History

The principle was applied by Auguste Rateau and in Frank Whittle's and Hans von Ohain's first turbojet engines, whose compressors were centrifugal; most large modern jet engines instead use axial compressors, but centrifugal stages remain common in small engines.
