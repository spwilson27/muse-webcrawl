Ely, Cambridgeshire

Ely Cathedral, the "Ship of the Fens".

Ely is a cathedral city and civil parish in the East Cambridgeshire district of Cambridgeshire, England, about 14 miles (23 km) north-northeast of Cambridge and about 80 miles (129 km) by road from London. With a population of around 20,000, it is one of England's smallest cities.

History

Ely grew around an abbey founded in 673 by St Etheldreda (Æthelthryth), daughter of King Anna of East Anglia, on what was then an island of higher ground rising from the surrounding marshy Fens — the "Isle of Ely". The name is thought to derive from the presence of eels in the local waters. The abbey was destroyed by Danish invaders in 870 and refounded in 970. In the 11th century the Isle was a centre of resistance to William the Conqueror under Hereward the Wake. Ely Cathedral, begun in 1083, is famous for its central octagonal tower and Lady Chapel, and the city remained largely isolated by water until the drainage of the Fens in the 17th century.

Geography and transport

The city stands on a low Kimmeridge clay island, at about 26 m the highest land in the Fens. The River Great Ouse flows through the city, which has a marina and riverside walks. Ely railway station is a significant junction on lines between London, Cambridge, Norwich, Peterborough and King's Lynn.

Notable features

  • Ely Cathedral — formally the Cathedral Church of the Holy and Undivided Trinity, seat of the Bishop of Ely.
  • Oliver Cromwell's House — the Lord Protector's residence from 1636 to 1646, now a museum.
  • Ely Museum — housed in the old gaol.
  • Stained Glass Museum — located in the cathedral's south triforium.
