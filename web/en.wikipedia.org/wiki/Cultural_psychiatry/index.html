Cultural psychiatry

Cultural psychiatry studies how culture shapes the experience and expression of mental distress.

Cultural psychiatry is a branch of psychiatry concerned with the ways in which cultural and social contexts influence the causes, manifestations, course and treatment of mental disorders. It examines how symptoms are expressed differently across societies and how diagnostic categories themselves reflect cultural assumptions.

Key concepts

Central topics include culture-bound syndromes (now framed in DSM-5 as "cultural concepts of distress"), such as ataque de nervios, koro and taijin kyōfusho; the difference between disease (a biological process) and illness (the lived experience of suffering); and the category fallacy — the error of applying diagnostic categories developed in one culture to another without revalidation.

Practice

Clinically, cultural psychiatry promotes the use of the DSM-5 Cultural Formulation Interview, attention to patients' explanatory models of their illness, and awareness of migration, racism and language barriers in care. The field grew out of early transcultural psychiatry and comparative studies by figures such as Emil Kraepelin, and was shaped by anthropologists and psychiatrist-anthropologists including Arthur Kleinman and Byron Good.

Criticism and debate

The field debates the universality of conditions such as schizophrenia and depression, the global spread of Western diagnostic frameworks, and whether some disorders have been "exported" along with their treatments. Cultural psychiatrists generally argue for combining biological understanding with cultural interpretation rather than choosing between them.
