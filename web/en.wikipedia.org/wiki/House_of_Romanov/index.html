The House of Romanov (Russian: Романовы) was the reigning imperial house of Russia from 1613 to 1917. It succeeded the Rurik dynasty, which had fallen into disgrace during the Time of Troubles, and it ruled Russia until its overthrow in the February Revolution of 1917.

History

The Romanovs were an old boyar family; Anastasia Romanovna had been the first wife of Ivan the Terrible. In 1613, after the chaos of the Time of Troubles, a Zemsky Sobor elected the sixteen-year-old Michael Romanov as Tsar. His descendants ruled for three centuries, among them Peter the Great, who proclaimed the Russian Empire in 1721; Elizabeth; Catherine the Great, who extended the empire to the Black Sea and partitioned Poland; Alexander I, victor over Napoleon; Alexander II, who emancipated the serfs; and the last Tsar, Nicholas II.

From the accession of Anna in 1730 the line of succession frequently passed through female heirs and was continued biologically by the Holstein-Gottorp line — so from the mid-eighteenth century the dynasty was, in strict genealogy, the House of Holstein-Gottorp-Romanov, though it retained the Romanov name.

Fall and aftermath

Nicholas II abdicated in March 1917. He and his family were executed by the Bolsheviks at Yekaterinburg on 17 July 1918, along with several grand dukes elsewhere; other Romanovs escaped into exile via Crimea and abroad. Survivors formed the Romanov Family Association (1979), and the headship of the house in exile has been disputed between branches, notably the descendants of Grand Duke Kirill Vladimirovich and those of Grand Duke Nicholas Nikolaevich. Nicholas II and his family were canonised by the Russian Orthodox Church and, after DNA identification of their remains, reburied in St Petersburg in 1998.
