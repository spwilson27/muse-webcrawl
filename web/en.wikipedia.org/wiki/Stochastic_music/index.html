Stochastic music

Stochastic music is a composition method in which elements of the music are determined by probability and statistical laws rather than by fixed decisions of the composer. The term is most closely associated with the Greek composer and architect Iannis Xenakis (1922–2001), who coined the term in the 1950s and developed it as an alternative both to strict serialism and to the aleatoric music of John Cage: in stochastic music, chance operates at the level of distributions and densities, while the overall form remains composed.

Iannis Xenakis

Xenakis's breakthrough work Metastaseis (1953–54) applied statistical mechanics — notably the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution of gas molecule velocities — to the glissandi of a large string ensemble. In Pithoprakta (1955–56; from the Greek for "actions by probability") individual string players follow independent parts whose pitches and attacks were computed from Poisson distributions and Gaussian functions. His book Formalized Music (1963, expanded 1971) set out the theory, including Markov chains and game theory as compositional tools.

In the early 1960s Xenakis used an IBM 7090 computer in Paris to generate scores with programs written in Fortran — works such as the ST/48, ST/10 and ST/4 pieces — in which probability distributions controlled the density, register, timbre and ordering of sound events, which were then transcribed into notation by hand. Later he devised the UPIC system, allowing music to be drawn graphically and converted into sound.

Concepts

Key stochastic concepts in music include:

  • Stochastic distributions (Poisson, Gaussian, exponential) controlling clouds of sounds, or "sound masses".
  • Markov chains, in which the probability of the next event depends on the current state.
  • Sieves — Xenakis's later theory of scales constructed from set-theoretic operations.

Influence

Stochastic methods fed into algorithmic composition and computer music generally. Composers such as Gottfried Michael Koenig explored related algorithmic procedures, and probability-driven processes are now standard in generative and electronic music software.
