Sensitivity and specificity are statistical measures of the performance of a binary classification test, widely used in medicine and machine learning. Sensitivity (the true positive rate) measures the proportion of actual positives that are correctly identified — for example, the percentage of sick people correctly identified as having a condition. Specificity (the true negative rate) measures the proportion of actual negatives that are correctly identified — for example, the percentage of healthy people correctly identified as not having the condition.

Definitions

Given true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), and false negatives (FN):

  • Sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN)
  • Specificity = TN / (TN + FP)

A perfect test has sensitivity and specificity of 100%. In practice there is usually a trade-off: raising the threshold for a positive result increases specificity but lowers sensitivity, a relationship summarized by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve.

Interpretation

A highly sensitive test is useful for ruling a disease out when it is negative ("SnNout"), while a highly specific test is useful for ruling a disease in when it is positive ("SpPin"). Sensitivity and specificity do not depend on disease prevalence, unlike predictive values, which do — so even a very accurate test yields many false positives when screening for a rare condition.
