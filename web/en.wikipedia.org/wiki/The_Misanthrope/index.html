The Misanthrope

AuthorMolière
Original titleLe Misanthrope, ou L'Atrabilaire amoureux
LanguageFrench
GenreComedy of manners
First performance4 June 1666
Place premieredPalais Royal, Paris

The Misanthrope, or the Cantankerous Lover (French: Le Misanthrope ou l'Atrabilaire amoureux, pronounced [lə mizɑ̃tʁɔp u latʁabilɛːʁ amuʁø]) is a 17th-century comedy of manners in verse written by Molière. It was first performed on 4 June 1666 at the Théâtre du Palais-Royal in Paris by the King's Troupe, with Molière himself playing the title role of Alceste.

The play satirizes the hypocrisies of French aristocratic society, but it also engages in a more serious tone when pointing out the flaws that all humans possess. The play differs from other farces of the time by employing dynamic characters like Alceste and Célimène rather than flat caricatures drawn from the tradition of commedia dell'arte. The play, which is partly autobiographical, is considered one of the best of Molière's works and one of the greatest works in French literature.

Plot summary

Alceste, the protagonist, hates all humanity for its hypocrisy, deceit, and flattering lies. He tells Philinte, his friend, that he expects everyone to be completely honest with him and admits to hating all people because of their dishonesty. To his embarrassment, Alceste falls in love with Célimène, a frivolous young widow and the best expression of the very behavior he despises. Despite his loathing of her, she refuses to change, charged by Alceste with being coquettish and malicious. When Alceste demands that she renounce society and live with him alone in exile, Célimène refuses, and Alceste leaves society behind, disgusted.

Characters

The principal characters are Alceste, the misanthrope himself; Célimène, the young widow he loves; Philinte, his friend and foil; Éliante, Célimène's cousin and Alceste's unrequited admirer; Oronte, a vain poet; Arsinoé, a duplicitous older woman; and the courtiers Acaste and Clitandre.

Reception and influence

Contemporary audiences received the play coolly compared with Molière's broader farces, since its comedy is subtler and its hero sympathetic in his sincerity yet ridiculous in his rigidity. Rousseau famously criticized the play in his Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre, arguing that Molière had made virtue — Alceste's honesty — ridiculous and vice charming. Modern productions, including notable translations by Richard Wilbur, Tony Harrison, and Martin Crimp, regularly revive the play in English and French, and it remains one of the most frequently performed works of classical French theatre.
