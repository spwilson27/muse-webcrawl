CategoryIgneous rock
Main mineralsOlivine (>90%)
Accessory mineralsPyroxene, chromite, magnetite, pyrope

Dunite is an igneous, plutonic rock of ultramafic composition, consisting almost entirely of the mineral olivine. The rock is named after Dun Mountain near Nelson, New Zealand, where it was first described. Dunite is defined as containing more than 90% olivine, with small amounts of pyroxene, chromite, magnetite and pyrope among its accessory minerals.

Occurrence

Dunite forms a major constituent of the Earth's mantle above a depth of about 400 kilometres and occurs in layered igneous intrusions, ophiolite complexes and alpine peridotite massifs. It may form by the accumulation of dense olivine crystals on the floor of a magma chamber, or by the dissolution of pyroxene from earlier-formed peridotite as new magma percolates through it.

Well-known occurrences include the type locality at Dun Mountain, the Twin Sisters range in Washington state in the United States, and extensive bodies in ophiolites in Oman, Cyprus and Newfoundland. Xenoliths of dunite are frequently brought to the surface in kimberlite pipes, providing samples of mantle material.

Uses and significance

Dunite is mined in some locations as a source of olivine for refractory materials and foundry sand. It has also attracted attention as a candidate rock for carbon dioxide sequestration, because olivine reacts with CO2 to form stable carbonate minerals. Deposits of chromite and platinum-group elements are in places hosted within dunite bodies.
