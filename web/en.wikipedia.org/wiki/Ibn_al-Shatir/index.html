Ibn al-Shatir

Ibn al-Shāṭir (1304–1375), full name ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn ʿAlī ibn Ibrāhīm ibn al-Shāṭir, was a Syrian astronomer, mathematician, and engineer who served as muwaqqit (timekeeper) at the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus. He is regarded as one of the most important astronomers of the Islamic Golden Age's later period and a leading figure of the Maragha tradition of astronomy.

Life

Born in Damascus in 1304, Ibn al-Shatir was orphaned young and raised by a relative who taught him the craft of inlaying ivory, from which his surname "al-Shatir" may derive. He travelled to Cairo and Alexandria to study astronomy and mathematics, then returned to Damascus, where he spent most of his career at the Umayyad Mosque determining prayer times and designing astronomical instruments.

Astronomy

In his major work, Nihāyat al-suʾl fī taṣḥīḥ al-uṣūl ("The Final Quest Concerning the Rectification of Principles"), Ibn al-Shatir presented new planetary models that eliminated the Ptolemaic equant and other devices he considered physically objectionable, using instead combinations of epicycles — including a version of the Ṭūsī couple — to produce uniform circular motions. His lunar model corrected errors in Ptolemy's predicted variation of the Moon's apparent size, and his solar model improved the calculation of the Sun's distance.

Scholars have noted strikingly exact mathematical similarities between Ibn al-Shatir's models and those published nearly two centuries later by Nicolaus Copernicus, including identical diagrams and devices, raising longstanding questions about possible transmission of these ideas to Renaissance Europe, though no direct documentary chain has been established.

Instruments and timekeeping

Ibn al-Shatir designed sophisticated sundials and astronomical instruments, including a celebrated horizontal sundial installed on the minaret of the Umayyad Mosque in 1371–72, considered a masterpiece of medieval timekeeping. He also wrote on the astrolabe and other instruments.
