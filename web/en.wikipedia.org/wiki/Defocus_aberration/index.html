Defocus aberration

In optics, defocus is the aberration in which an image is simply out of focus. This aberration is familiar to anyone who has used a camera, microscope, telescope, or a pair of binoculars. Optically, defocus refers to a translation of the focal plane along the optical axis away from the detector surface. In general, defocus reduces the sharpness and contrast of the image.

Description

An optical system images a point source to a point only when the image plane coincides with the focal plane. If the image is recorded at a plane displaced from focus, the point spreads into a blur spot — in a simple system, a uniformly illuminated disc (the circle of confusion); with real apertures, the blur takes the shape of the aperture, an effect exploited artistically as bokeh in photography. In wavefront terms, defocus is the quadratic term of the wavefront error expansion (one of the Zernike polynomials), corresponding to a spherical wavefront centered on a plane other than the detector plane.

Defocus in vision

In the human eye, defocus arises when the eye's optical power does not match its axial length, producing refractive errors: myopia (image focused in front of the retina), hyperopia (behind the retina), and loss of accommodation with age (presbyopia). These are corrected with spectacles, contact lenses, or refractive surgery. Research on animal models shows that imposed defocus drives eye growth — the eye elongates under hyperopic defocus — a mechanism implicated in the development of myopia and in treatments such as orthokeratology.

Computational treatment

Because defocus blur is spatially varying with scene depth, it is both a nuisance — addressed by autofocus systems and deblurring algorithms — and a signal: depth-from-defocus techniques estimate scene geometry from the amount of blur, and synthetic-aperture and light-field cameras allow refocusing after capture.
