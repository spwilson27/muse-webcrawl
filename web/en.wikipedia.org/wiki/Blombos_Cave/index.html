Blombos Cave is an archaeological site located in a limestone cliff near Still Bay on the southern coast of South Africa. It is renowned for evidence of early symbolic behavior by anatomically modern humans during the Middle Stone Age, including engraved ochre plaques, shell beads, and an ochre-processing workshop dated to about 100,000 years ago.

Excavation and finds

Excavations led by Christopher Henshilwood began in 1991. The cave's occupation layers, dated to between roughly 100,000 and 70,000 years ago, belong largely to the Still Bay technocomplex, noted for finely crafted bifacial stone points. Among the most famous finds are two pieces of red ochre engraved with cross-hatched geometric patterns (about 75,000 years old), perforated Nassarius shell beads, bone tools, and ochre-processing kits with abalone-shell containers.

In 2018 researchers reported a cross-hatched drawing made with an ochre crayon on a silcrete flake, dated to about 73,000 years ago and described as the earliest known drawing by a human.

Significance

The Blombos finds are central to debates about the origins of modern human cognition and symbolic culture. The engraved ochres and personal ornaments suggest that symbolic thinking emerged in Africa tens of thousands of years earlier than the "Upper Palaeolithic revolution" once proposed for Europe.
