Comet Swift–Tuttle (formally designated 109P/Swift–Tuttle) is a large periodic comet with an orbital period of about 133 years. It fits the classical definition of a Halley-type comet (period between 20 and 200 years). It was independently discovered in July 1862 by American astronomers Lewis Swift and Horace Parnell Tuttle.

Orbit and history

The comet's orbit was later linked to observations going back to antiquity, including Chinese records of 69 BC and AD 188. It was recovered in September 1992 by Japanese astronomer Tsuruhiko Kiuchi, confirming predictions by Brian G. Marsden, who had also suggested the identification with the historical apparitions. Its nucleus, about 26 kilometres (16 mi) across, is among the largest of any periodic comet — larger than the object believed to have caused the extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs if it were to strike Earth, though no impact is predicted: the comet's next return is in 2126, when it will pass at a safe distance, and subsequent returns have been assessed as posing no threat for at least the next two millennia.

Perseid meteor shower

Swift–Tuttle is the parent body of the Perseid meteor shower, the most prolific annual meteor shower as seen from Earth. Debris shed by the comet along its orbit intersects Earth's path each August, producing meteors radiating from the constellation Perseus.
