Insulin pump

Insulin pump
Type: Medical device
Uses: Diabetes mellitus management
Delivery: Subcutaneous, continuous

An insulin pump is a medical device used for the administration of insulin in the treatment of diabetes mellitus, also known as continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) therapy. A typical pump is a small computerized device that delivers rapid-acting insulin through a thin plastic tube ending in a cannula inserted under the skin.

How it works

The pump delivers insulin in two ways: a continuous basal rate that mimics the background insulin released by a healthy pancreas, and user-triggered bolus doses to cover meals and correct high blood glucose. Pump users test glucose with fingersticks or, increasingly, a continuous glucose monitor (CGM). Hybrid closed-loop systems combine a pump with a CGM and a control algorithm that automatically adjusts basal delivery—an approach toward an artificial pancreas.

History

The first wearable insulin pump, a large backpack-sized device, was designed by Arnold Kadish in 1963. Commercial pumps appeared in the late 1970s and 1980s, and by the 2000s miniaturized pumps and tubeless "patch pumps" such as the OmniPod became widely used.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include more flexible dosing, fewer injections, and often improved glycemic control with fewer severe hypoglycemic episodes. Disadvantages include cost, the need for ongoing training, risk of infusion-set failure leading to rapid hyperglycemia and ketoacidosis (because no long-acting insulin depot is present), and skin infection at the insertion site.
