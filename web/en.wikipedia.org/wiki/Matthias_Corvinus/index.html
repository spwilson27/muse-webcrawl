Matthias Corvinus

Matthias Corvinus (Hungarian: Hunyadi Mátyás; Romanian: Matei Corvin; 23 February 1443 – 6 April 1490) was King of Hungary and Croatia from 1458 to 1490. The younger son of the regent John Hunyadi, he was elected king as a teenager and grew into one of the most powerful rulers of late medieval Central Europe, a centraliser who built Hungary's first professional standing army, reformed taxation and law, and patronised a brilliant Renaissance court.

Accession

After the death of John Hunyadi (1456) and the execution of his elder son Ladislaus by King Ladislaus V, young Matthias was held prisoner in Prague by George of Poděbrady. When King Ladislaus died suddenly in 1457, Hungarian nobles elected Matthias king in January 1458 — legend holds the election took place with crowds gathered on the frozen Danube. He bought his release and, after recovering the Holy Crown of Hungary from Emperor Frederick III, was crowned in 1464.

Reforms and wars

Matthias curbed the great barons, introduced frequent taxation (including the controversial annual "extraordinary tax"), reformed the judiciary and royal administration, and created the Black Army — a standing mercenary force of perhaps 20,000 men, highly unusual for its time. He held the Ottoman frontier in the south, supporting Bosnian and Serbian buffer states, while campaigning in the west against Bohemia (claiming its crown lands of Moravia, Silesia and Lusatia) and against Emperor Frederick III, whose Austrian capital, Vienna, he captured in 1485 and made his residence.

Renaissance court and legacy

Matthias's court at Buda was among the earliest centres of Renaissance culture north of the Alps. His second wife, Beatrice of Naples, brought Italian artists and scholars; his library, the Bibliotheca Corviniana, with some 2,000–2,500 illuminated volumes, was one of the largest in Europe. He founded the Universitas Istropolitana in Pressburg (Bratislava) in 1465. In Hungarian folklore he survives as "Matthias the Just," a king who walked among the people in disguise to right wrongs. His death without a legitimate heir led to the election of the weak Vladislaus II and the rapid undoing of many of his reforms.
