Agreement (linguistics)

In linguistics, agreement or concord occurs when a word changes form depending on the other words to which it relates. It is an instance of inflection, and usually involves making the value of some grammatical category (such as gender or person) "agree" between varied words or parts of the sentence.

For example, in Standard English, one may say I am or he is, but not I is or he am. This is because English verbs am and is have different forms depending on the subject of the sentence. The forms of the verb must be consistent with the person and number of the subject; this is an example of subject–verb agreement.

Categories of agreement

Agreement typically involves features such as:

  • Person — first, second, or third person
  • Number — singular, dual, or plural
  • Gender — masculine, feminine, or neuter
  • Case — nominative, accusative, and so on

In many languages, adjectives agree with the nouns they modify in gender, number and case; determiners and verbs may also agree with their arguments. The phenomenon is especially rich in highly inflected languages such as Latin, Russian and Arabic.

Agreement in English

Modern English has relatively little agreement marking compared with its ancestor Old English. The main surviving pattern is subject–verb agreement in the present tense, where a third-person singular subject takes the suffix -s (she walks), while other persons and numbers take the bare form. Pronouns also retain case distinctions (I/me, he/him), and demonstratives distinguish number (this/these, that/those).

Theoretical treatment

In generative grammar, agreement is commonly modelled as a relation between a probe (an unvalued feature on a head such as T or v) and a goal (a noun phrase bearing matching valued features). Minimalist accounts following Chomsky treat agreement as feature valuation under c-command. In other frameworks, such as head-driven phrase structure grammar, agreement is handled through structure sharing of feature values.
