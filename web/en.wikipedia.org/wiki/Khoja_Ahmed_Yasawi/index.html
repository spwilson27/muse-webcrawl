Khoja Ahmed Yasawi (Kazakh: Қожа Ахмет Ясауи; 1093–1166) was a Turkic poet and Sufi mystic, regarded as one of the most influential figures in the spread of Islam among the Turkic peoples of Central Asia. He founded the Yasawiyya order of Sufism and is honoured across Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Turkey and the wider Turkic world as a spiritual patriarch — sometimes called the "second teacher" of the Turks.

Life

Yasawi was born in Sayram, in present-day southern Kazakhstan, and orphaned young. He moved to Bukhara, then one of the great centres of Islamic learning, where he studied under the Sufi master Yusuf Hamadani. After his teacher's death he briefly led the community in Bukhara, but later returned to Yasi (modern Turkestan), where he spent the rest of his life teaching. According to legend, at the age of 63 — the age at which the Prophet Muhammad died — he withdrew into an underground cell and spent his remaining years in prayer and contemplation.

Works and teachings

His collection of mystical poems, the Diwan-i Hikmat ("Book of Wisdom"), is the earliest major work of Turkic Islamic literature. Written in a Turkic vernacular (Karluk/Chagatai-type language) rather than Arabic or Persian, its short, accessible quatrains on humility, renunciation, love of God and the fleeting nature of the world made Islamic teaching intelligible to nomadic audiences. The poems were memorised and recited by dervishes across the steppe for centuries.

Mausoleum and legacy

Two centuries after his death, the conqueror Timur ordered a monumental mausoleum raised over his grave in Turkestan (1389–1405). The unfinished but colossal brick structure — with one of the largest domes in Central Asia — became a major pilgrimage site and is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site and a national symbol of Kazakhstan. Yasawi's influence extends to later Sufi orders, including connections claimed by the Naqshbandiyya and the Bektashi tradition in Anatolia.
