The ducat (Italian: ducato) was a gold trade coin used throughout Europe from the late Middle Ages until the early twentieth century, famous for the constancy of its weight and purity over centuries. Many types bore the image of the issuing ruler—in Venice the Doge kneeling before St Mark—and the coin's name derives from the Latin ducatus, "duchy," echoed in the legend of the first issues ("Sit tibi, Christe, datus, quem tu regis, iste ducatus": "O Christ, let this duchy, which you rule, be dedicated to you").

History

The name was first used for silver coins issued in 1140 by Roger II of Sicily as Duke of Apulia. The definitive gold ducat was introduced by the Republic of Venice in 1284 under Doge Giovanni Dandolo, modeled on Florence's florin but slightly heavier at about 3.5 grams of nearly pure gold. Venice maintained the ducat—later called the zecchino or sequin after the Zecca mint—at essentially unchanged weight and fineness for over 500 years, until the fall of the Republic in 1797, making it a byword for sound money. The type spread across Europe: Hungary, the Netherlands, the German states, Austria, Spain, and many others struck ducats; the Dutch ducat and the Austrian ducat became standard trade coins of the Levant and the Baltic.

Legacy

Because of their reliability, ducats circulated long after their states vanished, and restrikes continue today: Austria still issues 1- and 4-ducat pieces (official restrikes dated 1915) as bullion, and the Royal Dutch Mint issues gold and silver ducats annually. The ducat is embedded in literature and idiom—Shylock's "my ducats!" in The Merchant of Venice being the most famous—and for centuries it served as the benchmark against which European gold coinages were weighed.
