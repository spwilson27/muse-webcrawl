Morale

Morale is the capacity of a group's members to maintain belief in an institution or goal, particularly in the face of opposition or hardship. Morale is assessed by the group's confidence, enthusiasm, and discipline, and the willingness of members to perform assigned tasks. It is a term that is also used in business, and in other social settings such as schools, sports teams, and workplaces.

The word derives from the French moral ("morale" as a feminine noun meaning "moral strength, spirits"), and entered English usage in this collective sense chiefly through military writing in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

Military morale

In military science, morale is considered one of the principal factors in combat power. Napoleon is frequently paraphrased as holding that "the moral is to the physical as three is to one." High morale sustains units under stress, reduces surrender and desertion, and supports initiative; low morale produces apathy, malingering, fragging in extreme cases, and collapse under attack. Factors influencing military morale include leadership, perceived legitimacy of the cause, unit cohesion, supply and rest, mail from home, confidence in weapons and commanders, and the expectation of victory.

Historians debate the sources of cohesion in combat units. Studies of the Wehrmacht and of American troops in the Second World War emphasised the "primary group" — soldiers fighting for their immediate comrades rather than for ideology. Modern doctrine treats morale as a command responsibility, monitored through welfare provision, rotation policies, chaplaincy, and after-action care.

Workplace morale

Employee morale describes the overall outlook, attitude, satisfaction, and confidence that employees feel at work. It is often said that positive morale leads to increased productivity, better attendance, and lower staff turnover, while poor morale produces disengagement and "quiet quitting". Organisations attempt to measure morale with engagement surveys and to improve it through recognition, fair pay, job security, autonomy, and credible communication from management. Research distinguishes morale — a group-level, forward-looking confidence — from individual job satisfaction, though the two overlap.

Measurement

Because morale is a collective psychological state, it is measured indirectly: questionnaires, absenteeism and turnover rates, disciplinary statistics, productivity, and in military settings rates of self-inflicted injury, desertion, and surrender. Critics note that morale is difficult to separate from the material conditions that produce it.
