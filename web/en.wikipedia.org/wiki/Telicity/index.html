Telicity

In linguistics, telicity (from Greek τέλος, "end, goal") is the property of a verb or verb phrase that presents an event as having a definite endpoint. A telic predicate describes an action tending toward a goal — for example, "build a house" or "arrive" — while an atelic predicate describes an action with no inherent endpoint, such as "run" or "push a cart". The term was introduced in this sense by Henrik Rosenstand (1957); telicity is an aspectual property of predicates rather than of verbs alone.

Diagnostics

Classic tests: telic predicates combine naturally with "in"-adverbials ("She read the book in an hour"), while atelic ones take "for"-adverbials ("She ran for an hour"). The imperfective paradox distinguishes the classes: "John was running" entails "John ran", but "John was building a house" does not entail "John built a house", because the telic event's culmination need not occur. Telicity often depends on the whole verb phrase: a quantized object ("eat an apple", "drink a glass of wine") yields a telic reading, whereas a bare plural or mass object ("eat apples", "drink wine") yields an atelic one — an interaction analysed through the mereology of objects and events (Krifka, Verkuyl).

Relation to Vendler classes

In Vendler's classification, accomplishments and achievements are telic, while activities and states are atelic. Some languages grammaticalize the distinction: Slavic perfective/imperfective aspect correlates strongly with telicity, and Finnish marks it through object case (partitive for atelic, total object for telic). Telicity must be distinguished from grammatical aspect itself: a telic event can be presented imperfectively, and vice versa.
