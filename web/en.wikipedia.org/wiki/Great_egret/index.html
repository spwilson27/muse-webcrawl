Great egret

The great egret (Ardea alba), also known as the common egret, large egret, or (in the Old World) great white egret or great white heron, is a large, widely distributed egret. It is found across much of the world, in tropical and warm temperate regions of Asia, Africa, the Americas, and southern Europe.

Description

The great egret is a large heron with all-white plumage, standing up to 1 m (3.3 ft) tall, with a length of 80–104 cm and a wingspan of 131–170 cm. It has a yellow bill and black legs and feet. In breeding plumage it grows long, delicate ornamental feathers (aigrettes) on its back. It flies slowly with its neck retracted, characteristic of herons.

Behaviour

Great egrets forage in shallow water and wetlands, standing motionless or wading slowly to spear fish, frogs, small mammals, and invertebrates with their sharp bills. They breed in colonies, often with other wading birds, building stick nests in trees near water. The clutch is usually three to four pale blue-green eggs.

Conservation

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, great egrets were hunted nearly to extinction in North America for their plumes, which decorated ladies' hats. Outrage over the plume trade spurred early conservation laws and was a founding cause of the National Audubon Society; the great egret in flight is the symbol of the Audubon Society. Protected populations recovered strongly, and the species is now listed as Least Concern by the IUCN.
