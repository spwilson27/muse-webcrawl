Profanity

Profanity, also known as swearing, cursing or obscenity, is a socially offensive use of language. Profanities are generally words or expressions considered vulgar, blasphemous, or insulting within a given culture, and may show disrespect for sacred things, serve as intensifiers, or express strong emotion such as frustration, surprise or pain.

Etymology and categories

The word "profanity" comes from Latin profanus, "outside the temple" — that which is secular rather than sacred. Scholars commonly divide profanities into categories: religious profanity (blasphemy, taking God's name in vain), excretory terms, sexual terms, terms related to disease and pestilence (historically strong in Dutch), slurs based on personal background, and animal terms. Which category dominates varies by culture and era: in medieval England religious swearing was considered most serious, whereas modern English obscenity centres largely on sexual and excretory vocabulary.

Psychology and neurolinguistics

Swearing is processed differently from ordinary language. People with aphasia may retain the ability to swear when other speech is lost, and involuntary swearing (coprolalia) occurs in a minority of people with Tourette syndrome. Experiments by psychologists such as Richard Stephens have found that swearing aloud increases pain tolerance and physical strength, suggesting a link to emotional arousal and the fight-or-flight response. Taboo words also attract attention and memory more strongly than neutral equivalents.

Social norms and law

Acceptability of profanity depends on context: audience, setting, region and medium. Broadcasting regulators in many countries restrict strong language before evening "watershed" hours. Legally, some jurisdictions penalise obscene or profane speech in public, while in the United States much profanity is protected by the First Amendment, as affirmed in Cohen v. California (1971). Workplaces and schools typically maintain their own standards, and attitudes have generally liberalised over the past half-century.

Minced oaths and euphemism

Speakers frequently soften taboo words into "minced oaths" — altered forms such as gosh, darn, heck or shoot — which signal the emotion while avoiding the offence. Written English sometimes uses typographic conventions like asterisks or the cartoon convention of mixed punctuation symbols to indicate swearing without spelling it out.
