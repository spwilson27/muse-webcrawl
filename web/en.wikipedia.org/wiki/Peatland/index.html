Peatland

A peatland is a type of wetland whose soils consist largely of peat — accumulated, partially decayed organic matter, mainly plant material, that forms where waterlogging slows decomposition more than plant growth adds new material. Peatlands cover roughly 3 per cent of the Earth's land surface, some 4 million square kilometres, yet they store a disproportionately large share of the world's soil carbon.

Formation and types

Peat forms when saturated, oxygen-poor (anaerobic) conditions prevent the complete breakdown of plant remains; sphagnum mosses are especially important peat-formers in northern bogs. The main types of peatland include bogs, which are fed only by rainwater (ombrotrophic) and are acidic and nutrient-poor; fens, which receive groundwater or surface water (minerotrophic) and are richer in nutrients; and tropical peat swamp forests, extensive in Southeast Asia and the Congo Basin. Terms such as mire, muskeg and moor overlap with these categories.

Distribution

The largest peatland areas lie in the boreal and subarctic zones of Canada, Russia (notably the West Siberian Lowland) and northern Europe, with significant areas in Alaska, Indonesia, the Republic of the Congo and the Peruvian Amazon. The Cuvette Centrale peatlands of the Congo Basin, mapped in detail in the 2010s, are among the largest tropical peat deposits.

Carbon storage and climate

Peatlands store an estimated 600 billion tonnes of carbon — more than all the world's vegetation combined. Intact peatlands act as long-term carbon sinks, but when drained for agriculture, forestry or peat extraction, they oxidise and release carbon dioxide, and disturbed peatlands are estimated to contribute around 5 per cent of global anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions. Rewetting and restoration are therefore priorities in climate policy, and peatland protection features in international efforts such as the Global Peatlands Initiative.

Uses and threats

Peat has traditionally been cut for fuel in Ireland, Scotland and elsewhere, and is widely used in horticulture as a growing medium — a use increasingly discouraged because of habitat damage. Peatlands also preserve archaeological material and pollen records and support specialised wildlife. Threats include drainage, fire (especially in tropical peatlands, where burning has caused regional haze crises), and climate change itself.
