General election

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A general election is an election in which all or most members of a given political body are chosen — as opposed to a by-election, which fills a single vacancy, or a primary election within a party. The term is used mainly in parliamentary systems for the election of the national legislature, and in the United States for the final stage of elections for public office (as against primaries).

Timing

Parliamentary systems may fix terms (with early dissolution constrained, as in Germany or Norway) or allow governments to call elections at advantageous moments ("snap elections"), subject to maximum terms — classically five years in the UK tradition. Presidential systems hold legislative elections on fixed cycles, sometimes coinciding with presidential elections or deliberately staggered as midterms. Election campaigns are regulated to varying degrees: official campaign periods, spending limits, broadcasting rules and silence periods differ widely between countries.

Administration

General elections are run by electoral commissions or government ministries, covering registration, polling, counting and adjudication of disputes, and are commonly observed by domestic and international monitors. Turnout varies with compulsion (Australia, Belgium), competitiveness and registration systems, and is a standard indicator of democratic health.
