Heavy cavalry was a class of cavalry intended to deliver a battlefield charge and to engage in melee combat. Unlike light cavalry, which was used for reconnaissance, screening, skirmishing, and raiding, heavy cavalry was generally mounted on large horses, often protected by armour, and employed as a decisive shock force intended to break enemy formations by the momentum and violence of its charge.

History

Heavy cavalry appeared in antiquity with the fully armoured cataphracts of Persia and the steppe peoples, and the companion cavalry of Alexander the Great. In medieval Europe the armoured knight, charging with couched lance, dominated the battlefield for centuries; Byzantine cataphracts and later Polish winged hussars represented the type at its most formidable. The spread of pike-and-shot tactics reduced the cavalry charge's dominance, but heavy cavalry — cuirassiers, carabiniers, and heavy dragoons — remained a battlefield arm through the Napoleonic Wars, when massed charges could still decide battles such as Eylau and Waterloo.

Decline

The increasing range, accuracy, and rate of fire of infantry weapons in the later nineteenth century made frontal charges increasingly costly. Heavy cavalry regiments survived into the First World War, where they found limited opportunities on the Western Front, and by the Second World War armoured fighting vehicles had fully inherited the shock role. Many former heavy cavalry regiments converted to armoured units, and the term survives in some ceremonial and armoured regiment titles.

Equipment and tactics

Heavy cavalrymen characteristically wore metal armour — mail, plate, or the cuirass that gave cuirassiers their name — and carried straight heavy sabres, lances, or pistols. Formations charged in line or column at the gallop, aiming to strike with cohesion at a vulnerable point; success depended on timing, ground, and the enemy's morale as much as on physical collision.
