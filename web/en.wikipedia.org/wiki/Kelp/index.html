Kelp

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Kelps are large brown algae (order Laminariales) that grow in cool, nutrient-rich coastal waters, forming underwater forests that are among the most productive and dynamic ecosystems on Earth. Some species, such as giant kelp (Macrocystis pyrifera), can grow tens of centimetres a day and reach lengths over 30 metres.

Kelp forests

Rooted by holdfasts to rocky reefs, kelps create a three-dimensional habitat sheltering fish, invertebrates and marine mammals; sea otters, by eating the sea urchins that graze kelp, famously protect the forests. Kelp forests fringe the Pacific coasts of the Americas, and coasts of South Africa, Australia, New Zealand and the North Atlantic.

Uses and threats

Kelp is harvested for alginates (food and industrial thickeners), eaten directly in East Asian cuisines (kombu), used as fertiliser, and increasingly farmed for food, biofuel and carbon sequestration. Forests are threatened by warming seas, marine heatwaves and urchin outbreaks following the loss of predators such as otters and sunflower stars.
