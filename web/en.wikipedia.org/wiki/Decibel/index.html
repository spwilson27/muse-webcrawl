The decibel (symbol: dB) is a relative unit of measurement equal to one tenth of a bel. It expresses the ratio of two values of a power or root-power quantity on a logarithmic scale. Two signals whose levels differ by one decibel have a power ratio of 101/10 (about 1.259), or root-power ratio of 101/20.

Definition

For power quantities, the level difference is L = 10 log10(P₁/P₀) dB. For root-power (field) quantities such as voltage or sound pressure, L = 20 log10(A₁/A₀) dB, because power is proportional to the square of amplitude. A change of +3 dB corresponds roughly to a doubling of power; +10 dB is a tenfold increase, perceived as roughly a doubling of loudness for sound.

History

The unit originates in telephone engineering of the 1920s: the bel, named for Alexander Graham Bell, measured power loss per mile of cable; the decibel was standardised as the practical working unit. It is now used throughout acoustics, electronics, control theory and antenna engineering.

Suffixes and reference levels

Appended letters specify the reference: dBm (1 milliwatt), dBW (1 watt), dBV and dBu (voltage references), dB SPL (sound pressure relative to 20 micropascals), and dBA (A-weighted sound level, approximating human hearing sensitivity).
