Giuseppe Garibaldi (4 July 1807 – 2 June 1882) was an Italian general, revolutionary and republican who played a central role in the unification of Italy (the Risorgimento). Nicknamed the "Hero of the Two Worlds" for his exploits in both Europe and South America, he led the volunteer force known as the Redshirts, whose Expedition of the Thousand (1860) conquered Sicily and Naples and made Italian unification possible. He is regarded, with Cavour, Victor Emmanuel II and Mazzini, as one of Italy's "fathers of the fatherland".

Early life and South America

Garibaldi was born in Nice (then under French rule) into a family of coastal traders and went to sea as a merchant sailor. In 1833 he joined Giuseppe Mazzini's Young Italy movement and took part in a failed uprising in Piedmont in 1834; condemned to death in absentia, he fled to South America. There he spent fourteen years as a soldier of fortune: he fought for the separatist Rio-Grandense Republic in Brazil's Ragamuffin War, where he met his wife Anita, and later commanded an Italian legion in Uruguay's civil war, winning fame defending Montevideo. The red shirts his legion wore — originally cheap garments intended for slaughterhouse workers — became his enduring emblem.

Return to Italy and the wars of independence

Returning in 1848, Garibaldi defended the short-lived Roman Republic against French troops in 1849; Anita died during the retreat. After years of exile in New York and Peru, and a spell as a candle-maker on Staten Island, he returned to Italy, bought part of the island of Caprera off Sardinia, and in 1859 commanded the Hunters of the Alps in the Second Italian War of Independence against Austria.

Expedition of the Thousand

In May 1860 Garibaldi sailed from Genoa with about a thousand volunteers aboard two steamers. Landing at Marsala in Sicily, he defeated Bourbon forces at Calatafimi, took Palermo, and crossed to the mainland, winning at Milazzo and entering Naples in September. After the decisive Battle of the Volturno, he met Victor Emmanuel II at Teano and, with the famous handshake, handed the conquered south to the King — setting aside his own republican ideals for the sake of unity. The Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed in March 1861.

Later campaigns and final years

Garibaldi twice marched on Rome against the Pope's temporal power: in 1862 he was wounded and captured by Italian royal troops at Aspromonte, and in 1867 he was defeated by French and Papal forces at Mentana. In 1866 he led volunteers against Austria in the Trentino. During the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71) he came out of retirement to command the Army of the Vosges for the French Third Republic, winning its few successes around Dijon. Elected to the Italian parliament, he campaigned for democratic reform, universal suffrage and the emancipation of women. He died on Caprera in 1882.

Legacy

Garibaldi became one of the most famous men of the 19th century — a popular hero in Britain, the United States and Latin America as well as Italy, admired by figures from Abraham Lincoln (who offered him a command in the Union Army) to Victor Hugo. Statues and monuments to him stand in cities across the world, and his name is borne by ships, a class of Italian naval vessels, and even a species of bright orange damselfish, Hypsypops rubicundus, the state fish of California.
