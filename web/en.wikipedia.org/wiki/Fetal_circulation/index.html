In animals that give live birth, the fetal circulation is the circulatory system of a fetus and is quite different from that of an adult. Because the fetus receives all of its oxygen and nutrients from the placenta rather than from its own (fluid-filled, non-functioning) lungs and gut, the fetal system uses three shunts — the ductus venosus, the foramen ovale, and the ductus arteriosus — to bypass the liver and lungs and direct the best-oxygenated blood to the heart and brain.

Pathway of blood

Oxygenated blood returns from the placenta through the umbilical vein. About half passes through the ductus venosus directly into the inferior vena cava; the rest perfuses the liver. In the right atrium, the stream is directed across the foramen ovale into the left atrium, then through the left ventricle into the aorta, supplying the coronary and cerebral circulations first. Deoxygenated blood returning via the superior vena cava enters the right ventricle and pulmonary artery, but because pulmonary vascular resistance is high, most of it crosses the ductus arteriosus into the descending aorta and returns to the placenta through the two umbilical arteries.

Changes at birth

With the first breaths, the lungs expand, pulmonary resistance falls, and clamping of the cord removes the low-resistance placenta from the circuit. Left atrial pressure rises above right atrial pressure, closing the flap of the foramen ovale. Rising blood oxygen and falling prostaglandins constrict the ductus arteriosus, which normally closes functionally within the first days and anatomically within weeks, becoming the ligamentum arteriosum. The ductus venosus becomes the ligamentum venosum, and the umbilical vessels are obliterated.

Clinical significance

Failure of the ductus arteriosus to close (patent ductus arteriosus) is a common congenital cardiac problem, especially in preterm infants, and may be treated with prostaglandin inhibitors such as indomethacin or ibuprofen, or by surgical/catheter closure. Conversely, some congenital heart lesions (such as hypoplastic left heart syndrome and transposition of the great arteries) depend on a patent ductus for survival, so prostaglandin E1 is given to keep it open until surgery. Fetal circulation was first described in part by Galen, and its shunts were characterised by the anatomists Aranzi, Botallo, and Harvey's contemporaries in the 16th–17th centuries.
