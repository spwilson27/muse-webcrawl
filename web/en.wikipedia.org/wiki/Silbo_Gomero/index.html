Silbo Gomero

Silbo Gomero (Spanish: "Gomeran whistle") is a whistled register of Spanish used on La Gomera in the Canary Islands to communicate across the island's deep ravines. It transforms the sounds of spoken Spanish into whistles that can be heard at distances of up to several kilometres.

How it works

The whistler produces a continuous stream of sound, usually by whistling with one or two fingers in the mouth. The five vowels of Spanish are distinguished by pitch, and the consonants are represented by modulations — interruptions and pitch glides — in the whistle, yielding a reduced phonology that hearers reconstruct from context. Messages are typically practical: announcements, invitations, warnings, and summonses.

History

Whistled speech probably arrived with the island's original North African (Guanche) inhabitants and was adapted to Spanish after the Castilian conquest. With the coming of telephones and roads its use declined sharply in the later twentieth century, but a revival followed: since 1999 Silbo has been taught in La Gomera's primary schools. In 2009 UNESCO inscribed Silbo Gomero on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Related systems

Whistled languages exist elsewhere — for example "bird language" in Kuşköy, Turkey, and whistled Mazatec in Mexico — but Silbo is among the most thoroughly studied by linguists and neuroscientists, who have shown that fluent silbadores process the whistles in the brain's language areas.
