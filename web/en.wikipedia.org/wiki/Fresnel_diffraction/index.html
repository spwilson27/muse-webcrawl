Fresnel diffraction

In optics, Fresnel diffraction is the pattern of waves diffracted by an object when observed relatively close to it — the near field — where the curvature of the wavefronts must be taken into account. It is named after the French physicist Augustin-Jean Fresnel, whose 1818 memoir founded the mathematical theory. At large distances the pattern crosses over to the simpler far-field regime of Fraunhofer diffraction.

Theory

Fresnel's approach applies the Huygens–Fresnel principle: each point of the aperture emits a spherical wavelet, and the field at an observation point is the sum of these wavelets with their phase differences, computed via the Fresnel integrals. A convenient construction divides the wavefront into Fresnel zones (half-period zones); successive zones contribute with alternating sign, explaining effects such as the bright Arago spot at the centre of a circular obstacle's shadow, where the unblocked zones sum constructively.

The regime is characterized by the Fresnel number F = a²/(Lλ), where a is the aperture size and L the observation distance: Fresnel diffraction applies when F is of order unity or larger, Fraunhofer when F ≪ 1.

Applications

Near-field diffraction matters in X-ray imaging, holography, beam propagation in lasers, and the design of zone plates — concentric-ring optics that focus using Fresnel zones.
