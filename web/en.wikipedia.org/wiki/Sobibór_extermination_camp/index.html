Sobibór (Polish: Sobibór) was a German Nazi extermination camp built and operated by the SS during World War II, located in the forest of southeastern occupied Poland near the village of Sobibór, close to the Bug River and the present-day border with Ukraine. It was one of the three killing centres of Operation Reinhard, alongside Bełżec and Treblinka, established for the mass murder of the Jews of the General Government. Between May 1942 and October 1943, between roughly 170,000 and 250,000 people — Jews from Poland, the Netherlands, France, Germany, Slovakia and elsewhere, as well as Soviet prisoners of war — were murdered there, almost all of them in gas chambers using carbon monoxide.

Operation of the camp

The camp was built in early 1942 under the direction of SS-Hauptsturmführer Franz Stangl, who had previously served at the Hartheim euthanasia centre. Victims arrived by rail at a siding where the station was disguised to resemble an ordinary transit stop. Deceived with promises of showers and disinfection, the deportees were stripped, the women had their hair shorn, and they were driven along a fenced path nicknamed the "Road to Heaven" (Himmelstraße) to gas chambers disguised as shower rooms. Bodies were buried in mass graves and, from late 1942, burned on open-air pyres to erase evidence. A small number of prisoners were kept alive as slave labourers (Sonderkommando work details) to sort victims' property and operate the camp.

Prisoner uprising

On 14 October 1943, the prisoners of Sobibór staged one of the most successful revolts in any Nazi camp. Led by the Polish-Jewish prisoner Leon Feldhendler and the Soviet Jewish officer Alexander Pechersky, the conspirators killed about eleven SS men and several guards with knives and axes made in the camp workshops, then cut the fences under fire. Around 300 of the roughly 600 prisoners escaped into the surrounding forest; many were killed by mines, pursuit, or later betrayal, and about 50–70 escapees survived the war.

Closure and aftermath

After the uprising, the Germans dismantled the camp, shot the remaining prisoners, levelled the site and planted trees to conceal it. Postwar trials convicted some Sobibór personnel; Franz Stangl, extradited from Brazil, was sentenced to life imprisonment in 1970. Guard John Demjanjuk was convicted in Munich in 2011 as an accessory to murder at Sobibór. A memorial and museum now stand at the site; archaeological excavations from 2007 onward uncovered the foundations of the gas chambers and thousands of victims' personal items.
