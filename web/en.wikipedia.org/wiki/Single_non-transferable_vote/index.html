Single non-transferable vote

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The single non-transferable vote (SNTV) is a multi-winner electoral system: in a district electing several members, each voter casts one vote for a single candidate, and the candidates with the highest vote totals win the seats. Simple to ballot and count, it produces semi-proportional results — a party with a disciplined plurality can take several seats, while organised minorities (including party factions) can reliably win representation with a modest vote share roughly equal to the Droop quota of 1/(seats+1).

Effects and history

Japan's House of Representatives used SNTV in medium-sized districts from 1947 to 1993; political scientists credited it with the LDP's factionalism and money politics (multiple LDP candidates competing in the same district against each other, cultivating personal support machines — koenkai — rather than party platforms), and its replacement by a mixed system in 1994 was a centrepiece of political reform. SNTV survives in Taiwan's legislative elections (until the 2008 reform to parallel voting it was the main system), Jordan, Vanuatu and some local systems. Its strategic demand is vote management: nominate the right number of candidates and divide votes evenly among them — parties that over- or under-nominate waste their support, giving party organisations a central, error-punished role.
