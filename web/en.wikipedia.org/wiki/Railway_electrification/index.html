Railway electrification

Railway electrification is the use of electricity to power railway trains, supplied from an external source through overhead lines (catenary) or a conductor rail (third rail), rather than from fuel carried on board as in diesel traction. Electric traction offers higher acceleration, lower energy costs per journey in many conditions, regenerative braking, and no local exhaust emissions.

Systems and voltages

Major standards include 25 kV AC at 50 Hz, now the preferred system for new main-line electrification worldwide; 15 kV AC at 16.7 Hz in much of German-speaking and Scandinavian Europe; and DC systems at 600–750 V (third rail metros), 1.5 kV and 3 kV (overhead). High-voltage AC systems require transformers and rectifiers or power electronics on board the train.

History

The first practical electric railway was Werner von Siemens's demonstration line in Berlin in 1879. Main-line electrification expanded in the early 20th century in Switzerland, Italy, and the United States, driven by tunnel smoke problems, abundant hydroelectric power, and dense traffic. Today high-speed rail is almost universally electric, and countries such as Switzerland have nearly fully electrified networks, while others, including the United States, remain largely diesel for freight.

Advantages and drawbacks

Benefits include energy efficiency, the ability to use any primary energy source, and lower maintenance for locomotives. Drawbacks include the high capital cost of fixed infrastructure, making electrification economical mainly on busy routes, and dependence on power-supply reliability.
