Multilingualism

Multilingualism is the use of more than one language, either by an individual speaker or by a group of speakers. It is believed that multilingual speakers outnumber monolingual speakers in the world's population. Multilingualism is the norm in many parts of the world, and more than half of all Europeans claim to speak at least one language other than their mother tongue.

Definitions

A person who can speak two languages is bilingual; three languages, trilingual. The term "polyglot" is used for someone who speaks many languages. Definitions vary: some require native-like proficiency, while others accept functional communicative ability in a language. Multilingualism may be individual, or societal, where a whole community routinely uses several languages — as in India, where Hindi, English and regional languages coexist, or Switzerland, with four national languages.

Acquisition

Simultaneous bilinguals acquire two languages from birth, while sequential bilinguals learn a second language after the first is established. Research suggests bilingual children may show advantages in executive function and metalinguistic awareness, though the strength of the so-called bilingual advantage remains debated. Later in life, multilingualism has been associated with a delayed onset of dementia symptoms, a finding attributed to cognitive reserve.

Societal multilingualism

Language policy shapes multilingual societies. The European Union works in 24 official languages; India recognises 22 scheduled languages in its constitution; South Africa has 11 (now 12, with sign language) official languages. Diglossia — the use of distinct "high" and "low" varieties for different functions — is common, as between Modern Standard Arabic and regional dialects. Code-switching, alternating between languages within a conversation, is a widespread multilingual practice.
