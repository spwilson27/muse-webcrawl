Stop-and-frisk in New York City

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The stop-and-frisk program of the New York City Police Department (NYPD) was a practice of temporarily detaining, questioning, and at times searching civilians and suspects on the street for weapons and other contraband. The legal basis derives from the U.S. Supreme Court decision in Terry v. Ohio (1968), which permits an officer with reasonable suspicion of criminal activity to briefly stop a person and pat down outer clothing.

Scale and controversy

The number of recorded stops rose dramatically in the 2000s, peaking at about 686,000 in 2011. Statistics showed that the overwhelming majority of those stopped — around 88 percent in many years — were neither arrested nor issued a summons, and that roughly nine in ten were Black or Hispanic, prompting accusations of racial profiling. Supporters, including Mayor Michael Bloomberg and Police Commissioner Raymond Kelly, credited the tactic with reducing crime and removing illegal guns; critics argued the evidence for its effectiveness was weak and that it damaged police-community relations.

Legal challenges and decline

In Floyd v. City of New York (2013), federal judge Shira Scheindlin ruled that the city's application of stop-and-frisk violated the Fourth Amendment's protection against unreasonable searches and the Fourteenth Amendment's equal protection guarantee, and appointed a federal monitor to oversee reforms. The number of stops fell sharply thereafter — to tens of thousands annually, and lower still in later years — while major crime in New York continued to decline, a combination frequently cited in debates over the policy.
