Algic languages

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Algic languages (also called Algonquian–Wiyot–Yurok or Algonquian–Ritwan) are a family of Indigenous languages of North America. The family consists of the large Algonquian branch, spoken across the continent from the Atlantic coast to the Rocky Mountains, together with two small languages of northwestern California, Wiyot and Yurok.

Classification

Edward Sapir proposed in 1913 that Wiyot and Yurok (which he grouped as "Ritwan") were related to Algonquian. The hypothesis was controversial for decades, but comparative work, notably by Mary Haas and Ives Goddard, established the relationship to most scholars' satisfaction. Although Wiyot and Yurok are not closely related to each other, both descend from Proto-Algic alongside Proto-Algonquian.

Distribution and status

Algonquian languages include Cree, Ojibwe, Blackfoot, Cheyenne, Arapaho and the extinct languages of much of the Atlantic seaboard. Wiyot has no remaining first-language speakers, though revival efforts are under way, and Yurok has a small number of native speakers and an active revitalisation programme. The family's original homeland has been placed by many linguists in the Pacific Northwest or around the Great Lakes, with the Californian languages representing a westward migration.
