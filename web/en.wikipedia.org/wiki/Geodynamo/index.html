The geodynamo is the process by which Earth generates and maintains its magnetic field. In dynamo theory, a rotating, convecting, electrically conducting fluid — the molten iron alloy of Earth's outer core — acts as a self-exciting dynamo, converting kinetic energy of motion into magnetic energy.

A numerical simulation of the geodynamo (the Glatzmaier–Roberts model).

Mechanism

Heat escaping from the core, together with the slow freezing of the inner core — which releases latent heat and light elements at its boundary — drives convection in the outer core. Earth's rotation organises this motion through the Coriolis effect into helical flows. Moving conducting fluid stretches and twists magnetic field lines, amplifying the field through magnetic induction faster than it decays by electrical resistance, sustaining the predominantly dipolar field observed at the surface. The theory requires a seed field to start, which may have come from the Sun or early electrochemical processes.

Evidence and behaviour

The paleomagnetic record in rocks shows the field has existed for at least 3.4 billion years and that the dipole repeatedly weakens, wanders, and reverses polarity on timescales of hundreds of thousands of years — behaviour reproduced by numerical simulations since the landmark Glatzmaier–Roberts model of 1995. Comparable dynamos operate in the Sun, Jupiter, and other planets; the absence of a strong present-day dynamo in Mars is attributed to its core having cooled and stopped convecting vigorously.
