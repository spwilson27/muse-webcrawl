Swahili people

The Swahili people (Swahili: Waswahili) are a Bantu ethnic group inhabiting the Swahili Coast, an area encompassing the Zanzibar archipelago and the mainland coasts of Kenya, Tanzania, and northern Mozambique along the Indian Ocean. Their culture arose over more than a thousand years of trade between Bantu-speaking Africans and merchants from Arabia, Persia, India, and beyond, producing a distinctive urban, maritime, and predominantly Muslim civilization whose language, Swahili (Kiswahili), became the lingua franca of East Africa and is spoken by over a hundred million people.

Stone Town in Zanzibar is the historic heart of Swahili culture.

History

From around the eighth century, coastal settlements such as Lamu, Mombasa, Kilwa, and Sofala grew rich on the monsoon trade in gold, ivory, and slaves, exchanging goods with the wider Indian Ocean world. City-states built in coral stone produced grand mosques, palaces, and houses with intricately carved doors; Kilwa's Great Mosque and the palace of Husuni Kubwa were among the largest structures in sub-Saharan Africa. The arrival of the Portuguese in 1498 disrupted the trading system, and Omani Arabs later dominated the coast, making Zanzibar the seat of a sultanate in the nineteenth century. British and German colonial rule followed, and Zanzibar's 1964 revolution led to union with Tanganyika as Tanzania.

Culture

Swahili culture blends African, Arab, and Persian influences in its architecture, cuisine (pilau, biryani, coconut-based curries), dress (kanga and kitenge cloths), and music, notably taarab. Swahili poetry, in forms such as the epic utenzi, has a centuries-old written tradition in Arabic script, and the language — Bantu in structure with many loanwords from Arabic and other languages — is now an official language of the African Union and several states.
