Phosphene

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


An artist's depiction of a phosphene pattern.

A phosphene is a phenomenon characterised by the experience of seeing light without light actually entering the eye. The word phosphene comes from the Greek words phos (light) and phainein (to show). Phosphenes that are induced by movement or sound may be associated with optic neuritis.

Phosphenes can be directly induced by mechanical, electrical, or magnetic stimulation of the retina or visual cortex, as well as by random firing of cells in the visual system. Phosphenes have also been reported by meditators, by people who spend long periods without visual stimulation (such as prisoners and sensory-deprivation subjects), and by astronauts exposed to cosmic rays.

Causes

Mechanical stimulation

The most common phosphenes are pressure phosphenes, caused by applying pressure to the closed eye — for example by rubbing the eyes. Mechanical stimulation of the retina is interpreted by the brain as light, producing spots, swirls, or checkerboard patterns of colour.

Other causes

Phosphenes can be produced by transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) of the occipital cortex, by electrical stimulation in cortical visual prostheses, and by intense magnetic fields that induce currents in the retina (magnetophosphenes). Experience among Apollo astronauts of flashes of light in darkness was attributed to cosmic-ray particles interacting with the retina or visual cortex, a phenomenon later studied on the International Space Station.

History

Phosphenes were investigated by nineteenth-century physiologists including Isaac Newton, who experimented on himself with pressure on the eyeball, and the Czech physiologist Jan Evangelista Purkyně, who catalogued subjective visual phenomena. In the twentieth century, Wilder Penfield and others elicited phosphenes by stimulating the exposed cortex, and German researchers used phosphene patterns in early attempts at a cortical visual prosthesis.
