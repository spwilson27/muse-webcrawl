Bluff (geography)

Bluffs along a river valley

In geography, a bluff is a type of cliff or steep slope, typically formed by erosion, that rises above a surrounding low area such as a river floodplain, coastal plain, or lake shore. Bluffs commonly occur where a stream or body of water undercuts the edge of a plateau, terrace, or upland.

Formation

River bluffs mark the boundary between a valley floor and the uplands. As a river meanders across its floodplain, it erodes the outer banks of its bends; where the valley wall is composed of resistant sediment such as loess, sandstone, or glacial till, the slope stands steep and forms a bluff. Famous examples include the loess bluffs of the Missouri and Mississippi Rivers and the chalk bluffs of the Missouri River in Montana, recorded by the Lewis and Clark Expedition.

Use of the term

"Bluff" is also embedded in many place names in North America and Australia, where bluffs served as landmarks, defensive sites, and favoured locations for towns above flood level — for example Bluffton, Council Bluffs, and Pine Bluff. In coastal settings, bluffs are subject to slumping and retreat as waves erode their base, a hazard for shoreline property.
