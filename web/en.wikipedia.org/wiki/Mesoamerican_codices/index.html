Mesoamerican codices

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mesoamerican codices are manuscripts that present traits of the Mesoamerican indigenous pictorial traditions, whether actual pre-Columbian books or colonial-era works written in the same style. They were produced by cultures including the Aztec (Mexica), Maya, Mixtec, Zapotec, and Purépecha peoples, and were typically painted on bark paper (amate), deer skin, or cloth, folded accordion-style, or presented as screenfolds, lienzos, and single leaves.

Pre-Columbian codices

Only a handful of genuinely pre-Hispanic screenfolds survive, most of them Maya and Mixtec-Borgia group manuscripts, because Spanish conquerors and missionaries destroyed large numbers of books as idolatrous. The four surviving Maya codices — the Dresden, Madrid, Paris, and Maya Codex of Mexico (formerly Grolier) — deal largely with astronomy, calendars, and ritual. The Borgia group, including the Codex Borgia, contains divinatory almanacs and ritual imagery, while Mixtec codices such as the Codex Zouche-Nuttall record dynastic histories and genealogies.

Colonial codices

After the Spanish conquest, indigenous scribes continued the tradition under colonial patronage, often combining pictorial writing with alphabetic text in Nahuatl, Spanish, or Latin. Famous examples include the Florentine Codex, Bernardino de Sahagún's great encyclopedia of Aztec culture compiled with Nahua informants; the Codex Mendoza, recording Aztec tribute and conquests; the Codex Telleriano-Remensis; and numerous local documents such as maps, land titles, and the Relaciones geográficas. Testerian codices used pictographs to teach Christian doctrine.

Content and format

Subjects range across ritual and divination, astronomy and calendrics (including the 260-day tonalpohualli), dynastic history and genealogy, tribute rolls, maps and territorial claims, medicine, and genealogy. Pre-Columbian books were usually screenfolds of amate paper coated with gesso, read by unfolding; colonial works also adopt the bound European book form.

Survival and study

Surviving codices are held in libraries and museums in Mexico, Europe, and the United States. Their study by ethnohistorians, art historians, and epigraphers has been central to reconstructing pre-Hispanic history, religion, and writing systems, including the decipherment of Maya hieroglyphic writing.
