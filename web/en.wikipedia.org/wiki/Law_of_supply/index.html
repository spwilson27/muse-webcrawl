Law of supply

The law of supply is a fundamental principle of microeconomics which states that, all other factors being equal (ceteris paribus), as the price of a good or service increases, the quantity of goods or services offered by suppliers increases, and vice versa. Together with the law of demand, it forms the basis of the supply-and-demand model that explains price formation in markets.

Explanation

The law reflects the rising marginal cost of production: to produce additional units, firms typically must pay more per unit — for scarcer inputs, overtime labour, or less efficient capacity — so they will only expand output if the price they receive rises correspondingly. From the individual supplier's perspective, a higher price also increases the profit available on each unit sold, drawing resources into the industry and attracting new entrants in the long run.

Supply schedule and curve

A supply schedule tabulates the quantity supplied at each price; plotted, it gives the supply curve, conventionally drawn with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the curve (a change in quantity supplied), whereas changes in other determinants — input prices, technology, taxes and subsidies, expectations, and the number of sellers — shift the whole curve (a change in supply).

Exceptions and qualifications

The law can fail in special cases. Labour supply may bend backward at high wages if workers choose leisure over additional income. Goods in fixed supply — land in a city centre, seats in a stadium, or the works of a deceased artist — have a vertical supply curve, so quantity does not respond to price. In markets with strong speculation or bandwagon effects, higher prices can occasionally reduce the quantity some sellers offer because they expect prices to rise further. The law is usually understood to hold in the long run even where short-run supply is inelastic.

History

Ideas of supply responding to price were articulated by early economists such as John Locke and later formalised in the marginal revolution; Alfred Marshall's Principles of Economics (1890) gave the supply-and-demand apparatus its canonical textbook form, comparing the two sides of the market to the blades of a pair of scissors.
