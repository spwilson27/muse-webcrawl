Binge drinking is the consumption of a large quantity of alcohol over a short period with the primary intention of becoming intoxicated. The United States National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) defines it as a pattern of drinking that brings blood alcohol concentration to 0.08 percent or above — typically reached with five or more drinks for men, or four or more for women, within about two hours. The practice is common among adolescents and young adults and is a major focus of public health concern.

Binge drinking is associated with acute and chronic harms.

Definitions

Definitions vary between countries and agencies. Epidemiological surveys commonly define a binge as five or more (sometimes six or more) drinks on one occasion in the past month. Health researchers have criticized simple drink-count thresholds because body weight, speed of drinking, food intake, and drink strength all affect intoxication; the NIAAA's blood-alcohol-based definition was adopted to tie the term to measurable impairment.

Health effects

Acute risks include alcohol poisoning, accidents and injuries, risky sexual behavior, assaults, and impaired driving; binge episodes account for a large share of alcohol-related emergency department visits. Even in people who do not drink heavily on average, repeated binges are associated with cardiovascular harm, liver disease, cognitive deficits, and increased risk of developing alcohol use disorder. Binge drinking during pregnancy risks fetal alcohol spectrum disorders.

Prevalence and prevention

Surveys in the United States and Europe consistently find the highest binge-drinking rates among people aged roughly 18–34, with significant prevalence among secondary-school and university students. Prevention strategies with evidence of effect include higher alcohol taxes, restrictions on outlet density and trading hours, minimum unit pricing, screening and brief intervention in primary care, and campus programs that correct students' overestimates of peer drinking.
