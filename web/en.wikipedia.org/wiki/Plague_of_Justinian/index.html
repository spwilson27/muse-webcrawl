Plague of Justinian

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Plague of Justinian
Period: 541–549 (first wave), recurring to c. 750
Pathogen: Yersinia pestis (bubonic plague)
Named for: Emperor Justinian I

The Plague of Justinian was the first historically recorded pandemic of bubonic plague, striking the Byzantine Empire and the wider Mediterranean world from 541 AD. Named after Emperor Justinian I, who himself caught the disease and recovered, it is now identified — including by ancient DNA from victims' graves — as caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis, the same organism behind the fourteenth-century Black Death.

The plague arrived at the Egyptian port of Pelusium in 541 and reached Constantinople in 542, where the historian Procopius described deaths at their peak numbering in the thousands per day, overwhelming burial arrangements. It spread along trade routes through the Mediterranean and Near East, and returned in waves for two centuries, with the last outbreaks around 750.

Its demographic and economic toll is debated: traditional accounts describe a catastrophe killing a quarter or more of the empire's population and crippling Justinian's reconquests, while some recent scholarship argues for a more limited impact. Either way, together with the extreme weather events of 536 onwards, the pandemic marks a turning point between the ancient and medieval worlds.
