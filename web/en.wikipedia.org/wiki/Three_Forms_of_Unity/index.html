Three Forms of Unity

The Three Forms of Unity is a collective name for three confessional documents of Reformed Christianity: the Belgic Confession (1561), the Heidelberg Catechism (1563) and the Canons of Dort (1618–19). Together they form the doctrinal standards of many Dutch Reformed churches and their daughter churches around the world, including the Christian Reformed Church in North America and the Reformed Church in America, whose ministers historically subscribed to them.

The documents

The Belgic Confession, written by Guido de Brès in 1561, comprises 37 articles setting out Reformed doctrine; de Brès famously threw a copy over the castle wall at Tournai for the authorities, and was martyred in 1567. The Heidelberg Catechism, commissioned by Elector Frederick III of the Palatinate and drafted chiefly by Zacharias Ursinus and Caspar Olevianus, is arranged in 52 Lord's Days for weekly preaching and is noted for its warm, devotional tone, opening with the question of the believer's "only comfort in life and in death". The Canons of Dort, produced by the Synod of Dort (1618–19), answered the Arminian Remonstrance on the doctrines of grace; its five heads of doctrine are the source of the popular "five points of Calvinism" later summarised by the acronym TULIP.

Use

Office-bearers in churches holding the Three Forms traditionally sign a "form of subscription" pledging agreement with them. The documents are distinct from the Westminster Standards used by most English-speaking Presbyterian churches, though the two traditions overlap extensively in doctrine.
