Cassava (Manihot esculenta), also called yuca or manioc, is a woody shrub of the spurge family native to South America, cultivated for its edible starchy tuberous roots. It is a staple food for roughly 800 million to a billion people, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, and the world's third-largest source of food carbohydrates in the tropics after rice and maize.

Cultivation

Cassava is propagated from stem cuttings and tolerates poor soils and drought, making it a vital food-security crop. Roots are harvested 8–24 months after planting.

Toxicity and processing

Bitter varieties contain cyanogenic glycosides (linamarin) that release hydrogen cyanide; they must be processed by grating, soaking, fermenting, pressing, or cooking before eating. Inadequate processing during drought and famine is associated with konzo, a paralytic disease. Sweet varieties contain far less and need only cooking.

Uses

Products include fresh roots, flour, garri, fufu, tapioca starch and pearls, and the flatbread casabe. Cassava is also used for animal feed, bioethanol, and industrial starch.
