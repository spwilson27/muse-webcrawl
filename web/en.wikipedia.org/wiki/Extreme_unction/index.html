Extreme unction

Extreme unction is an older name for the sacrament known since the Second Vatican Council as the Anointing of the Sick in the Roman Catholic Church. It is the anointing with blessed oil (consecrated by a bishop) administered by a priest to a Catholic who, having reached the age of reason, is in danger of death from sickness or old age, for the spiritual and sometimes physical benefit of the recipient.

The name "extreme unction" derives from the Latin unctio extrema, meaning "final anointing", because historically it was the last of the anointings a Christian received — after those of Baptism, Confirmation, and Holy Orders — and was ordinarily given only when death was imminent, in conjunction with the sacraments of Penance and Viaticum (Holy Communion given to the dying), together called the Last Rites.

History and reform

The practice is grounded in the Epistle of James (5:14–15): "Is anyone among you sick? Let them call the elders of the church to pray over them and anoint them with oil in the name of the Lord." The Council of Trent (1551) defined extreme unction as a true sacrament instituted by Christ. The constitution Sacrosanctum Concilium (1963) of Vatican II directed that the sacrament "may also, and more fittingly, be called 'anointing of the sick'", and that it is not a sacrament for those only who are at the point of death; it may be received by the seriously ill and elderly, and may be repeated. Pope Paul VI promulgated the revised rite in the apostolic constitution Sacram Unctionem Infirmorum (1972).

Effects and administration

According to Catholic teaching, the sacrament confers a special grace strengthening the sick person, forgives sins if the recipient is unable to confess, unites the suffering of the sick person to the Passion of Christ, and sometimes restores bodily health if conducive to salvation. The essential rite is the anointing of the forehead and hands with oil while the priest speaks the sacramental formula.
