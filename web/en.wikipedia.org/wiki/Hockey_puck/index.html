Hockey puck

Hockey puck
Material: Vulcanised rubber
Diameter: 3 in (7.6 cm)
Thickness: 1 in (2.5 cm)
Weight: 5.5–6 oz (156–170 g)

A hockey puck is either a short flat cylinder of vulcanised rubber, as used in ice hockey, or a ball adapted from other sports in some variants of hockey. The standard ice hockey puck is black, 1 inch (25 mm) thick, 3 inches (76 mm) in diameter, and weighs between 5.5 and 6 ounces (156–170 g).

History

Early hockey was played with wooden blocks, frozen cow dung or rubber balls. The first recorded use of a flat puck was in Kingston, Ontario, in the 1860s, and the McGill University rules of 1877 specified a wooden puck; rubber pucks made by slicing a ball became common in the 1880s. The modern design, with knurled edges for grip, was standardised in the early 20th century; Art Ross is credited with refining its bevelled-edge form.

Manufacture and use

Pucks are made of vulcanised rubber with bonding agents and carbon black. Game pucks are frozen before play to reduce bouncing and friction changes; NHL teams keep pucks in freezers at about −10 °C (14 °F) and replace them regularly during games. A puck can travel at over 160 km/h (100 mph) on a slapshot, making it a hazard to spectators and a reason protective netting and glass surround rinks.

Variants

Inline and street hockey use lighter plastic pucks, often with rollers or sliders for use on dry surfaces. Training pucks include heavier weighted pucks, lighter blue pucks for youth players, and off-ice "green biscuit" types. Sponge and foam pucks are used in children's and recreational games. Field hockey, by contrast, uses a hard ball rather than a puck.
