Social marketing

Social marketing is the use of commercial marketing principles and techniques to influence target audiences to voluntarily accept, reject, modify or abandon a behaviour for the benefit of individuals, groups or society as a whole. The term was coined by Philip Kotler and Gerald Zaltman in a 1971 article asking whether marketing could be used to "sell" ideas and healthy behaviours as well as products. Social marketing is most often applied in public health — smoking cessation, road safety, vaccination, safe sex and environmental behaviour — and is distinct from social media marketing and from corporate social business.

Principles

Social marketing treats behaviour change as an exchange: the audience gives up time, money, effort or pleasure, and in return receives benefits that must be made to seem worth the cost. Practitioners use the marketing mix — product (the desired behaviour and its benefits), price (costs and barriers), place (where and how the behaviour can be performed) and promotion — together with formative audience research, segmentation and measurable objectives. Andreasen's benchmark criteria additionally require that behaviour change, not merely awareness, be the bottom line, and that campaigns consider competition from the current behaviour and from commercial marketing.

Examples

Classic applications include anti-smoking campaigns such as "truth" in the United States, condom social marketing programmes that distributed subsidised contraceptives through commercial outlets in developing countries, designated-driver campaigns, and energy- and water-conservation drives. The approach underlies much modern behaviour-change practice in government, including the "nudge"-adjacent work of behavioural insights teams, though nudging and social marketing differ in method and theory.

Criticism

Critics question the ethics of using persuasive techniques on citizens, argue that social marketing focuses on individual behaviour while neglecting structural causes of ill health, and note the difficulty of evaluating long-term effects. Defenders reply that voluntary, audience-centred methods compare favourably with coercion or information-only campaigns.
