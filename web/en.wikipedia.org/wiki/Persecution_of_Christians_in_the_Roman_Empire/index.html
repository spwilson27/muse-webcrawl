Persecution of Christians in the Roman Empire

Christians were intermittently persecuted by authorities of the Roman Empire from the first century AD until the Edict of Milan in 313 legalised Christianity. Persecution was sporadic and locally variable for most of this period, punctuated by empire-wide campaigns under Decius (250–251), Valerian (257–260) and Diocletian (303–311, the "Great Persecution").

Causes

Roman suspicion centred on Christians' refusal to sacrifice to the gods and to the emperor's image, which officials read as disloyalty and atheism; their secretive meetings, exclusivism and separation from civic cult made them targets of popular hostility and scapegoating after disasters. Nero's punishment of Christians after the Great Fire of Rome in 64 AD, described by Tacitus, was a local episode but set a precedent that Christianity could be treated as a criminal association. The correspondence between Pliny the Younger and Trajan (c. 112) established that Christians were not to be hunted out, but those brought before a governor who refused to sacrifice could be punished.

Major episodes

Local persecutions produced many early martyrs, including those at Lyon and Vienne in 177. Decius's edict of 250 requiring universal sacrifice created the first empire-wide crisis, and the problem of readmitting the "lapsed" split the church. Valerian targeted clergy and church property. After forty years of toleration, Diocletian's edicts of 303 ordered churches demolished, scriptures burned and clergy arrested; enforcement was fiercest in the East under Galerius and Maximinus Daia. Galerius's Edict of Toleration (311) and the Edict of Milan (313), issued by Constantine and Licinius, ended persecution and restored confiscated property.

Historiography

Numbers of martyrs are debated: ancient church sources magnified persecution, while modern historians such as G. E. M. de Ste. Croix and, more controversially, Candida Moss have argued over how systematic and how extensive it truly was. The memory of the martyrs nonetheless shaped Christian identity, liturgy and the cult of saints for centuries.
