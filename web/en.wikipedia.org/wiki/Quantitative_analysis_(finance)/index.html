Quantitative analysis (finance)

Quantitative analysis in finance is the use of mathematical and statistical methods in finance and investment management. Practitioners — quantitative analysts, or "quants" — apply techniques from stochastic calculus, numerical analysis and statistics to problems such as pricing derivatives, managing risk and developing algorithmic trading strategies.

Areas

  • Front office quants support trading desks, building pricing models for derivatives — the most famous being the Black–Scholes model — and managing libraries of valuation code.
  • Quantitative investment management uses systematic, model-driven strategies, including statistical arbitrage and factor investing; pioneers include Renaissance Technologies and D. E. Shaw.
  • Risk management quants develop credit, market and model-risk frameworks, including value-at-risk.
  • Quantitative development focuses on the high-performance software infrastructure behind the models.

History

The field traces to Louis Bachelier's 1900 thesis on speculation and Harry Markowitz's portfolio theory (1952), and took off after Black, Scholes and Merton published their option-pricing formula in 1973. Physicists and mathematicians moved into finance in large numbers from the 1980s, a migration chronicled by Emanuel Derman. The role of quantitative models in the 2007–08 financial crisis — particularly Gaussian copula models of mortgage default — prompted lasting debate about model risk.
