Absorption refrigerator

Absorption refrigerator
Working fluidsAmmonia + water (with hydrogen) or water + lithium bromide
Energy sourceHeat (gas flame, electricity, solar, waste heat)
Moving partsNone in the classic design (no compressor)
InventorsFerdinand Carré (1858); Baltzar von Platen and Carl Munters (1922, gas refrigerator)
Typical usesCaravans/RVs, hotels, off-grid cooling, industrial chilling

An absorption refrigerator is a refrigerator that uses a heat source — rather than an electric compressor — to drive the cooling cycle. Because it has essentially no moving parts, it runs silently, and it can operate on propane, kerosene, natural gas, solar heat or industrial waste heat, making it common in recreational vehicles, hotel minibars and off-grid settings.

How it works

In the classic ammonia–water–hydrogen design (the Platen–Munters cycle, commercialised by Electrolux and Servel), heat applied to a generator boils ammonia out of a strong ammonia–water solution. The ammonia vapour condenses, then evaporates at low partial pressure — maintained by hydrogen gas in the evaporator — absorbing heat from the cabinet. The ammonia is then re-absorbed into weak solution in the absorber, and the cycle repeats by gravity and thermosiphon action without any pump. Large industrial systems instead pair water as refrigerant with lithium bromide as absorbent.

Efficiency and history

Absorption machines are considerably less energy-efficient than vapour-compression refrigerators, with coefficients of performance typically around 0.5–1 versus 2–4, but they can exploit heat that would otherwise be wasted. Patented by Ferdinand Carré in 1858, the technology powered early domestic gas refrigerators in the 1920s–30s before electric compression won the home market; absorption survives where silence, fuel flexibility or heat availability matters more than efficiency. Albert Einstein and Leó Szilárd patented an improved absorption design (the Einstein refrigerator) in 1930.
