The common murre or common guillemot (Uria aalge) is a large auk. It has a circumpolar distribution, occurring in low-Arctic and boreal waters in the North Atlantic and North Pacific. It spends most of its time at sea, only coming to land to breed on rocky cliff shores or islands.

Common murres are fast fliers, but are not very agile. They are more manoeuvrable underwater, typically diving to depths of 30–60 m (98–197 ft), with depths of up to 180 m (590 ft) recorded. They pursue prey "flying" underwater with powerful strokes of their short wings.

Description

The common murre is 38–46 cm (15–18 in) in length with a 61–73 cm (24–29 in) wingspan. The male and female are indistinguishable in the field, and weight ranges between 945 g (2.08 lb) in the south of their range to 1,044 g (2.302 lb) in the north. In breeding plumage, the nominate subspecies has a black head, back and wings, and white underparts. It has a thin dark pointed bill and a small rounded dark tail. After the pre-basic moult, its face is white with a black spur behind the eye.

Breeding

Common murres breed in colonies at high densities. Pairs produce a single egg, which is incubated on bare rock ledges for about 30 to 35 days. The egg is pyriform (pear-shaped), an adaptation once thought to prevent it rolling off the cliff, though research suggests the shape instead aids stability, heat transfer and cleanliness on crowded ledges. Both parents take turns incubating and feeding the chick, which fledges at about three weeks old by jumping from the cliff to the sea below, accompanied by the male parent, who cares for it at sea for several weeks.

Murres probably have the highest flight cost, for their body size, of any animal, a consequence of their compromise wings adapted for both flight and diving.

Conservation

The species has a large population and wide range and is classed as Least Concern by the IUCN, though it is vulnerable to oil spills, gill-net bycatch and declines in forage fish stocks; mass mortality events linked to marine heatwaves, such as the 2014–2016 Pacific event, have killed very large numbers of birds.
