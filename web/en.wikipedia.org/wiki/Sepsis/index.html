Sepsis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition arising when the body's response to infection damages its own tissues and organs. Rather than a single germ, sepsis is a dysregulated host response: widespread Inflammation, leaky blood vessels and disordered clotting can progress to septic shock — a dangerous fall in blood pressure — and multiple organ failure. It is a leading cause of death in hospitals worldwide.

Causes and recognition

Sepsis most often complicates bacterial infections of the lungs, urinary tract, abdomen or skin; viruses (including influenza and SARS-CoV-2) and fungi can also cause it. Anyone can be affected, but risk is highest in the very young, the elderly, pregnant women and people with weakened immunity or chronic disease. Warning signs include fever or low temperature, rapid breathing and heart rate, confusion, mottled skin and reduced urine output.

Treatment

Sepsis is a medical emergency: survival falls with every hour of delay. Treatment combines rapid antibiotics after blood cultures, intravenous fluids, oxygen, drugs to support blood pressure, and treating the source of infection (for example draining an abscess). Global estimates suggest tens of millions of cases and around 11 million deaths a year — roughly one in five deaths worldwide — though counting is difficult.
