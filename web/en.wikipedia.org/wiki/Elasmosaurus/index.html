Elasmosaurus is a genus of plesiosaur that lived in North America during the Campanian stage of the Late Cretaceous period, about 80.5 million years ago. The only known specimen was discovered in the Pierre Shale near Fort Wallace, Kansas, in 1867 and described by Edward Drinker Cope in 1868. It gave its name to the family Elasmosauridae, the group of extremely long-necked plesiosaurs.

Discovery and the head-on-tail error

The holotype was collected by army surgeon Theophilus Turner and scout William Comstock, who sent the bones to Cope. In reconstructing the skeleton, Cope famously placed the skull at the end of the short tail rather than the long neck, mistaking the neck vertebrae for tail vertebrae. The error was pointed out — legend says by his rival Othniel Charles Marsh — and became a celebrated episode of the Bone Wars, though Cope corrected it and modern research suggests the popular story was embellished.

Description

Elasmosaurus reached roughly 10.3 metres (34 ft) in length, about half of it neck. The neck contained an estimated 72 cervical vertebrae, among the most of any known animal, supporting a small head with sharp interlocking teeth suited to catching fish and other small prey. The body was rigid and streamlined, propelled by four large paddle-like flippers. It inhabited the Western Interior Seaway, the shallow sea that covered central North America. Gastroliths (swallowed stones) found with elasmosaur specimens may have aided digestion or buoyancy control.

Classification and palaeobiology

Elasmosaurus is the type genus of Elasmosauridae within Plesiosauroidea. Despite reconstructions in older art showing the neck rearing swan-like out of the water, biomechanical studies indicate the neck was relatively inflexible vertically and was more likely held horizontally, sweeping the small head through schools of prey below or ahead of the body.
