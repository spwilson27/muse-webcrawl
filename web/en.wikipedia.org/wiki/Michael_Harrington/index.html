Edward Michael Harrington Jr. (24 February 1928 – 31 July 1989) was an American democratic socialist author, political activist, and professor. He is best known as the author of The Other America: Poverty in the United States (1962), a book widely credited with drawing national attention to poverty and helping to inspire the federal War on Poverty of the 1960s.

Early life

Harrington was born in St. Louis, Missouri, into a middle-class Irish-American family. He attended the College of the Holy Cross and earned a master's degree in English literature from Yale University. Moving to New York in the early 1950s, he worked for Dorothy Day's Catholic Worker movement, living in its Houses of Hospitality among the poor of the Bowery — an experience that shaped his writing on poverty.

The Other America

The Other America argued that some 40 to 50 million Americans — roughly a quarter of the population — lived in poverty amid postwar affluence, invisible to the middle-class majority. An influential review by Dwight Macdonald in The New Yorker brought the book to wide attention; it is often said to have reached President John F. Kennedy and influenced the anti-poverty programs of Kennedy and Lyndon B. Johnson.

Activism

Harrington was a leading figure in the Socialist Party of America and later chaired the Democratic Socialist Organizing Committee, which in 1982 merged with the New American Movement to form the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA), with Harrington as co-chair. He taught political science at Queens College in New York and wrote numerous books, including Socialism (1972) and The Twilight of Capitalism (1977). His strategy of working within and alongside the Democratic Party influenced later democratic socialists, including Bernie Sanders.

Death and legacy

Harrington died of cancer in 1989. He remains the most prominent American advocate of democratic socialism of his generation, and DSA's growth in the twenty-first century renewed interest in his work.
