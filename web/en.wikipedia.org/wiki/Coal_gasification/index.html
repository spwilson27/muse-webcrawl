Coal gasification is the process of producing syngas — a mixture consisting primarily of carbon monoxide (CO), hydrogen (H2), carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4) and water vapour — from coal and water, air and/or oxygen. The coal is reacted at high temperature (typically above 700 °C) with a controlled amount of oxygen and steam, so that it is partially oxidized rather than fully burned.

History

Gas made from coal — "town gas" — lit streets and homes from the early 19th century. In the 20th century, Germany used gasification and the Fischer–Tropsch process to make synthetic fuels during the Second World War, and South Africa's Sasol built a coal-to-liquids industry on the same chemistry during the apartheid era.

Applications

Modern gasification feeds integrated gasification combined cycle (IGCC) power plants, hydrogen production, ammonia and methanol manufacture, and synthetic natural gas. Underground coal gasification attempts the process in situ in unmined seams. Because gasification produces a concentrated CO2 stream, it is often proposed in combination with carbon capture and storage, though costs and emissions remain controversial.
