Promagistrate

A promagistrate (Latin: pro magistratu, "in place of a magistrate") was a person in the Roman Republic and early Empire who acted with the authority (imperium) of a magistrate without actually holding the magistracy itself. The most common promagistracies were the proconsul and the propraetor, who typically governed provinces after completing a term as consul or praetor at Rome.

A proconsul governing a Roman province

Origins

The institution arose from practical necessity. Rome's annual magistracies were held for one year, but military commands and provincial governorships often could not be concluded within that span. Rather than elect a new magistrate, the Senate could prorogue (prorogatio) the command of an existing one, who then continued to act pro consule ("in place of a consul") or pro praetore ("in place of a praetor"). The first recorded prorogation was that of Quintus Publilius Philo in 327 BC, whose command against the Samnites was extended so he could finish a siege.

Types of promagistrate

  • Proconsul (pro consule): holder of consular imperium, usually assigned to a province requiring a large army. Under the late Republic, ex-consuls routinely governed provinces as proconsuls.
  • Propraetor (pro praetore): holder of praetorian imperium, assigned to smaller or more peaceful provinces.
  • Proquaestor (pro quaestore): an official acting in place of a quaestor in a province, sometimes a legatus performing quaestorian duties.

Late Republic and abuse

By the first century BC the promagistracy had become central to Roman politics and a frequent source of corruption and extortion in the provinces; Cicero's prosecution of Verres, a former governor of Sicily, is the classic case. Extraordinary grants of promagisterial imperium — such as Pompey's commands under the lex Gabinia (67 BC) and lex Manilia (66 BC), which gave him authority equal or superior to sitting governors — set precedents for the concentration of power that contributed to the Republic's fall.

Under the Empire

Under Augustus, the system was regularised: the emperor himself held proconsular imperium over the "imperial" provinces, governed day to day by his legates (legati Augusti pro praetore), while the Senate appointed proconsuls to the public provinces by lot from among ex-consuls and ex-praetors.
