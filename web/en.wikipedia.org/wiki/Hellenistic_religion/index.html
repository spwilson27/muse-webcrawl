Hellenistic religion

Hellenistic religion is any of the various systems of beliefs and practices of the people who lived under the influence of ancient Greek culture during the Hellenistic period (from the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BCE to the emergence of the Roman Empire) and the Roman Empire (c. 31 BCE to 476 CE).

Characteristics

There was much continuity in Hellenistic religion: people continued to worship the Greek gods and practise the same rites. Change came from the addition of new religions from other countries — the Egyptian cults of Isis and Serapis, Syrian gods such as Atargatis, and the Jewish and later Christian faiths — and from syncretism, in which foreign deities were identified with Greek ones. Ruler cults deifying Hellenistic monarchs, and mystery religions promising personal salvation, also flourished.

Philosophy and religion

Philosophical schools such as Stoicism, Epicureanism and Neoplatonism offered ethical and theological frameworks that interacted with traditional cult practice, while astrology and magic became increasingly prominent in popular devotion.

Decline

Traditional cults waned from the 4th century CE as Christianity, backed by imperial authority, suppressed public pagan sacrifice and closed temples, most famously the Serapeum of Alexandria.
