Surveillance

Surveillance is the monitoring of behavior, many activities, or information for the purpose of information gathering, influencing, managing, or directing. This can include observation from a distance by means of electronic equipment, such as closed-circuit television (CCTV), or interception of electronically transmitted information like Internet traffic. It can also include simple technical methods, such as human intelligence gathering and postal interception. Surveillance is used by governments for intelligence gathering, prevention of crime, the protection of a process, person, group or object, or the investigation of crime; by criminal organizations to plan crimes; and by businesses for competitive intelligence.

Closed-circuit television cameras are a common form of surveillance in public spaces.

Types

Surveillance takes many forms. Computer and network surveillance monitors data and traffic on the Internet and telephones; visual surveillance uses cameras and, increasingly, facial recognition; biometric surveillance measures physical characteristics; aerial surveillance uses aircraft and drones; and social network surveillance mines the connections between persons. Mass surveillance — the surveillance of an entire population or a substantial fraction of it — became a major public controversy following the 2013 disclosures by Edward Snowden of global surveillance programs.

Theories and criticism

The philosopher Jeremy Bentham's panopticon prison design, analyzed by Michel Foucault in Discipline and Punish, became a central metaphor for modern surveillance societies, in which the possibility of being watched induces self-discipline. Critics argue that pervasive surveillance chills free expression and association, enabling function creep and abuse, while supporters cite crime prevention and national security. Shoshana Zuboff popularized the term "surveillance capitalism" for business models built on extracting behavioral data.

Counter-surveillance

Individuals and organizations respond with encryption, anonymity networks such as Tor, privacy law, and sousveillance — watching the watchers, as in citizens recording police. Legal frameworks such as the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation constrain the collection and processing of personal data.
