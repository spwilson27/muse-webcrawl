Chlorella

Chlorella is a genus of single-celled green algae found in freshwater worldwide. The spherical cells, roughly 2–10 μm in diameter, contain a single cup-shaped chloroplast and reproduce asexually. The genus was named in 1890 by the Dutch microbiologist Martinus Beijerinck.

Research and uses

Chlorella became famous through Melvin Calvin's experiments using its photosynthesis to trace the path of carbon in the Calvin cycle, work that contributed to his 1961 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Because it is rich in protein, chlorophyll, and micronutrients, Chlorella — especially C. vulgaris and C. pyrenoidosa types — is mass-cultivated, mainly in Asia, and sold as a dietary supplement in powder and tablet form. Its rigid cell wall must be mechanically cracked for digestibility. Health claims for chlorella supplements are only weakly supported by clinical evidence.

Chlorella has also been studied for biofuel production, wastewater treatment, and as feed in aquaculture.
