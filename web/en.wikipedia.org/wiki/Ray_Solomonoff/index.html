Ray Solomonoff

BornJuly 25, 1926, Cleveland, Ohio, U.S.
DiedDecember 7, 2009 (aged 83)
Known forAlgorithmic probability; Solomonoff induction

Ray Solomonoff (July 25, 1926 – December 7, 2009) was an American mathematician and a founder of the field of machine learning theory. He is best known for inventing algorithmic probability and the theory of universal inductive inference known as Solomonoff induction, which he described in papers beginning in 1960 and in a landmark 1964 two-part paper.

Work

Solomonoff proposed that prediction should be based on all computable hypotheses that could have generated the observed data, weighted by their simplicity — specifically, by two raised to the negative power of the length of the shortest program that produces the data. This universal prior simultaneously embodies Occam's razor and Epicurus's principle of multiple explanations. The theory is closely related to Kolmogorov complexity, developed independently by Andrey Kolmogorov and Gregory Chaitin, and it provides a formal, if incomputable, ideal of inductive reasoning.

Solomonoff attended the 1956 Dartmouth workshop that launched artificial intelligence as a field, and he worked for much of his career at Zator Company in Cambridge, Massachusetts, funded largely by the U.S. Air Force. His ideas influenced later work on minimum description length and on theoretical models of artificial general intelligence such as AIXI.
