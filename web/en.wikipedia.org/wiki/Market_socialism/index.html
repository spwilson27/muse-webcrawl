Market socialism

Market socialism is a type of economic system involving the social ownership of the means of production within the framework of a market economy. Social ownership can take the form of public, collective, cooperative or employee ownership, while allocation of goods and productive inputs is coordinated by markets rather than by central economic planning. Profits generated by socially owned firms may fund public services (a "social dividend"), be retained by the firm, or be distributed to its employees.

History and theory

Early conceptions are found in the Ricardian socialists and in John Stuart Mill's sympathy for worker cooperatives. The modern theoretical debate began with the socialist calculation debate of the 1920s–1930s: responding to Ludwig von Mises's claim that rational allocation is impossible without markets in capital goods, Oskar Lange and Abba Lerner proposed the Lange–Lerner model, in which a planning board sets prices parametrically and state-enterprise managers follow rules to equate price with marginal cost, adjusting output by trial and error to mimic a competitive market.

Models

  • Lange model: public ownership with administratively simulated markets for producer goods.
  • Cooperative market socialism: worker-owned and self-managed firms competing in markets, exemplified in theory by proposals from Jaroslav Vanek and in practice (partially) by Yugoslav self-management after 1950.
  • Coupon / pragmatic models: e.g. John Roemer's proposal for equal citizen shareholding via non-transferable coupons.
  • Social dividend models: state-owned firms whose profits fund public expenditure or a basic income, historically associated with James Meade.

Practice

Yugoslavia's system of workers' self-management (1950s–1980s) is the closest large-scale historical approximation. Elements of market-socialist practice are also cited in debates over China's "socialist market economy" and Vietnam's "socialist-oriented market economy", though the degree of public ownership and planning in those systems remains contested, and many economists classify them differently. Hungary's New Economic Mechanism (from 1968) introduced market elements into a planned economy.

Criticism

Critics from the Austrian school, notably Friedrich Hayek, argued that simulated markets cannot reproduce the information and incentive functions of real price formation and private ownership. Other critics raise problems of soft budget constraints, under-investment or over-consumption in labour-managed firms, and political capture of public enterprises. Supporters respond that market socialism combines allocative efficiency with greater equality and workplace democracy than capitalism.
