Angra Mainyu (Avestan: Aŋra Mainiiu) is the destructive, evil spirit in Zoroastrianism, the adversary of Spenta Mainyu, the holy or bounteous spirit. In Middle Persian texts he is called Ahriman. Angra Mainyu is the source of all evil, disease, darkness and death in the world, and leads the host of demons (daevas) against the good creation of Ahura Mazda, the supreme god.

In the texts

In the Gathas, the oldest Zoroastrian scriptures attributed to the prophet Zoroaster himself, Angra Mainyu is one of two primal spirits — twins — of whom the evil one "chose the worst deeds" while his brother chose righteousness (asha). This free choice of evil is central to Zoroastrian ethics. In the later Avesta, notably the Vendidad, Angra Mainyu creates plagues and counter-creations: for every good land and creature made by Ahura Mazda he produces serpents, winter, sickness and sin. In the Pahlavi books, especially the Bundahishn, the conflict is framed as a cosmic struggle of fixed duration — 9,000 or 12,000 years — at the end of which evil will be defeated at the Frashokereti, the final renovation of the world.

Theology and interpretation

The exact metaphysical status of Angra Mainyu has long been debated: orthodox Zoroastrian theology insists Ahura Mazda is wholly good and uncreated, and that evil is a lesser, ultimately doomed power; the heretical Zurvanite sect made both spirits the twin sons of Zurvan (Time). Zoroastrians traditionally curse Ahriman in prayer and regard truth-telling, good thoughts, words and deeds as the practical fight against him. The figure influenced later religious conceptions of a personified evil adversary, and "Ahriman" entered Western esotericism and literature as a byword for the principle of darkness.
