The Jura Mountains (French: Massif du Jura; German: Jura) are a sub-alpine mountain range located north of the Western Alps, mainly along the France–Switzerland border. The range stretches for about 360 km from the Rhine near Basel to the Rhône near Geneva and beyond into France. Its highest summit is the Crêt de la Neige at 1,720 m in the French department of Ain.

Geology

The Jura is a classic fold-and-thrust belt formed when Mesozoic sedimentary rocks — principally limestones — were detached from their basement and folded into a series of regular anticlines and synclines during the Alpine orogeny, mainly in the Miocene. Geographers divide it into the folded Jura of high parallel ridges in the west and the tabular Jura of plateaus in the east. The limestones of the region, studied in the early 19th century, gave their name to the Jurassic period of geological time.

Geography and nature

The range is characterised by forested ridges, high pastures, deep gorges (such as the Doubs valley), and numerous lakes, including Lake Neuchâtel and Lac de Joux. Extensive forests of spruce, fir and beech cover the slopes. The region is protected in part by the Jura Vaudois and Haut-Jura regional nature parks.

Economy and culture

The Swiss Jura is the historic heartland of Swiss watchmaking, centred on towns such as La Chaux-de-Fonds, Le Locle and the Vallée de Joux, where firms produce many of the world's finest mechanical watches. The French Jura is known for Comté and Morbier cheeses and for vin jaune wine. Tourism includes cross-country skiing, hiking along the Crêtes du Jura trail, and caving in the range's extensive karst systems.
