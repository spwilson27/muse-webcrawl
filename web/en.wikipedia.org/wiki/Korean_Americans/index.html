Korean Americans (Korean: 한국계 미국인) are Americans of Korean ancestry. The Korean American community comprises about 2 million people and is the fifth-largest Asian American subgroup. The largest communities are in the Los Angeles, New York, Washington, D.C., and Atlanta metropolitan areas.

History

Korean immigration to the United States began in earnest in 1903, when a group of Korean labourers arrived in Hawaii to work on sugar plantations. Early immigration was curtailed by Japanese colonial rule over Korea and by American exclusion laws. A second wave followed the Korean War (1950–1953), including war brides and orphans adopted by American families.

The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 abolished national-origins quotas and opened the way for mass migration. Hundreds of thousands of Koreans immigrated in the 1960s–1980s, many of them professionals who, facing credential and language barriers, entered small business ownership — dry cleaners, grocery stores, liquor stores and nail salons became associated niches. Korean immigration peaked in the late 1980s and slowed as South Korea's economy developed.

Community and culture

Koreatowns in Los Angeles — the largest Korean enclave outside Korea — and in Flushing and Manhattan, New York, serve as commercial and cultural hubs, with Korean-language media, churches, restaurants and businesses. Korean Protestant churches play a central social role in immigrant life. The 1992 Los Angeles riots, in which many Korean-owned businesses were damaged or destroyed, were a formative trauma and turning point for the community's political engagement.

Second-generation Korean Americans have become prominent in politics, entertainment, academia and business. Cultural exports from South Korea — K-pop, Korean film and television, and Korean cuisine — have raised the community's visibility in American life.

Demographics

Korean Americans have comparatively high levels of educational attainment and self-employment. A substantial share of the first generation remains Korean-dominant in language, while later generations are primarily English-speaking. Remittances, transnational ties and return migration link the community closely with South Korea.
