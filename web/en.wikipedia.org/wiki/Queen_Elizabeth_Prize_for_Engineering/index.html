Queen Elizabeth Prize for Engineering

The Queen Elizabeth Prize for Engineering (QEPrize) is a global prize for engineering, administered by the Queen Elizabeth Prize for Engineering Foundation (a charity) and associated with the Royal Academy of Engineering in the United Kingdom. It is awarded to engineers whose innovation has been of global benefit to humanity. The prize carries an award of £500,000 (originally £1 million) and a trophy.

Background

The prize was launched in 2011 at a reception at No. 10 Downing Street, with cross-party support, and was created to raise the profile of engineering and inspire young people to consider engineering careers. Queen Elizabeth II agreed to lend her name to the prize, and the first award was presented at Buckingham Palace in 2013 during her Diamond Jubilee era.

Laureates

The inaugural 2013 prize was shared by five engineers who created the Internet and the World Wide Web: Robert Kahn, Vint Cerf, and Louis Pouzin for the protocols underpinning the Internet; Tim Berners-Lee for the World Wide Web; and Marc Andreessen for the Mosaic web browser.

Subsequent winners include Robert Langer (2015) for controlled-release drug delivery; George Daniels (2016) for the co-axial escapement in watches (awarded to his estate, the prize having been announced after his death); the 2017 prize shared by Richard Friend, Nick Holonyak Jr., Shuji Nakamura, and Isamu Akasaki... (for LED lighting technology contributions); John Goodenough among later laureates; the prize was awarded to the developers of deep learning-related technologies, and in 2023 to the pioneers of PERC solar cell technology, including Martin Green, Andrew Blakers, Aihua Wang, and Jianhua Zhao. The 2025 prize recognised seven pioneers of artificial intelligence and machine learning, including Yoshua Bengio, Geoffrey Hinton, John Hopfield, Yann LeCun, Fei-Fei Li, Jensen Huang, and Bill Dally.

Organisation

The QEPrize is awarded by a judging panel of international engineering experts. Nominations are invited globally. The prize cycle was biennial initially and, from 2021, became annual. Its trophy design was selected through a competition and is inspired by engineering geometry.
