Endocardium

The endocardium is the innermost layer of tissue that lines the chambers of the heart. Its cells are embryologically and biologically similar to the endothelial cells that line blood vessels. The endocardium also provides protection to the valves and heart chambers, and it underlies the much thicker myocardium, the muscular middle layer of the heart wall.

Structure

The endocardium consists of a surface layer of simple squamous endothelium resting on a thin layer of connective tissue. Beneath this lies a deeper subendocardial layer of connective tissue containing blood vessels, nerves and branches of the heart's impulse-conducting system, including the Purkinje fibres. The endocardium is continuous with the inner lining (tunica intima) of the great vessels entering and leaving the heart, and it folds over the chordae tendineae and papillary muscles. The heart valves are essentially folds of endocardium reinforced by fibrous connective tissue.

Function

Beyond forming a smooth, non-thrombogenic lining that minimises friction as blood flows through the chambers, endocardial endothelial cells regulate the exchange of molecules between blood and myocardium and release signalling molecules such as nitric oxide and endothelin that modulate the contractility of the underlying muscle. The endocardium also participates in the formation of the heart during development: endocardial cushions in the embryonic heart give rise to the valves and septa.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the endocardium, usually involving the valves, is endocarditis, most commonly caused by bacterial infection of the endothelial surface. Chronic thickening of the endocardium occurs in endomyocardial fibrosis, a restrictive cardiomyopathy seen mainly in tropical regions, and in the hypereosinophilic (Löffler) syndrome.
