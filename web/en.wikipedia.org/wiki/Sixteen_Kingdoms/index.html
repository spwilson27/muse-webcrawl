The Sixteen Kingdoms (Chinese: 十六國; pinyin: Shíliù Guó), sometimes called the Sixteen States, is a collective name for the many short-lived sovereign states that ruled northern China between 304 and 439 CE, after the collapse of Western Jin authority during the War of the Eight Princes and the invasions collectively remembered as the Uprising of the Five Barbarians. Most of the kingdoms were founded by rulers of Xiongnu, Xianbei, Di, Qiang and Jie origin, peoples long settled within or along the empire's frontiers.

Background

The Jin dynasty's civil wars (291–306) devastated the north and emptied the treasury. In 304, the Xiongnu noble Liu Yuan declared himself King of Han, founding Han-Zhao; in 311 his forces sacked Luoyang (the Disaster of Yongjia) and in 316 captured Chang'an, ending Western Jin. The Jin court re-established itself in the south as Eastern Jin at Jiankang (Nanjing), while the north fragmented into competing regimes.

The kingdoms

The canonical sixteen, as enumerated in the 7th-century Spring and Autumn Annals of the Sixteen Kingdoms by Cui Hong, are:

  • Han-Zhao (Former Zhao)
  • Later Zhao
  • Cheng-Han
  • Former Liang
  • Later Liang, Southern Liang, Northern Liang, Western Liang
  • Former Qin
  • Later Qin
  • Western Qin
  • Former Yan, Later Yan, Southern Yan, Northern Yan
  • Xia

Numerous other states — including Dai, Ran Wei and Western Yan — are excluded from the traditional count. Several kingdoms were substantial: the Di-led Former Qin under Fu Jian nearly unified the north by 376 and attempted to conquer the south, but was catastrophically defeated by Eastern Jin at the Battle of Fei River in 383, after which it disintegrated.

Reunification

The period ended when the Tuoba Xianbei state of Northern Wei conquered the last northern rivals, annexing Northern Liang in 439 and unifying northern China. The era is the first phase of the Northern and Southern dynasties period (420–589). Despite warfare, the age saw the spread of Buddhism, major cave-temple art (e.g., the early Yungang and Mogao works), and deep cultural mixing between Han Chinese and the steppe peoples.
