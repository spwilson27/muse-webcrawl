No-knock warrant

A no-knock warrant is a search warrant authorising law enforcement officers in the United States to enter a property without first notifying the residents by knocking and announcing their presence. The practice derives from exceptions to the traditional "knock-and-announce" rule of common law, recognised by the U.S. Supreme Court in cases such as Wilson v. Arkansas (1995) and Richards v. Wisconsin (1997), and is generally justified on the grounds that announcement would endanger officers, allow suspects to flee, or permit the destruction of evidence, particularly drugs.

History

No-knock raids expanded sharply from the 1970s onward with the federal war on drugs and the authorisation of no-knock entries in the District of Columbia and federal law in 1970 (the latter repealed in 1974). By the early 2000s, tens of thousands of paramilitary-style raids were being conducted annually in the United States, the majority for drug searches.

Controversy

Critics argue that no-knock entries are inherently dangerous: occupants may mistake police for intruders, and officers entering unannounced have shot residents — and been shot — in confrontations. Notorious cases include the deaths of Aiyana Stanley-Jones (2010) and Breonna Taylor (2020), whose killing in Louisville, Kentucky, during a botched no-knock raid sparked nationwide protests. In Hudson v. Michigan (2006) the Supreme Court held that evidence obtained after a knock-and-announce violation need not be excluded, a ruling civil-liberties groups criticised.

Reforms

Following Taylor's death, Louisville banned no-knock warrants outright ("Breonna's Law"), and several states — including Virginia, Oregon, and Florida (longstanding) — have prohibited or sharply restricted them, while other jurisdictions now require higher judicial standards, daytime service, body cameras, and waiting periods. Federal policy since 2021 has restricted no-knock entries by federal agents.
