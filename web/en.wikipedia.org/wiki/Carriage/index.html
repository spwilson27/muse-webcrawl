Carriage

A carriage is a two- or four-wheeled passenger vehicle pulled by horses or other draft animals. In modern use the term usually refers to horse-drawn vehicles designed for private use or ceremony, including types such as the barouche, landau, phaeton, victoria and brougham. Related terms include coach (a large, enclosed carriage) and wagon (used for freight rather than passengers).

History

Wheeled vehicles date to antiquity, including the war chariots of the Bronze Age, but suspended passenger carriages developed in medieval and early modern Europe; the town of Kocs in Hungary gave its name to the "coach". Improvements in springs — leather thoroughbraces, then steel elliptical springs — and in road surfaces made carriage travel faster and more comfortable through the 18th and 19th centuries, and a sophisticated trade of coachbuilders, harness makers and coachmen developed around major cities.

Decline and survival

Railways displaced long-distance stagecoaches from the 1830s, and the rise of the automobile in the early 20th century ended the carriage's role as everyday transport. Carriages survive in ceremonial use (such as royal state coaches), in tourism, and in the sport of combined driving.
