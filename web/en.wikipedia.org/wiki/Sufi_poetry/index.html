Sufi poetry

Sufi poetry is the body of verse produced within Sufism, the mystical tradition of Islam, expressing the soul's longing for union with God through the language of love, wine, intoxication and the beloved. Written principally in Persian, Arabic and Turkish, and later in Urdu, Punjabi, Sindhi and other languages, it became one of the most influential literary traditions of the Islamic world and, through modern translations, of world literature generally.

Themes and imagery

Sufi poets worked within a shared symbolic vocabulary: the Beloved stands for God; the lover is the seeker; wine and the tavern represent divine ecstasy and the world of spiritual experience; the wine-bearer (saqi) is the spiritual master; and annihilation of the self (fana) in God is the goal of the path. This ambiguity — earthly love poem or mystical allegory — is deliberate, allowing the same ghazal to be read on several levels.

Major poets

The tradition's towering figures include the Persian poets Sanai, Attar of Nishapur — whose Conference of the Birds is an allegorical epic of the soul's journey — and Jalal al-Din Rumi, whose Masnavi and Divan made him among the most widely read poets in the world. Hafez of Shiraz perfected the ghazal; in Arabic, Ibn Arabi and the Egyptian Ibn al-Farid composed celebrated mystical verse; in Anatolia, Yunus Emre wrote in vernacular Turkish; and in South Asia, Bulleh Shah and Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai composed in Punjabi and Sindhi. Sufi poetry is also performed musically, as in the qawwali tradition of South Asia.

Influence

Sufi verse shaped the literatures of Persia, Ottoman Turkey and Mughal India, influenced Goethe's West–Eastern Divan, and in English-language versions — notably Coleman Barks's renderings of Rumi — reached a mass modern readership in the late 20th century.
