Rebuttable presumption

A rebuttable presumption is an assumption made by a court that is taken to be true unless someone comes forward to contest it and prove otherwise. Both in common law and civil law systems, rebuttable presumptions are rules of evidence that require a fact to be treated as established until sufficient contrary evidence is produced.

Operation

A rebuttable presumption typically shifts the burden of production — and sometimes the burden of persuasion — onto the party against whom the presumption operates. For example, a properly addressed and mailed letter is presumed to have been received by the addressee; the presumption disappears or is weighed against evidence that the letter was never sent or never arrived. A presumption that can be overturned by any contrary evidence is sometimes called a "bursting bubble" presumption, after a theory associated with James Bradley Thayer.

Examples

  • The presumption of innocence in criminal cases: the accused is presumed innocent until proven guilty.
  • The presumption of legitimacy: a child born during a marriage is presumed to be the child of the spouses.
  • The presumption of death after a prolonged unexplained absence (commonly seven years).
  • The presumption of regularity of official acts.

Conclusive presumptions

A rebuttable presumption is distinguished from a conclusive (or irrebuttable) presumption, which cannot be challenged by evidence and functions in substance as a rule of law rather than of evidence.
