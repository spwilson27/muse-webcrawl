Ignimbrite

Welded ignimbrite with flattened pumice clasts (fiamme).

Ignimbrite is a type of volcanic rock formed from the deposit of pyroclastic density currents — hot, fast-moving mixtures of gas, ash, pumice and rock fragments — during explosive eruptions, most famously of caldera-forming supervolcanoes. The term (from Latin ignis, fire, and imber, shower) was coined by the New Zealand geologist Patrick Marshall in 1935.

Formation

When a pyroclastic flow comes to rest, its fragments accumulate in a sheet that may be tens of metres thick. If the deposit is still hot enough, the glassy pumice fragments deform under the load and weld together, producing welded ignimbrite, a hard rock with flattened, flame-like pumice streaks called fiamme. Unwelded deposits are loose and easily eroded. Ignimbrites are typically rhyolitic to dacitic in composition, poorly sorted, and may grade from lithic-rich bases to pumice-rich tops.

Distribution

Famous ignimbrite fields include the Taupo Volcanic Zone of New Zealand, the Bishop Tuff of California (erupted from Long Valley Caldera some 760,000 years ago), the vast sheets of the Altiplano-Puna volcanic complex in the Andes, the Campanian Ignimbrite of Italy (from the Campi Flegrei eruption about 39,000 years ago), and the deposits of the Yellowstone caldera eruptions. Single eruptions can distribute ignimbrite over thousands of square kilometres.

Uses

Welded ignimbrite ("volcanic tuff") has been quarried as a building stone since antiquity — much of Rome's travertine-adjacent construction and the carved churches of Cappadocia and the moai platforms of Easter Island's quarries use comparable tuffs — and lightweight pumiceous ignimbrite is used as aggregate.
