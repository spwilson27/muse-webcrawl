Monopolistic competition

[Image placeholder: differentiated competing firms]

Monopolistic competition is a type of imperfect competition in which many firms sell products or services that are differentiated from one another — by brand, quality, design or location — and hence are not perfect substitutes. Each firm has some independent power to set its price, facing a downward-sloping demand curve like a monopolist, but free entry and exit of competing firms erode profits in the long run. The theory was developed in 1933 by Edward Chamberlin, and independently by Joan Robinson.

Characteristics

The defining features are many sellers, differentiated products, free entry and exit, and sellers that act independently. Restaurants, clothing brands, hairdressers, and consumer packaged goods are standard real-world approximations: firms compete through advertising and product design as well as price.

Short run and long run

In the short run a monopolistically competitive firm behaves like a monopolist, producing where marginal revenue equals marginal cost and possibly earning economic profit. Entry of new rivals shifts each incumbent's demand curve leftward until, in long-run equilibrium, price equals average total cost and economic profit is zero — but price still exceeds marginal cost, so output is below the efficient scale. Chamberlin called the resulting gap the "excess capacity" of the system, the price consumers pay for product variety.

Welfare

Compared with perfect competition, monopolistic competition produces less output at higher prices, but delivers diversity that consumers value. Whether markets provide too much or too little variety is ambiguous in theory; advertising, a hallmark of the market form, may inform or merely persuade.
