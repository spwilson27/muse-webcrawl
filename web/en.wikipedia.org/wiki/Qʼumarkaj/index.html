Qʼumarkaj (also spelt Gumarcaaj or Cumarcaj, and known in Spanish as Utatlán) was the capital of the Kʼicheʼ (Quiché) Maya kingdom in the highlands of modern Guatemala shortly before the Spanish conquest. Founded around 1400 by the king Qʼuqʼumatz, the city stood on a defensible plateau near the modern town of Santa Cruz del Quiché, protected on three sides by deep ravines.

The Kʼicheʼ capital

At its height in the late fifteenth century, under aggressive kings such as Kʼiqʼab, the Kʼicheʼ kingdom was the most powerful state in the Guatemalan highlands, dominating or exacting tribute from neighbouring peoples. Qʼumarkaj was laid out around a central plaza with temples dedicated to the patron deities of the ruling lineages — Tohil, Awilix, and Jacawitz — a great ballcourt, palaces of the lords of the ruling confederation, and scores of residential compounds for the nobility. The Popol Vuh, the famous Kʼicheʼ book of mythology and history set down shortly after the conquest, is closely associated with the city's royal court.

Conquest and destruction

In 1524 the Spanish conquistador Pedro de Alvarado defeated Kʼicheʼ armies in battle and was received into Qʼumarkaj; suspecting a trap, he seized the Kʼicheʼ lords Oxib-Quieh and Beleheb-Tzy and burned them alive, then torched the city. The population was resettled in the new Spanish town of Santa Cruz del Quiché. Today the site is an archaeological park whose ruined platforms, much quarried for building stone, remain a place of living Maya ritual, with altars still in use by Kʼicheʼ daykeepers.
