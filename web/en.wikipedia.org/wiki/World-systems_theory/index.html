World-systems theory (also known as world-systems analysis) is a multidisciplinary approach to world history and social change that emphasizes the world-system (rather than nation-states) as the primary unit of social analysis. It was developed principally by the sociologist Immanuel Wallerstein, beginning with the first volume of The Modern World-System in 1974.

Origins

Wallerstein drew on several intellectual traditions: the Marxist emphasis on capital accumulation, Latin American dependency theory (especially the work of André Gunder Frank and Raúl Prebisch), and the Annales School of history, particularly Fernand Braudel's concept of the économie-monde (world-economy). Wallerstein founded the Fernand Braudel Center at Binghamton University in 1976 to promote the approach.

Core, semi-periphery and periphery

The theory divides the world-economy into a spatial hierarchy. Core countries dominate high-profit, capital-intensive production and set the terms of trade. Peripheral countries supply raw materials and cheap labour under unfavourable terms. Semi-peripheral countries occupy an intermediate position, exploiting the periphery while being exploited by the core, and serve a politically stabilising role. States may rise or fall between zones, but the structure of unequal exchange persists.

Hegemony and cycles

Wallerstein described long cycles of expansion and stagnation (drawing on Kondratiev waves) and rare periods of hegemony in which one power — successively the Dutch Republic, the United Kingdom, and the United States — dominates the system economically, militarily and ideologically. He argued that the modern world-system originated in sixteenth-century Europe and expanded to encompass the entire globe by the late nineteenth century.

Criticism

Critics have charged the theory with economic determinism, with understating the autonomy of states and cultures, and with Eurocentrism in its periodisation. Others, including Janet Abu-Lughod, extended the approach to pre-capitalist world systems.
