Political machine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A political machine is a political organisation in which a leader or small group (the "boss") commands the support of a corps of supporters and businesses (usually campaign workers), who receive patronage — jobs, contracts, services — as reward for their efforts. Machines dominated many US cities in the late 19th and early 20th centuries — Tammany Hall in New York (Boss Tweed's ring stealing millions through padded contracts), the Hague machine in Jersey City, and, lasting longest, Richard J. Daley's Cook County organisation in Chicago.

Function and decline

Machines were governance technology for immigrant, weakly administered cities: they naturalised newcomers, found them jobs and coal, fixed small troubles with the authorities, and in exchange delivered their votes in blocs — a private welfare state financed by graft, described sympathetically by the ward heeler George Washington Plunkitt ("honest graft") and anatomised by reformers (Lincoln Steffens' The Shame of the Cities). They declined under civil-service reform, direct primaries, the Australian ballot, professional city management, and welfare-state programmes that displaced the machine's services; students of politics note both their corruption and their integration function, and use the model comparatively for clientelist parties worldwide — machine-like exchange of services for votes remains a global constant under different labels.
