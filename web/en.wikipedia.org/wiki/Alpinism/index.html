Alpinism, known in the English-speaking world as mountaineering, is the sport of climbing mountains, typically involving a combination of hiking, rock climbing, ice climbing, and travel over snow and glaciers, often at high altitude. The term derives from the Alps, where the sport developed in the nineteenth century. Unlike simple hillwalking, alpinism emphasises technical difficulty, self-reliance, and the management of objective hazards such as avalanches, rockfall, storms, and altitude sickness.

History

Mountains were climbed for religious, scientific, and strategic reasons for centuries, but climbing for its own sake began in the "golden age of alpinism" (roughly 1854–1865), when British gentlemen and their Swiss and French guides made the first ascents of most of the great Alpine peaks, culminating in Edward Whymper's ascent of the Matterhorn in 1865, which ended in the deaths of four of his party. National alpine clubs — the Alpine Club (London, 1857), the Austrian, Swiss, German, and French clubs — codified the sport. Attention then shifted to the greater ranges: the Caucasus, Andes, Rockies, and ultimately the Himalaya and Karakoram, where Everest was first climbed in 1953 by Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay.

Styles

Two broad ethics contrast in modern alpinism. Expedition style uses fixed ropes, stocked camps, porters, and often bottled oxygen to lay siege to a peak. Alpine style, by contrast, prizes a small, self-sufficient team carrying everything it needs and moving fast in a single push; it was championed notably by Reinhold Messner, the first person to climb all fourteen eight-thousanders (1970–1986) and to climb Everest solo without oxygen. Technical difficulty is summarised by grading systems for rock, ice, and overall commitment.

Hazards

Alpinism is intrinsically dangerous. Altitude illness (acute mountain sickness, cerebral and pulmonary oedema), frostbite, avalanches, crevasse falls, and sudden weather changes kill climbers every season; several of the eight-thousanders have historical fatality rates above ten percent for summiters. Modern equipment — crampons, ice axes, dynamic ropes, weather forecasting, and satellite communication — has reduced but not eliminated the risks.
