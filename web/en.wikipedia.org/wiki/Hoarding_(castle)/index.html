Hoarding (castle)

A hoarding (also called a hourd, brattice or, in French, hourd) was a temporary wooden gallery mounted on the outside of a medieval castle wall or tower during a siege. Projecting out from the top of the wall on wooden beams, it allowed defenders to look and shoot directly down at attackers at the foot of the wall, a zone otherwise in the wall's blind spot.

Design

Hoardings were supported by beams inserted into rows of holes (putlog holes) left in the masonry, or resting on stone corbels. The gallery floor had openings through which stones, boiling water or other missiles could be dropped on soldiers attempting to mine, batter or scale the wall. The structure was roofed and sometimes protected with wet hides against fire arrows. Because they were made of wood, hoardings were usually erected only when a siege threatened, and removed afterwards.

Development

Timber hoardings were widely used from the twelfth century. In later medieval fortifications their function was taken over by permanent stone structures: overhanging parapets with machicolations, bartizans and stone galleries, which offered the same vertical defence without the fire risk of wood. Surviving rows of putlog holes and corbels on castle walls are the main archaeological evidence for hoardings, and reconstructions have been built at sites such as the Cité de Carcassonne in France.
