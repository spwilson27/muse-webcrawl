Irish Wolfhound

Irish Wolfhound
Origin: Ireland
Height: 76–86 cm (30–34 in)
Weight: 41–70 kg
Coat: Rough, wiry
Colour: Grey, brindle, red, black, white, fawn
Litter size: 4–12
Life span: 6–10 years

The Irish Wolfhound (Irish: Cú Faoil) is a breed of large sighthound that has, by its presence and substantial size, inspired literature, poetry and mythology. One of the largest of all breeds of dog, it was originally bred in Ireland as a war dog and wolf hunter, and is the national dog of Ireland. Like many large dogs, the Irish Wolfhound is very gentle despite its size.

History

The breed is very old; there are references to the Irish Wolfhound in Roman records dating to 391 CE, when the Roman consul Quintus Aurelius Symmachus was presented with seven of them, described as a wonder at the Roman games. The breed was used for hunting wolves, deer and wild boar, and as a guard dog and war hound by Irish chieftains. By the 19th century the breed had nearly become extinct following the disappearance of wolves from Ireland and heavy exportation, but Captain George Augustus Graham of Dursley revived it from 1862 onwards by crossing the few remaining specimens with Scottish Deerhounds, Great Danes and other breeds.

Characteristics

The Irish Wolfhound is the tallest of all dog breeds, with a minimum height of 79 cm (31 in) for males. Its rough, wiry coat comes in grey, brindle, red, black, white and fawn. Despite its imposing stature, the breed is known for a calm, dignified and friendly temperament, often described as "gentle giants". The breed's motto, recorded by breed enthusiasts, is "Gentle when stroked, fierce when provoked".

Health

Like many very large breeds, Irish Wolfhounds have a relatively short lifespan of six to ten years. Health issues include dilated cardiomyopathy, osteosarcoma (bone cancer), pneumonia, liver shunt and gastric dilatation-volvulus (bloat).
