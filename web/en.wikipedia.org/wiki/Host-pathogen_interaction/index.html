Host–pathogen interaction

Infection results from the interplay of pathogen virulence and host defence.

The host–pathogen interaction is the process by which a pathogenic microbe — a bacterium, virus, fungus, or parasite — infects and causes disease in a host organism, and by which the host resists. Disease outcome depends on both the pathogen's virulence factors and the effectiveness of the host immune response; much of the tissue damage in infection is caused not by the microbe itself but by the immune reaction it provokes.

Stages of infection

A typical interaction proceeds through encounter and entry, adhesion to host surfaces, invasion of tissues or cells, evasion of immune defences, multiplication, and transmission to a new host. Pathogens use adhesins, capsules, toxins, and secretion systems to colonise; obligate intracellular pathogens such as viruses must enter host cells to replicate.

Host defence

Hosts respond with physical barriers, innate immunity — pattern-recognition receptors detecting conserved microbial molecules, phagocytes, inflammation, and complement — and adaptive immunity mediated by antibodies and T cells. The damage-response framework of Casadevall and Pirofski emphasises that disease reflects damage arising from either the pathogen or the host response.

Evolutionary dynamics

Host and pathogen exert strong selective pressure on each other, an arms race reflected in rapid evolution of virulence factors and immune genes. Coevolution can lead toward commensalism or remain antagonistic, and it shapes phenomena from antigenic variation in influenza to resistance genes in plants.
