Mincome (short for "Manitoba Basic Annual Income Experiment") was a Canadian guaranteed annual income (GAI) social experiment conducted in Manitoba in the 1970s. Jointly funded by the federal government under Pierre Trudeau and the Manitoba government under Edward Schreyer, it ran from 1974 to 1979 in Winnipeg and in the rural town of Dauphin, where every family was eligible — making Dauphin a "saturation site" in which the effects on a whole community could be studied.

Dauphin, Manitoba — the only saturation site among the North American income-maintenance experiments.

Design

Families received a guaranteed income set at roughly 60 per cent of Statistics Canada's low-income cut-off, which was reduced by 50 cents for each dollar of other income earned — a negative income tax design. The programme was intended to measure work incentives: whether a guaranteed income floor would cause people to reduce their labour supply, and by how much.

Termination and the forgotten data

Amid recessions, inflation and changes of government in both Ottawa and Manitoba, the project was wound down in 1979 without a final report. Roughly 1,800 boxes of data — surveys, administrative records and interviews — were warehoused and largely forgotten for decades.

Later findings

Economist Evelyn Forget of the University of Manitoba recovered and analysed the data in the 2000s. Her 2011 study, "The Town with No Poverty", found that in Dauphin hospitalisations fell by 8.5 per cent relative to comparison towns, with fewer accidents, injuries and mental-health admissions; adolescents stayed in school longer, with higher grade-12 completion; and work reductions were modest, concentrated among new mothers and teenage boys who stayed in education. Mincome is frequently cited in modern debates over basic income and informed later pilots such as the Ontario Basic Income Pilot (2017–2019).
