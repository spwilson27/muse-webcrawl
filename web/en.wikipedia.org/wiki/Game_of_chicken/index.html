Chicken (game)

Game of chicken
Field: Game theory
Players: 2
Also known as: Hawk–dove game, snowdrift game
Key concept: Brinkmanship

The game of chicken, also known as the hawk–dove game or snowdrift game, is a model of conflict for two players in game theory. The principle is that while the outcome is ideal for a player if their opponent yields, the yield is not safe if the opponent does not yield either. Its name derives from a dangerous game in which two drivers race toward each other on a collision course: the first to swerve is the "chicken", but if neither swerves, both may crash.

Each player would prefer to go straight while the other swerves; the worst outcome for both is mutual stubbornness. Unlike the prisoner's dilemma, chicken has two pure-strategy Nash equilibria, each favouring a different player.

Applications

The model has been applied to nuclear brinkmanship (notably the Cuban Missile Crisis), evolutionary biology in the form of the hawk–dove game introduced by John Maynard Smith, and industrial disputes. Bertrand Russell used the analogy to criticise nuclear deterrence.
