Parliament Act 1911

Parliament Act 1911
Citation: 1 & 2 Geo. 5. c. 13
Royal assent: 18 August 1911
Jurisdiction: United Kingdom
Amended by: Parliament Act 1949
Purpose: Restricts the power of the House of Lords to block legislation

The Parliament Act 1911 (1 & 2 Geo. 5. c. 13) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom. It asserted the supremacy of the elected House of Commons by removing the power of the House of Lords to veto money bills, and replacing its veto over other public bills with a power to delay them for up to two years. It also reduced the maximum duration of a Parliament from seven years to five.

Background

The Act followed the constitutional crisis of 1909–1911, when the Conservative-dominated House of Lords rejected the Liberal government's "People's Budget" of 1909, breaking a long-standing convention. After two general elections in 1910 and the threat by King George V to create enough new Liberal peers to swamp the Lords' majority, the Lords passed the bill in August 1911.

Provisions

Under the Act, a money bill certified by the Speaker could receive royal assent without the Lords' consent if not passed by them within one month. Other public bills could be enacted without the Lords' approval if passed by the Commons in three successive sessions and at least two years had elapsed. The Act did not apply to bills extending the life of a Parliament beyond five years, nor to private bills or bills originating in the Lords.

Later use

The Parliament Act 1949, passed using the 1911 Act's own procedure, reduced the delaying power to one year and two sessions. The procedure was used for measures including the Government of Ireland Act 1914, the Welsh Church Act 1914, the War Crimes Act 1991, and the Hunting Act 2004, whose validity under the Acts was upheld by the House of Lords in Jackson v Attorney General (2005).
