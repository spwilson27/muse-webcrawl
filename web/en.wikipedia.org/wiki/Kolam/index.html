Kolam

A pulli kolam drawn at dawn before a Tamil home

A kolam (Tamil: கோலம், kōlam) is a traditional decorative art form drawn by hand at the thresholds of homes, temples, and businesses in Tamil Nadu, Sri Lanka, and Tamil diaspora communities. Traditionally drawn by women each morning with rice flour or powdered white stone, the kolam is at once an offering, a welcome to visitors and to the goddess Lakshmi, and a daily exercise in geometric design.

Method

The ground at the entrance is first washed with water (traditionally cow-dung solution, believed antiseptic) to give a clean dark surface. The artist works from a grid of dots (pulli) laid out in rectangular, radial, or hexagonal arrays. In the most common style, a single continuous line loops around the dots without lifting the hand from the ground, enclosing each dot; in others, lines pass through the dots themselves. Skilled practitioners complete intricate designs from memory in minutes, letting the flour stream between thumb and forefinger.

Materials

The classic medium is rice flour or coarsely ground rice, chosen deliberately so that ants, birds, and other small creatures can eat it — the kolam is thus a daily act of charity, feeding a thousand beings. Today coloured powders (made from coloured stone, turmeric, kumkum, and synthetic dyes) are used especially for festivals, and flower petals and lamps are added for special occasions. Less ephemeral versions are painted or laid as stencils.

Significance and occasions

Daily kolams are usually small and white. During the month of Margazhi (mid-December to mid-January), households draw large elaborate kolams in a season of devotion and informal competition. For the Pongal harvest festival the kolam incorporates sugarcane, turmeric plants, and a new clay pot; for weddings, Diwali, and temple festivals, large coloured designs fill courtyards and streets.

Mathematics

Kolam patterns have attracted serious mathematical study. The looping dot kolams correspond to unicursal curves and Eulerian paths in graph theory; their symmetries, recursion, and generative rules have been modelled with formal grammars, array languages, and Lindenmayer systems, and kolams are used as examples of ethnomathematics — complex algorithmic art practised as daily routine. Related threshold arts include the rangoli of northern India, muggu of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, and alpona of Bengal.
