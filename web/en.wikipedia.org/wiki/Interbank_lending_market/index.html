Interbank lending market

The interbank lending market is a global market in which banks and other financial institutions lend to and borrow from one another, typically for very short maturities — frequently overnight, and generally for no longer than a year. It allows banks with surplus reserves to earn a return while banks facing temporary shortfalls can meet reserve requirements and settlement obligations.

Rates and benchmarks

The price of interbank funds is expressed in benchmark rates. Historically the most famous was LIBOR, the London Interbank Offered Rate, calculated from panel banks' submissions for several currencies; manipulation of those submissions, exposed in the LIBOR scandal of 2012, led to LIBOR's discontinuation by 2023 and its replacement by risk-free reference rates such as SOFR (US dollar) and €STR (euro). In the United States the federal funds rate — the rate on overnight loans of reserves held at the Federal Reserve — is the principal target of monetary policy.

Role in crises

Because interbank loans are usually unsecured, the market runs on trust. In 2007–08, doubts about banks' exposure to subprime mortgage losses caused interbank lending to seize up: rates spiked relative to expected policy rates (the TED spread widened dramatically), institutions hoarded liquidity, and the freeze in wholesale funding contributed to the failures of Northern Rock and Lehman Brothers and to the wider global financial crisis. Central banks responded by massively expanding their own lending facilities to substitute for the market.
