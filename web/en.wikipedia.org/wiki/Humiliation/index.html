Humiliation

Humiliation is the abasement of pride, which creates mortification or leads to a state of being humbled or reduced in status. It can be brought about through intimidation, physical or mental mistreatment, or trickery, or by embarrassment suffered when an action is socially or legally unacceptable. Whereas humility can be sought alone as a means to de-emphasise the ego, humiliation must involve other persons, though not necessarily directly or willingly.

Punishments such as the pillory were designed to humiliate offenders publicly.

Psychology

Humiliation is a potent emotion. Researchers such as Evelin Lindner have described it as "the nuclear bomb of the emotions", arguing that experiences of humiliation can fuel cycles of violence, conflict, and retaliation, while Donald Klein characterized the "humiliation dynamic" as a powerful force in human relationships. Feelings of humiliation are associated with a sudden loss of status before an audience, and may give rise to shame, anger, depression, and desires for revenge.

Humiliation as punishment

Historically, many punishments were deliberately humiliating: the stocks and pillory, public whipping, branding, tarring and feathering, and the charivari or "rough music" directed at those who transgressed community norms. Public humiliation remains a feature of some modern practices, including certain forms of shaming penalties in criminal justice, and it is strictly prohibited as "outrages upon personal dignity, in particular humiliating and degrading treatment" by the Geneva Conventions for prisoners of war and detainees.

Social and political aspects

Philosophers such as Avishai Margalit, in The Decent Society, argue that a decent society is one whose institutions do not humiliate its members. Political theorists have also examined humiliation in international relations, where perceived national humiliation can shape collective memory and foreign policy.
