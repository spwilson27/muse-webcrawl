Pork

Pork is the culinary name for the meat of the pig (Sus domesticus). It is the most widely eaten meat in the world, accounting for roughly a third or more of global meat consumption, with evidence of pig husbandry dating back to around 5000 BC. Pork is eaten both freshly cooked and preserved; curing processes extend its shelf life, producing ham, bacon, gammon, salami, and various sausages. In English culinary usage, the vocabulary of pork reflects the Norman French influence after 1066: the animal in the field remained the Anglo-Saxon "pig" or "swine," while its meat at table took the French name porc.

Consumption patterns

China is by far the largest consumer and producer of pork, followed by the European Union and the United States. Pork dominates the diets of East and Southeast Asia and much of Europe; per-capita consumption is especially high in countries such as Spain, Germany, Poland, and South Korea. By contrast, pork is forbidden under Jewish dietary law (kashrut) and Islamic law (halal), so consumption is negligible in Israel and much of the Muslim world, and it is avoided by some other religious communities, including many Hindus, Seventh-day Adventists, and Rastafari.

Cuts and products

The pig carcass is divided into primal cuts — typically the shoulder (Boston butt and picnic shoulder), loin, leg (ham), belly, and spareribs — which are then processed into retail cuts such as chops, roasts, tenderloin, and ribs. The loin provides the leanest fresh cuts; the belly yields bacon and pancetta; the leg is commonly cured as ham. Pork fat (lard) was historically a staple cooking fat and remains important in baking and charcuterie. Processed products include fresh and smoked sausages, frankfurters, pâté, and cured specialties such as jamón ibérico, prosciutto, and speck. Offal, blood (in black pudding and blood sausages), skin (crackling and chicharrón), and trotters are also widely used in various cuisines.

Nutrition and health

Fresh pork is a good source of complete protein, thiamin (vitamin B1 — richer than in most other meats), other B vitamins, selenium, phosphorus, and zinc; its fat content and composition vary strongly by cut. Public-health guidance generally treats lean fresh pork similarly to other unprocessed red meats, while processed pork products are associated in epidemiological studies with higher risks of colorectal cancer and cardiovascular disease, leading the WHO's IARC in 2015 to classify processed meat as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1).

Food safety

Raw pork can carry the parasites Trichinella and the pork tapeworm (Taenia solium) and bacteria such as Salmonella and Yersinia enterocolitica. Modern husbandry and inspection have greatly reduced trichinellosis in commercial supply chains, and food-safety authorities recommend cooking ground pork to 71 °C (160 °F) and whole cuts to 63 °C (145 °F) with a rest period. Outbreaks of swine diseases such as African swine fever — harmless to humans — periodically disrupt world markets, as in China's 2018–19 epidemic, which culled a substantial fraction of the world's largest herd.
