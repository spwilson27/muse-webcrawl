Solvay process

The Solvay process or ammonia-soda process is the major industrial process for the production of sodium carbonate (soda ash). It was developed by the Belgian chemist Ernest Solvay in the 1860s and largely displaced the older, more polluting Leblanc process by the end of the 19th century.

Chemistry

The process uses readily available raw materials — brine (sodium chloride), limestone (calcium carbonate), and ammonia, which is recycled in a nearly closed loop:

  1. Carbon dioxide, produced by calcining limestone, is bubbled through ammoniated brine; sodium bicarbonate precipitates because it is the least soluble product: NaCl + NH3 + CO2 + H2O → NaHCO3 + NH4Cl.
  2. The sodium bicarbonate is filtered and heated, decomposing to sodium carbonate, water, and carbon dioxide, which is reused.
  3. Ammonia is recovered from the ammonium chloride by treatment with lime (from the calcined limestone), producing calcium chloride as the main byproduct.

The overall reaction is 2 NaCl + CaCO3 → Na2CO3 + CaCl2.

Significance

Soda ash is essential for glass, soap and detergent, paper, and chemical manufacture. The Solvay process made it cheaply and at scale, and Ernest Solvay used the resulting fortune to found the famous Solvay Conferences in physics and chemistry. Environmental issues centre on disposal of calcium chloride and limestone quarrying; in some regions, mining of natural trona deposits competes with the synthetic process.
