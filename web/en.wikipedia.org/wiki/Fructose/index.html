Fructose, or fruit sugar, is a simple ketonic monosaccharide found in many plants, where it is often bonded to glucose to form the disaccharide sucrose. It is one of the three dietary monosaccharides, along with glucose and galactose, that are absorbed directly into the blood during digestion.

Properties and sources

Fructose is the sweetest of the naturally occurring carbohydrates. It occurs naturally in fruits, fruit juices, some vegetables and honey, and it makes up about half of table sugar (sucrose). Commercially, large quantities are used in the form of high-fructose corn syrup, produced by enzymatically converting corn starch, and as crystalline fructose.

Metabolism

Unlike glucose, fructose is metabolised primarily in the liver, where it is phosphorylated by fructokinase to fructose-1-phosphate and cleaved into three-carbon intermediates that can enter glycolysis, be converted to glucose and glycogen, or contribute to fatty-acid synthesis. Its metabolism largely bypasses the rate-limiting, insulin-regulated step of glycolysis. Very high intakes of fructose have been studied in connection with insulin resistance, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and elevated triglycerides, though the health effects of fructose at ordinary dietary levels — particularly from whole fruit — remain a subject of scientific discussion.
