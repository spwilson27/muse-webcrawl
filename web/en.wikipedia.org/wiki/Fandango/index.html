Fandango

The fandango is a lively Spanish dance for two people, typically accompanied by guitars, hand-clapping (palmas), and castanets, in triple meter. Originating in the Baroque era and prominent in Andalusia, it is a root form of flamenco and has many regional variants, including the fandangos de Huelva and the fandango malagueño.

History and music

The fandango first appears in the early 18th century and was controversial in its day for its sensuous movements. Its characteristic harmonic pattern (the Andalusian cadence in Phrygian mode) and alternation of sung coplas with guitar interludes influenced many composers: Gluck, Mozart (in The Marriage of Figaro), Boccherini, whose Fandango quintet includes castanet parts, Rimsky-Korsakov, and later flamenco guitarists.

Variants

In flamenco, fandango grande allows improvisation while fandangos personales are associated with particular singers; the free-rhythm fandangos naturales contrast with metric forms. Related dances include the fandanguillo and the Mexican fandango gatherings of son jarocho in Veracruz, centred on the tarima platform and zapateado footwork.
