Flash fiction

Flash fiction is a genre of fiction defined by extreme brevity: complete stories told in only a few hundred words, typically under 1,000 words. Despite their length, flash-fiction works characteristically offer plot, character development and setting, working largely through implication and suggestion.

Forms and terminology

A number of word-count categories are used within the genre. A drabble is exactly 100 words; a story of 55 words is sometimes called "55 Fiction"; a micro-story may be only a sentence or two. The six-word story attributed to Ernest Hemingway — "For sale: baby shoes, never worn" — is a frequently cited example of extreme compression, though its attribution is doubtful.

History

Very short stories have a long history, from Aesop's fables and parables through nineteenth-century practitioners such as Anton Chekhov and Ambrose Bierce. The modern form was popularised in the 1980s and 1990s by editors and anthologists such as Robert Shapard and James Thomas, whose anthology Flash Fiction (1992) helped give the genre its English name. The rise of online literary magazines greatly expanded publication venues for very short work.

Characteristics

Because every sentence must carry unusual weight, flash fiction typically begins in the middle of the action, relies on a single scene or image, and often ends with a resonant turn. Writers in the form frequently exploit what is left unsaid.
