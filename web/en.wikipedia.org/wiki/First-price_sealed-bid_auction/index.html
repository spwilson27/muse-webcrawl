First-price sealed-bid auction

A first-price sealed-bid auction is a type of auction in which all bidders simultaneously submit sealed bids so that no bidder knows how much the others have offered. The highest bidder wins and pays the amount of their own bid — hence "first-price", in contrast to a second-price (Vickrey) auction, in which the winner pays the second-highest bid. The format is widely used for government procurement, treasury and spectrum sales in some countries, and the sale of assets such as mineral rights.

Bidding strategy

Because the winner pays their own bid, bidding one's true valuation guarantees zero surplus if one wins; rational bidders therefore "shade" their bids below their valuation, trading a lower probability of winning against a larger profit when they do win. The optimal amount of shading depends on the number of rivals and the bidder's beliefs about their valuations. This strategic problem was analysed in the foundational auction-theory literature by William Vickrey (1961) and in the symmetric equilibrium models developed in the 1970s.

Revenue equivalence

Under the standard assumptions — independent private values, risk-neutral bidders and symmetric distributions — the revenue equivalence theorem (Riley and Samuelson, 1981; Myerson, 1981) shows that the first-price auction yields the seller the same expected revenue as a second-price auction and other standard formats. When these assumptions fail — for example with risk-averse bidders, who bid more aggressively in first-price auctions — the first-price format can raise more revenue, one reason it remains common in practice.

Applications

First-price sealed bids have been used for offshore oil-lease sales in the United States, construction and supply procurement, privatisation sales, and, since the late 2010s, much online display-advertising inventory, as major ad exchanges shifted from second-price to first-price auctions.
