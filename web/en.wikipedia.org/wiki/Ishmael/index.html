Ishmael

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Hagar and Ishmael in the wilderness, a traditional depiction

Ishmael (Hebrew: יִשְׁמָעֵאל, Yīšmāʿēʾl, "God hears"; Arabic: إسماعيل, Ismāʿīl) is a figure in the Hebrew Bible, the New Testament and the Quran. According to the Book of Genesis, he was the first son of Abraham, born to Sarah's Egyptian handmaid Hagar. In Jewish, Christian and Islamic traditions he is regarded as the ancestor of the Ishmaelites, generally associated with Arab peoples, and in Islam he is honoured as a prophet.

In Genesis

Genesis 16 recounts that the childless Sarah gave her slave Hagar to Abraham; when Hagar conceived and tension arose, she fled and was met by an angel who promised her son would be named Ishmael because God had heard her affliction, and that he would be "a wild ass of a man" living at odds with his kin. After the birth of Isaac to Sarah, Genesis 21 relates that Hagar and the young Ishmael were sent away into the wilderness of Beersheba, where God saved them from dying of thirst. Ishmael grew up in the wilderness of Paran, became an archer, and married an Egyptian woman.

Genesis 25 lists Ishmael's twelve sons — Nebaioth, Kedar, Adbeel, Mibsam, Mishma, Dumah, Massa, Hadad, Tema, Jetur, Naphish and Kedemah — described as princes of twelve tribes, and records Ishmael's death at the age of 137.

In Islam

In the Quran, Ishmael (Ismail) is a prophet and, together with Abraham, is said to have raised the foundations of the Kaaba in Mecca. Islamic tradition associates him with the Zamzam well and with the sacrifice commemorated at Eid al-Adha (where the son intended for sacrifice is commonly identified as Ishmael rather than Isaac in Islamic exegesis). He is considered the ancestor of Muhammad through the line of Kedar and the Adnanite Arabs.

Interpretation

The figure is read differently across the three traditions: in Judaism and Christianity the covenant line passes through Isaac, while Ishmael receives his own blessing and nation; Paul's Epistle to the Galatians uses Hagar and Sarah allegorically. Scholarship also treats the Ishmaelite genealogies as reflecting historical North Arabian tribal groups known from Assyrian sources.
