Kretek

Kretek (pronounced "cree-tech") are cigarettes made with a blend of tobacco, cloves and other flavours. The word "kretek" itself is an onomatopoetic term for the crackling sound of burning cloves. Kretek can be either machine-rolled or hand-rolled (in which case they are called sigaret kretek tangan).

Kretek were invented in the early 1880s in Kudus, Central Java, Indonesia. Their inventor, Haji Djamhari, suffered from chest pain and experimented with applying clove oil to his chest; seeking a more direct delivery, he rolled tobacco mixed with dried clove buds and local spices in dried corn husks, and found relief. He began selling his "rokok cengkeh" (clove cigarettes) in his pharmacy under the brand name "kretek".

History and industry

Kudus remains the historic centre of kretek production. Major Indonesian producers include Djarum, Gudang Garam (based in Kediri), HM Sampoerna and Bentoel. The kretek industry is one of Indonesia's largest employers, particularly of women who hand-roll the cigarettes, and kretek account for the overwhelming majority of cigarettes consumed in Indonesia, where smoking rates are among the highest in the world.

Machine-rolled kretek (sigaret kretek mesin) dominate by volume, though hand-rolled kretek retain a significant market share and cultural cachet. Flavoured variants, including mild and low-tar "mild" kretek introduced in the 1990s and 2000s, are particularly popular with younger smokers.

Composition and health

Traditional kretek contain roughly 60–80% tobacco and 20–40% ground cloves and clove oil, together with flavourings such as cocoa, coffee, cinnamon and fruit extracts ("sauce"). The eugenol in clove oil acts as a mild anaesthetic, allowing deeper inhalation. Despite perceptions of being more "natural" or safer, kretek deliver comparable or greater amounts of tar, nicotine and carbon monoxide than conventional cigarettes, and are associated with distinctive forms of acute lung injury among some users.

Regulation abroad

In 2009 the United States banned cigarettes with characterizing flavours other than menthol under the Family Smoking Prevention and Tobacco Control Act, which effectively prohibited clove cigarettes; Indonesia challenged the ban at the World Trade Organization, arguing it discriminated against kretek while exempting menthol, and the WTO panel ruled largely in Indonesia's favour in 2012. Kretek remain restricted or banned in a number of other jurisdictions.
