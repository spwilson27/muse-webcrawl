Luther v. Borden, 48 U.S. (7 How.) 1 (1849), is a United States Supreme Court decision that established the political question doctrine. The Court held that whether a state government is the legitimate, republican government guaranteed by the Guarantee Clause of the U.S. Constitution is a question for Congress and the President, not for the judiciary.

Background

The case arose from the Dorr Rebellion in Rhode Island (1841–1842). Rhode Island still governed itself under its colonial charter of 1663, which restricted voting to landowners. Thomas Wilson Dorr and his supporters drafted a "People's Constitution," held elections, and formed a rival government, while the charter government under Governor Samuel Ward King declared martial law. Martin Luther, a Dorr supporter, sued Borden, a state militiaman, for trespass after Borden entered and searched his home under martial law orders. Luther argued that the charter government was not a lawful republican government, so its orders were void.

Decision

Chief Justice Roger Taney, for the Court, declined to decide which government was legitimate. Under the Guarantee Clause (Article IV, Section 4), it rests with Congress to decide what government is established in a state, and the President's recognition — President John Tyler had recognised the charter government — bound the courts. The case became the foundational authority that some constitutional questions are non-justiciable political questions. Its reasoning was later considered in cases such as Baker v. Carr (1962), which framed the modern political-question criteria.
