Additional-member system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The additional-member system (AMS) is a two-vote electoral system in which voters elect constituency representatives (usually by first-past-the-post) and also vote for a party; additional members are then allocated from party lists so that each party's total seats more closely reflect its share of the party vote. It is a form of mixed-member system, closely related to mixed-member proportional representation (MMP); the terms are often used interchangeably, though AMS is sometimes used for variants — such as those used in Scotland and Wales — where the compensatory calculation is done regionally and proportionality is somewhat less exact than under national MMP.

How it works

Each voter casts a constituency vote and a regional (party) vote. Constituency winners are elected first. List seats are then distributed using a divisor method (commonly modified D'Hondt) applied iteratively to the party vote, taking seats already won into account, so parties under-represented by constituency results receive top-up members from their lists — usually closed lists. If a party wins more constituency seats in a region than its party vote entitles it to, an overhang can arise.

Usage

AMS is used for the Scottish Parliament, the Welsh Senedd, and the London Assembly. Germany's Bundestag system and New Zealand's MMP (see electoral system of New Zealand) are fully compensatory relatives. Supporters value the combination of a local MP link with fairer overall results; critics point to two classes of member, the influence of party list-makers, and strategic split-ticket campaigning ("vote X locally, Y on the list").
