Youth court

A youth court (also called a teen court, peer court, or student court) is a diversion programme within the juvenile justice system in which young people who have committed minor offences are judged and sentenced by their peers rather than through the formal court process. Youth courts typically handle first-time, non-violent misdemeanours such as shoplifting, vandalism, truancy, and minor drug or alcohol offences, with the young person's agreement and that of their parents.

How youth courts work

Cases are referred by police, schools, or juvenile courts. In a typical hearing, trained youth volunteers serve as jurors, attorneys, bailiffs, and sometimes judge, under the supervision of adult coordinators or legal professionals. The respondent admits responsibility for the offence, and the peer jury imposes constructive sanctions — commonly community service, apology letters, essays, restitution, counselling, or serving on a future youth court jury. Successful completion usually results in the offence being dismissed and no formal juvenile record.

Models

Common formats include the adult-judge model, the youth-judge model, the peer jury model, and the tribunal model (with youth attorneys but no jury). Programmes operate in most US states and in a number of other countries; in England and Wales, similar functions are performed by youth offender panels under referral orders.

Effectiveness

Supporters argue that youth courts embody restorative justice: offenders confront the human impact of their actions, make amends, and are reintegrated rather than stigmatised. Studies of teen courts have generally found recidivism rates comparable to or lower than those of conventional juvenile processing, though results vary by programme design, and the volunteer participants themselves are reported to develop civic and legal skills.
