The Lord of the Rings

The Lord of the Rings is an epic high-fantasy novel by the English author and scholar J. R. R. Tolkien. Set in Middle-earth, it was written in stages between 1937 and 1949 and published in three volumes in 1954–55.

Synopsis

The Dark Lord Sauron seeks the One Ring, which he forged to dominate Middle-earth and which has come into the possession of the hobbit Frodo Baggins. Frodo and a Fellowship of companions — the wizard Gandalf, the men Aragorn and Boromir, the elf Legolas, the dwarf Gimli, and the hobbits Sam, Merry and Pippin — journey to destroy the Ring in the fires of Mount Doom in Mordor, the only place it can be unmade. As war engulfs Gondor and Rohan, Frodo and Sam press on with the treacherous Gollum as their guide; the Ring is destroyed, Sauron falls, and Aragorn is crowned king.

Themes and influences

Tolkien, a philologist, drew on Norse and Germanic mythology, Old English literature such as Beowulf, Finnish folklore, and his experiences in the First World War, while insisting the work was not an allegory. Themes include the corrupting nature of power, pity and mercy, death and immortality, and environmental destruction by industrialisation. His Roman Catholic faith also shaped the book's moral framework.

Reception and legacy

Initially received with mixed reviews, the novel became a cultural phenomenon in the 1960s and has sold more than 150 million copies, making it one of the best-selling books of all time. It effectively founded the modern high-fantasy genre. Peter Jackson's film trilogy (2001–2003) won 17 Academy Awards in total, and the work has been adapted for radio, stage, games and television.
