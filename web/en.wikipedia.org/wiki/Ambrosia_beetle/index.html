Ambrosia beetle

Ambrosia beetles
KingdomAnimalia
OrderColeoptera
FamilyCurculionidae (weevils)
GroupsScolytinae (incl. Xyleborini) and Platypodinae
Shared traitFungus farming in wood

Ambrosia beetles are not a single taxon but an ecological grouping of weevils — chiefly in the subfamilies Scolytinae and Platypodinae — that bore into dead or stressed trees and cultivate symbiotic "ambrosia" fungi, on which both adults and larvae feed. The lifestyle has evolved independently many times and is one of the few examples of agriculture in non-human animals.

Biology

A female beetle excavates a gallery system (tunnels) in the xylem and inoculates the walls with fungal spores carried in specialised pockets called mycangia. The fungus grows into the wood, concentrating nutrients, and the beetle family grazes on the fungal gardens (xylomycetophagy) rather than eating the wood itself. Many species are haplodiploid with extreme inbreeding; in the tribe Xyleborini, males are dwarfed, flightless and rare, and a single mated female can found a new colony.

Ecology and economic impact

Ambrosia beetles are important decomposers in forests, but their fungi can be serious tree pathogens when introduced to new regions. The redbay ambrosia beetle (Xyleborus glabratus) carries the laurel wilt fungus that has killed millions of redbay and avocado trees in the southeastern United States, and the polyphagous shot hole borer complex spreads Fusarium dieback in California and elsewhere. Invasions are frequently linked to trade in wood packaging and logs.
