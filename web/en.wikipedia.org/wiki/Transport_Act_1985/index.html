The Transport Act 1985 is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that introduced deregulation and privatisation of bus services in Great Britain and established the framework that still largely governs local bus operation outside London. It was a flagship measure of the Thatcher government's transport policy, promoted by Secretary of State Nicholas Ridley.

Provisions

From "Deregulation Day", 26 October 1986, the old system of road service licensing was abolished in Great Britain outside London: any licensed operator could register a commercial bus service on 56 days' notice, and competition "on the road" was permitted. Local authorities could subsidise socially necessary services only by competitive tender. The publicly owned National Bus Company was broken up into some 70 subsidiaries and privatised, and municipal bus companies were required to operate at arm's length as separate companies (many later sold). The Act also deregulated taxis in some respects and introduced concessionary travel provisions.

Effects and later developments

London was specifically excluded; its buses remained regulated under London Regional Transport (later Transport for London) with franchised routes — a contrast often cited in policy debates. Outside London, bus ridership continued a long decline and fares rose in real terms, though costs fell and minibus competition initially flourished before the industry consolidated into large groups (Stagecoach, FirstGroup, Arriva, Go-Ahead). The Bus Services Act 2017 later allowed mayoral combined authorities to adopt franchising, which Greater Manchester implemented with its Bee Network from 2023 — see Transport for Greater Manchester.
