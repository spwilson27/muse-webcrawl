Primary motor cortex

The primary motor cortex (Brodmann area 4) is a region of the cerebral cortex involved in the planning, control and execution of voluntary movements. It is located in the dorsal portion of the frontal lobe, on the anterior wall of the central sulcus and the adjoining precentral gyrus. Together with the premotor and supplementary motor areas it forms the motor cortex, but the primary motor cortex is the principal source of descending signals that initiate movement through the corticospinal tract.

Structure

The primary motor cortex is distinguished histologically by the absence of an inner granular layer and by the presence of giant pyramidal cells known as Betz cells in layer V, among the largest neurons in the central nervous system. Its axons form a major part of the corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts. The region is organised somatotopically: neighbouring parts of the cortex control neighbouring parts of the body, a layout classically depicted as the motor homunculus, with disproportionately large areas devoted to the hands and face.

History

In 1870 Gustav Fritsch and Eduard Hitzig showed that electrical stimulation of the dog's cortex produced movements of the opposite side of the body. John Hughlings Jackson's observations of focal seizures suggested a topographic map of the body in cortex. In the 1930s and 1940s Wilder Penfield mapped the human motor cortex by stimulating the exposed brains of epilepsy patients, producing the homunculus illustration. Later work with microstimulation and single-unit recording in monkeys refined the map, showing that it is fractured and overlapping rather than a neat point-to-point replica.

Function

Neurons in the primary motor cortex encode features of movement such as direction, force and speed. Populations of neurons collectively specify movement direction through a population-vector code, as demonstrated by Apostolos Georgopoulos. The area receives input from the thalamus (relaying cerebellar and basal ganglia signals), the somatosensory cortex, premotor areas, and the contralateral motor cortex, and its output is continuously adjusted by sensory feedback during movement.

Clinical significance

Damage to the primary motor cortex or its descending pathways — for example from stroke affecting the middle cerebral artery — causes weakness or paralysis of the opposite side of the body, often with spasticity and loss of fine finger control. Because of the homunculus layout, small lesions produce characteristic patterns of deficit. Recovery after injury is aided by cortical plasticity, the same property exploited by brain–machine interfaces that decode motor cortical activity to control prosthetic limbs.
