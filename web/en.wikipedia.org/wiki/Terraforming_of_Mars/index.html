Terraforming of Mars

The terraforming of Mars is a hypothetical procedure that would consist of planetary engineering projects or concurrent projects aiming to transform Mars from a planet hostile to terrestrial life into one that can sustainably host humans and other life forms free of protection or mediation. The process would involve the modification of the planet's existing atmosphere, climate, and surface through a variety of resource-intensive initiatives, and the installation of new technologies.

Motivations

Proponents argue that terraforming Mars would provide a "backup" home for humanity, expand access to resources, and fulfill a drive for exploration. Critics note the immense timescales (centuries to millennia), the uncertain availability of volatiles such as nitrogen, and ethical questions about altering another planet and any indigenous microbial life.

Proposed methods

Proposals generally begin with warming the planet to release frozen carbon dioxide from the polar caps and regolith, thickening the atmosphere and raising temperatures in a runaway greenhouse feedback. Suggested techniques include orbital mirrors to concentrate sunlight on the poles, importing ammonia- or methane-rich asteroids, producing super-greenhouse gases such as perfluorocarbons on Mars, and darkening the polar ice to reduce albedo. Later stages would introduce liquid water, an oxygen-producing biosphere of photosynthetic organisms, and eventually breathable air — the last being by far the slowest step.

Challenges

Mars lacks a global magnetic field, so its atmosphere is stripped by the solar wind; some proposals envision an artificial magnetic shield at the Mars–Sun L1 point. Low gravity (38% of Earth's) may have unknown long-term health effects, and the total inventory of accessible CO2 may be insufficient to warm the planet to Earth-like conditions with current technology.
