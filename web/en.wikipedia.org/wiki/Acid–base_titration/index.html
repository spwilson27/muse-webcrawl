Acid–base titration

An acid–base titration is a method of quantitative chemical analysis used to determine the concentration of an acid or base (the analyte) by neutralizing it with a solution of known concentration (the titrant). The titrant is added from a burette to a measured volume of analyte containing a pH indicator; the volume required to reach the equivalence point — where moles of acid and base are stoichiometrically equal — yields the unknown concentration.

Titration curves

A titration curve plots pH against volume of titrant added. A strong acid titrated with a strong base shows a steep rise through pH 7 at the equivalence point. For a weak acid titrated with a strong base, the equivalence point lies above pH 7 (because the conjugate base hydrolyses), the initial pH is higher, and a buffer region appears at half-neutralization where pH equals the acid's pKa. Weak base–strong acid titrations mirror this behaviour, with the equivalence point below pH 7.

Indicators and endpoints

An indicator is chosen whose color change (endpoint) occurs close to the equivalence-point pH: phenolphthalein (colorless to pink, pH ≈ 8.2–10) suits weak acid–strong base titrations, while methyl orange (pH ≈ 3.1–4.4) suits weak base–strong acid ones. Alternatively, a pH meter can record the curve continuously, and derivatives of the curve locate the endpoint precisely.

Applications

Acid–base titrations are used to standardize solutions, determine alkalinity and acidity in water and food analysis, assay pharmaceuticals, and measure quantities such as the acid value of oils. Karl Fischer titration (for water) and redox and complexometric titrations are related volumetric methods.
