Malbec (pronounced [mal.bɛk]) is a purple grape variety used in making red wine. The grapes tend to have an inky dark colour and robust tannins, and are known as one of the six grapes allowed in the blend of red Bordeaux wine. In France it is called Côt, and it is the principal variety of the Cahors appellation.

History

Malbec originated in south-western France, where it was once widely planted in Bordeaux and the Loire. Its susceptibility to frost and mildew, and losses in the great frost of 1956, reduced French plantings. Cuttings reached Argentina in the mid-19th century, before phylloxera devastated European vineyards, and Argentina preserved pre-phylloxera clones.

Argentina

Argentina is now by far the world's leading Malbec producer, with the Mendoza region — especially the high-altitude vineyards of Luján de Cuyo and the Uco Valley at the foot of the Andes — producing the most acclaimed wines. Argentine Malbec is typically plush and fruit-forward, with flavours of blackberry, plum and violet, softer tannins than the more structured, earthy wines of Cahors. The grape is so central to Argentine identity that 17 April is celebrated as Malbec World Day.

Wine characteristics

Malbec wines are deeply coloured, full-bodied reds with notable tannins, moderate acidity and dark-fruit flavours; they pair well with grilled and roasted meats, the classic accompaniment to Argentine asado.
