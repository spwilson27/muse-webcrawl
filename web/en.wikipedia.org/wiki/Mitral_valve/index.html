The mitral valve, also known as the bicuspid valve or left atrioventricular valve, is one of the four valves of the heart. It lies between the left atrium and the left ventricle and allows blood to flow from the atrium into the ventricle while preventing backflow. Its name derives from its resemblance to a bishop's mitre.

Structure

Unlike the three-cusped tricuspid valve on the right side of the heart, the mitral valve has two leaflets, a larger anterior leaflet and a smaller posterior leaflet. The leaflets are anchored by chordae tendineae to the papillary muscles of the ventricular wall, which prevents them from prolapsing into the atrium when the ventricle contracts. The valve orifice has an area of roughly 4 to 6 square centimetres and opens during diastole.

Clinical significance

Disease of the mitral valve includes mitral stenosis — narrowing, classically caused by rheumatic fever — and mitral regurgitation, in which the valve leaks, often due to mitral valve prolapse, infective endocarditis, or dilatation of the ventricle. Severe disease can lead to heart failure and is treated medically or by surgical repair or replacement, increasingly including catheter-based procedures such as edge-to-edge repair.
