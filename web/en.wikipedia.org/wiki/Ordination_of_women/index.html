The ordination of women to ministerial or priestly office is a practice adopted in many — though not all — religious traditions. Within Christianity, most Mainline Protestant denominations ordain women, while the Roman Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church reserve priestly ordination to men; in Judaism, Reform, Conservative, and Reconstructionist movements ordain women as rabbis, while Orthodox Judaism generally does not. The question has been one of the most debated issues in modern religion.

Christianity

Women were ordained in some 19th-century movements — Antoinette Brown Blackwell was ordained by a Congregational church in 1853 — and several Pentecostal bodies ordained women early in the 20th century. Major milestones included the Church of Sweden (1958), the Episcopal Church in the United States (regularized in 1976, after the irregular ordination of the Philadelphia Eleven in 1974), and the Church of England, which ordained its first women priests in 1994 and consecrated its first woman bishop, Libby Lane, in 2015. The Catholic Church teaches, as in the 1994 apostolic letter Ordinatio sacerdotalis, that it has no authority to confer ordination on women; some Old Catholic and independent Catholic churches do so, and unofficial Roman Catholic Womenpriests ordinations since 2002 are not recognized by Rome.

Judaism

Regina Jonas was ordained privately in Germany in 1935. Sally Priesand became the first woman ordained by a rabbinical seminary in 1972 (Reform), followed by Sandy Eisenberg Sasso (Reconstructionist, 1974) and Amy Eilberg (Conservative, 1985). Orthodox debate continues, with some communities granting women advanced titles and leadership roles short of the title "rabbi".

Other traditions

In Buddhism, the ordination of nuns (bhikkhuni) died out in the Theravada tradition and has been revived controversially from the late 20th century. Hindu traditions include women priests in some movements, while in Islam women may serve as imams of women-only congregations by longstanding custom in some regions, such as China's women's mosques.

Arguments

Supporters cite the equality of believers, historical female leaders, and the needs of ministry; opponents cite scriptural injunctions and unbroken tradition. The issue remains a dividing line within and between denominations.
