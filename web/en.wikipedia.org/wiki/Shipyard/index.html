Shipyard

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A half-built ship hull surrounded by giant gantry cranes in a shipyard]
Block construction in a modern yard.

A shipyard is a place where ships are built and repaired, combining slipways or building docks, heavy cranes, fabrication shops and outfitting quays. Modern shipbuilding is concentrated in Asia: China, South Korea and Japan together build the overwhelming majority of the world\'s tonnage, with giant yards (Hyundai Samho, Samsung Geoje, CSSC\'s Shanghai and Dalian complexes) assembling ships from prefabricated blocks of thousands of tonnes.

Modern construction

Designs are built as blocks in panel lines and sub-assembly halls, then erected in a building dock and welded into a hull before launch (or float-out), outfitting alongside, trials and delivery. Cruise ships are built this way in Europe (Meyer Werft, Fincantieri, Chantiers de l\'Atlantique), one of the few segments where European yards lead.

History

Venice\'s Arsenale mass-produced galleys with standardised parts in the Middle Ages; 19th-century yards on the Clyde, Tyne and Belfast (Harland & Wolff, builders of Titanic) launched much of the world\'s fleet. Post-war competition moved the industry to Japan, then Korea, then China, hollowing out Western commercial building while naval work remained.

Repair and recycling

Repair yards with dry docks cluster at chokepoints (Singapore, Dubai, Rotterdam, Bahrain). Ship recycling (breaking) concentrates at Alang (India), Chittagong (Bangladesh) and Aliaga (Turkey), a trade criticised for safety and pollution and now regulated by the Hong Kong Convention, in force from 2025.
