Whitefish (fisheries term)

Placeholder image: whitefish as a fisheries category.

Whitefish or white fish is a fisheries term for several species of demersal fish with white flesh, particularly cod (Gadus morhua), whiting (Merlangius merlangus), hake (Merluccius merluccius), haddock (Melanogrammus aeglefinus), and pollock (Pollachius spp.), among others. It is also used for the freshwater fishes of the subfamily Coregoninae, such as lake whitefish. The term distinguishes these mostly bottom-dwelling, mild-flavoured fish from oily fish such as salmon, tuna, and mackerel.

Characteristics

White fish live on or near the seafloor (demersal), and their flesh is white and relatively low in fat, with most of the oil concentrated in the liver — cod liver oil being a well-known product. Because of their mild flavour and flaky texture, white fish are staples of dishes such as fish and chips and are among the most commercially valuable groups of fish worldwide.

Freshwater whitefish

In North America and northern Eurasia, "whitefish" also denotes the freshwater coregonines — lake whitefish (Coregonus clupeaformis), ciscoes, and European whitefish — important commercial and subsistence fishes of the Great Lakes and northern lakes and rivers. These are members of the salmon family, Salmonidae.

Fisheries

White fish fisheries were historically prosecuted across the North Atlantic and North Pacific. Many stocks, notably Atlantic cod, suffered severe declines from overfishing in the late 20th century, leading to moratoria and quota regimes, such as the 1992 Newfoundland cod moratorium.
