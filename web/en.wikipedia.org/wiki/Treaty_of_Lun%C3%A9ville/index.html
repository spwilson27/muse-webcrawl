Treaty of Lunéville

The Treaty of Lunéville was signed on 9 February 1801 in Lunéville, France, between the French Republic and Emperor Francis II, acting for both the Habsburg Monarchy and the Holy Roman Empire. It ended hostilities between France and Austria in the War of the Second Coalition, following the French victories at Marengo and Hohenlinden.

Terms

The treaty largely confirmed the earlier Treaty of Campo-Formio (1797). Austria recognized French control of the Austrian Netherlands (Belgium) and the left bank of the Rhine. France's client republics — the Batavian, Helvetic, Cisalpine, and Ligurian republics — were acknowledged. German princes dispossessed on the left bank of the Rhine were to be compensated with territory on the right bank, a process that led to the great territorial reshuffle of the Reichsdeputationshauptschluss of 1803.

Consequences

Lunéville left Britain as France's only remaining opponent in the war; the Treaty of Amiens followed in 1802, bringing a brief general peace. The territorial settlement in Germany fatally weakened the Holy Roman Empire, which was dissolved in 1806 during the War of the Third Coalition.
