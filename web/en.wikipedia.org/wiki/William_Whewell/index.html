William Whewell (24 May 1794 – 6 March 1866) was an English polymath: scientist, Anglican priest, philosopher, theologian, and historian of science. He was a Fellow and later Master of Trinity College, Cambridge, and wrote widely on subjects from tidal physics to moral philosophy and architecture. He famously coined the word "scientist" — proposed at the British Association for the Advancement of Science in 1833 and printed in 1834 — along with terms such as "physicist", "anode", "cathode", and "ion" (the latter coined in correspondence with Michael Faraday).

Early life and Cambridge career

Whewell was born in Lancaster, the son of a carpenter. He won a scholarship to Trinity College, Cambridge, graduating as second wrangler in 1816. He became a fellow and tutor of the college, professor of mineralogy (1828–1832), Knightbridge Professor of Moral Philosophy (1838–1855), and Master of Trinity in 1841, a post he held until his death. He was a central figure in the Cambridge network of science that included Charles Babbage, John Herschel, and George Peacock, and he pushed to reform mathematical education toward analysis.

History and philosophy of science

His three-volume History of the Inductive Sciences (1837) surveyed the development of the sciences from Greek times, and The Philosophy of the Inductive Sciences, Founded Upon Their History (1840) developed his theory of induction. Whewell argued that scientific progress occurs when facts are "colligated" by a new conception supplied by the mind, and emphasised consilience — when an induction drawn from one class of facts coincides with inductions from other classes, as with Newtonian gravitation. His debate with John Stuart Mill, who criticised his philosophy in A System of Logic (1843), became a classic exchange in nineteenth-century thought.

Other work

Whewell conducted extensive research on the tides, organising coordinated observations and winning the Royal Medal in 1837. He wrote on Gothic architecture, political economy, moral philosophy (The Elements of Morality, 1845), and natural theology, contributing the Bridgewater Treatise Astronomy and General Physics Considered with Reference to Natural Theology (1833). He died in 1866 from injuries sustained in a fall from his horse. Whewell's Court at Trinity College and the mineral whewellite are named after him.
