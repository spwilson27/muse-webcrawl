Mindfulness

Mindfulness is the psychological process of purposely bringing one's attention to experiences occurring in the present moment without judgment, which one develops through the practice of meditation and through other training. Mindfulness is derived from sati, a significant element of Hindu and Buddhist traditions, and is based on Zen, Vipassanā, and Tibetan meditation techniques. Though definitions and techniques vary, mindfulness has been adapted into a variety of clinical psychology and psychiatry programs since the 1970s.

Definitions

In contemporary psychology, mindfulness is commonly defined as the awareness that arises from paying attention on purpose, in the present moment, nonjudgmentally, a formulation associated with Jon Kabat-Zinn, founder of the Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) program at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center. The Buddhist term sati (Sanskrit: smṛti) is often translated as mindfulness or awareness, and originally connoted a faculty of memory and recollection as well as present-moment attention.

Practice

Formal mindfulness practice typically involves meditation exercises in which attention is anchored on an object such as the breath, bodily sensations, sounds, or a sequence of movements, with distractions noticed and allowed to pass without elaboration. Common exercises include the body scan, sitting meditation, mindful walking, and the "three-minute breathing space" used in mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT). Informal practice consists of applying the same attentive attitude to everyday activities such as eating, listening or walking.

Clinical applications and evidence

Clinical studies have documented both physical and mental health benefits of mindfulness in various patient categories, including those with chronic pain, stress, anxiety disorders and depression. MBCT is recommended in some national guidelines for people at risk of depressive relapse. Reviews note that effect sizes are generally moderate, research quality is variable, and adverse effects — while uncommon — have been reported. Mindfulness programs have also been adopted in schools, workplaces, prisons and the military.

Criticism

Critics have raised concerns about the commercialisation of mindfulness — sometimes called "McMindfulness" — arguing that the secular technique has been detached from the ethical and communal framework of its Buddhist origins and marketed as a productivity tool. Others question methodological weaknesses in the research literature, including publication bias and inconsistent definitions of the term.
