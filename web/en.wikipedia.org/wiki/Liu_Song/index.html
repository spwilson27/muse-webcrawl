The Liu Song dynasty (420–479), also known as the Song dynasty of the south to distinguish it from the later Song dynasty (960–1279), was the first of the four Southern Dynasties of China during the Northern and Southern Dynasties period. It ruled southern China from its capital at Jiankang (modern Nanjing) while the Northern Wei controlled the north.

History

The dynasty was founded by Liu Yu, a general of humble origins who rose through the Eastern Jin army, suppressed rebellions, and led successful expeditions against the Later Qin and Southern Yan. In 420 he deposed the last Jin emperor and took the throne as Emperor Wu. His reign and that of his son Emperor Wen (r. 424–453) — the Yuanjia era — were a period of relative stability, prosperity and cultural achievement, with the capital a centre of poetry and scholarship associated with figures such as Xie Lingyun and the historian Fan Ye. Emperor Wen's ambitious campaigns against the Northern Wei ended in costly failure by 450. After his assassination in 453, the dynasty descended into bloody succession struggles: the reigns of the later emperors were marked by purges of imperial princes, the civil war under Emperor Ming, and the tyranny of the boy emperor Liu Yu (the "Later Deposed Emperor"). In 479 the general Xiao Daocheng seized the throne and founded the Southern Qi, ending the Liu Song after fifty-nine years.

Culture

The period saw major developments in landscape poetry, literary criticism — Liu Xie's Wenxin Diaolong ("The Literary Mind and the Carving of Dragons") — and Buddhist and Daoist scholarship, alongside the mathematical work of Zu Chongzhi, who calculated pi to seven decimal places.
