The Peruvian Amazon Company was a British-registered rubber company, incorporated in London in 1907, which controlled the extraction of wild rubber in the Putumayo River region of the Amazon, on the disputed border between Peru and Colombia. Built on the enterprises of the Peruvian rubber baron Julio César Arana, it became internationally notorious after revelations that its agents had enslaved, tortured and murdered tens of thousands of Indigenous people — among them the Huitoto (Witoto), Bora, Andoque and Ocaina — during the Amazon rubber boom. The affair is known as the Putumayo genocide or Putumayo scandal.

The Putumayo regime

The company's stations were run on a system of debt peonage enforced by terror: Indigenous people were rounded up in raids (correrías), forced to deliver quotas of rubber, and punished by flogging, mutilation, starvation and execution for shortfalls. Barbadian overseers employed by the company later testified to systematic atrocities. Population estimates for the region fell catastrophically — from perhaps 50,000 or more to a fraction of that within a decade.

Exposure and investigation

Reports by the American engineer Walter Hardenburg, published in the London magazine Truth from 1909 ("The Devil's Paradise"), forced the British government to act: the consul Roger Casement, fresh from investigating abuses in the Congo Free State, was sent to the Putumayo in 1910. His 1911 report confirmed the atrocities in damning detail, and a House of Commons select committee examined the company in 1913. The company went into liquidation, though Arana himself was never convicted and prospered in Peru.

Legacy

The Putumayo scandal stands with the Congo atrocities as a defining scandal of the rubber boom era. It was chronicled by Casement's report and later writers, fictionalised in Mario Vargas Llosa's novel The Dream of the Celt (2010), and remains central to the historical memory of the Amazonian Indigenous peoples affected.
