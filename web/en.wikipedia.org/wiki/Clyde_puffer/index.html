Clyde puffer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Type: Small steam cargo vessel
Length: about 66 ft (20 m)
Area: Firth of Clyde and Hebrides, Scotland

A Clyde puffer is a small single-masted cargo ship, of a type that provided a vital supply link around the west coast and Hebrides of Scotland from the Victorian era to the late 20th century. The name comes from the Clyde, the river where most were built and worked, and from the "puffing" of their steam exhaust.

Design

The classic puffer was about 66 feet (20 m) long—small enough to pass through the locks of the Forth and Clyde and Crinan canals—with a blunt bow, a cargo hold amidships, the engine aft, and the crew (typically a master, engineer, and two deckhands) living in cramped quarters in the forecastle. Coal-fired steam engines of modest power drove most of them; later examples, the VIC (Victualling Inshore Craft) boats built for the Admiralty during the Second World War, became the archetype of the type. Capacity was typically around 100–150 tons.

Work

Puffers carried coal, grain, whisky, building stone, and general goods to islands and remote coastal communities that had no roads, beaching at low tide on sand or gravel when there was no pier. They were the "auld puffers" celebrated in Neil Munro's Para Handy tales about the puffer Vital Spark, later adapted for television.

Decline

Roll-on/roll-off ferries, improved roads, and container shipping displaced the puffers from the 1960s onward. A handful survive in preservation, including VIC 32 and VIC 96, offering heritage cruises on the Clyde and the Caledonian Canal.
