Sir William Crookes (17 June 1832 – 4 April 1919) was a British chemist and physicist who attended the Royal College of Chemistry, now part of Imperial College London, and worked on spectroscopy. He was a pioneer in vacuum tubes, inventing the Crookes tube, which made possible the discovery of cathode rays and ultimately of the electron. He also discovered the element thallium and invented the Crookes radiometer (the "light mill").

Early life

Crookes was born in London, the eldest of sixteen children of a prosperous tailor. He studied under August Wilhelm von Hofmann at the Royal College of Chemistry and served as his assistant before taking posts at the Radcliffe Observatory, Oxford, and in Chester.

Scientific career

In 1861, using spectroscopic analysis of selenium ores, Crookes detected a bright green emission line and announced the discovery of thallium (from the Greek thallos, a green shoot). His precision work on atomic weights led him to high-vacuum experiments. In the 1870s he built improved vacuum tubes — Crookes tubes — and showed that cathode rays travel in straight lines, cast shadows, and exert mechanical force, describing them as a "fourth state of matter" or "radiant matter". The Crookes radiometer, whose vanes rotate in sunlight, demonstrated the pressure of radiation (though its motion is in fact driven mainly by thermal effects in the residual gas).

Crookes later studied radioactivity, devising the spinthariscope (1903) to detect alpha particles, and speculated in his 1886 address on the "genesis of the elements" about a primordial substance he called protyle.

Spiritualism

From the late 1860s Crookes investigated mediumship and séances, publishing reports favourable to the mediums Florence Cook and D. D. Home that proved highly controversial and damaged his standing with some contemporaries, though he was never shown to have endorsed fraud knowingly and continued to be honoured as a scientist.

Honours and legacy

Crookes was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1863, served as its president from 1913 to 1915, was knighted in 1897 and appointed to the Order of Merit in 1910. He founded and edited the journal Chemical News for nearly fifty years.
