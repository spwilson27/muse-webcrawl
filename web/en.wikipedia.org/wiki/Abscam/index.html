Abscam

Abscam (sometimes written ABSCAM) was an FBI sting operation in the late 1970s and early 1980s in which agents, posing as representatives of a fictitious Arab sheikh, offered bribes to American public officials in exchange for political favours. The operation led to the convictions of a United States senator, five members of the House of Representatives, and several state and local officials, and it remains a landmark in debates over entrapment and investigative ethics.

The operation

The operation began in 1978 as an investigation into stolen art and securities, run with the help of convicted con artist Melvin Weinberg. The FBI created a front company, Abdul Enterprises — the source of the name "Abscam" (Abdul scam) — ostensibly owned by Sheikh Kambir Abdul Rahman, who was said to seek investment opportunities and help with immigration and money matters. Officials were recorded on videotape accepting cash in exchange for promises to introduce private immigration bills, grant casino licences in Atlantic City, or steer contracts. Among those convicted were Senator Harrison A. Williams Jr. of New Jersey, who resigned in 1982 facing expulsion, and Representatives John Jenrette, Richard Kelly, Raymond Lederer, Michael "Ozzie" Myers, Frank Thompson and John M. Murphy.

Controversy

When the investigation became public in February 1980, it provoked congressional hearings and criticism that the FBI had manufactured crimes and targeted officials without prior suspicion. Defendants raised entrapment defences, but courts largely upheld the convictions, distinguishing permissible opportunity-creation from impermissible inducement. Abscam led to new Attorney General guidelines on undercover operations, and it inspired the 2013 film American Hustle.
