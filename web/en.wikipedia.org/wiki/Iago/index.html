Iago is a fictional character in William Shakespeare's tragedy Othello (c. 1603–1604). Iago is the play's main antagonist, and Othello's standard-bearer (ensign, or "ancient"). He is the husband of Emilia, who is in turn the attendant of Othello's wife Desdemona. With 1,097 lines, Iago has more lines than Othello himself and is among the largest roles in Shakespeare.

Angered at being passed over for promotion in favour of Michael Cassio, and professing various other grievances, Iago plots revenge. He manipulates Othello into believing that Desdemona has been unfaithful with Cassio, chiefly through the planted handkerchief, and drives him to murder her. When the plot unravels, Iago kills his own wife Emilia and is arrested, refusing to explain himself: "Demand me nothing. What you know, you know. From this time forth I never will speak word."

Motives and criticism

Iago's stated motives — the lost promotion, suspicion that Othello has slept with Emilia, his own attraction to Desdemona — have struck critics as inadequate to his actions. Samuel Taylor Coleridge famously described the soliloquies as "the motive-hunting of motiveless malignity." Scholars have read Iago as a figure of the stage Machiavel, a Vice figure from morality plays, and a study in improvisational manipulation: he adapts his plans opportunistically as events unfold.

Role and reputation

Iago is considered one of the greatest villains in English literature. The role has been played by many leading actors, and the character's name has become a byword for treacherous, dissembling malice. Paradoxically, other characters repeatedly call him "honest Iago" — his reputation for blunt honesty is the instrument of his deception.
