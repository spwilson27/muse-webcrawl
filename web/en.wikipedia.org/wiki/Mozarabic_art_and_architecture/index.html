Mozarabic art refers to the art and architecture of the Mozarabs, the Christians who lived under Muslim rule in al-Andalus and of the communities they founded when they migrated north into the Christian kingdoms of León, Castile, Navarre and Aragon between the 9th and 11th centuries. Its architecture — also called the art of the Repoblación — fuses Visigothic, Asturian and Islamic (Caliphal) elements in small, distinctive churches.

Architecture

Mozarabic churches are generally small stone buildings, basilican or centralised in plan, whose interiors are divided by rows of horseshoe arches — exceeding the semicircular arch of Umayyad Córdoba — carried on columns with carved Corinthianising capitals. Rectangular apses, transverse arches, and in some examples ribbed vaults derived from the Great Mosque of Córdoba appear early: the monastery church of San Miguel de Escalada (consecrated 951) near León shows a portico of horseshoe arches, while Santa María de Lebeña in Cantabria, San Cebrián de Mazote in Valladolid, and Santiago de Peñalba and Santo Tomás de las Ollas in El Bierzo illustrate the type's variety. A defining feature is the alfiz, the rectangular frame enclosing an arch, borrowed from Islamic practice.

Illuminated manuscripts

The most famous Mozarabic art is manuscript illumination, above all the illustrated commentaries on the Apocalypse by Beatus of Liébana (the Beatus manuscripts). Copied in northern monasteries such as San Salvador de Tábara and San Miguel de Escalada in the 10th and 11th centuries, they display flat, intensely coloured figures set in banded registers, horseshoe-arched frames, and ornamental schemes mixing Visigothic interlace with Andalusi motifs. The Morgan Beatus and the Girona Beatus are celebrated examples. Mozarabic scribes also produced liturgical books in Visigothic script for the Hispanic (Mozarabic) rite.

Legacy

As Romanesque art spread from France after about 1080, Mozarabic building receded, though its horseshoe arch and decorative vocabulary survived in Mudéjar work — art produced by Muslims and their techniques under Christian rule. The term "Mozarabic" for the architecture was popularised by the historian Manuel Gómez-Moreno in his 1919 study Iglesias Mozárabes.
