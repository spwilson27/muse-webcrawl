The 35 mm format, also known as full frame, is a film and image sensor format whose frame dimensions are 36 mm by 24 mm, based on the 35 mm-wide photographic film stock developed for motion pictures in the 1890s and adopted for still photography with the Leica camera in the 1920s. For much of the twentieth century it was the dominant format for both cinema and still photography, and its dimensions live on as the reference standard for digital camera sensors and lens focal lengths.

History

The 35 mm gauge originated with Thomas Edison's Kinetoscope films, using film stock standardised in the 1890s at 35 mm wide with sprocket holes along both edges. Oskar Barnack at Leitz adapted this movie film for a compact still camera, doubling the cinema frame to produce a 24 × 36 mm still image; the resulting Leica I went on sale in 1925. Kodak's introduction of the 135 cartridge in 1934 made the format convenient and universal, and after the Second World War 35 mm became the standard format for photojournalism, amateur photography and most professional still work, displacing larger roll-film formats for most purposes.

Characteristics

On 135 film the standard frame is 36 × 24 mm, giving a 3:2 aspect ratio and a diagonal of about 43.3 mm; a lens with a focal length near this diagonal (about 50 mm) is considered a "normal" lens for the format. In cinema, 35 mm frames are smaller and vary with the projection format (Academy ratio, Super 35, anamorphic), but the gauge remains a benchmark of image quality against which digital cinema formats are compared. Some still cameras used half-frame (18 × 24 mm) or panoramic formats on the same stock.

Digital era

Digital sensors matching the 36 × 24 mm frame are marketed as "full frame", and smaller consumer sensors (such as APS-C) are described by crop factors relative to it. Focal lengths and depth-of-field behaviour quoted for lenses assume the 35 mm frame as reference, and full-frame digital cameras from the mid-2000s onward restored the format to professional dominance in still photography. Despite the decline of film, 35 mm film stock continues to be manufactured and the format remains in use by enthusiasts and some film directors.
