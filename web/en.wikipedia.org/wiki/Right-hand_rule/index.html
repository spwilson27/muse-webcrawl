Right-hand rule

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics and physics, the right-hand rule is a convention and a mnemonic for deciding the orientation of axes in three-dimensional space and for determining the direction of the cross product of two vectors, as well as the direction of the force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field.

Cross product

For two vectors a and b, the direction of a × b is given by pointing the fingers of the right hand along a, curling them toward b; the outstretched thumb then points in the direction of the cross product. Equivalently, with index finger along a and middle finger along b, the thumb gives a × b.

Rotation and angular vectors

If the fingers of the right hand curl in the direction of rotation, the thumb points along the angular velocity and angular momentum vectors. The same grip rule gives the direction of the magnetic field around a current-carrying wire: thumb along the conventional current, fingers curl in the direction of the field lines. For a coil or solenoid, the curled fingers follow the current and the thumb indicates the north pole.

Electromagnetism

Fleming's right-hand rule (for generators) assigns thumb, index and middle finger to motion, magnetic field and induced current respectively. The Lorentz force on a moving positive charge, F = qv × B, is also read off with the cross-product rule.

Coordinate systems

A right-handed coordinate system is one in which the x, y and z axes follow the right-hand rule: x̂ × ŷ = ẑ. Most of physics and mathematics uses right-handed coordinates; the choice fixes the sign conventions of cross products, curls and determinants.

History

The convention developed in the late 19th century as vector methods spread through electromagnetism, associated with the work of Oliver Heaviside and Josiah Willard Gibbs; John Ambrose Fleming popularized the hand mnemonics for electrical engineering.
