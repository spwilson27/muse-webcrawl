Histamine H2 receptor

The histamine H2 receptor is a G protein-coupled receptor that binds the neurotransmitter and hormone histamine. It is encoded in humans by the HRH2 gene, located on chromosome 5. The H2 receptor is coupled to the Gs protein, and its activation raises intracellular cyclic AMP levels, leading to protein kinase A activation.

Distribution and function

H2 receptors are expressed most notably on the parietal cells of the stomach lining, where their activation by histamine released from enterochromaffin-like cells is the principal stimulus for gastric acid secretion. They are also found on vascular smooth muscle (where they mediate vasodilation), on the heart (positive chronotropic and inotropic effects), on immune cells, and in the central nervous system.

Pharmacology

Antagonists of the H2 receptor — the H2 blockers — were a landmark development in the treatment of peptic ulcer disease and gastroesophageal reflux disease. The first clinically successful member, cimetidine, was developed in the 1970s under the direction of James Black, who shared the 1988 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in part for this work. Later agents include ranitidine, famotidine and nizatidine. Although proton pump inhibitors have largely superseded them for many indications, H2 blockers remain widely used, including for nocturnal acid suppression and mild reflux.

Agonists and antagonists

Selective H2 agonists include amthamine and histamine itself (non-selective). Besides the therapeutic antagonists, compounds such as tiotidine and zolantidine have been used in research. Activation of H2 receptors also modulates immune responses, generally suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine production in several cell types.
