Passion of Jesus

The Passion of Jesus is the short final period in the life of Jesus Christ, from his triumphal entry into Jerusalem to his crucifixion and burial, as narrated in the four canonical Gospels. The word "Passion" comes from the Latin passio, "suffering" or "enduring." In Christian theology, Jesus's suffering and death are understood as the means of humanity's salvation, and the Passion is commemorated with particular solemnity during Holy Week.

The Gospel narratives

After entering Jerusalem to acclaim, Jesus cleansed the Temple, taught in its courts, and shared a final Passover meal — the Last Supper — with his disciples, at which he instituted the Eucharist and predicted his betrayal. In the Garden of Gethsemane he prayed in anguish before being arrested, guided there by Judas Iscariot. He was tried before the Jewish council (Sanhedrin) and then before the Roman prefect Pontius Pilate, who, according to the Gospels, sentenced him to death under pressure from the crowds. Jesus was scourged, mocked, forced to carry his cross to Golgotha, and crucified between two criminals, dying after several hours. His body was laid in a rock tomb by Joseph of Arimathea.

Liturgical commemoration

The Passion is retold in church readings during Holy Week, especially on Palm Sunday and Good Friday, when some traditions chant the entire Passion narrative (from one Gospel each year in the Roman lectionary) with divided voices. Devotional practices include the Stations of the Cross, tracing fourteen episodes from the condemnation to the burial, and Tenebrae services.

In art and culture

The Passion is among the most depicted subjects in Western art and music: mystery and Passion plays (such as the Oberammergau Passion Play, performed since 1634), Bach's St Matthew Passion and St John Passion, countless altarpieces and cycles by artists such as Giotto, Duccio, and Grünewald (the Isenheim Altarpiece), and films including Mel Gibson's The Passion of the Christ (2004). The narrative's portrayal of Jewish authorities has also been the subject of historical and theological controversy, with modern churches formally repudiating the charge of collective Jewish responsibility for the crucifixion.
