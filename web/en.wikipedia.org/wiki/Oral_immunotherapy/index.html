Oral immunotherapy

Oral immunotherapy (OIT) is a medical treatment for food allergy in which a patient consumes gradually increasing amounts of the allergenic food under medical supervision, with the goal of raising the threshold that triggers an allergic reaction. It aims at desensitisation — protection from accidental exposure while dosing continues — rather than a permanent cure; lasting tolerance after stopping treatment ("sustained unresponsiveness") occurs in only a minority of patients.

Protocol

A typical protocol has three phases: an initial rapid dose escalation in clinic (from fractions of a milligram upward), a build-up phase of fortnightly up-dosing at home for months, and a maintenance phase of a fixed daily dose (e.g. 300 mg peanut protein for Palforzia) taken indefinitely. Doses are taken with food, at a consistent time, avoiding exercise and hot showers for a period afterwards because such co-factors lower reaction thresholds.

Evidence and risks

Randomised trials, notably the PALISADE trial of peanut OIT, showed about two-thirds of treated children could tolerate 600–1000 mg peanut protein versus a small fraction on placebo. Adverse reactions are common — abdominal pain, oral itching and hives during build-up, and anaphylaxis in a notable percentage; a minority develop eosinophilic oesophagitis. In January 2020 the U.S. FDA approved Palforzia for peanut allergy in patients aged 4–17 (later extended to younger children), the first drug approval for any food allergy; it is dispensed under a restricted (REMS) programme. Related approaches include sublingual (SLIT) and epicutaneous (patch) immunotherapy and adjunctive biologics such as omalizumab.
