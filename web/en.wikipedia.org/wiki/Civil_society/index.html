Civil society

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A busy public square filled with community group stalls and volunteers]
A busy public square filled with community group stalls and volunteers

Civil society is the sphere of social life between the family, the state and the market: the voluntary associations, charities, unions, churches, clubs, movements and media through which citizens organise collectively. Since its revival in the dissident politics of 1980s Eastern Europe and in Alexis de Tocqueville scholarship, the concept has been central to theories of democracy — civil society as the school of citizenship, a check on state power and the fabric of social capital.

History of the concept

For Aristotle and early modern thinkers (societas civilis) civil society meant civilised political society itself. Hegel first separated it from the state — the realm of particular interests between family and state — and Karl Marx treated it as the bourgeois sphere to be transcended. Antonio Gramsci relocated hegemony there: the trenches of consent that any ruling order must hold.

Democracy and social capital

Tocqueville marvelled at Americans' 'art of association'; Robert Putnam's Bowling Alone (2000) argued declining associational life erodes democracy's foundations. Development agencies made 'strengthening civil society' a global programme after 1989 — funding NGOs from Warsaw to Nairobi — while authoritarian governments from Russia to Egypt answered with 'foreign agent' laws and civic-space crackdowns, a contest over who legitimately speaks for society.

Critiques

Sceptics note civil society can be uncivil (extremist movements are associations too), unrepresentative (donor-driven NGOs), or a neoliberal substitute for state welfare. The digital era has moved association online, with social media both mobilising movements (the Arab Spring) and fragmenting the public sphere.
