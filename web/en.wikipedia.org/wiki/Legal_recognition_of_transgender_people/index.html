Legal recognition of transgender people

The legal recognition of transgender people concerns the processes by which a transgender person may change their name and legal gender or sex marker on official documents such as birth certificates, identity cards and passports. The requirements vary widely between jurisdictions, from self-declaration systems to regimes requiring medical treatment, surgery or court orders; some jurisdictions offer no route to recognition at all.

Models of recognition

Argentina's Gender Identity Law of 2012 was an early example of a pure self-determination model, allowing adults to amend their documents by declaration without medical or judicial approval. Similar laws have since been adopted in countries including Denmark, Ireland, Malta, Spain and Germany. Other jurisdictions historically required a diagnosis of gender dysphoria, hormone treatment or sterilisation; the European Court of Human Rights ruled in 2017 that a sterilisation requirement violates the European Convention on Human Rights.

Non-binary recognition

A number of jurisdictions also allow a third or unspecified marker (often "X") on some documents, recognising non-binary identities, and some recognise indigenous third-gender categories such as hijra in South Asia or fa'afafine in Samoa.

Effects

Recognition affects access to marriage, employment, travel, healthcare and protection from discrimination, and the mismatch between documents and presentation is associated with increased exposure to harassment and administrative barriers.
