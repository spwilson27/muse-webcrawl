Vermouth is an aromatized fortified wine flavoured with botanicals — roots, barks, flowers, seeds, herbs and spices — and traditionally with wormwood, from which its name derives (German Wermut, "wormwood"). It is drunk as an apéritif and is an essential ingredient of classic cocktails including the Martini, the Manhattan and the Negroni.

History

Infusing wine with herbs is ancient: wines flavoured with wormwood were used medicinally in antiquity, and Hippocrates is said to have prescribed them. The modern commercial product was created in Turin by Antonio Benedetto Carpano in 1786, whose sweet red vermouth was sold as an elegant café drink; houses such as Martini & Rossi and Cinzano followed in the 19th century. France developed a contrasting dry, pale style around Noilly Prat in the early 1800s. EU and US regulations require vermouth to be based on wine, with Artemisia character mandatory in the EU.

Production and styles

A neutral white-wine base is fortified with grape spirit to about 16–18% alcohol, infused or blended with a proprietary botanical extract (wormwood, and commonly gentian, quinine bark, coriander, citrus peel, cloves, chamomile and many others), and sweetened according to style. Sweet (rosso) vermouth, the original Italian style, contains up to about 15% sugar and takes its colour largely from caramel. Dry vermouth, associated with France, is pale and low in sugar (under 4%). Blanc/bianco is sweet but white; rosé styles are newer. Once opened, vermouth oxidizes and should be refrigerated.

Use

In Italy, France and Spain (la hora del vermut), vermouth is sipped neat or on ice with a twist or olive. In cocktails, dry vermouth partners gin in the Martini, while sweet vermouth partners whiskey in the Manhattan and gin and Campari in the Negroni.
