Tone mapping

Tone mapping is a technique used in image processing and computer graphics to map one set of colours to another, in order to approximate the appearance of high dynamic range (HDR) images on a medium that has a more limited dynamic range. Displays, printers and projectors all have a restricted range of luminance they can reproduce, so an HDR image captured from a real scene must be compressed — its tones remapped — before it can be viewed, while preserving the image detail and colour appearance important to appreciate the original scene content.


Tone mapping compresses high dynamic range for display

Tone mapping operators

Methods are divided into global operators, which apply the same curve to every pixel based on overall image characteristics, and local operators, which adapt the mapping according to the pixel's neighbourhood, preserving local contrast at greater computational cost. Simple global approaches use logarithmic or sigmoidal curves; the photographic tone reproduction operator of Reinhard et al. (2002) is a widely used example. In film and games, the Academy Color Encoding System (ACES) filmic curve is commonly used for its pleasing highlight roll-off.

Applications

Tone mapping is essential in HDR photography, where multiple exposures are merged into a single HDR image and then tone mapped for display; in real-time 3D rendering and video games; and in displaying HDR video formats such as HDR10 and Dolby Vision on consumer televisions. Its reverse, expanding low dynamic range content for HDR displays, is called inverse tone mapping.
