Halftone

Halftone is the reprographic technique that simulates continuous-tone imagery through the use of dots, varying either in size or in spacing, thus generating a gradient-like effect. "Halftone" can also be used to refer specifically to the image that is produced by this process. It made mass reproduction of photographs in newspapers and books practical, because printing presses can only lay down ink or leave paper blank.

History

William Fox Talbot patented the idea of photographic screens in 1852. Practical processes followed in the 1880s: Stephen Horgan printed the first halftone photograph in a newspaper (the New York Daily Graphic, 1880), Frederic Ives developed influential commercial methods, and the Levy brothers' etched glass crossline screens of the 1890s made the process standard. By the 1890s newspapers could print photographs alongside type at full press speed.

Technique

In traditional AM (amplitude-modulated) halftoning, dots sit on a regular grid; larger dots represent darker tones. Screen ruling is measured in lines per inch (lpi). Colour printing uses four halftone separations — cyan, magenta, yellow, and black (CMYK) — printed at different screen angles to avoid moiré patterns; overlapping dots form rosettes. Modern FM (frequency-modulated, or stochastic) screening varies dot spacing with fixed-size dots, and digital imaging software largely replaced photographic screens.
