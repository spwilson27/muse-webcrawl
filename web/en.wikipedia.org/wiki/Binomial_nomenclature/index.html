From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Binomial nomenclature
Introduced by: Carl Linnaeus (1735/1753)
Format: Genus + specific epithet, in Latin form, italicised

Binomial nomenclature is the formal system of naming species by a two-part Latin name: the genus name (capitalised) followed by the specific epithet (lowercase), both italicised — as in Homo sapiens for humans or Tyrannosaurus rex among the dinosaurs. The name may be followed by the authority who described the species.

The system was established by the Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus in Species Plantarum (1753) for plants and the tenth edition of Systema Naturae (1758) for animals, replacing long descriptive phrases with a stable, universal two-word label. It is governed today by international codes (for zoology, botany, bacteria and viruses) that regulate priority, type specimens and name changes when species are reclassified.

Binomials are not mere labels: the genus name embeds a hypothesis of kinship, so renaming a species records evolving understanding of evolution — a working tool from the naming of plants and algae to the pathogens of the germ theory era.
