Firewood

Firewood Form: split logs, billets, kindling
Measurement: cord, stere (cubic metre)
Moisture target: below 20% ("seasoned")
Uses: heating, cooking, campfires
Global users: roughly 2.4 billion people rely on wood fuels (estimates vary)

Firewood is any wooden material gathered and used as fuel, generally processed by cutting, splitting, and drying (seasoning) logs from trees. It is burned for heating homes, cooking, and campfires, and remains a primary energy source for billions of people worldwide, especially in rural areas of developing countries.

Preparation

Green wood contains up to half its weight in water and burns poorly; seasoning stacked wood for six months to two years reduces moisture below about 20 percent, improving heat output and reducing creosote deposition in chimneys. Hardwoods such as oak, ash, and beech are denser and burn longer, while softwoods such as pine ignite easily and are popular for kindling, though they produce more creosote.

Measurement

Firewood is commonly sold by the cord, a stacked volume of 128 cubic feet (3.62 m³) in North America, or by the stere (one cubic metre of stacked wood) in countries using the metric system.

Environmental and health issues

Unsustainable harvesting contributes to deforestation, and smoke from inefficient stoves is a significant source of indoor and outdoor air pollution. Modern certified wood stoves and pellet fuels improve efficiency and reduce particulate emissions.
