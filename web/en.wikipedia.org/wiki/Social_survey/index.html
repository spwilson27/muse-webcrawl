Social survey

A social survey is a method of social research that collects standardised information from a sample of people, typically by questionnaire or structured interview, in order to describe a population or test relationships between variables.

History

Early social surveys documented urban poverty: Charles Booth's Life and Labour of the People in London (1889–1903) and Seebohm Rowntree's study of York (1901) combined household questionnaires with observation, mapping poverty street by street. In the 20th century, probability sampling, developed by Jerzy Neyman and others, put survey inference on a statistical footing, and surveys became central tools of governments, market research and academic sociology.

Design

Key components include defining a target population, drawing a sample (ideally a random one), designing valid and reliable questions, choosing a mode of administration (face-to-face, telephone, mail or web), and weighting responses to correct for non-response. Major recurring surveys include the US General Social Survey (since 1972), the British Social Attitudes survey, and Eurobarometer.

Limitations

Surveys are subject to sampling error, non-response bias, social desirability effects and question-wording effects, and they capture attitudes at a moment in time rather than behaviour observed directly.
