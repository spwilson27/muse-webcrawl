Parole

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Parole is the conditional release of a prisoner before the completion of their sentence, on the condition that they comply with certain requirements (reporting to a parole officer, residence and employment conditions, no new offences) and remain liable to recall to prison for violations. Originating in 19th-century penal reform (Maconochie's "mark system" on Norfolk Island, Crofton's Irish system, and the ticket-of-leave), parole institutionalised the idea that release should be earned and supervised.

Operation and debate

Parole is granted by parole boards or, in some systems, automatically at fixed fractions of the sentence; determinate-sentencing and truth-in-sentencing reforms in the US (and the federal system's 1984 abolition of parole) curtailed discretionary release, while indeterminate and life sentences still hinge on it — including the agonising politics of life-sentence ("lifer") hearings. Evidence associates well-run parole and re-entry support with lower recidivism than unsupervised release at sentence expiry, but high-profile crimes by parolees repeatedly provoke restrictive backlash. Probation — supervision instead of custody from the outset — is the system's twin.
