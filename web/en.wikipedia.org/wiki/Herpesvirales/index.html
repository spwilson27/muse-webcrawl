Herpesvirales

The Herpesvirales is an order of large double-stranded DNA viruses that infect animals, including humans. Members share a distinctive virion architecture: a linear dsDNA genome packed in an icosahedral capsid, surrounded by a protein layer called the tegument and a lipid envelope bearing glycoprotein spikes. A hallmark of the group is the ability to establish latent infection in the host for life.

Taxonomy

The order contains three families: the Orthoherpesviridae (the herpesviruses proper, infecting mammals, birds and reptiles), the Alloherpesviridae (fish and frogs), and the Malacoherpesviridae (bivalve molluscs). Nine herpesvirus types are known to infect humans primarily, designated HHV-1 through HHV-8.

Human herpesviruses

These include herpes simplex viruses 1 and 2, varicella zoster virus (chickenpox and shingles), Epstein–Barr virus (infectious mononucleosis), cytomegalovirus, the roseola viruses HHV-6 and HHV-7, and Kaposi's sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (HHV-8). Most adults worldwide carry at least one of them.

Replication and latency

During lytic replication the genome is transcribed in a cascade of immediate-early, early and late genes, and new capsids assemble in the nucleus. Latency is maintained in cell types characteristic of each virus — neurons for the simplex viruses, B lymphocytes for Epstein–Barr virus — with the genome persisting as an episome until reactivation.
