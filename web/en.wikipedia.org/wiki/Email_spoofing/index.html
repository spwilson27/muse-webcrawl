Email spoofing

Email spoofing is the creation of email messages with a forged sender address. Because the core email protocol, SMTP, has no built-in authentication, it is trivial to send mail that appears to come from any address; early mail systems assumed a trusted network. Spoofing is used in spam and phishing campaigns, in business email compromise fraud, and to disguise the origin of messages.

How it works

A spoofer connects to a mail server (or compromises one) and supplies arbitrary values for the envelope sender and the From header displayed by mail clients. The recipient's client typically shows the forged display name and address, so a phishing message can convincingly impersonate a bank, an employer or a colleague. Related techniques include using look-alike domains (homoglyph and typosquatting attacks) and compromising legitimate accounts.

Countermeasures

Three widely deployed DNS-based standards authenticate sending domains: SPF (Sender Policy Framework), which lists authorised sending servers; DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail), which cryptographically signs messages; and DMARC, which builds on both, telling receivers how to handle mail that fails authentication and providing reporting. DMARC policies of "reject" substantially block exact-domain spoofing, though they cannot stop look-alike domains. Receiver-side filtering, user education and display warnings against external senders provide further defence in depth.
