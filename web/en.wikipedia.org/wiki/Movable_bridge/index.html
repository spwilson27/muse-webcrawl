Movable bridge

A movable bridge, or moveable bridge (commonly known as a drawbridge in the broad sense), is a bridge that moves to allow passage for boats or barges beneath it. Such bridges are used where a fixed span high enough for navigation would be impractical or too costly, typically on low-lying waterways.

Types

Common types include the bascule bridge, whose span rises on a hinge like a seesaw (London's Tower Bridge is a famous example); the vertical lift bridge, whose deck rises between two towers; the swing bridge, which pivots horizontally on a central pier; the retractable bridge, which slides back over the bank; and submersible, folding, and tilt bridges in rarer applications.

Operation

Movable bridges are operated by bridge tenders according to schedules or on request from vessel traffic, balancing the needs of road or rail users against navigation rights, which in many jurisdictions take legal priority. Modern spans are powered by electric or hydraulic machinery, while historic examples used steam, counterweights, or manual labor.

Advantages and disadvantages

Compared with high fixed bridges, movable bridges require shorter approach ramps and can be less expensive, but they need machinery, staffing, and maintenance, and they interrupt surface traffic whenever they open.
