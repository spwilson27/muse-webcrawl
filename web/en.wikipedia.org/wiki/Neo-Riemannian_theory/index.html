Neo-Riemannian theory

Neo-Riemannian theory is a body of ideas in music theory that describes harmonic relationships among triads in terms of parsimonious voice-leading transformations. It takes its name from the 19th-century theorist Hugo Riemann, though its modern form was developed from the 1980s by theorists such as David Lewin, Brian Hyer, Richard Cohn and Dmitri Tymoczko.

PLR transformations

The core of the theory is a set of operations on major and minor triads:

  • P (Parallel) — changes a triad to its parallel major or minor (e.g. C major ↔ C minor), moving one note by semitone.
  • L (Leading-tone exchange) — e.g. C major ↔ E minor, moving one note by semitone.
  • R (Relative) — e.g. C major ↔ A minor, moving one note by whole tone.

Combinations of P, L and R connect any major triad to any other triad, and the transformations form a group that acts on the set of 24 major and minor triads. The operations can be visualised on Riemann's Tonnetz, a triangular lattice of pitches.

Applications

Neo-Riemannian analysis is especially valued for chromatic music of the late Romantic era (Wagner, Liszt, Mahler) and for film music, where smooth voice leading often links triads that are remote in conventional functional harmony, such as the "hexatonic" cycles produced by alternating P and L.
