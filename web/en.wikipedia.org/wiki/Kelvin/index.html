Kelvin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A block of ice melting at a measured temperature, illustrating the absolute temperature scale]
A block of ice melting at a measured temperature, illustrating the absolute temperature scale

The kelvin, symbol K, is the base unit of temperature in the International System of Units (SI). It is an absolute scale whose zero point, Absolute zero, is the theoretical state in which particles have minimal thermal motion. Since the 2019 SI redefinition, the kelvin is defined by fixing the value of the Boltzmann constant. One kelvin has the same magnitude as one degree Celsius; water freezes at 273.15 K and boils at 373.15 K at standard pressure.

History

The scale was proposed by William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, in 1848, building on the gas laws' extrapolated zero of volume and on Carnot's thermodynamics. For most of the twentieth century the kelvin was defined by the triple point of water (273.16 K exactly). The unit is written without the word 'degree' (a usage formalised in 1968).

Use

The kelvin is standard in science — stellar temperatures, cryogenics (liquid helium at 4.2 K), the 2.725 K of the cosmic microwave background — and in engineering colour temperature for lighting and photography. Conversions: K = °C + 273.15; the Fahrenheit scale relates by K = (°F + 459.67) × 5⁄9.

Absolute zero

Absolute zero cannot be reached in finite steps (third law of thermodynamics), but laser and evaporative cooling have brought atomic gases within billionths of a kelvin of it, creating Bose–Einstein condensates and other exotic states.
