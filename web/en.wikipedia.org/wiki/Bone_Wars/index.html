Bone Wars

The Bone Wars, also known as the Great Dinosaur Rush, was a period of intense and ruthlessly competitive fossil hunting and discovery during the Gilded Age of American history, marked by a heated rivalry between the palaeontologists Edward Drinker Cope of the Academy of Natural Sciences in Philadelphia and Othniel Charles Marsh of the Peabody Museum at Yale. Between 1877 and 1892 the two men used their fortunes and influence to out-collect each other in the fossil beds of Colorado, Wyoming and Nebraska.

Background

Cope and Marsh began as friends in the 1860s, even naming species after each other. Relations collapsed after Cope reconstructed the plesiosaur Elasmosaurus with its head on the wrong end — an error Marsh publicised gleefully — and after Marsh bribed quarrymen to divert fossils from Cope's sites. The rivalry became open warfare in 1877 when rich dinosaur beds were found at Garden Park, Colorado, Morrison, Colorado, and Como Bluff, Wyoming.

The feud

Each man employed teams of collectors who raced to excavate, crate and ship bones east by rail, sometimes spying on, sabotaging or allegedly dynamiting the other's digs to keep fossils out of rival hands. Both rushed descriptions into print, generating a flood of new names — including Allosaurus, Apatosaurus, Stegosaurus, Triceratops, Diplodocus and Coelophysis — along with many errors and synonyms. The feud spilled into the newspapers in 1890 in a bitter public exchange of accusations.

Outcome and legacy

Between them, Cope and Marsh described more than 130 new dinosaur species and assembled collections that stocked America's great museums. But the struggle ruined both men financially and professionally: Cope died in 1897 having sold much of his collection, and Marsh, stripped of his government survey funding, died in 1899. Scientifically, the Bone Wars transformed vertebrate palaeontology, vastly expanding knowledge of dinosaurs, mammals and other Mesozoic life — and left a cautionary tale of priority disputes whose naming tangle (such as the Apatosaurus/Brontosaurus question) persists today.
