Bulbous bow

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: A ship in dry dock showing the protruding bulbous bow below the waterline]
A bulbous bow visible with the ship in dry dock

A bulbous bow is a rounded, protruding bulb at the front (bow) of a ship, located just below the waterline. The bulb modifies the way water flows around the hull, reducing drag and thereby increasing speed, range, and fuel efficiency. Large ships with bulbous bows generally gain an efficiency advantage of about 12 to 15 percent over comparable vessels without them, and the feature is now standard on most large cargo ships, tankers, and many naval vessels.

How it works

A ship moving through water creates a bow wave, which costs energy to sustain. The bulbous bow generates its own wave system in front of the hull's bow wave. When the two systems are properly phased, the trough of the bulb's wave coincides with the crest of the bow wave, and they partially cancel — a phenomenon known as destructive interference. The result is lower wave-making resistance, which is a major component of total drag at cruising speeds for large displacement vessels.

History

The concept emerged from experiments in the early 20th century and is associated particularly with the work of naval architects such as David W. Taylor of the United States Navy. Bulbous bows were fitted to some warships in the 1920s and 1930s — famously, the Japanese battleship Yamato benefited from extensive model testing of bulb designs — but they became widespread on merchant ships only after the Second World War, when rising fuel costs made efficiency gains decisive.

Design considerations

A bulb is most effective near the ship's design speed and draft; a bulb optimised for full load may be less effective in ballast or at slower speeds, a concern that has grown with slow steaming. Bulbs can complicate anchoring arrangements, are vulnerable in collisions and groundings, and must be maintained against fouling like the rest of the hull. Some modern designs use alternative forms, and the benefit is smaller for very fast or very slow vessels.
