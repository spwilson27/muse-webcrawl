Andrew Moray (c. 1270 – 1297), also known as Andrew de Moray or Andrew Murray, was a Scottish military leader during the First War of Scottish Independence. With William Wallace, he co-commanded the Scottish army that defeated the English at the Battle of Stirling Bridge on 11 September 1297. Moray was mortally wounded in the battle and died shortly afterwards; his role was long overshadowed by Wallace's fame, though modern historians regard him as at least an equal partner in the victory.

Background

Moray was the son of Sir Andrew Moray of Petty, a prominent landowner in the north of Scotland whose estates lay in Moray and around Inverness. The younger Andrew was captured fighting the English at the Battle of Dunbar in 1296 and imprisoned in Chester Castle. He escaped during the winter of 1296–97 and returned to Scotland.

The rising of 1297

In May 1297 Moray raised the banner of revolt at Avoch Castle on the Black Isle, in defiance of the English occupation. His rising drew rapid support across the north: the burghs of the Moray coast and the men of the province flocked to him. Rather than confronting the English garrisons directly, he waged a guerrilla campaign, retaking castles and harrying English forces until, by the autumn, virtually all of Scotland north of the Forth was in Scottish hands.

Stirling Bridge and death

In September 1297 Moray joined forces with William Wallace, who led a parallel rising in the south. Their combined army took position north of the River Forth at Stirling, where on 11 September they destroyed the English army of the Earl of Surrey as it crossed the narrow bridge. Moray was badly wounded in the fighting. Documents continued to be issued in the joint names of Moray and Wallace as "commanders of the army of the Kingdom of Scotland" into the autumn, but Moray died of his wounds before the end of 1297.

Legacy

Because Wallace survived and Moray did not, popular memory long assigned Stirling Bridge to Wallace alone. A plaque at Stirling Bridge and recent scholarship have restored Moray's standing as a figure of comparable importance in the Scottish war effort.
