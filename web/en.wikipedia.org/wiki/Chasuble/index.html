The chasuble is the outermost liturgical vestment worn by priests and bishops when celebrating the Eucharist in the Roman Catholic, Anglo-Catholic, and some Lutheran traditions. It is a sleeveless, poncho-like garment, worn over the alb and stole, and is traditionally colored according to the liturgical season or feast being celebrated.

History

The chasuble derives from the casula, a hooded traveling cloak of the late Roman world — the name means "little house," as the garment enveloped the wearer. Retained as ceremonial dress while secular fashion changed, it developed into a full, nearly circular conical vestment in the early Middle Ages. From the later Middle Ages, stiffened and embroidered chasubles were cut away at the sides for ease of movement, eventually producing the highly truncated baroque "Roman" or "fiddleback" form. The liturgical movement of the 20th century revived the fuller "Gothic" shape, which became standard after the Second Vatican Council.

Use and symbolism

The chasuble is put on with a prayer asking that the wearer be clothed with the "yoke of Christ" and charity; it is traditionally interpreted as symbolizing charity covering all things. Its color follows the liturgical calendar — white for feasts, red for martyrs and the Holy Spirit, green for Ordinary Time, violet for penitential seasons, and black or violet for requiems in older usage. Eastern Orthodox and Eastern Catholic priests wear the analogous phelonion.
