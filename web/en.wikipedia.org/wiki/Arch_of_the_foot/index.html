Arches of the foot

The medial longitudinal arch, the most prominent arch of the foot.

The arches of the foot, formed by the tarsal and metatarsal bones and strengthened by ligaments and tendons, allow the foot to support the weight of the body in the erect posture with the least expenditure of material. They act as a spring, bearing the weight of the body and absorbing the shocks produced in walking, running, and jumping. The foot has three arches: two longitudinal (medial and lateral) and one transverse.

The longitudinal arches

The medial longitudinal arch is the highest and most important of the three. It is formed by the calcaneus, talus, navicular, the three cuneiform bones, and the first three metatarsals, and is supported by the plantar fascia (plantar aponeurosis), the spring ligament, and muscles including tibialis posterior. The lateral longitudinal arch, much lower and flatter, rests on the ground during standing and is formed by the calcaneus, cuboid, and the fourth and fifth metatarsals.

The transverse arch

The transverse arch runs across the foot at the level of the tarsometatarsal joints and is formed by the cuboid, the cuneiforms, and the bases of the metatarsals.

Clinical significance

Weakening of the arches produces flat feet (pes planus), while an abnormally high medial arch is called pes cavus. Inflammation of the plantar fascia (plantar fasciitis) is a common cause of heel pain.
