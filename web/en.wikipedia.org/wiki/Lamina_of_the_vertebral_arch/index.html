The lamina of the vertebral arch (Latin: lamina arcus vertebrae) is one of the two broad, flattened plates of bone that form the posterior part of the vertebral (neural) arch of a vertebra. The two laminae extend backwards and medially from the pedicles and meet in the midline, where they fuse and give rise to the spinous process. Together with the pedicles, the laminae complete the vertebral foramen, enclosing and protecting the spinal cord.

Structure

Each lamina is a broad, strong plate directed backwards and medialwards from the posterior end of a pedicle. In the thoracic region the laminae are broad, thick and imbricated, overlapping the lamina of the vertebra below like shingles on a roof. The ligamentum flavum connects the laminae of adjacent vertebrae, attaching to their anterior surfaces and helping to maintain the upright posture and the integrity of the spinal canal.

Clinical significance

The laminae are of major surgical importance. In a laminectomy, part or all of one or more laminae is removed to gain access to the spinal canal, most commonly to decompress the spinal cord or nerve roots in conditions such as spinal stenosis, disc herniation or tumour. A laminotomy removes only a portion of the lamina, while laminoplasty reshapes and repositions the lamina rather than removing it, with the aim of preserving the posterior elements of the spine.
