Ochronosis

Ochronosis
SpecialtyMedical genetics, rheumatology
CauseAccumulation of homogentisic acid (in alkaptonuria); also exogenous agents
Associated conditionAlkaptonuria

Ochronosis is the bluish-black discoloration and degeneration of connective tissues — particularly cartilage, skin and the sclera of the eye — caused by the deposition of an ochre-like pigment derived from homogentisic acid. The term was coined by Rudolf Virchow in 1866, from the Greek ōchros (pale yellow), because the tissue appears yellowish under the microscope despite looking dark to the naked eye.

Causes

Endogenous ochronosis is the hallmark of alkaptonuria, an autosomal recessive disorder of tyrosine metabolism caused by deficiency of the enzyme homogentisate 1,2-dioxygenase. Homogentisic acid accumulates and polymerises in cartilage, leading to dark, brittle cartilage, early destructive arthritis of the spine and large joints, dark urine on standing, and pigment in the ears and eyes. Exogenous ochronosis is a localised skin pigmentation caused by prolonged topical application of agents such as hydroquinone skin-lightening creams, phenol, or certain antimalarials.

Treatment

There is no definitive cure for endogenous ochronosis. Management includes dietary restriction of tyrosine and phenylalanine, vitamin C, physical therapy, and joint replacement for advanced arthropathy; the drug nitisinone, which reduces homogentisic acid production, has been approved for alkaptonuria in some jurisdictions. Exogenous ochronosis is managed by stopping the offending agent.
