Shekhinah

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Shekhinah (Hebrew: שְׁכִינָה‎, Šəḵīnā, lit. "dwelling" or "settling") is the English transliteration of a Hebrew word denoting the dwelling or settling of the divine presence of God in the world. Although the word itself does not appear in the Hebrew Bible, the concept is central to rabbinic Judaism, where the Shekhinah is God's immanent presence — manifested, for example, above the Ark of the Covenant in the Tabernacle and Temple, among those who study Torah, and in exile with the people of Israel. In later Jewish mysticism, particularly Kabbalah, the Shekhinah is personified as the feminine aspect of God.

In rabbinic literature

The rabbis of the Talmud and Midrash used Shekhinah as a way of speaking about God's nearness without compromising divine transcendence. The Shekhinah rested in the Tabernacle and in the Temple in Jerusalem; after the destruction, it was said to accompany Israel into exile. Classical sayings include "Wherever ten are gathered for prayer, the Shekhinah is present", "the Shekhinah rests above the sick person's bed", and the notion that the Shekhinah dwells between husband and wife when their marriage is worthy. The word is grammatically feminine in Hebrew, which later theology developed further.

In Kabbalah

In the Kabbalistic system of the ten sefirot, the Shekhinah is identified with Malkuth, the tenth and lowest sefirah, the receptacle of the divine flow and the point of contact between God and creation. She is also associated with the Community of Israel (Knesset Yisrael), the moon, the Sabbath Queen and the Bride. Kabbalistic teaching holds that exile and sin separate the Shekhinah from the Holy One, blessed be He (identified with Tiferet), and that the performance of the commandments (mitzvot) effects their reunification (yihud), contributing to the repair of the world (tikkun).

In other traditions

The concept has been compared with the Arabic sakīna (سكينة), the tranquillity sent down by God mentioned in the Qur'an, a word probably related to the Hebrew. Some Christian theologians and feminist theologians have drawn on the Shekhinah in discussions of God's immanence and feminine imagery for the divine.
