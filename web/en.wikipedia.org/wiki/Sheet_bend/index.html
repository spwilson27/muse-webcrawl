The sheet bend (also known as becket bend, weaver's knot and weaver's hitch) is a bend knot used to join two ropes together. Doubled, it is the double sheet bend. It is especially useful for joining ropes of different sizes or stiffness, where it is more secure than a square knot; it is nonetheless a relatively insecure knot unless the free ends are seized or the double version is used.

History

As a bend, the sheet bend is mentioned in The Young Sea Officer's Sheet Anchor by Darcy Lever (1819) although it was known to neolithic peoples for tying together fishing nets. The name "sheet bend" derives from its use of bending (tying) a sheet — a rope controlling the lower corner of a sail — to a becket on the clew of the sail.

Method

Form a bight (loop) in the end of the thicker rope. Pass the working end of the thinner rope up through the bight, around behind both parts of the bight, and back under itself. For the double sheet bend, take a second turn around the bight before tucking. Dress the knot and pull it tight.

Use

The sheet bend may be used to join lines of unequal size, to attach a line to an eye (a becket), or — as the weaver's knot — to join threads in weaving and net-making. It can spill if one of the free ends is tugged when unloaded, so it is not recommended for critical loads.
