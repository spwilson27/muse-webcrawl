Raw files record unprocessed data directly from the camera's image sensor.

A camera raw image file contains unprocessed data from the image sensor of a digital camera, image scanner, or motion-picture film scanner. Raw files are so named because they are not yet processed and converted into a printable or editable format; they function as a digital equivalent of a photographic negative. Each camera manufacturer uses its own, usually proprietary, format — Canon's CR2/CR3, Nikon's NEF, Sony's ARW, Olympus/OM System's ORF, and Panasonic's RW2, among many others.

Characteristics

A raw file stores the sensor's photosite values — typically 12- or 14-bit data behind a Bayer color filter array — together with metadata such as exposure settings, white balance, and lens information. Unlike a JPEG, a raw file has not been demosaiced, sharpened, noise-reduced, or gamma-corrected in camera, and its white balance and exposure can be adjusted non-destructively in software such as Adobe Lightroom, Capture One, or the open-source darktable and RawTherapee. The costs are much larger file sizes and the need for conversion before images can be printed or shared.

DNG and standardization

The proliferation of incompatible proprietary formats raised fears that older raw files would become unreadable as software support lapsed. In 2004 Adobe introduced the Digital Negative (DNG) format as an open, documented alternative, and some manufacturers (Leica, Pentax/Ricoh, Samsung, Hasselblad) adopted it natively. Adobe provides a free converter that translates most proprietary raw files into DNG. A related standardized format, TIFF/EP (ISO 12234-2), underlies the internal structure of many raw formats but is rarely exposed directly to users.
