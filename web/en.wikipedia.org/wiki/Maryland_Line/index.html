Maryland Line

The Maryland Line was the Continental Army designation for the quota of troops furnished by the state of Maryland during the American Revolutionary War. Maryland's regiments won a reputation as some of the finest in the army, and tradition credits them with earning Maryland its nickname, the "Old Line State."

Service

Maryland first organised an independent battalion under Colonel William Smallwood in early 1776. At the Battle of Long Island in August 1776, Maryland troops — famously the "Maryland 400" — repeatedly charged British positions at the Old Stone House to cover the American retreat, suffering devastating losses and winning immortal fame.

The Maryland Line was reorganised into seven regiments in the Continental establishment of 1776–77 and served through the middle-state campaigns, including Brandywine, Germantown, and Monmouth. In 1780 the line went south, where it was shattered at the Battle of Camden but rebuilt to fight with distinction at Cowpens, Guilford Courthouse, Hobkirk's Hill, and Eutaw Springs under commanders including Otho Holland Williams and John Eager Howard. Maryland units remained in service until the army was disbanded in 1783.

"Old Line State"

Maryland tradition holds that George Washington praised the steadiness of his Maryland regiments and called Maryland the "Old Line," giving rise to the state nickname "Old Line State," commemorated in the state song and in the name of the modern Maryland Army National Guard's 175th Infantry Regiment, which perpetuates the line's lineage.
