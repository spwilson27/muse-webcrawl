Moveable feast

A moveable feast is an observance in a Christian liturgical calendar whose date varies from year to year, as opposed to a fixed feast that falls on the same calendar date annually (such as Christmas on 25 December). Most moveable feasts are determined by the date of Easter, whose computation — the computus — is based on the lunar cycle: in Western Christianity, Easter falls on the first Sunday after the first ecclesiastical full moon on or after 21 March.

Feasts depending on Easter

A large part of the liturgical year is anchored to Easter. Before it come Septuagesima and the pre-Lenten Sundays, Shrove Tuesday (Mardi Gras), Ash Wednesday (46 days before Easter) and the whole of Lent, including Palm Sunday and Holy Week. After Easter follow the Easter octave, Ascension Day (39 days after Easter), Pentecost (49 days after) and Trinity Sunday, as well as Corpus Christi in the Roman Catholic tradition. Because Easter can fall between 22 March and 25 April in the Gregorian calendar, these feasts shift across a five-week range each year.

Other moveable observances

Not all moveable feasts depend on Easter. In the Western calendar, Advent Sunday is determined by its proximity to 30 November, and feasts such as Christ the King are placed relative to it; the weekdays of Advent govern others. Eastern Orthodox usage follows a similar Easter-based cycle, but because the Julian calendar is used for the computus in most Orthodox churches, the dates frequently differ from Western ones. Beyond the Christian calendar, the term is sometimes applied by analogy to holidays in lunisolar or lunar calendars, such as the Jewish Passover, the Islamic Ramadan, and various Hindu festivals, which drift against the solar year.

Cultural usage

The phrase took on a famous extended meaning through Ernest Hemingway's posthumously published Paris memoir A Moveable Feast (1964), in which the experience of Paris in one's youth is "a moveable feast" carried within a person wherever they go.
