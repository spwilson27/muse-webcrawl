Tunguska event

Date30 June 1908
LocationPodkamennaya Tunguska River, Siberia, Russian Empire
CauseAir burst of an asteroid or comet fragment
EnergyEstimated 3–15 megatons of TNT
Effects~80 million trees flattened over 2,150 km²

The Tunguska event was a massive explosion that occurred near the Podkamennaya Tunguska River in Siberia, Russia, on the morning of 30 June 1908. The explosion flattened an estimated 80 million trees over an area of about 2,150 square kilometres (830 sq mi) of taiga forest. It is attributed to the air burst of a stony asteroid (or possibly a comet fragment) some 50–60 metres across, which disintegrated 5–10 kilometres above the surface, releasing energy equivalent to several megatons of TNT — roughly a thousand times the yield of the Hiroshima bomb.

The event

At about 7:17 a.m. local time, witnesses across the region — including Evenki hunters and Russian settlers — reported a brilliant fireball crossing the sky, a column of fire, and a series of thunderous blows that knocked people off their feet hundreds of kilometres away. Seismic stations across Eurasia recorded the shock, and barographs in England registered the atmospheric pressure wave, which circled the globe twice. For several nights afterwards, the skies over Europe and Asia glowed brightly enough to read by at midnight, an effect attributed to high-altitude dust and ice.

Investigation

Because of the remoteness of the site and the turmoil of war and revolution, the first scientific expedition, led by mineralogist Leonid Kulik, reached the epicentre only in 1927. Kulik found a vast "butterfly" pattern of radially felled trees, with trunks at the centre left standing but stripped of branches, yet — surprisingly — no impact crater and no recoverable meteorite mass. Later expeditions recovered microscopic silicate and magnetite spheres consistent with an extraterrestrial body. The small Cheko lake near the epicentre was proposed in 2007 as a possible crater, a claim that remains disputed.

Significance

The Tunguska event is the largest impact-related event in recorded history. Although reported deaths were few because the area was almost uninhabited, an equivalent air burst over a city would be catastrophic; the 2013 Chelyabinsk meteor, far smaller, injured about 1,500 people. Tunguska is a central reference case for planetary defense and for the assessment of airburst hazards in modern risk studies.
