Schmaltz

Schmaltz (also spelled schmalz or shmalts; Yiddish: שמאָלץ) is rendered (clarified) chicken or goose fat used for frying or as a spread in Ashkenazi Jewish cuisine. The word comes from the German Schmalz, meaning rendered animal fat generally. Schmaltz is a staple of traditional Jewish cooking, developed in part because Jewish dietary law forbids cooking meat in butter, and because olive oil was scarce in northern and eastern Europe.

Preparation and use

Schmaltz is made by cutting chicken or goose fat and skin into small pieces and rendering them slowly, often with onion. The liquid fat is strained and solidifies when cool into a pale, savoury shortening used to fry latkes and potatoes, enrich kasha and chopped liver, flavour matzah balls and roast vegetables, or spread on bread with salt. The crispy browned bits of skin and onion left from rendering are gribenes, eaten as a snack or garnish.

In culture

Because schmaltz connotes richness and excess, the adjective schmaltzy entered English slang for excessively sentimental art, music or writing. The fat itself fell out of favour in the late 20th century over cholesterol concerns but has returned with renewed interest in traditional and nose-to-tail cooking.
