Pedometer

A pedometer, or step-counter, is a device, usually portable and electronic or electromechanical, that counts each step a person takes by detecting the motion of the person's hands or hips. Because the distance of each step varies, an informal calibration, performed by the user, is required if presentation of the distance covered is desired, though most pedometers now automatically calculate distance using an entered stride length.

History

Leonardo da Vinci sketched a mechanical pedometer for military use. Practical devices appeared in the 18th century: the Swiss watchmaker Abraham-Louis Perrelet built a self-winding watch mechanism with a pedometer in 1770, and Thomas Jefferson reportedly used a pedometer made in France. In Japan, the manpo-kei ("ten-thousand-step meter") was marketed from 1965 by Y. Hatano, popularising the goal of 10,000 steps per day — a target later adopted worldwide, though researchers note it began as marketing rather than science.

How it works

Traditional pedometers use a horizontal pendulum or a ball-bearing switch that closes a contact with each step. Modern electronic versions use piezoelectric sensors or microelectromechanical (MEMS) accelerometers, often counting motion along two or three axes and filtering out non-step movements. Step counting is now built into most smartphones and fitness trackers.

Use and accuracy

Pedometers are used as motivational tools in public-health and workplace wellness programmes. Accuracy is typically within a few per cent at normal walking speeds but declines at slow speeds and in people with shuffling gaits; waist-worn devices are generally more accurate than wrist-worn ones. Studies suggest that simply wearing a pedometer can increase physical activity by roughly 2,000 steps per day.
