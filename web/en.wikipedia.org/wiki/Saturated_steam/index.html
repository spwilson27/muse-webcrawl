Saturated steam

Saturated steam is water vapour that exists at the saturation temperature corresponding to its pressure — the boiling point at that pressure — so that liquid water and vapour can coexist in equilibrium. If saturated steam is cooled or compressed slightly, some of it condenses; if it is heated at constant pressure beyond saturation, it becomes superheated steam. Steam that contains suspended liquid droplets is called wet steam; saturated steam with no liquid present is dry saturated steam.

Properties

For each pressure there is a unique saturation temperature: at standard atmospheric pressure this is 100 °C; at 10 bar absolute it is about 180 °C. Engineering steam tables tabulate, for each pressure, the specific volume, enthalpy and entropy of saturated liquid and dry saturated vapour, and the latent heat of vaporisation that must be supplied to convert one into the other. The proportion of vapour in a wet mixture is expressed as its dryness fraction (steam quality).

Applications

Saturated steam is widely used for process heating, sterilisation (autoclaves), humidification, district heating and food processing because condensing steam releases large amounts of latent heat at a constant, controllable temperature. Superheated steam is preferred for driving turbines, where moisture would damage blades. In steam locomotive and boiler practice, managing saturation, priming and carryover of water into cylinders was a central engineering concern.
