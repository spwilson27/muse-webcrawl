Bumper (automobile)

A modern car bumper assembly

A bumper is a structure attached to or integrated with the front and rear ends of a motor vehicle, designed to absorb impact in a minor collision, ideally minimising repair costs, and to reduce injury to pedestrians struck by the vehicle. Bumpers do not protect occupants in high-speed crashes, but are engineered, together with crumple zones, as part of the vehicle's overall impact-absorption system.

Construction

Modern bumper systems consist of a plastic outer cover (fascia), often painted to match the body; a foam or plastic honeycomb energy absorber beneath it; and a rigid reinforcement beam, usually steel or aluminium, bolted to the vehicle's rails. This layered design sacrifices the cheap cover and absorber in a low-speed impact while protecting expensive components such as headlights, the radiator and body panels.

Regulation

In the United States, federal bumper standards introduced in the 1970s required cars to survive 5 mph impacts without damage to safety systems, leading in the 1970s and 1980s to heavy, protruding "5 mph bumpers"; the standard was later relaxed to 2.5 mph. European and international regulations emphasise pedestrian protection, requiring softer, higher bumper shapes that reduce leg injuries. Standards differ between jurisdictions, so bumper designs vary by market.

History

Early automobiles had simple spring-mounted bars. Chrome-plated steel bumpers became styling features in the mid-20th century, grew into the large impact-absorbing units of the 1970s safety era, and then largely disappeared under body-coloured plastic covers in the 1980s and 1990s as aerodynamics and styling took precedence.
