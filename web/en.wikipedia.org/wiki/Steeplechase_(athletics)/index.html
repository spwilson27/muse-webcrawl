Steeplechase (athletics)

Runners negotiate the water jump, the signature obstacle of the steeplechase.

The steeplechase is an obstacle race in athletics, which derives its name from the steeplechase races in horse racing. The foremost version of the event is the 3000 metres steeplechase. The 2000 metres steeplechase is the next most common distance; the 1000 metres steeplechase is occasionally contested by youth and masters athletes.

The event originated in the British Isles, inspired by cross-country horse races run from church steeple to church steeple. Runners on foot adapted the format, and the steeplechase has been part of the Olympic Games for men since 1920 (in its 3000 m form) and for women since 2008.

Rules

A 3000 metre steeplechase comprises seven and a half laps of a standard track. Each lap features four solid barriers, 91.4 cm (36 inches) high for men and 76.2 cm (30 inches) for women, plus one water jump: a barrier followed by a pit of water 3.66 m square, sloping from 70 cm deep at the barrier to level with the track at the far end. Unlike hurdles, the barriers do not fall over when struck.

Technique

Athletes may hurdle the plain barriers in the usual manner or place a foot on top of them. For the water jump, competitors typically plant one foot on the barrier and drive outward to land as far into or beyond the water as possible, minimizing the loss of momentum from wading.

Dominance

Kenyan athletes have been especially dominant in the event, winning the men's Olympic title at every Games from 1968 to 2016 — a streak broken by Morocco's Soufiane El Bakkali in 2021, who retained the title in 2024. The men's world record is held by Lamecha Girma of Ethiopia (7:52.11, set in 2023), and the women's record by Beatrice Chepkoech of Kenya (8:44.32, set in 2018).
