Resistor

A resistor is a passive two-terminal electrical component that implements electrical resistance as a circuit element. In electronic circuits, resistors are used to reduce current flow, adjust signal levels, divide voltages, bias active elements and terminate transmission lines, among many other uses. The electrical resistance of a resistor is measured in ohms (Ω), and the relationship between voltage across it and current through it is given by Ohm's law, V = IR.

Types

Fixed resistors are made as carbon composition, carbon film, metal film and wirewound types, and in surface-mount chip form; variable resistors include potentiometers and rheostats. Resistor values follow standard E-series and are marked with a colour code or printed digits. Specialised types include thermistors, whose resistance varies with temperature, and photoresistors, which vary with light.

Power dissipation

Electrical energy converted by a resistor becomes heat: the power dissipated is P = VI = I²R = V²/R. Resistors are therefore also rated by maximum power (commonly 0.25 W for small through-hole parts), and exceeding the rating destroys the component. High-power wirewound resistors serve as loads and heaters.

Series and parallel

Resistances in series add directly (R = R₁ + R₂ + …); in parallel their reciprocals add (1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …), so the combination is always smaller than the smallest individual resistor. These rules, together with Kirchhoff's laws, underpin basic circuit analysis and the design of voltage dividers.
