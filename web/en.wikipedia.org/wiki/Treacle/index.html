Treacle

Treacle is any uncrystallised syrup made during the refining of sugar. The most common forms of treacle are golden syrup, a pale variety, and black treacle, a darker, more bitter variety similar to molasses. The word derives via Old French from the Latin theriaca, a medicinal compound originally made with viper's flesh, and only later came to mean a sweet syrup — an ancient remedy transformed by association with sweet medicine.

Types

Black treacle is a dark, viscous by-product of sugar refining, with a strong, slightly bitter flavour. It is used in traditional British baking, including treacle tart, parkin, Christmas pudding, and treacle toffee associated with Bonfire Night. Golden syrup, by contrast, is a light amber syrup made by refining sugar cane or beet juice, famously sold in the green and gold tins of Lyle's since the 1880s.

Production

Treacle is produced when sugar syrup is concentrated and treated so that some of the sucrose is inverted into glucose and fructose, preventing crystallisation. The darker grades come from later stages of refining and contain more of the minerals and flavour compounds of the original cane juice.

In culture

Treacle appears in Lewis Carroll's Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, where the Dormouse tells of three sisters living at the bottom of a treacle well. "Treacle mining" is a jocular folk tradition in several English villages, including a fictional mine at Knotty Ash associated with the comedian Ken Dodd.
