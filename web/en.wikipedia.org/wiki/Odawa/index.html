Odawa

The Odawa (also spelled Ottawa) are an Indigenous North American people of the Great Lakes region, closely related to the Ojibwe and Potawatomi, with whom they form the Council of Three Fires. Their name is generally understood to derive from an Algonquian word meaning "traders", reflecting their historic role as intermediaries in the fur trade. Their language, Odawa, is a dialect of Ojibwe within the Algonquian family.

History

At the time of European contact, the Odawa lived around Manitoulin Island and the northern shores of Lake Huron and Georgian Bay. They became major partners of the French in the fur trade in the 17th century. Wars with the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) dispersed many Odawa westward. The war chief Pontiac, who led the uprising against the British in 1763, was Odawa. During the 19th century, many Odawa in the United States ceded their Michigan lands under treaties, while others moved to Canada; some removed to Kansas and later Oklahoma.

Contemporary communities

Federally recognised Odawa tribes in the United States include the Little Traverse Bay Bands of Odawa Indians and the Little River Band of Ottawa Indians in Michigan, and the Ottawa Tribe of Oklahoma. In Canada, Odawa communities include those on Manitoulin Island, such as Wiikwemkoong First Nation, and the Ottawa are also represented among the Algonquins of Ontario.

Culture

Traditional Odawa life combined farming (corn, beans and squash), fishing, hunting, and gathering, organised in bands with clan (doodem) structures. Odawa birchbark canoes and trading skills were renowned throughout the Great Lakes.
