My Fair Lady is a musical with a book and lyrics by Alan Jay Lerner and music by Frederick Loewe. Adapted from George Bernard Shaw's 1913 play Pygmalion, it tells of Eliza Doolittle, a Cockney flower seller, who takes speech lessons from the phonetician Professor Henry Higgins so that she may pass as a lady. The original Broadway production of 1956 was a landmark of the American musical theatre, and the 1964 film adaptation won the Academy Award for Best Picture.

Plot

Outside Covent Garden, Higgins wagers Colonel Pickering that in six months he can pass the flower girl Eliza off as a duchess by changing her speech. After grueling lessons — crowned by her breakthrough in "The Rain in Spain" — Eliza triumphs first at Ascot (lapsing memorably into racetrack slang at the climax of a race) and then at an embassy ball. Higgins takes the credit and ignores her triumph; Eliza, realizing she is treated as an experiment rather than a person, leaves. In Shaw's original ending they part for good; the musical softens the close: Eliza returns to Higgins's study, and he, having admitted in "I've Grown Accustomed to Her Face" that he misses her, asks her to fetch his slippers as the curtain falls.

Songs and productions

The score includes "Wouldn't It Be Loverly", "With a Little Bit of Luck", "I Could Have Danced All Night", "On the Street Where You Live", "Get Me to the Church on Time", and "The Rain in Spain". The Broadway premiere starred Rex Harrison and Julie Andrews, directed by Moss Hart with choreography by Hanya Holm and costumes by Cecil Beaton; it ran 2,717 performances, then a record, and won six Tony Awards including Best Musical. The London production (1958) ran 2,281 performances. The 1964 Warner Bros. film starred Harrison and Audrey Hepburn (whose singing was largely dubbed by Marni Nixon) and won eight Academy Awards. Major revivals include the 2018 Lincoln Center production directed by Bartlett Sher.

Legacy

The original cast album spent years atop the charts, and My Fair Lady is routinely ranked among the greatest musicals ever written — "probably the most perfect musical of all time", in one common verdict. Its treatment of class, gender, and Higgins and Eliza's relationship has drawn increasing critical debate, even as its songs remain standards.
