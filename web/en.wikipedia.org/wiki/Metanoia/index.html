Metanoia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Metanoia (from the Greek μετάνοια, metanoia, "changing one's mind") is a concept with a range of meanings in theology, rhetoric, and psychology: an after-thought or change of mind, spiritual conversion, repentance, and in Jungian psychology a spontaneous attempt of the psyche to heal itself.

In Greek usage and rhetoric

In classical Greek, metanoia meant a change of mind or purpose, sometimes with regret for a past decision. Rhetoricians adopted metanoia as a figure of speech — also known by the Latin term correctio — in which a speaker retracts or qualifies a previous statement and replaces it with a stronger or more apt one.

In Christian theology

In the Greek New Testament, metanoia is the word most often translated "repentance": the call of John the Baptist and of Jesus to "repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand" (Matthew 3:2; 4:17). Theological debate has long surrounded its exact force: whether it denotes primarily a change of mind and orientation of the whole person toward God (as many scholars argue the Greek implies), or sorrow and penance for sins, emphasised by the Latin rendering paenitentia ("do penance") in the Vulgate — a translation Martin Luther criticised in the first of his Ninety-five Theses. In Eastern Orthodox theology, metanoia denotes a transformative change of the nous (mind/heart), an ongoing turning toward God closely linked with the concepts of theosis and with practices such as the Jesus Prayer; a deep prostration in prayer is also called a metania.

In psychology

Carl Jung used metanoia for the psyche's self-healing attempt during a breakdown of the ego's adaptation — a painful melting-down and re-forming of personality that, if supported, can lead to renewal, a usage taken up by R. D. Laing in describing aspects of psychosis as a potentially transformative voyage.
