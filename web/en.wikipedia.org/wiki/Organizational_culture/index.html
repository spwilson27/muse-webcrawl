Organizational culture

Organizational culture encompasses values and behaviours that contribute to the unique social and psychological environment of a business. It influences the ways in which people interact, the context within which knowledge is created, the resistance they will have towards certain changes, and ultimately the way they share (or do not share) knowledge. Organizational culture can be defined as "the set of shared assumptions, values and beliefs that governs how people behave in organizations", and it is often described informally as "the way things get done around here".

Schein's model describes culture at three levels: artifacts, espoused values, and basic underlying assumptions.

Definitions and origins

The term first appeared in the management literature in 1951, when Elliott Jaques used "culture" to describe the customary and traditional way of thinking and doing things in a factory. Interest in the concept grew rapidly in the early 1980s, driven by popular books such as In Search of Excellence by Tom Peters and Robert Waterman, and by studies of Japanese management practices. Scholars have since produced dozens of competing definitions, reflecting disputes about whether culture is something an organization has or something an organization is.

Schein's model

Edgar Schein proposed one of the most influential models, dividing culture into three levels. Artifacts are the visible structures, processes, rituals, dress codes and office layouts. Espoused beliefs and values are the stated strategies, goals and philosophies, which may or may not match observed behaviour. Basic underlying assumptions are unconscious, taken-for-granted beliefs that ultimately determine behaviour, perception and feeling; these are the hardest to change.

Typologies

Several frameworks classify cultures into types. The Competing Values Framework of Cameron and Quinn distinguishes clan, adhocracy, market and hierarchy cultures. Charles Handy's model, drawing on Roger Harrison, identifies power, role, task and person cultures. Geert Hofstede studied organizational culture across subsidiaries of a multinational company and identified dimensions such as process- versus results-orientation and employee- versus job-orientation, distinct from his earlier work on national culture.

Strong and weak cultures

A "strong" culture is one where core values are intensely held and widely shared, producing high behavioural consistency; such cultures are associated with strong performance in stable environments but can resist change. Weak cultures have fragmented or contradictory subcultures. Subcultures commonly form by department, profession or location, and may conflict with the dominant culture.

Culture change

Deliberately changing organizational culture is widely regarded as difficult and slow, because underlying assumptions are embedded in routines, stories, reward systems and hiring practices. Approaches include leadership role-modelling, changing structures and incentives, rituals and symbols, and sustained communication. Culture is also a major factor in the failure of mergers and acquisitions, where clashing cultures undermine integration.
