FunctionTransmits mechanical force from muscle to bone

A tendon (or sinew) is a tough band of dense fibrous connective tissue that connects muscle to bone, transmitting the force generated by muscular contraction to move the skeleton. Tendons differ from ligaments, which connect bone to bone, and from fascia, which surround muscles. Their high tensile strength comes from tightly packed, parallel bundles of collagen fibres, principally type I collagen, embedded in a proteoglycan matrix and maintained by elongated fibroblast cells called tenocytes.

Structure

Collagen molecules assemble hierarchically into fibrils, fibres, fascicles and finally the whole tendon, each level wrapped in connective tissue sheaths (endotenon and epitenon). A crimped, wavy arrangement of fibres gives tendons slight elasticity and a "toe region" in their stress–strain curve. Some tendons, such as those of the fingers, run within synovial sheaths that reduce friction; others are served by a paratenon. The Achilles tendon, attaching the calf muscles to the heel bone, is the thickest and strongest in the human body.

Function and injury

Beyond force transmission, tendons store and release elastic energy — the Achilles tendon acts like a spring during running — and contain proprioceptive sensors (Golgi tendon organs) that monitor tension. Tendons have a poor blood supply and heal slowly. Common disorders include tendinopathy and tendinitis from overuse, ruptures of the Achilles or rotator cuff tendons, and lacerations in the hand requiring surgical repair.
