Apéritif

An apéritif is an alcoholic beverage usually served before a meal to stimulate the appetite, and is therefore usually dry rather than sweet. Common choices include champagne, gin, dry vermouth, and bitter liqueurs such as Campari and Aperol. The word derives from the Latin aperire, "to open", and the custom is particularly associated with France and Italy (where it is called aperitivo).

History

Stimulating drinks before meals date to ancient Egypt and the medicinal wines of Hippocrates. The modern apéritif developed in 19th-century Europe: Antonio Benedetto Carpano created vermouth in Turin in 1786, distillers in Turin, Milan and Paris commercialised bitter and aromatised wines, and by the later 1800s the pre-dinner drink was an established ritual in French and Italian cafés.

Types

Apéritifs include dry sparkling wines (champagne, prosecco, cava), fortified wines (dry sherry, vermouth), bitter liqueurs (Campari, Aperol, Suze), anise spirits (pastis, ouzo) and cocktails mixed from them, such as the Negroni, the Aperol Spritz and the Martini. The counterpart served after a meal is the digestif.
