Republic of Ragusa

The Republic of Ragusa (Latin: Respublica Ragusina; Croatian: Dubrovačka Republika) was a maritime republic centred on the city of Ragusa — modern Dubrovnik, Croatia — which existed from 1358 to 1808. A small aristocratic city-state of merchants on the eastern Adriatic coast, it rose to prominence as a trading intermediary between the Ottoman Balkans and the ports of Italy, and in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries rivalled Venice itself in the carrying trade of the Mediterranean; at its height its merchant fleet numbered in the hundreds of vessels, its consuls sat in ports across Europe and the Levant, and its argosies (the very word "argosy" derives from Ragusa) were proverbial. Ragusa survived for four and a half centuries between greater powers by a diplomacy of suppleness — tribute to the Ottoman sultan from 1458 purchased protection and trading privileges while the republic kept its Catholic faith, its Latin institutions and its freedom — until in 1806 French troops occupied the city in the upheavals of the Napoleonic Wars, and Marshal Marmont formally abolished the republic on 31 January 1808.

History

Ragusa grew from a settlement on the rocky islet of Lausa, traditionally founded by refugees from Roman Epidaurum (modern Cavtat) destroyed in the seventh-century Slavic and Avar invasions, facing a Slavic settlement (Dubrava, the oak grove — hence Dubrovnik) across a marshy channel later filled in to form the Stradun, the city's great street. The two communities fused; the city prospered under Byzantine, then (1205–1358) Venetian sovereignty. When Louis I of Hungary defeated Venice, the Treaty of Zadar (1358) transferred Dalmatia to the Hungarian crown, and Ragusa, nominally acknowledging the king, became in practice a self-governing republic: governed by its hereditary nobility through a Great Council, a Senate and a rector elected for a single month and confined to the Rector's Palace during his term — an institutional caution against tyranny modelled on, and outlasting, Venice's own.

The golden age

From 1458 Ragusa paid annual tribute to the Ottoman sultan — eventually some 12,500 ducats a year — in exchange for near-complete internal freedom and unique trading privileges throughout the Ottoman Empire; Ragusan colonies of merchants operated from Belgrade and Sofia to Alexandria and the Black Sea, while the republic's ships carried for all flags in the western Mediterranean and even, under charter, in the Spanish and English trades (Ragusan ships were among those wrecked with the Spanish Armada of 1588). The city adorned itself accordingly: the great public works of Onofrio's fountains and aqueduct (1438), the Rector's Palace, the Sponza customs house, the Franciscan and Dominican churches, Orlando's column and the civic clock — and a culture to match, producing Latin humanists and Croatian poets (Marin Držić among them) in a Slavic vernacular literature that Ragusa pioneered. The republic prided itself on early humanitarian distinctions it genuinely earned: it abolished the slave trade and slavery in its territory in 1416 — among the first European states to do so — and in 1377 had established the world's first recorded quarantine, the trentino, on the approaches to the city, the direct ancestor of the Venetian forty-day quarantena.

Decline and fall

The seventeenth century brought the shifting of trade to the Atlantic, Venetian and Ottoman wars around its frontiers, and catastrophe: the earthquake of 6 April 1667 destroyed most of the city and killed perhaps 5,000 people, including the rector, and Ragusa never recovered its commercial primacy — though it rebuilt in the Baroque style visible today and its diplomacy still kept it intact through the Treaty of Karlowitz (1699) and the eighteenth century, when its flag flew neutral in every maritime war. The end came with Napoleon: in 1806, nominally to forestall a Russo-Montenegrin seizure of the Bay of Kotor, French forces marched in; the republic's protests were useless, and in 1808 Marmont dissolved it, annexing its territory to the French Illyrian Provinces. The Congress of Vienna assigned Dubrovnik to Austria, under which it remained until 1918. The republic's four centuries of independence remain the core of Dubrovnik's civic identity, its motto still lettered over the gate of St Lawrence's fortress: liberty is not to be sold for all the gold in the world.
