The springbok (Antidorcas marsupialis) is a medium-sized antelope found mainly in the dry regions of southern and southwestern Africa. One of the fastest land animals, it is noted for its distinctive bouncing display known as pronking. The springbok is the national animal of South Africa, and the country's national rugby union team is nicknamed the Springboks.

Description

The springbok stands about 71–86 cm at the shoulder and weighs 27–48 kg. Both sexes carry black, lyre-shaped horns ringed with ridges, though those of the female are more slender. The coat is cinnamon-brown above and white below, separated by a dark reddish-brown stripe along the flank. The face is white with a dark stripe from the eye to the mouth, and the rump bears a white patch bordered by a dark band; a fold of skin along the back conceals a crest of white hair that is raised during pronking.

Behaviour

Springbok are grazers and browsers, feeding on grasses, leaves, and shrubs, and can survive long periods without drinking, taking moisture from their food. They live in herds that vary in size with the season. Historically they formed some of the largest migrations ever recorded, with treks of millions of animals crossing the Karoo and Kalahari; these mass movements have largely disappeared because of fencing and hunting.

In pronking (also called stotting), the springbok leaps repeatedly into the air — up to about 2 metres — with a stiff-legged gait, arched back, lowered head, and the white back crest fanned out. Pronking is thought to signal fitness to predators and to alarm the herd; animals may pronk when startled, when playing, or when rain approaches.

Distribution and habitat

The species ranges across the dry interior of Namibia, Botswana, and South Africa, and into southern Angola. It inhabits open plains, semi-desert, and Karoo scrub, and is common in reserves such as the Kalahari Gemsbok and Etosha national parks.

Conservation

The springbok is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN, with a large and stable population estimated in the millions. It is widely farmed and hunted for meat and trophies, and farmed populations include selectively bred colour variants such as black and white springbok.

Cultural significance

The springbok appears on South African emblems, coinage, and the badge of the national rugby team, which retained the name and emblem after the end of apartheid. The animal also features in the coat of arms of South Africa.
