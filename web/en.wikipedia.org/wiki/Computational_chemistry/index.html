Computational chemistry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Computational chemistry is a branch of chemistry that uses computer simulation to solve chemical problems. It applies theoretical chemistry — methods built on quantum mechanics and statistical mechanics — implemented in software to calculate molecular structures, energies, reaction mechanisms, spectra, and the properties of condensed phases. Computational chemistry complements experiment by predicting quantities that are hard to measure and by explaining observed behaviour at the atomic level.

Methods

  • Ab initio quantum chemistry — Hartree–Fock theory and post-Hartree–Fock methods (MP2, coupled cluster, configuration interaction) solve approximations to the Schrödinger equation without empirical parameters.
  • Density functional theory (DFT) — computes properties from the electron density; the most widely used electronic-structure method for molecules and materials.
  • Semi-empirical methods — simplified quantum methods with parameters fitted to data (e.g. AM1, PM7).
  • Molecular mechanics (force fields) — classical ball-and-spring models (AMBER, CHARMM) for large biomolecules.
  • Molecular dynamics and Monte Carlo — simulate the time evolution or statistical sampling of many-atom systems.
  • QM/MM — treats an active site quantum mechanically within a classical environment, recognised by the 2013 Nobel Prize in Chemistry (Karplus, Levitt, Warshel); the 1998 prize honoured Pople and Kohn for computational methods and DFT.

Applications

Uses include drug design and docking, catalyst and materials design, prediction of NMR/IR/UV spectra, reaction-pathway and transition-state searches, and modelling of proteins and polymers. Increasingly, machine-learned potentials and GPU computing extend accuracy and scale.

Software

Widely used packages include Gaussian, ORCA, VASP, Quantum ESPRESSO, NAMD, GROMACS, LAMMPS and CP2K, spanning commercial and open-source codes.

Accuracy and validation

Results depend on method, basis set, and model assumptions: "chemical accuracy" (≈1 kcal/mol) is attainable for small systems with high-level methods, while large systems require trade-offs. Benchmarking against experiment and higher-level theory is standard practice.
