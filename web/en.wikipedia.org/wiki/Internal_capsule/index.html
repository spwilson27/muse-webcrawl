Internal capsule

The internal capsule is a white matter structure situated in the inferomedial part of each cerebral hemisphere of the brain. It carries information past the basal ganglia, separating the caudate nucleus and the thalamus from the putamen and the globus pallidus. The internal capsule contains both ascending and descending axons going to and coming from the cerebral cortex.

Structure

In horizontal section it appears as a V-shaped band of white matter, divided into an anterior limb, a genu (bend), and a posterior limb, plus retrolenticular and sublenticular parts. The posterior limb contains the corticospinal tract, whose fibers control movement of the opposite side of the body, and thalamocortical sensory fibers.

Blood supply

The internal capsule is supplied by branches of the middle cerebral artery, including the lenticulostriate arteries, and by the anterior choroidal artery. This supply is clinically significant because the region is a common site of lacunar stroke.

Clinical significance

Because so many critical fibers are packed into a small area, even a small lesion — such as a lacunar infarct — can cause major deficits, classically a dense paralysis of the contralateral face, arm, and leg (pure motor hemiparesis) without cortical signs.
