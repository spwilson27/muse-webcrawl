Freeboard

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia · This article is about the term in hydraulic engineering and hydrology. For the nautical term, see Freeboard (nautical).

In hydraulic engineering and hydrology, freeboard is the vertical distance between the expected maximum water surface elevation — for example, the design flood level in a river, reservoir, dam, levee, or drainage channel — and the top of the structure or channel bank. It is a safety margin built into design to accommodate uncertainties in flood estimation, wave action, wind setup, settlement, debris, and future changes in hydrology such as those caused by urbanisation or climate change.

Applications

Dam safety standards typically require freeboard sufficient to prevent overtopping by the design flood plus wave run-up; overtopping is the leading cause of failure of embankment dams. Levee design standards in the United States, such as those of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers and FEMA's levee accreditation rules, commonly call for minimum freeboard of about 3 feet (0.9 m) above the 1-percent-annual-chance flood level, with additional allowance near structures and at the upstream ends of levees. Freeboard requirements also appear in stormwater management (for detention basins and channels) and in floodplain building codes, where "freeboard" may denote the height a building's lowest floor is elevated above the base flood elevation — a practice that reduces flood-insurance premiums under the National Flood Insurance Program.

Design considerations

Selecting freeboard balances safety against cost: higher embankments and walls require more land and material. Designers account for hydrologic uncertainty, hydraulic modelling error, sedimentation, ice and debris jams, and the consequences of failure, with critical facilities (hospitals, nuclear plants) receiving larger margins. Some modern guidance frames freeboard as part of a risk-based design approach rather than a fixed allowance, and several jurisdictions have increased freeboard standards in response to observed increases in extreme rainfall.
