Enkephalin

An enkephalin is a pentapeptide involved in regulating pain (nociception) in the body. The enkephalins are termed endogenous ligands, as they are internally derived and bind to the body's opioid receptors. Discovered in 1975, two forms were identified: Met-enkephalin (Tyr-Gly-Gly-Phe-Met) and Leu-enkephalin (Tyr-Gly-Gly-Phe-Leu). Both are products of the proenkephalin gene, PENK.

Receptors

There are three well-characterised families of opioid receptors: the μ (mu), κ (kappa) and δ (delta) receptors. The enkephalins bind most strongly to the δ receptor, in contrast to the endorphins, which prefer the μ receptor. Activation of these receptors reduces the release of pain-signalling neurotransmitters and produces analgesia.

Function

Enkephalins are produced in the central nervous system, the adrenal medulla and other tissues. They act as neurotransmitters and neuromodulators, suppressing pain signals in the spinal cord and brainstem, and also influence mood, movement (in the basal ganglia) and gastrointestinal motility. They are rapidly degraded by enzymes including aminopeptidase N and neutral endopeptidase ("enkephalinases"); inhibitors of these enzymes have been investigated as potential analgesics.

History

John Hughes and Hans Kosterlitz isolated the enkephalins from pig brain extracts at the University of Aberdeen in 1975, identifying them as the natural ligands for the opioid receptors whose existence had been predicted from the action of morphine. Their discovery launched the modern study of endogenous opioid peptides, including the endorphins and dynorphins.
