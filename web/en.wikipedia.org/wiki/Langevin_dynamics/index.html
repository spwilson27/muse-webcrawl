Langevin dynamics is an approach to the mathematical modelling of the dynamics of molecular systems. It was originally developed by French physicist Paul Langevin. The approach is characterized by the use of simplified models while accounting for omitted degrees of freedom by the use of stochastic differential equations. Langevin dynamics simulations are a form of Monte Carlo and molecular dynamics simulation in which solvent effects are treated implicitly.

The Langevin equation

A particle of mass M with position X subject to a potential U(X) obeys the Langevin equation:

M d²X/dt² = −∇U(X) − γ (dX/dt) + √(2γkBT) R(t)

Here γ is a friction (damping) coefficient, kB is Boltzmann's constant, T is the temperature, and R(t) is a delta-correlated Gaussian random process — the "noise" representing collisions with solvent molecules. The friction and noise terms are linked by the fluctuation–dissipation theorem, so the dynamics sample the correct canonical (Boltzmann) distribution at temperature T. A Langevin dynamics simulation therefore acts as a thermostat, maintaining constant temperature in molecular dynamics.

Applications

Langevin dynamics is widely used to simulate polymers, proteins, and colloids in implicit solvent, to enhance sampling of rare events, and as the basis of stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics in machine learning for Bayesian sampling of neural network parameters. In the overdamped (high-friction) limit, inertial terms are neglected and the equation reduces to Brownian dynamics. Numerical integration schemes include the Euler–Maruyama method and more accurate splittings such as BAOAB, designed to preserve equilibrium distributions with large time steps.
