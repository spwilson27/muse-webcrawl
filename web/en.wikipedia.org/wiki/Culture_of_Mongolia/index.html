Culture of Mongolia

Placeholder image: Culture of Mongolia

The culture of Mongolia has been shaped by the country's nomadic pastoral way of life, its Tibetan Buddhist heritage, and the legacy of the Mongol Empire founded by Genghis Khan in 1206. Despite rapid urbanization centered on the capital, Ulaanbaatar, traditional customs surrounding hospitality, livestock, and the steppe remain central to Mongolian identity.

Nomadic heritage

For centuries, Mongolian herders have lived in portable felt dwellings called ger (yurts), moving seasonally with herds of sheep, goats, cattle, yaks, horses, and camels. The "five snouts" (tavan khoshuu) livestock are fundamental to the traditional economy and diet. Hospitality to travelers is a deeply held value in a land of vast distances and harsh winters.

Religion

Tibetan Buddhism became dominant from the 16th century onward and profoundly influenced Mongolian art, literature, and architecture, as seen in monasteries such as Erdene Zuu and Gandantegchinlen. Shamanism, centered on reverence for the Eternal Blue Sky (Mönkh Tengri), predates Buddhism and has experienced a revival since the democratic transition of 1990, after decades of religious suppression under socialism.

Music and performing arts

Traditional Mongolian music includes throat singing (khöömii), in which a singer produces multiple pitches simultaneously, and the horsehead fiddle (morin khuur), both inscribed on UNESCO's lists of intangible cultural heritage. The long song (urtyn duu) is another celebrated vocal form. Contortion and traditional dance are featured at festivals and in state ensembles.

Festivals

Naadam, held each July, is the most important festival, featuring the "three games of men": wrestling, horse racing, and archery. Tsagaan Sar, the lunar new year, is a major family celebration marked by greetings (zolgokh), traditional foods such as buuz (steamed dumplings), and visits to elders.

Cuisine

Mongolian cuisine centers on meat and dairy products suited to the continental climate: mutton, beef, airag (fermented mare's milk), cheese, and cream. Dishes include buuz, khuushuur (fried meat pastries), khorkhog (meat cooked with hot stones), and milk tea (süütei tsai).

Script and literature

The traditional Mongolian script, written vertically, was created in the 13th century based on the Uyghur alphabet. The epic Secret History of the Mongols is the oldest surviving Mongolian literary work. Cyrillic has been the official script since the 1940s, though the government has promoted a revival of the classical script for official use.
