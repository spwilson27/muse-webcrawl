Ketogenic diet

The ketogenic diet is a high-fat, adequate-protein, low-carbohydrate dietary therapy that in conventional medicine is used mainly to treat hard-to-control (refractory) epilepsy in children. The diet forces the body to burn fats rather than carbohydrates.

Normally, carbohydrates in food are converted into glucose, which is then transported around the body and is important in fueling brain function. However, if only a little carbohydrate remains in the diet, the liver converts fat into fatty acids and ketone bodies, the latter passing into the brain and replacing glucose as an energy source. An elevated level of ketone bodies in the blood (a state called ketosis) eventually lowers the frequency of epileptic seizures. Around half of children and young people with epilepsy who have tried some form of this diet saw the number of seizures drop by at least half, and the effect persists after discontinuing the diet.

The original therapeutic diet for paediatric epilepsy provides just enough protein for body growth and repair, and enough calories to maintain the correct weight for age and height. The classic therapeutic ketogenic diet was developed for treatment of paediatric epilepsy in the 1920s and was widely used into the next decade, but its popularity waned with the introduction of effective anticonvulsant medications. This classic ketogenic diet contains a 4:1 ketogenic ratio by weight of fat to combined protein and carbohydrate.

History

The ketogenic diet is a mainstream dietary therapy that was developed to reproduce the success and remove the limitations of the non-mainstream use of fasting to treat epilepsy. Although popular in the 1920s and 1930s, it was largely abandoned in favour of new anticonvulsant drugs. Most individuals with epilepsy can successfully control their seizures with medication. However, 20–30% fail to achieve such control despite trying a number of different drugs. For this subgroup, and for children in particular, the diet has once again found a role in epilepsy management.

Mechanism

The diet works by producing ketone bodies — acetoacetate, acetone and beta-hydroxybutyrate — which the brain can use as an alternative fuel. The precise anticonvulsant mechanism remains incompletely understood; proposed mechanisms include altered neurotransmitter synthesis, increased mitochondrial biogenesis, antioxidant effects, and direct effects of fatty acids on neuronal ion channels.

Indications

The diet is indicated for children with epilepsy that has not responded to two or three anticonvulsant drugs, and is especially useful in certain conditions such as glucose transporter type 1 deficiency syndrome (Glut1DS) and pyruvate dehydrogenase deficiency, where ketones provide an alternative fuel the brain can use.

Popular use

In the 21st century the ketogenic diet has become popular as a weight-loss diet and is used by some athletes and bodybuilders. Critics note the lack of long-term evidence for most popular uses and possible adverse effects including nutrient deficiencies, kidney stones, and increased LDL cholesterol in some individuals.
