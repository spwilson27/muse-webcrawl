Hate speech

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hate speech is speech that attacks or uses pejorative or discriminatory language about a person or group on the basis of attributes such as race, religion, ethnic origin, nationality, sex, disability, sexual orientation or gender identity. There is no single legal definition; states treat hate speech very differently. Most European democracies, Canada and many other countries criminalise incitement to hatred and, in several (Germany, Austria, France), Holocaust denial specifically, as part of post-war “militant democracy”. The United States, by contrast, protects hate speech as such under the First Amendment unless it falls into an unprotected category (incitement to imminent lawless action, true threats, or discriminatory harassment in specific settings): the Supreme Court has upheld the right to burn crosses with intent to intimidate as criminal (Virginia v. Black, 2003) while striking down ordinances punishing hate speech as such (R.A.V. v. St. Paul, 1992), and protected even a racist group's march (Skokie, 1977–78).

International law requires prohibition of “advocacy of national, racial or religious hatred that constitutes incitement to discrimination, hostility or violence” (ICCPR Article 20) — a duty the US accepts with a reservation. Scholars debate whether hate-speech laws reduce or merely displace abuse, whether they are turned against minorities themselves, and how platforms should moderate lawful-but-harmful content.
