Rocky Mountain National Park is an American national park located approximately 55 miles (89 km) northwest of Denver in north-central Colorado, within the Front Range of the Rocky Mountains. The park straddles the Continental Divide and protects mountain environments encompassing montane, subalpine, and alpine ecosystems, with elevations from 7,860 feet to 14,259 feet at the summit of Longs Peak.

Geography and ecosystems

The park contains 265,807 acres, 124 named peaks above 10,000 feet, 147 lakes, and the headwaters of the Colorado River, which flows through its northwest corner. Trail Ridge Road — the highest continuous paved road in the United States, cresting at 12,183 feet — crosses the park from Estes Park to Grand Lake. Roughly one-third of the park lies above treeline as alpine tundra, a fragile ecosystem where plants grow only inches tall in a growing season of about 40 days.

Wildlife

The park hosts elk (famous for the autumn rut in its meadows), moose (reintroduced on the west side), bighorn sheep, mule deer, black bears, mountain lions, and hundreds of bird species; the endangered greenback cutthroat trout has been restored to some waters. Abert's squirrels, marmots, and pikas inhabit the high country.

History

The area was long used by Ute and Arapaho peoples. Naturalist Enos Mills, the "Father of Rocky Mountain National Park," campaigned for its creation; Congress passed the enabling act, signed by President Woodrow Wilson on January 26, 1915. The Civilian Conservation Corps built trails and Trail Ridge Road in the 1930s. In 1976 UNESCO designated the park a Biosphere Reserve, and in 2009 Congress designated most of it as wilderness. The park is consistently among the five most-visited U.S. national parks, with over 4.5 million visitors in record years; a timed-entry reservation system was introduced in 2020.
