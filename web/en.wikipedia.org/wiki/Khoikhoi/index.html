Khoikhoi

The Khoikhoi (Khoekhoe in standardised Khoekhoegowab orthography; also spelled Khoi Khoi), historically known to Europeans by the exonym "Hottentots"—now regarded as pejorative—are a traditionally nomadic pastoralist indigenous population of southwestern Africa, in present-day Namibia and South Africa. Together with the San, hunter-gatherers who spoke related click languages, they are often grouped as the Khoisan.

History

The ancestors of the Khoikhoi adopted sheep and cattle pastoralism, probably derived from groups migrating southward from the region of modern Botswana, by roughly two thousand years ago, spreading herding across the Cape and the Namibian highlands. When the Dutch East India Company founded a refreshment station at the Cape under Jan van Riebeeck in 1652, trade and conflict with Khoikhoi chiefdoms (such as the Goringhaiqua and Goringhaicona) became central to the colony's history. A series of wars, land dispossession, a devastating smallpox epidemic in 1713, and incorporation as labourers largely destroyed independent Khoikhoi society at the Cape during the eighteenth century.

Khoikhoi individuals became famous, and infamous, in Europe: most notably Saartjie Baartman, exhibited in London and Paris as the "Hottentot Venus", whose remains were repatriated to South Africa in 2002.

Language and culture

The Khoikhoi language, Khoekhoegowab (Nama/Damara), belongs to the Khoe family and is characterised by click consonants. Clan organisation, cattle wealth, and rituals such as the tsamma-melon dance and the veneration of the culture hero Heitsi-eibib marked traditional Khoikhoi culture. In post-apartheid South Africa, Khoikhoi identity has seen a revival, with Khoi and San leadership structures and land and recognition claims recognised in law, including the Traditional and Khoi-San Leadership Act of 2019.
