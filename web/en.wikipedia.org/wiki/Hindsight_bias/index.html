Hindsight bias

Hindsight bias, also known as the knew-it-all-along phenomenon or creeping determinism, is the tendency, after an event has occurred, to see the event as having been predictable, despite there having been little or no objective basis for predicting it.

Research

Baruch Fischhoff's 1975 experiments first demonstrated the effect systematically: participants told an outcome rated it as having been more likely beforehand than participants not told the outcome. The bias affects memory, judgments of inevitability, and personal foreseeability.

Consequences

Hindsight bias can distort legal judgments, medical decisions, historical analysis, and assessments of decision-makers, leading people to blame others for failing to foresee events that were not reasonably foreseeable.
