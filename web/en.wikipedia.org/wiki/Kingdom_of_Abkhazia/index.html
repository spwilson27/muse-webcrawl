The Kingdom of Abkhazia (Georgian: აფხაზთა სამეფო) was a medieval feudal state in the Caucasus, established in the 780s on the eastern Black Sea coast in what is now western Georgia and Abkhazia. It grew from a Byzantine client principality into the dominant power of western Georgia before merging into the unified Kingdom of Georgia in 1008.

Origins

Abkhazia had been ruled by hereditary archons under Byzantine suzerainty. Around 778–786, Leon II, aided by the Khazars and exploiting Byzantine weakness during the Arab wars, declared independence and took the title of king, with his capital at Anacopia and later Kutaisi. His realm united the Abkhazian and Apsilian tribes and the former Lazican territories.

Expansion

During the 9th and early 10th centuries the Abkhazian kings intervened repeatedly in the affairs of the eastern Georgian principalities, occupying Kartli for long periods and fighting the Armenian Bagratids, the emirate of Tbilisi, and Kakheti. Kings such as Demetrius III and Theodosius III "the Blind" struggled over the succession; Theodosius's childlessness opened the way to a dynastic union.

Union with Georgia

In 978 the young Bagrat, of the Bagrationi dynasty and heir through his mother Gurandukht (sister of Theodosius III), was crowned king of Abkhazia. In 1008, on the death of his father Gurgen, Bagrat III inherited Iberia as well, becoming the first king of a united Georgia. The title "King of the Abkhazians" remained first among the Georgian monarchs' styles for centuries, reflecting the kingdom's prestige.

Church and culture

The kingdom transferred its ecclesiastical allegiance from Constantinople to the Georgian patriarchate at Mtskheta. The period saw the construction of notable churches, including Bedia, Mokvi, and Lykhny cathedrals, and the flourishing of a shared Georgian literary culture. The history of the kingdom is a contested topic in modern Georgian–Abkhaz historiographical disputes.
