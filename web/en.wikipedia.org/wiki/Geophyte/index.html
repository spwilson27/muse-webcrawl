Geophyte

A geophyte is a perennial plant that survives unfavourable seasons as a resting bud borne on an underground storage organ. The term was introduced in the life-form classification of Christen C. Raunkiær, in which geophytes (also called cryptophytes when including marsh and water plants) are plants whose renewal buds lie below the soil surface.

Storage organs

Geophytes store food reserves and buds in structures such as true bulbs (tulips, onions, daffodils), corms (crocus, gladiolus), tubers and tuberous roots (potato, dahlia), and rhizomes (iris, ginger). Strictly, "geophyte" refers to the life form, while the organs themselves are collectively termed geophytic organs; many rhizomatous plants are also counted as hemicryptophytes in Raunkiær's system.

Ecology and distribution

Underground storage allows geophytes to die back to the ground during drought, cold or fire and to regrow rapidly when conditions improve. They are especially diverse in Mediterranean-climate regions with dry summers — the Cape Floristic Region of South Africa, southwestern Australia, California, Chile and the Mediterranean Basin — where they form a conspicuous spring wildflower flora. Geophytes also figure prominently in horticulture (spring bulbs) and agriculture (potato, onion, garlic, yam).
