Mandate (international law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mandate in international law, in this sense, was a territory administered by a state under the authority of the League of Nations after the First World War — the former colonies of Germany and the Ottoman Empire, distributed under Article 22 of the Covenant as a “sacred trust of civilisation” until their peoples could “stand alone”. The administering powers (mainly Britain, France, Belgium, South Africa, Japan, Australia and New Zealand) reported annually to the Permanent Mandates Commission.

Mandates were classed by supposed readiness for independence: Class A (ex-Ottoman Middle East — Iraq, Syria/Lebanon, Palestine/Transjordan) were provisionally recognised as independent nations under tutelage; Class B (central African) were to be administered under conditions of open door and no militarisation; Class C (South West Africa, Pacific islands) could be administered as integral portions of the mandatory's territory. Iraq became independent in 1932; most mandates passed into the UN trusteeship system after 1945, and the last, Namibia (South West Africa), became independent only in 1990 after the International Court held South Africa's continued presence illegal.

Legacy

The mandate system is now read both as a step toward decolonisation (international accountability for colonial rule) and as empire rebranded; its Palestine provisions — incorporating the Balfour Declaration's “national home for the Jewish people” while safeguarding other inhabitants' rights — remain among the most consequential legal instruments of the twentieth century.
