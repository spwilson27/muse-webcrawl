Strong verb

Strong verbs mark their principal parts by ablaut — patterned vowel changes.

In the grammar of Germanic languages, a strong verb is a verb that marks its principal parts by changing the vowel of its stem — a process called ablaut (or gradation) — rather than by adding a dental suffix (‑d/‑t) as weak verbs do. The terms were coined by Jacob Grimm in his Deutsche Grammatik (1819–1837). Examples in modern English include sing–sang–sung, drive–drove–driven, and begin–began–begun. In English grammar, strong verbs are a subset of irregular verbs; not all irregular verbs are strong (some are weak but irregular in spelling or suffix).

Origins

The Germanic strong verb system descends from the Proto-Indo-European system of ablaut, in which the vowel of a root alternated between e-grade, o-grade, and zero-grade for different grammatical functions. Germanic reorganized these alternations into seven classes of strong verbs, each with a characteristic sequence of vowels across four principal parts: infinitive/present, past singular, past plural, and past participle.

Development

Over the history of the Germanic languages, many strong verbs have become weak by analogy (e.g., English help once had past holp), and the four-part system has collapsed toward three or two distinct forms in most languages. Modern English retains roughly 200 strong verbs (many in regular use), German about 170, and Icelandic and Faroese preserve the richest systems. The past participles of English class 1–5 strong verbs typically keep the suffix ‑en (driven, sung, borne), a visible trace of the old system.
