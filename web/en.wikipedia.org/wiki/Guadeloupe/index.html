Guadeloupe (French: [ɡwad(ə)lup]; Guadeloupean Creole: Gwadloup) is an archipelago and overseas department and region of France in the Caribbean. It consists of six inhabited islands — Basse-Terre, Grande-Terre, Marie-Galante, La Désirade, and the Îles des Saintes — as well as many uninhabited islands and outcroppings. It is south of Antigua and Barbuda and Montserrat and north of the Commonwealth of Dominica.

The two main islands, high volcanic Basse-Terre and low limestone Grande-Terre, are separated by a narrow sea channel and together resemble a butterfly, giving Guadeloupe its nickname l'île papillon ("butterfly island"). The active volcano La Grande Soufrière on Basse-Terre is the highest peak in the Lesser Antilles.

History

The islands were inhabited by Arawak and then Carib peoples before Christopher Columbus sighted them in 1493 and named the main island Santa María de Guadalupe. France colonised Guadeloupe from 1635; the colony was briefly held by Britain and ceded to Sweden for a year (1813–1814) during the Napoleonic Wars. Sugar plantations worked by enslaved Africans drove the colonial economy; slavery was abolished in 1848. Guadeloupe became an overseas department in 1946, and its inhabitants are French citizens with representation in the French Parliament.

Economy and culture

Traditional exports include sugar and bananas; tourism and public-sector employment are major parts of the modern economy, which depends heavily on transfers from metropolitan France. Guadeloupean culture blends French, African, and Caribbean influences, expressed in Creole language, gwoka music and dance (inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list), and carnival traditions. The archipelago hosted significant unrest in 2009 over the cost of living, and periodic debates continue over autonomy.
