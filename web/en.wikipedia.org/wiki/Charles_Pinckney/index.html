Charles Pinckney (October 26, 1757 – October 29, 1824) was an American Founding Father, planter, and politician who was a signer of the United States Constitution. He was elected and served as the 37th governor of South Carolina, later serving two more non-consecutive terms. He also served as a U.S. senator and a member of the House of Representatives, and was the U.S. minister (ambassador) to Spain under President Thomas Jefferson.

Early life and military service

Pinckney was born into a prominent South Carolina planter family, a cousin of Charles Cotesworth Pinckney. He was educated in Charleston and studied law, and also received part of his education in England and France. During the American Revolutionary War he served in the militia, participated in the defense of Charleston, and was taken prisoner when the city fell to the British in 1780.

Constitutional Convention

At 29, Pinckney was among the youngest delegates to the Constitutional Convention of 1787 in Philadelphia. He presented his own draft plan of government — the so-called "Pinckney Plan" — and his authorship of portions of the final Constitution was a subject of later controversy, as he claimed in old age a larger role than most scholars credit him with. Significantly, his proposal that the chief executive be elected and his advocacy of a strong central government influenced the proceedings. He later helped secure South Carolina's ratification of the Constitution.

Later career

Pinckney served three terms as governor of South Carolina. Originally a Federalist, he broke with the party over policy disputes and supported Thomas Jefferson, who appointed him minister to Spain in 1801; there he helped negotiate Spanish acquiescence in the Louisiana Purchase. He returned to South Carolina politics, served in the state legislature, and was elected to the U.S. House of Representatives in 1819, where he opposed the Missouri Compromise's restrictions on slavery in debates, though he supported the compromise itself as a settlement.

Legacy

The Charles Pinckney National Historic Site in Mount Pleasant, South Carolina, preserves a portion of his Snee Farm plantation, interpreting both his political career and the lives of the enslaved people whose labor sustained his family's wealth.
