Lignosulfonate

Lignosulfonates (also spelled lignosulphonates) are water-soluble anionic polyelectrolyte polymers. They are byproducts of the production of wood pulp using sulfite pulping. Most delignification in sulfite pulping involves acidic cleavage of ether bonds in lignin, which connects many of the constituents of lignin. The electrophilic carbocations produced during ether cleavage react with bisulfite ions (HSO3−) to give sulfonates.

Production and properties

Lignosulfonates are recovered from the spent liquor (brown liquor) of sulfite mills. Ultrafiltration, fermentation of residual sugars, and chemical modification can be used to produce grades of varying purity and molecular weight; commercial products have molecular weights from roughly a thousand to over a hundred thousand daltons. Most lignosulfonates are sold as sodium, calcium, magnesium, or ammonium salts, and world production amounts to roughly a million tonnes per year.

Uses

Lignosulfonates are used as dispersants in cement and concrete admixtures (as water-reducing plasticizers), in the manufacture of gypsum board, as binders in animal feed pellets and particleboard, in dust control on roads, as drilling mud additives and emulsifiers, and as a raw material for vanillin production. Because they are renewable and biodegradable, they are also researched as feedstocks for chemicals and materials.
