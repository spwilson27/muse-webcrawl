Administrative divisions of Nazi Germany

Image placeholder: map of the Nazi Party Gaue, 1944

The administrative divisions of Nazi Germany were characterised by the overlapping jurisdictions typical of the Third Reich. Although the federal states (Länder) of the Weimar Republic formally survived, they were stripped of real power through the process of Gleichschaltung, and the Nazi Party's regional districts — the Gaue — became the effective tier of territorial administration.

States and provinces

The Länder — including Prussia, Bavaria, Saxony, Württemberg and others — lost their sovereignty under the Law on the Reconstruction of the Reich of 30 January 1934. Reichsstatthalter (Reich governors) were placed over them, and their parliaments were abolished. Prussia, by far the largest state, retained its provinces, such as East Prussia, Brandenburg, Silesia (later divided), Hanover and the Rhineland, each under an Oberpräsident.

The Gaue

Each Gau was headed by a Gauleiter appointed by Hitler and responsible to him personally. By 1939 there were 32 Gaue within the 1937 borders of Germany, and annexations during the war raised the number. In annexed territories such as Austria, the Sudetenland, Danzig-West Prussia and the Wartheland, the Gauleiter typically also served as Reichsstatthalter, fusing party and state authority in a Reichsgau.

Occupied territories

Conquered lands outside the Reich were administered separately: as Reichskommissariate (for example Ostland and Ukraine) under the Ministry for the Occupied Eastern Territories, as civil administration areas attached to neighbouring Gaue, or under military government. The General Government in occupied Poland stood outside the Reich's Gau system entirely.
