Human branding

Human branding
TypeBody modification; historically, punishment and slave marking
TechniquesStrike branding, cautery pen, laser, cold branding
ResultPermanent scar

Human branding, or stigmatizing, is the process by which a mark, usually a symbol or ornamental pattern, is burned into the skin of a living person, with the intention that the resulting scar makes it permanent. This is performed either with a hot or a very cold branding iron. It is a form of scarification and, historically, of corporal punishment and slave-marking; in the modern era it is also practised voluntarily as a form of body modification.

History

Branding was used across the ancient and early-modern worlds to mark enslaved people, criminals and soldiers. In Roman law, fugitive slaves could be branded on the forehead (prompting Constantine to forbid face-branding); the mark F (for fur, thief, or fugitivus) appears in legal sources. In British practice, deserters, bigamists and clergy pleading benefit of clergy were branded on the thumb or cheek, and the 1698–99 statute book still prescribed branding for manslaughter. In the French penal code, convicts bore letters such as TF (travaux forcés) on the shoulder. The transatlantic slave trade used branding extensively — trading companies and owners burned initials into captives' chests, shoulders or arms — a practice abolished alongside slavery itself in the 19th century. Prisoners in Nazi concentration camps were instead tattooed, a related process with different technique.

Modern voluntary practice

From the late 20th century, branding re-emerged within body-modification culture and, notoriously, among some fraternities and sororities — particularly historically Black Greek-letter organisations in the United States, where brand marks of Greek letters signify lifelong membership. Professional techniques include multi-strike branding with heated steel strips, electrocautery pens (adapted from surgical devices, giving finer lines), laser branding, and freeze branding (using supercooled metal), the latter scarring less predictably on human skin.

Risks and legality

Branding produces a third-degree burn; risks include infection, unpredictable keloid scarring, loss of fine detail as the scar spreads, and legal ambiguity: in many jurisdictions consensual branding by unlicensed practitioners occupies the same contested ground as extreme body modification, and may expose practitioners to assault charges even with consent (as in the English case law following R v Brown and later prosecutions of modification artists).
