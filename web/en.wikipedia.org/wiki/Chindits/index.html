Chindits

The Chindits, officially the Long Range Penetration Groups, were special operations units of the British and Indian armies that fought behind Japanese lines in Burma during the Second World War. Their name derives from the Burmese mythical lion-like creature, the chinthe, whose statues guard pagodas — adopted through their commander, Brigadier Orde Wingate.

Operation Longcloth (1943)

In February 1943, Wingate's 77th Indian Infantry Brigade, about 3,000 men, crossed the Chindwin River and marched deep into Japanese-held Burma, supplied entirely by air drops. The columns cut railway lines, blew bridges and ambushed Japanese forces, then fought their way back to India by April. Roughly a third of the force was killed, wounded or captured, and many survivors were unfit for further service, but the expedition was a major propaganda success for Allied morale at a low point in the war.

Operation Thursday (1944)

The second operation was far larger: some 20,000 men, largely landed by glider and transport aircraft into clearings deep behind Japanese lines, where they established fortified strongholds such as "Broadway" and "White City". Wingate was killed in an air crash in March 1944, shortly after the landings. The Chindits disrupted Japanese supply lines during the battles of Imphal and Kohima but suffered heavy casualties, particularly from disease, and disputes over their employment contributed to the force's disbandment in 1945.

Legacy

The Chindits remain controversial among military historians: admirers credit Wingate's long-range penetration concept with pioneering air-supplied deep operations, while critics argue the strategic effect did not justify the cost in elite manpower. The Chindit badge — a chinthe before a pagoda — and memorials in London and at the National Memorial Arboretum commemorate the force.
