States' rights

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In American political discourse, states' rights are political powers held for the state governments rather than the federal government of the United States, according to the United States Constitution, especially the Tenth Amendment, which reserves to the states or the people all powers not delegated to the federal government nor prohibited to the states.

The doctrine has been invoked across American history in causes both defensive and expansionary: against federal tariffs (the Nullification Crisis of 1832–33), in defence of slavery and later of racial segregation, and in modern debates over environmental regulation, firearms, abortion, marijuana and health care. Critics note this history and observe that the phrase became closely associated with resistance to federal civil-rights enforcement in the mid-twentieth century; supporters frame it as the working principle of Federalism and a check on central power.

Constitutional foundations

The founding settlement divided sovereignty: the federal government holds enumerated powers, expanded by the Commerce Clause and the Necessary and Proper Clause, while states retain general police powers. The balance has shifted over time — through the Civil War and the Reconstruction Amendments, the New Deal settlement, and modern federalism cases in which the Supreme Court of the United States has limited Congress's commerce and spending powers and developed anti-commandeering rules preventing Congress from directing state officials.

Nullification and interposition

The strongest historical version of states' rights, articulated in the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions (1798) and by John C. Calhoun, held that states could nullify unconstitutional federal laws. The Supreme Court rejected unilateral nullification, and the Civil War settled secession by force; yet milder forms — states declining to assist federal enforcement, or legalising conduct federal law prohibits — remain live features of American federalism.
