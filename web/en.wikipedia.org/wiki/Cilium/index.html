The cilium (plural cilia; from Latin "eyelash") is a membrane-bound organelle found on most types of eukaryotic cells. Cilia are slender protrusions, typically 0.25 µm in diameter and 2–20 µm long, built around a core of microtubules called the axoneme. They are broadly divided into motile cilia, which beat rhythmically to move fluid or propel the cell, and primary cilia, usually solitary and non-motile, which act as cellular "antennae" sensing the environment.

Structure

The axoneme of a motile cilium has the classic "9+2" arrangement: nine outer microtubule doublets surrounding a central pair. Dynein motor arms attached to the doublets slide them past one another, bending the cilium; nexin links and radial spokes coordinate the bend. Cilia grow from a basal body, a modified centriole, and their assembly and maintenance depend on intraflagellar transport (IFT), which ferries components to the tip. Primary cilia typically lack the central pair ("9+0") and the dynein arms. Eukaryotic flagella are structurally the same organelle, generally longer and few in number; many biologists use "cilium" and "flagellum" interchangeably.

Functions

Motile cilia line the human respiratory tract, where coordinated beating drives the mucociliary escalator that clears mucus and trapped particles; they move the ovum in the fallopian tubes and circulate cerebrospinal fluid in the brain ventricles. Free-living organisms such as Paramecium swim and feed with cilia. Primary cilia are sensory: they host receptors for signalling pathways including Hedgehog, Wnt, and PDGF, concentrate olfactory receptors in the nose, and contain the photoreceptor outer segments of the eye (highly modified cilia).

Ciliopathies

Defects in ciliary structure or function cause a family of human diseases called ciliopathies, including primary ciliary dyskinesia (chronic respiratory infections, infertility, and sometimes situs inversus), polycystic kidney disease, Bardet–Biedl syndrome, nephronophthisis, and some forms of retinal degeneration — reflecting the near-ubiquity of cilia in development and physiology.
