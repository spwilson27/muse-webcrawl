Prima facie

Prima facie (Latin for "at first sight" or "on its face") is a legal term describing evidence or a claim that is sufficient to establish a fact or a case unless disproved or rebutted. A prima facie case is one that, taking the claimant's evidence at its highest, contains enough on each essential element to require an answer from the opposing party and to support a judgment if unexplained.

In civil and criminal procedure

In civil litigation, a plaintiff who presents a prima facie case has satisfied the initial burden of production; the burden of producing contrary evidence shifts to the defendant, though in most systems the ultimate burden of persuasion stays where it began. If the claimant fails to reach the prima facie threshold, the case may be dismissed without the defence being called — in English practice, a successful "submission of no case to answer". In criminal law the prosecution must establish a prima facie case before the accused can be put on trial or called upon to present a defence; committal and preliminary hearings in many jurisdictions exist to test exactly this threshold. Statutes sometimes create prima facie presumptions, where proof of one fact is deemed sufficient evidence of another unless rebutted — for example, certain documents being prima facie evidence of their contents.

In discrimination law

The term is prominent in employment discrimination doctrine. Under the US Supreme Court's McDonnell Douglas framework, a complainant first establishes a prima facie case of discrimination (for example, belonging to a protected class, being qualified, suffering an adverse action, and circumstances suggesting discrimination), which creates a presumption that the employer then must answer with a legitimate, non-discriminatory reason, after which the complainant may show the reason is a pretext.

Everyday and philosophical usage

Outside law, prima facie describes anything that appears true on first examination, subject to closer inspection. In moral philosophy, W. D. Ross's "prima facie duties" are obligations — fidelity, gratitude, non-maleficence and others — that bind unless overridden by a weightier duty in the particular case, a usage deliberately modelled on the legal sense of a claim that stands until rebutted.
