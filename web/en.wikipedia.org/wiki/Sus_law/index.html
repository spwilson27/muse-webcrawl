Sus law

Policing in Britain during the era of the sus law

In British law, the phrase sus law (from "suspected person") referred to the power in section 4 of the Vagrancy Act 1824 that allowed police to stop, search, and arrest anyone they suspected of loitering with intent to commit an arrestable offence. The provision became notorious for its disproportionate use against Black and minority-ethnic young men in English cities during the 1970s.

Controversy and repeal

By the late 1970s the sus law was a central grievance in relations between police and Afro-Caribbean communities in areas such as Brixton, Toxteth, and Handsworth. Campaigners argued that arrests required no evidence of any actual offence, only an officer's suspicion. The law was repealed by the Criminal Attempts Act 1981, shortly after the Brixton riot of April 1981; the subsequent Scarman Report examined the policing and social conditions behind the disorders, though Scarman rejected claims of institutional racism.

Legacy

The repeal did not end debate over stop and search: successor powers under the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 and later legislation have continued to draw criticism for ethnic disproportionality. The phrase "sus" remains embedded in British culture, referenced in music and drama about the era, and the sus law is frequently cited in histories of Black British protest.
