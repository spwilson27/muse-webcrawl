The Alligatoridae are a family of crocodilians that includes the true alligators and the caimans. The eight living species are divided between the subfamilies Alligatorinae (the alligators) and Caimaninae (the caimans) and are found in the Americas and, in the case of the Chinese alligator, in eastern Asia.

Characteristics

Alligatorids have broad, rounded (U-shaped) snouts, unlike the pointed snouts of true crocodiles (family Crocodylidae). When the mouth is closed the large fourth tooth of the lower jaw fits into a socket inside the upper jaw and is hidden, whereas in crocodiles it remains visible in a notch. Members range from the dwarf caimans (Paleosuchus), among the smallest crocodilians at about 1.2–1.5 m, to the black caiman (Melanosuchus niger), which can exceed 4 m.

Distribution and species

The American alligator (Alligator mississippiensis) inhabits wetlands of the southeastern United States and is a famous conservation success, having recovered from near-endangerment in the 20th century. Its close relative, the Chinese alligator (Alligator sinensis) of the Yangtze basin, is critically endangered. The caimans — spectacled, broad-snouted, yacare, black and dwarf caimans — live in Central and South America.

Evolution

The family has a long fossil record extending to the Late Cretaceous of North America, and includes giants such as the giant horned alligator Deinosuchus and the enormous South American Purussaurus, among the largest crocodilians ever to live.
