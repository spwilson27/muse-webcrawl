The 2010 Copiapó mining accident, also known as the "Chilean mining accident", occurred on 5 August 2010 when the San José copper–gold mine near Copiapó in northern Chile collapsed, trapping 33 miners about 700 metres (2,300 ft) underground. The miners survived for a record 69 days before all were rescued on 13 October 2010, an event watched live by an estimated one billion people worldwide.

The collapse and discovery

The cave-in blocked the mine's main access ramp, and initial rescue attempts were hampered by further collapses. For 17 days the miners' fate was unknown. On 22 August, a probe drill broke through into the refuge where the men were sheltering, and a note attached to the drill bit read: "Estamos bien en el refugio los 33" ("We are well in the shelter, the 33 of us"). Supplies, communications and video links were then sent down through narrow boreholes.

Rescue

Three drilling plans (A, B and C) were pursued in parallel. Plan B, using a Schramm T130XD drill, reached the miners first on 9 October. The men were then raised one by one in the specially built Fénix steel capsule, each ascent taking about 15 minutes. The rescue, led on site by mining minister Laurence Golborne and rescue chief André Sougarret under President Sebastián Piñera's government, concluded on 13 October with the shift supervisor Luis Urzúa, the last man out.

Aftermath

The accident prompted reforms to Chilean mine safety regulation and scrutiny of the mine's owners, who had been accused of neglecting safety. The miners, known as "Los 33", became international celebrities; their story was later dramatised in the 2015 film The 33. Many of the men nonetheless struggled afterward with trauma, ill health and financial difficulties.
