Helmuth Johannes Ludwig von Moltke (25 May 1848 – 18 June 1916), also known as Moltke the Younger, was a German general and Chief of the German General Staff from 1906 to 1914. He was a nephew of Helmuth von Moltke the Elder, the victor of the Franco-Prussian War, whose reputation overshadowed his own career.

Early life and career

Moltke was born in Biendorf in Mecklenburg-Schwerin, the son of Adolf von Moltke, a civil servant. He joined the army in 1869 and served in the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71. He later served as aide-de-camp to Kaiser Wilhelm II, an appointment that gave him great influence at court, and rose through staff positions before succeeding Alfred von Schlieffen as Chief of the Great General Staff on 1 January 1906.

First World War

As chief of staff, Moltke modified the Schlieffen Plan, the German war plan for a two-front war against France and Russia. He strengthened the left wing in Lorraine at the expense of the great wheeling right wing that was to sweep through Belgium and the Netherlands, and he abandoned the plan to pass through Dutch Limburg. Critics have long blamed these changes for diluting the offensive's striking power, though historians continue to debate their significance.

When war came in August 1914, Moltke directed the German invasion of Belgium and France from headquarters in Luxembourg, increasingly losing control of his armies as communications broke down. After the German defeat at the First Battle of the Marne in September 1914, Moltke was replaced by Erich von Falkenhayn on 14 September 1914, officially for reasons of health. He reportedly told the Kaiser, "Your Majesty, we have lost the war." He died in Berlin on 18 June 1916.

Assessment

Moltke has often been judged harshly for his nervous disposition, poor health, and failure to uphold the Schlieffen Plan's concentration on the right wing. More recent scholarship has emphasised the structural problems of German planning and the near-impossibility of controlling armies of millions with the communications technology of 1914.
