Greek debt crisis

The Greek debt crisis is the sovereign debt crisis faced by Greece in the aftermath of the Great Recession of 2008–2009. Widely known in Greece as the Crisis (Η Κρίση), it reached a first peak in early 2010. It is part of the wider European debt crisis.

Causes

Following Greece's adoption of the euro in 2001, the country borrowed at interest rates close to those of Germany, while public spending, public-sector wages and pension commitments rose and tax collection remained weak. After the global financial crisis, a newly elected government in late 2009 revealed that the budget deficit was far larger than previously reported — revised to 15.4% of GDP for 2009 — and that public debt exceeded 100% of GDP. Credit rating agencies downgraded Greek bonds, and the country was effectively shut out of bond markets in spring 2010.

Bailouts and austerity

Between 2010 and 2015 Greece received three rescue programmes from the European Commission, European Central Bank and International Monetary Fund (the "Troika"), totalling around €289 billion in loans. In exchange, Greece enacted severe austerity: cuts to wages, pensions and public services, tax increases and privatisations. In 2012 private creditors accepted a restructuring (Private Sector Involvement) that cut roughly €107 billion from the face value of Greek debt — the largest sovereign restructuring in history.

The economy contracted by more than a quarter from its pre-crisis peak, unemployment rose above 27% (and youth unemployment near 60%), and a vast wave of emigration followed. In 2015 the government of Alexis Tsipras held a referendum rejecting creditor terms, but, facing bank closures and capital controls, accepted a third programme weeks later. Greece exited the bailout era in August 2018, though debt remained around 180% of GDP and fiscal surveillance continued.
