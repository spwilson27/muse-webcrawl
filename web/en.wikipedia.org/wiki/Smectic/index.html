Smectic

The smectic phases are a family of liquid crystal phases in which the molecules are arranged in well-defined layers. The word "smectic" derives from the Latin smecticus (cleaning, having detergent properties) via the Greek smēktikós, a reference to the soap-like appearance of the first materials in which these phases were observed. Smectic ordering is one step closer to a crystalline solid than the nematic phase: in addition to orientational order, smectics possess one-dimensional positional order, with molecules grouped into planes whose spacing is roughly one molecular length.

Smectic phases

Many smectic variants are distinguished by the arrangement of molecules within and between layers:

  • Smectic A (SmA): the molecules are oriented along the layer normal (the director coincides with the layer normal) and are randomly positioned within each layer. The phase is optically uniaxial.
  • Smectic C (SmC): the molecules are tilted at an angle to the layer normal. In chiral materials the tilt direction precesses from layer to layer, forming a helix.
  • Smectic B: molecules within each layer form a hexagonal net, giving additional in-plane order; in the hexatic B phase the order is short-range in position but long-range in bond orientation.
  • Further phases (SmI, SmF and others) combine tilt with hexagonal in-layer ordering, differing in the direction of tilt relative to the hexagonal lattice.

Textures and defects

Between crossed polarisers, smectic A typically shows fan-shaped or focal conic textures, arising from the ability of the layers to bend while keeping their spacing constant; layers curve around pairs of defect lines (an ellipse and a hyperbola). Homeotropic samples appear dark, while smectic C shows schlieren and broken-fan textures. Because the phase is layered, it responds to mechanical stress in ways intermediate between a liquid and a solid.

Chiral smectics and applications

In chiral smectic C (SmC*) materials, the combination of tilt and chirality produces a spontaneous electric polarisation in each layer, making the phase ferroelectric. Ferroelectric liquid crystals switch in microseconds under an applied field — far faster than nematics — and have been used in specialised microdisplays and spatial light modulators. Smectic materials more generally appear in temperature-sensing films, in cosmetic and detergent systems (lyotropic smectic-like lamellar phases of surfactants), and serve as model systems for the physics of layered soft matter, elasticity of one-dimensional crystals, and topological defects.
