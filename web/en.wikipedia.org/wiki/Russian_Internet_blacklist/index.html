Russian Internet blacklist

The Russian Internet blacklist (Roskomnadzor blacklist) is a unified register of prohibited information maintained by the Russian federal regulator Roskomnadzor. Created under Federal Law No. 139-FZ of 28 July 2012, which amended the law "On Protecting Children from Information Harmful to Their Health and Development", the register entered into force on 1 November 2012. Internet service providers in Russia are required to block access to websites, pages and IP addresses entered in the register.

Scope of the law

Under the original law, content could be blocked without a court order in three categories: child sexual abuse material, information promoting drug production or use, and information promoting suicide. Other categories, such as "extremist" materials, require court decisions. Subsequent amendments substantially widened the scope, extending blocking to websites accused of copyright infringement (the 2013 "anti-piracy" law), "undesirable" organisations, calls to unsanctioned protests, and, from 2022, "false information" about the Russian armed forces during the invasion of Ukraine.

Implementation

The register is administered by Roskomnadzor. ISPs must query the register regularly and block listed resources, using IP blocking, DNS tampering or deep packet inspection depending on their technical capacity. Because IP-based blocking can affect unrelated sites sharing hosting or content delivery networks, the system has repeatedly caused large-scale collateral blocking—most notoriously in April 2018, when Roskomnadzor's attempt to block the Telegram messenger led to millions of IP addresses of Amazon and Google cloud services being blocked, disrupting banking, retail and other services across Russia, while Telegram itself largely continued to work.

Blocked services

Notable resources blocked at various times include LinkedIn (2016, for refusal to localise Russian users' data), the Zello app, Telegram (2018–2020, after which the ban was formally lifted), and, following the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, Facebook, Instagram and Twitter/X, as well as numerous independent media outlets such as Meduza and BBC Russian-language pages. Tor, VPN services and various news sites have also been targeted.

Criticism

The law has been criticised by the Russian Internet industry, human rights groups and foreign governments as a vehicle for censorship and a step toward a sovereign, state-controlled "Runet". Supporters argue it is necessary to protect children and combat illegal content. The register's opacity—the full list is not public, only a lookup service for individual addresses—has also been widely criticised.
