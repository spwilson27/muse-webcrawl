Constant-velocity joint

[Hero image placeholder: cutaway diagram of a Rzeppa constant-velocity joint]

A constant-velocity joint (also called a CV joint and homokinetic joint) is a mechanical coupling whose purpose is to allow a drive shaft to transmit power through a variable angle, at constant rotational speed, without an appreciable increase in friction or play. CV joints are primarily used in front-wheel drive and all-wheel-drive vehicles, and many rear-wheel-drive cars with independent rear suspension.

Types

Rzeppa joint

Patented by Alfred H. Rzeppa in 1926, the Rzeppa joint consists of an inner spherical race with six grooves, six steel balls held in a cage, and an outer spherical housing. As the shafts articulate, the cage steers the balls into the plane that bisects the angle between the shafts — ensuring the balls always transmit torque at equal distances from the joint's centre, resulting in constant velocity.

Tripod joint

The tripod (or tripoid) joint has a three-pronged spider with spherical rollers running in tracks inside a tulip-shaped housing. It tolerates plunge (axial movement) and is commonly used at the inboard end of drive shafts.

Double Cardan

A double Cardan joint pairs two universal joints with a centring mechanism to cancel their speed fluctuations.

History and use

Early applications appeared in the 1920s front-wheel-drive cars of Tracta and in the Cord L-29. Mass adoption came with the front-wheel-drive boom of the 1970s and 1980s (e.g., the Mini and the Honda Civic). CV joints are protected from dirt and moisture by rubber boots filled with grease; a torn boot is the most common cause of CV joint failure, announced by a clicking noise when turning.
