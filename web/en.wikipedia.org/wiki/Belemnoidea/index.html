Belemnoidea

Belemnoidea is a superorder of extinct marine cephalopods, the group that includes the well-known belemnites. Belemnoids were squid-like animals with ten arms and an internal, chambered shell, and they were abundant and diverse in the oceans of the Mesozoic era, from the Triassic until their extinction at the end of the Cretaceous, about 66 million years ago. Their bullet-shaped shell guards (rostra) are among the most common fossils in Jurassic and Cretaceous rocks.

Anatomy and classification

The belemnoid shell consisted of a chambered phragmocone (counterpart of the external shell of ammonites), a thin pro-ostracum extending forward, and a solid calcite guard or rostrum at the rear, which acted as a counterweight. The superorder is generally divided into the orders Aulacocerida, Phragmoteuthida, Belemnitida (the true belemnites) and Diplobelida. Belemnoids had hooks on their arms rather than suckers, an ink sac, and were active predators of fish and other marine animals; in turn they were preyed upon by ichthyosaurs and other marine reptiles, whose fossilised stomach contents often contain belemnite remains.

Evolutionary significance

Belemnoidea belong to the coleoid cephalopods, and their internal shell is homologous with the cuttlebone of cuttlefish and the gladius of squid. Extinct belemnoids are thus close relatives of living squid and cuttlefish, and their fossil record illuminates how the external shell of ancestral cephalopods was reduced and internalised. The bullet-shaped guards were once interpreted in folklore as thunderbolts, and their sudden disappearance marks the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event.
