Sauerland

The Sauerland is a rural, hilly region in western Germany, spanning southeastern North Rhine-Westphalia and a small part of northern Hesse. It is one of Germany's largest contiguous upland areas, characterised by densely forested low mountains, river valleys, and reservoirs. Its name, first attested in the 13th century, probably derives from a word meaning "southern land" (as seen from the bishoprics to the north) rather than "sour land", as folk etymology suggests.

Geography

The Sauerland forms the northeastern part of the Rhenish Massif. Its highest peak, the Langenberg (843 m), is the highest point in North Rhine-Westphalia; nearby Winterberg is a well-known winter sports resort. Rivers such as the Ruhr and Lenne rise here, and large reservoirs — the Möhnesee, Biggesee, and Sorpesee — supply drinking water and recreation for the Ruhr industrial region to the west. Two nature parks, the Sauerland-Rothaargebirge and the Arnsberg Forest, protect much of the landscape.

History and economy

Mining of iron ore and slate and the smelting industry shaped the region from the Middle Ages onward; towns such as Lüdenscheid and Iserlohn became noted for metal goods, wire, and fittings, and today the Sauerland is home to many mid-sized manufacturers, including lighting, sanitary fittings, and automotive suppliers. Tourism is also important: hiking on the Rothaarsteig trail, cycling, ski resorts around Winterberg and Willingen, and historic half-timbered towns such as Brilon and Schmallenberg draw visitors from the Ruhr and the Netherlands. The region markets itself as the "land of a thousand mountains".
