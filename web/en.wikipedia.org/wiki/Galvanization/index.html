Galvanization


Galvanization coats iron or steel with zinc to prevent rusting.

Galvanization (or galvanisation) is the process of applying a protective zinc coating to steel or iron to prevent rusting. The most common method is hot-dip galvanizing, in which parts are submerged in a bath of hot molten zinc. The term derives from the name of Italian scientist Luigi Galvani.

Mechanism

The zinc coating protects the underlying metal in two ways: as a physical barrier against the environment, and by cathodic (sacrificial) protection — zinc is more electrochemically active than iron, so even where the coating is scratched, the surrounding zinc corrodes preferentially and protects the exposed steel. Over time the zinc surface reacts with the atmosphere to form a stable patina of zinc carbonate.

Process

In hot-dip galvanizing, steel is cleaned (degreased, pickled in acid, and fluxed) and then immersed in molten zinc at about 450 °C. The zinc metallurgically bonds with the steel, forming a series of zinc-iron alloy layers topped by a layer of pure zinc. Related processes include electrogalvanizing (electroplating a thinner zinc layer), sherardizing, and zinc spraying. A characteristic crystalline "spangle" pattern often appears on hot-dip galvanized surfaces.

Uses

Galvanized steel is used for roofing and cladding, fencing, guardrails, utility poles, nails and fasteners, ductwork, and vehicle bodies, in applications where long, maintenance-free corrosion protection is required. See also corrugated galvanised iron and electroplating.
