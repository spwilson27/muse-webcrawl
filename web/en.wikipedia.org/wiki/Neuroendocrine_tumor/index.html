Neuroendocrine tumor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) are neoplasms that arise from cells of the endocrine (hormonal) and nervous systems. They most commonly occur in the intestine, where they are often called carcinoid tumors, but they are also found in the pancreas, lung and the rest of the body. Although there are many kinds of NETs, they are treated as a group of tissue because the cells of these neoplasms share common features, such as looking similar, having special secretory granules, and often producing biogenic amines and polypeptide hormones.

The incidence of NETs is approximately 5–7 cases per 100,000 people per year and has been rising, in part because of improved detection. Many NETs are slow-growing and may produce symptoms only when they metastasise or secrete hormones in excess.

Classification

The World Health Organization classifies gastroenteropancreatic NETs by grade, based on the proliferation markers mitotic count and Ki-67 index: grade 1 (low), grade 2 (intermediate) and grade 3 (high). Well-differentiated tumours are termed NETs, while poorly differentiated high-grade tumours are termed neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs). Sites of origin include:

  • Gastrointestinal NETs — small intestine (the commonest site), appendix, rectum, colon and stomach.
  • Pancreatic NETs — including insulinomas, gastrinomas (Zollinger–Ellison syndrome), glucagonomas and VIPomas, some of them "functioning" hormone-secreting tumours.
  • Lung NETs — typical and atypical carcinoids, and the high-grade small-cell and large-cell neuroendocrine carcinomas.
  • Others — medullary thyroid carcinoma, pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma, pituitary tumors, and Merkel cell carcinoma of the skin.

Symptoms

Many NETs are asymptomatic and discovered incidentally. Functioning tumours cause syndromes from hormone excess: the carcinoid syndrome (flushing, diarrhoea, wheezing and, eventually, right-sided heart valve fibrosis) usually appears when gastrointestinal NETs metastasise to the liver, allowing serotonin and other amines to bypass first-pass metabolism. Pancreatic NET syndromes include hypoglycaemia (insulinoma) and refractory peptic ulcers (gastrinoma).

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis combines cross-sectional imaging with functional imaging exploiting somatostatin-receptor expression — gallium-68 or copper-64 DOTATATE PET/CT is now standard — together with biomarkers such as chromogranin A and urinary 5-HIAA, and biopsy. Treatment depends on site, grade and stage: surgical resection is the only curative option for localised disease; somatostatin analogues (octreotide, lanreotide) control symptoms and slow growth; other options include targeted agents (everolimus, sunitinib), chemotherapy, liver-directed therapies and peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) with lutetium-177 dotatate.
