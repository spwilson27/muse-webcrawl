Frederick II (26 December 1194 – 13 December 1250) was King of Sicily from 1198, King of Germany from 1212, and Holy Roman Emperor from 1220 until his death. A member of the Hohenstaufen dynasty, he was called stupor mundi — "the wonder of the world" — for his brilliance, learning, and unorthodoxy.

Early life

Frederick was the son of Emperor Henry VI and Constance of Sicily. Orphaned as a small child, he was raised as the ward of Pope Innocent III in Palermo, in a court where Norman, Arab, Byzantine and Latin cultures mingled. He grew up speaking several languages and steeped in the learning of the Mediterranean world.

Reign

After defeating Otto IV to secure the German crown, Frederick was crowned emperor in Rome in 1220. He concentrated on his hereditary Kingdom of Sicily, issuing the Constitutions of Melfi (1231), a landmark rationalising law code, founding the University of Naples, and building the enigmatic Castel del Monte. His Sixth Crusade (1228–29) recovered Jerusalem by negotiation with Sultan al-Kamil rather than battle — an achievement that nonetheless drew papal anger, as he had undertaken it while excommunicated.

Conflict with the papacy and death

Frederick's attempts to dominate Lombardy brought him into decades of war with the Lombard League and successive popes, who excommunicated him repeatedly and in 1245 had him deposed by the Council of Lyon. He died in Apulia in 1250, still at war. With his death the Hohenstaufen empire in Italy and Germany collapsed into the Great Interregnum.

Legacy

Frederick's court was a centre of science and poetry: he patronised translators of Aristotle and Avicenna, corresponded with scholars, and wrote a celebrated treatise on falconry, De arte venandi cum avibus. Historians have variously portrayed him as a proto-modern ruler ahead of his age and as a conventional medieval monarch of exceptional talent.

Not to be confused with Frederick the Great of Prussia.
