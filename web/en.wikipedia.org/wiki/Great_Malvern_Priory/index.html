Great Malvern Priory in Malvern, Worcestershire, England, was a Benedictine monastery from around 1085 until its dissolution in 1540; its great church survives as the parish church of St Mary and St Michael and is a Grade I listed building. Founded in the reign of William the Conqueror — tradition links its origins to a hermitage of St Werstan — the priory was a cell subordinate to Westminster Abbey for much of its history, a source of recurring jurisdictional disputes.

Architecture and glass

The church is architecturally remarkable for the completeness of its Perpendicular Gothic rebuilding in the 15th century over a largely Norman core, the great central tower being modelled on that of Gloucester Cathedral. It possesses the largest display of 15th-century medieval stained glass in England, including the great east window given by Henry VII and the St Anne's Chapel glass associated with Prince Arthur, as well as an outstanding set of medieval wall tiles (more than 1,300 designs) and carved misericords. The priory also preserves the Norman chapter-house site and the Priory Gatehouse, now a museum.

Dissolution and survival

At the Dissolution the townspeople purchased the church from the Crown for £20 to serve as their parish church — a remarkable survival, since the monastic buildings were demolished and the lead, bells, and stone sold off. The church was restored in the 19th century by George Gilbert Scott. Its bells, organ, and musical tradition are notable; the composer Edward Elgar, a local resident, is buried with his wife at nearby St Wulstan's, and the priory hosts concerts in the Malvern festival tradition.
