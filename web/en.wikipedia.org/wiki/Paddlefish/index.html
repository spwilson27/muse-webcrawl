Paddlefish (family Polyodontidae) are a family of ray-finned fish belonging to the order Acipenseriformes, which also includes the sturgeons. They are characterised by an elongated, paddle-shaped rostrum covered in electroreceptors used to detect plankton. Paddlefish are among the most primitive of living ray-finned fishes, with a fossil record extending back to the Early Cretaceous, roughly 120–125 million years ago.

Species

Only two species have survived into modern times. The American paddlefish (Polyodon spathula) inhabits the Mississippi River basin in North America and is listed as vulnerable. The Chinese paddlefish (Psephurus gladius), once found in the Yangtze River and among the largest freshwater fishes in the world, was declared extinct in 2022, having last been reliably recorded in 2003; its decline was driven by overfishing and the construction of dams such as the Gezhouba and Three Gorges dams, which blocked its spawning migrations.

Biology

Paddlefish are filter feeders, swimming with their mouths open to strain zooplankton from the water with fine gill rakers. Their rostrum is packed with ampullae—electroreceptors similar to those of sharks—that detect the weak electrical fields of individual plankton. The skeleton is mostly cartilaginous. American paddlefish can grow to 1.6 m (5.2 ft) or more and live for 30 years or longer; they are prized for their roe, which is processed as caviar, and are raised in aquaculture.
