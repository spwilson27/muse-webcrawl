Formal garden

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A formal garden is a garden laid out with strong geometric structure, symmetry and clearly defined axes, in which plants are arranged and clipped to emphasise order and human control over nature. Formal gardens are characteristic of the great palace landscapes of Renaissance Italy, Baroque France and much of Europe, and contrast with the naturalistic landscape gardens that became fashionable in eighteenth-century England.

Characteristics

Typical elements include parterres of low clipped hedges, topiary, gravel paths, terraces, avenues of trees, reflecting pools, canals, fountains and statuary, all organised around a central axis extending from the principal building. The garden is designed to be viewed from above, from a terrace or the piano nobile of the house, so that the pattern reads as a single composition.

History

The formal garden descends from ancient Persian paradise gardens and Roman villa gardens, transmitted through Islamic gardens such as those of Al-Andalus. Renaissance designers in Italy, including those who worked at the Villa d'Este and the Boboli Gardens, revived geometric layouts. The style reached its zenith in seventeenth-century France under André Le Nôtre, whose gardens at Vaux-le-Vicomte and Versailles set the pattern for jardins à la française across Europe. In the eighteenth century the informal English landscape garden of Capability Brown reacted against this formality, though formal layouts survived around houses and were revived by Arts and Crafts designers such as Gertrude Jekyll, who combined formal structure with lush, informal planting.
