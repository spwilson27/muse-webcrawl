Thin-film solar cell

Thin-film solar cell
Type: photovoltaic cell with absorber layers ≈ 1–10 micrometres thick
Main technologies: cadmium telluride (CdTe), CIGS, amorphous silicon (a-Si), gallium arsenide (GaAs)
Advantages: low material use, light weight, flexibility, good low-light and high-temperature behaviour
Typical module efficiency: below crystalline silicon (roughly 10–20% vs 20%+)

A thin-film solar cell is a photovoltaic cell made by depositing one or more very thin layers — micrometres or even nanometres thick — of light-absorbing semiconductor material onto a supporting substrate such as glass, plastic, or metal. Because the absorber layers are so thin compared with the roughly 200-micrometre wafers of conventional crystalline silicon cells, thin-film modules use far less semiconductor material and can be light, flexible, and integrated into building surfaces.

Technologies

Cadmium telluride (CdTe)

The commercially dominant thin-film technology, championed by the manufacturer First Solar. CdTe has an ideal bandgap for sunlight and absorbs strongly, so layers of a few micrometres suffice. Cell efficiencies above 22 percent have been achieved in the laboratory, with modules in the high teens.

CIGS

Copper indium gallium selenide cells offer the highest thin-film efficiencies — laboratory records around 23 percent — and can be made flexible, but their more complex four-element composition makes manufacturing control harder.

Amorphous silicon

Hydrogenated amorphous silicon (a-Si) is the oldest thin-film technology, long used in calculators and watches; its efficiencies are modest (around 10 percent or below) and it suffers light-induced degradation (the Staebler–Wronski effect), so it has lost ground for power generation.

Other

Gallium arsenide thin films reach the highest single-junction efficiencies of any technology but at high cost, suiting them to space and specialty uses. Emerging thin films include perovskites and organic photovoltaics, pursued for their low-temperature, solution-based processing.

Advantages and disadvantages

Thin-film modules generally perform relatively better than crystalline silicon in diffuse light and at high temperatures, shade more gracefully, and open applications — building-integrated photovoltaics, portable and wearable power — that rigid glass panels cannot serve. Their lower efficiency means more area is needed for the same output, and concerns have been raised about toxic constituents (cadmium in CdTe) and scarce ones (tellurium, indium), though manufacturers operate recycling schemes. Thin-film's share of the world market, once around 15–20 percent, fell to a few percent as crystalline silicon prices collapsed in the 2010s, but CdTe in particular remains a major utility-scale product.
