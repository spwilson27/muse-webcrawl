Continuous signal

A continuous signal or continuous-time signal is a varying quantity (a signal) whose domain — usually time — is a continuum, that is, the signal is defined for every instant within an interval rather than only at separated instants. The signal's values may themselves form a continuum (an analogue signal) or a finite set; "continuous" refers strictly to the domain. It contrasts with a discrete-time signal, which is defined only at discrete time instants, typically as a sequence of samples.

Definitions

Formally, a continuous-time signal is a function x(t) of a real variable t. Mathematical continuity of the function itself is not required: signals such as a square wave are continuous-time signals even though they have jump discontinuities. In the frequency domain, continuous-time signals are analysed with the Fourier transform and Laplace transform, whereas discrete-time signals use the discrete-time Fourier transform and Z-transform.

Conversion

Most real-world signals — sound pressure, temperature, voltage — are continuous in time. Digital systems process them by sampling (measuring the value at regular intervals) and quantisation (rounding values to finite precision), producing a digital signal. The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem states that a band-limited continuous signal can be perfectly reconstructed from samples taken at more than twice its highest frequency. Converting back, a digital-to-analogue converter typically applies interpolation and filtering to approximate the original waveform.

Applications

Continuous-time models remain fundamental in control theory, circuit analysis and communications engineering, where systems are described by differential equations; discrete counterparts are described by difference equations.
