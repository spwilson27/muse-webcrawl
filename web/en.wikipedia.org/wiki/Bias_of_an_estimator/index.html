Bias of an estimator

In statistics, the bias of an estimator is the difference between the estimator's expected value and the true value of the parameter being estimated. An estimator with zero bias is called unbiased. Formally, for an estimator θ̂ of a parameter θ, Bias(θ̂) = E[θ̂] − θ.

Examples

The sample mean is an unbiased estimator of the population mean. The sample variance computed with denominator n is biased (it underestimates the population variance on average); dividing by n − 1 instead — Bessel's correction — makes it unbiased. Maximum likelihood estimators are often biased in finite samples but typically consistent, with bias vanishing as the sample size grows.

Properties

Unbiasedness is a property of the sampling distribution of the estimator, not of any particular estimate. An unbiased estimator is not necessarily a good one: the mean squared error of an estimator decomposes into its variance plus the square of its bias, so a slightly biased estimator with much lower variance may be preferable — the bias–variance tradeoff. Median-unbiased and mode-unbiased alternatives are also defined by replacing the expectation with the median or mode of the sampling distribution.

Consistency

A sequence of estimators is consistent if it converges in probability to the true parameter value. Consistency implies asymptotic unbiasedness, but a consistent estimator may be biased for every finite sample size.
