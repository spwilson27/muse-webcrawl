Physics (Aristotle)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A manuscript page of the Physics.

The Physics (Greek: Φυσικὴ ἀκρόασις, Phusike akroasis; Latin: Physica) is a foundational text in the philosophy of nature by Aristotle, written in the fourth century BC. In eight books, it investigates the principles and causes of change (kinēsis) in natural things: matter and form, the four causes, nature, chance, the infinite, place, void, time, and motion, culminating in the argument for an unmoved mover.

Contents

Book I sets out the principles of natural things — matter, form, and privation. Book II defines nature and the four causes (material, formal, efficient, and final) and discusses chance and spontaneity. Book III treats motion and the infinite. Book IV examines place (topos), argues against the existence of void, and defines time as "the number of motion with respect to before and after". Books V and VI classify motion and analyse the continuum, arguing that motion cannot begin with a "first instant". Book VII considers that everything moved is moved by something. Book VIII argues that motion is eternal and must originate in a first, unmoved mover, which Aristotle identifies with the divine intellect.

Influence

The Physics organised natural philosophy for nearly two thousand years. Commented on by Philoponus, Avicenna, Averroes, and Aquinas, it framed medieval scholastic physics, including the doctrine of impetus that anticipated aspects of inertia. Its teleological account of nature and its denial of the void were central targets of the seventeenth-century Scientific Revolution, when Galileo and Newton replaced Aristotelian physics with mathematical mechanics.
