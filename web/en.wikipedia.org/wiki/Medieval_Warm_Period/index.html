The Medieval Warm Period (MWP), also known as the Medieval Climate Optimum or the Medieval Climate Anomaly, was a time of warm climate in the North Atlantic region that lasted from c. 950 to c. 1250. Climate proxy records show peak warmth reached at different times in different regions. The period was followed by a cooler period in the North Atlantic and elsewhere termed the Little Ice Age.

Evidence

The term was introduced by British climatologist Hubert Lamb, who studied historical records of European weather, harvests, and vineyards. Evidence includes Norse colonization of Greenland and the cultivation of grapes in England, tree-ring records, ice cores, lake sediments, and coral records. Warmth enabled Norse settlements in Greenland (from 985 until the 15th century) and possibly contributed to agricultural expansion and population growth in medieval Europe.

Global extent

Modern research indicates the warmth was largely regional rather than a globally synchronous phenomenon: recent reconstructions (such as the PAGES 2k consortium studies) find no evidence of a globally coherent warm or cold period in the pre-industrial Common Era, with the warmest decades occurring at different times in different regions. Proposed causes include increased solar activity, reduced volcanic eruptions, and changes in ocean circulation such as the North Atlantic Oscillation. The Medieval Warm Period was generally cooler than late 20th and early 21st century global temperatures.
