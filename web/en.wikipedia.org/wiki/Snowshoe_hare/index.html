Snowshoe hare

The snowshoe hare (Lepus americanus), also called the varying hare or snowshoe rabbit, is a species of hare found in North America. It has the name "snowshoe" because of the large size of its hind feet, which prevent it from sinking into the snow when it hops and walks; fur on the soles of the feet also provides insulation and traction.

Description

Snowshoe hares range in length from about 41 to 52 cm (16 to 20 in) and weigh roughly 1.4 to 1.6 kg. Their most striking feature is seasonal camouflage: the fur turns white in winter to blend with snow and mottled brown in summer to match earth and vegetation. The moult, triggered by photoperiod, takes about ten weeks. Under climate change, shorter snow seasons leave white hares conspicuous against snowless ground, increasing predation.

Habitat and diet

The species inhabits boreal forests and montane shrublands from Newfoundland to Alaska and south into the northern United States and along the Rockies, Sierra Nevada and Appalachians. Hares browse woody vegetation — twigs, bark and buds of willow, birch and spruce — in winter, and grasses, forbs and leaves in summer. They are largely nocturnal and solitary, and shelter in dense cover during the day.

Population cycles

Snowshoe hares are famous for their approximately ten-year population cycles across the Canadian boreal forest, in which numbers can vary ten- to thirty-fold. Their chief predator, the Canada lynx, cycles in phase with them — one of the classic examples of predator–prey dynamics in ecology, documented in Hudson's Bay Company fur records.

Reproduction

Breeding occurs from spring to late summer, with females producing up to four litters a year of one to eight leverets. Young are precocial — born furred, with eyes open — and leave the birth site within a day.
