Gothic War (376–382)

The Gothic War of 376–382, sometimes called the Gothic War in the Balkans, was fought between the Goths and the Roman Empire in the Balkans. It began when Gothic peoples, fleeing the Huns, were admitted into Roman territory south of the Danube and, mistreated by Roman officials, rose in revolt. The war included the disastrous Roman defeat at the Battle of Adrianople (378), in which the emperor Valens was killed. It ended in 382 with a treaty that settled the Goths within the empire as foederati, a landmark in the history of Rome's relations with barbarian peoples and a key episode in the empire's late-antique crisis.

Background

In the 370s the Huns, a nomadic people from the Central Asian steppe, moved westward and overwhelmed the Gothic kingdoms north of the Black Sea. In 376, large numbers of the Thervingian Goths under Fritigern and Alavivus petitioned the emperor Valens for permission to cross the Danube and settle in Roman Thrace, hoping for protection and land in exchange for military service. Valens, short of recruits and seeing a chance to turn refugees into soldiers and taxpayers, agreed. Perhaps 200,000 people — the figure is debated — crossed the river near Durostorum.

Causes of the revolt

The crossing and resettlement were badly mishandled. Roman officials, notably the generals Lupicinus and Maximus, profiteered from the Goths' misery, selling them food at extortionate prices — in the historian Ammianus Marcellinus's account, even dog meat, with slaves traded for provisions. When Lupicinus attempted to murder Fritigern and Alavivus at a banquet in Marcianople, the plot failed (Alavivus disappears from the record; Fritigern escaped) and the Goths rose in revolt. They were soon joined by the Greuthungi under Alatheus and Saphrax, who crossed the Danube unopposed, as well as by disaffected Roman miners, slaves and other Gothic groups already inside the empire.

Course of the war

Early fighting, 376–377

The Goths defeated Lupicinus outside Marcianople and spread through Thrace, plundering the countryside, though Roman forces retained the walled cities. In 377 Roman armies under Traianus and Profuturus, reinforced by Western troops sent by the emperor Gratian under Frigeridus and Richomeres, fought the Goths to a bloody draw at the Battle of Ad Salices ("at the Willows") near the Dobruja. The Romans adopted a strategy of containment, blockading the Goths in the region between the Danube and the Haemus (Balkan) mountains and attempting to starve them out, while a Sarmatian incursion into Pannonia drew away Western reinforcements.

Adrianople, 378

In 378 Valens marched east from Constantinople determined to settle the war before Gratian's arrival. On 9 August 378, near Adrianople, Valens attacked the Gothic wagon laager without waiting for his nephew's forces. The Roman army was encircled when Gothic and Alan cavalry returned from a foraging expedition and fell on its flanks; the result was one of the worst defeats in Roman history. Two-thirds of the eastern field army may have been destroyed, and Valens himself was killed — either on the field or, in one tradition, burned in a farmhouse where he had taken refuge. Ammianus compared the catastrophe to Cannae.

Theodosius and the settlement, 379–382

Gratian appointed the Spanish general Theodosius as emperor of the East in January 379. Theodosius rebuilt the eastern army with heavy recruitment, including many barbarians, and fought a grinding war of small actions from Thessalonica. In 380 the Goths raided into Macedonia and Thessaly and defeated a force under Theodosius himself, who was also distracted by illness; Gratian's generals Bauto and Arbogast helped stabilize the situation. By 382 both sides were exhausted. On 3 October 382 a peace was concluded: the Goths were settled in Thrace (and perhaps northern Macedonia) as foederati, receiving land and subsidies in return for military service under their own leaders.

Significance

The treaty of 382 was unprecedented: for the first time, a large barbarian people was settled inside the empire as a semi-autonomous body under its own rulers, rather than being dispersed or enslaved. Many historians see Adrianople and the settlement as milestones on the road to the fall of the Western Roman Empire: the Goths who were settled in 382, under the young Alaric, would revolt again in the 390s and sack Rome in 410. Others caution that the empire recovered militarily from Adrianople and that the settlement was a pragmatic success in its own terms. The war is described in detail by Ammianus Marcellinus, whose Res Gestae ends with Adrianople.
