Giant sequoia

Scientific name: Sequoiadendron giganteum
Family: Cupressaceae
Native range: Western Sierra Nevada, California
Maximum height: ~95 m; lifespan over 3,000 years

The giant sequoia (Sequoiadendron giganteum) is the sole living species in the genus Sequoiadendron and one of three species of coniferous trees known as redwoods. It is among the most massive trees on Earth: mature specimens grow 50–85 metres tall with trunk diameters of 6–8 metres, and the largest individual, the General Sherman Tree in Sequoia National Park, contains an estimated 1,487 cubic metres of wood. Giant sequoias occur naturally only in about 68 groves on the western slopes of California's Sierra Nevada, at elevations of roughly 1,400–2,000 metres.

Description and ecology

The species has thick, fibrous, cinnamon-red bark — up to 90 cm thick at the base — that is highly resistant to fire, and fire itself plays a key role in its reproduction: heat opens the serotinous cones and clears the forest floor so the tiny seeds can germinate in mineral soil. Giant sequoias are extraordinarily long-lived; ring counts have established ages exceeding 3,200 years. Their wood is brittle and shatters when felled, which limited (but did not prevent) destructive logging in the late 19th century.

Conservation

Public outrage at the felling of giant sequoias helped spur the creation of Sequoia and Yosemite National Parks in 1890 and the modern conservation movement. Today all native groves are protected, but the species faces new threats from climate change: hotter droughts and unusually severe wildfires driven by a century of fire suppression killed an estimated 13–19% of the world's large giant sequoias in the wildfires of 2020–2021 alone.
