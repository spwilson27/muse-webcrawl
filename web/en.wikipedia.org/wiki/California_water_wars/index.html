California water wars

The California water wars were a series of political conflicts between the city of Los Angeles and farmers and ranchers in the Owens Valley of eastern California over water rights, chiefly in the early 20th century.

LocationOwens Valley and Los Angeles, California
Periodc. 1905–1928
Key figuresWilliam Mulholland, Fred Eaton
ResultLos Angeles Aqueduct built; Owens Lake largely dried

Background

Los Angeles grew rapidly after 1900 and sought a reliable water supply beyond the Los Angeles River. Former mayor Fred Eaton and water superintendent William Mulholland identified the Owens River, fed by Sierra Nevada snowmelt, as a source.

Acquisition and aqueduct

Agents for Los Angeles, posing as ranchers and federal officials, quietly bought land and water rights in the Owens Valley. The Los Angeles Aqueduct, completed in 1913, carried water 233 miles (375 km) by gravity to the city. See Los Angeles Aqueduct and William Mulholland.

Conflict

Valley residents resented the secrecy, the loss of irrigation water, and the collapse of local agriculture. In the 1920s angry groups seized the Alabama Gates and dynamited sections of the aqueduct. The conflict eased after Los Angeles bought much of the valley and after the 1928 St. Francis Dam disaster damaged Mulholland's reputation.

Legacy

Owens Lake dried up, becoming a major source of dust pollution, later partly mitigated. The story inspired the film Chinatown (1974) and remains a symbol of urban water power, environmental cost, and conflict — later echoed in disputes over Mono Lake.
