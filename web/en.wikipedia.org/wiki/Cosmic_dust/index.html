Cosmic dust

Cosmic dust — also called extraterrestrial dust, space dust, or interstellar dust — consists of tiny solid particles floating in space, generally smaller than 0.1 mm, composed mainly of carbon, silicates, and ices. Cosmic dust pervades the universe: it fills interstellar space, gathers in planetary systems, and continuously falls to Earth as micrometeorites, at an estimated rate of tens of thousands of tonnes per year.

Types

Interstellar dust moves through the galaxy and obscures starlight, producing extinction and reddening, while absorbing ultraviolet and re-emitting in the infrared. Interplanetary dust within the Solar System — shed by comets and asteroid collisions — scatters sunlight to produce the zodiacal light. Circumstellar dust around young stars collects into protoplanetary disks from which planets form; dust that falls to Earth is studied as micrometeorites collected from Antarctic ice and the stratosphere.

Significance

Dust grains are the raw material of planets and provide surfaces on which interstellar chemistry proceeds, forming molecules including water and organics. Spacecraft missions such as Stardust returned cometary dust samples to Earth, and the Ulysses and Cassini missions detected interstellar dust streaming through the Solar System.
