Rundbogenstil

Rundbogenstil (German for "round-arch style") is a nineteenth-century historicist architectural style that emerged in Germany in the 1820s and 1830s. It is characterized by the use of semicircular (round) arches for windows, doors, and arcades, drawing on Romanesque, early Christian, Byzantine, and Italian Renaissance precedents.

Development

The style is closely associated with the architect Heinrich Hübsch, whose 1828 pamphlet In welchem Style sollen wir bauen? ("In What Style Shall We Build?") challenged the dominance of Neoclassicism and proposed a style better suited to modern materials and building types. Architects such as Friedrich von Gärtner in Munich and Karl Friedrich Schinkel's successors applied the style to churches, museums, railway stations, factories, and synagogues.

Spread

German emigrant architects carried the Rundbogenstil to the United States, where related round-arch modes appeared in Romanesque Revival architecture, and examples can be found in cities such as Cincinnati and in Texas. The style's flexibility — it could be executed economically in brick — made it a common choice for industrial and institutional buildings through the mid-nineteenth century.
