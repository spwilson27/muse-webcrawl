The Umayyad invasion of Gaul (719–759) was a series of campaigns by the Umayyad Caliphate, operating from al-Andalus, into the south of Frankish Gaul. After conquering Visigothic Hispania, Umayyad armies crossed the Pyrenees, seized Septimania, and raided deep into Aquitaine and Burgundy before Frankish resistance under Odo of Aquitaine, Charles Martel, and Pepin the Short expelled them, ending with the fall of Narbonne in 759.

The Battle of Tours (732) is the most famous episode of the invasion, traditionally seen as halting Muslim expansion into western Europe.

Course of the campaigns

Umayyad forces took Narbonne in 719–720 and made it the base of Muslim Septimania. In 721 Duke Odo of Aquitaine crushed an Umayyad army besieging Toulouse, killing its commander Al-Samh ibn Malik. Raids nonetheless continued into the Rhône valley and as far as Autun. In 732 a major expedition under Abd al-Rahman al-Ghafiqi defeated Odo at the Garonne, sacked Bordeaux, and advanced toward the Loire before Charles Martel's Franks defeated it at the Battle of Tours (also called Poitiers) in October 732, where Abd al-Rahman was killed.

Martel subsequently campaigned in the south, winning at the River Berre (737) though failing to retake Narbonne. His son Pepin the Short, allied with local Gothic nobles, reduced the remaining strongholds and took Narbonne in 759 after a long siege, completing the reconquest of Septimania.

Significance

The campaigns marked the north-western limit of Umayyad expansion in Europe. The Battle of Tours acquired an outsized reputation in later European historiography as a decisive turning point; modern historians generally place it in the broader context of raid and counter-raid along a frontier that stabilised along the Pyrenees, while noting that the Frankish victories helped consolidate Carolingian power.
