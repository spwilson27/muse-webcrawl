Toroid

In mathematics, a toroid is a surface of revolution with a hole in the middle. The axis of revolution passes through the hole and so does not intersect the surface. For example, when a rectangle is rotated around an axis parallel to one of its edges, then a hollow rectangle-section ring is produced. If the revolved figure is a circle, then the object is called a torus.

The term toroid is also used to describe a toroidal polyhedron. In this context a toroid need not be circular and may have any number of holes. A g-holed toroid can be seen as approximating the surface of a torus with a topological genus, g, of 1 or greater.

Equations

A toroid is specified by the radius of revolution R, measured from the centre of the hollow, and the radius a of the cross-section. When a > R, the toroid is known as a spindle toroid; when a = R it is a horn toroid (the cross-section is tangent to the axis), and when a < R it is a ring toroid. For the standard circular toroid (a torus), the surface area is 4π²Ra and the enclosed volume 2π²Ra².

Applications

Toroidal shapes are widespread in engineering and physics. A toroidal core wound with wire forms a toroidal inductor or transformer, confining magnetic flux within the core. In nuclear fusion research, toroidal magnetic fields confine plasma in devices such as tokamaks and stellarators. Toroidal propellers and toroidal antennas also exploit the geometry.
