American literature is literature written or produced in the United States and its preceding colonies, in English and in the many other languages spoken in the country, including Indigenous oral traditions. For much of its history it was closely connected to English literature, but it developed a distinctive voice shaped by the American experience of revolution, westward expansion, slavery, immigration, and industrialization.

Colonial and early national period (to 1820)

Early writing was dominated by Puritan sermons and histories (William Bradford, Cotton Mather), captivity narratives such as Mary Rowlandson's, and religious poetry (Anne Bradstreet, Edward Taylor). The revolutionary era produced political writing of lasting influence: Thomas Paine's Common Sense, the Declaration of Independence, and The Federalist Papers. Benjamin Franklin's Autobiography became a model of the self-made American life.

Romanticism and the American Renaissance (1820–1865)

Washington Irving and James Fenimore Cooper won international recognition, and by the 1850s an extraordinary flowering produced Nathaniel Hawthorne's The Scarlet Letter, Herman Melville's Moby-Dick, Harriet Beecher Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin, and the poetry of Walt Whitman (Leaves of Grass) and Emily Dickinson. Ralph Waldo Emerson and Henry David Thoreau led transcendentalism. Frederick Douglass's slave narrative (1845) became a central abolitionist text.

Realism, modernism, and beyond

Mark Twain's Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (1884) is often called the Great American Novel; Henry James and Edith Wharton explored social realism. The 20th century brought modernism (T. S. Eliot, Ezra Pound, William Faulkner, Ernest Hemingway), the Harlem Renaissance (Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston), the Beat Generation, and postmodernists such as Thomas Pynchon. Toni Morrison, winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature (1993), redefined the canon with novels like Beloved. American writers who have won the Nobel Prize include Sinclair Lewis, Eugene O'Neill, Pearl S. Buck, William Faulkner, Ernest Hemingway, John Steinbeck, Saul Bellow, Isaac Bashevis Singer, Toni Morrison, and Bob Dylan.

Genres and popular literature

The United States is a major centre of genre fiction — detective fiction, science fiction, westerns, and horror — and of the commercial bestseller market, exemplified by authors such as Stephen King, whose novels The Shining and It are landmarks of modern horror.
