E-democracy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Online participation in a public consultation.

E-democracy (from "electronic democracy"), also called digital democracy or internet democracy, is the use of information and communication technology (ICT) in political and governance processes. The term covers a family of practices intended to support democratic participation: online deliberation, electronic voting, e-petitions, participatory budgeting platforms, open government data, and digital consultation by parliaments and local authorities.

Forms

Electronic voting is the most prominent form. Estonia has offered internet voting (i-voting) in national elections since 2005, and by the 2023 parliamentary election roughly half of all votes were cast online. Switzerland has trialled internet voting in referendums, and several countries use electronic counting or voting machines at polling stations.

Deliberative and consultative platforms include tools such as Decidim (used by the city of Barcelona), Consul (originating in Madrid), and Taiwan's vTaiwan process, which uses the Polis platform to map consensus on contested issues such as ride-hailing regulation. Iceland's crowdsourced constitutional process of 2011–2012, in which a draft constitution was developed with public input via social media, is frequently cited as an early experiment, although the draft was never enacted.

E-petitions are operated by many legislatures, including the UK Parliament (where petitions reaching 100,000 signatures are considered for debate) and the German Bundestag.

Advantages and criticism

Supporters argue that digital tools lower the cost of participation, engage younger citizens, and make government more transparent and accountable. Critics point to the digital divide, the risk of manipulation and astroturfing, security and coercion problems in remote voting, and the tendency of online debate to polarise. Political scientists also distinguish between "thin" participation, such as clicking or signing, and the thicker deliberation that democratic theory values.
