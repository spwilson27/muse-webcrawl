Malik Kafur

Malik Kafur (died February 1316), also known as Taj al-Din Izz al-Dawla, was a prominent slave-general and viceroy of the Delhi Sultanate under Alauddin Khalji. Originally a Hindu from Gujarat, he was captured during the Sultanate's conquest of Gujarat in 1299, converted to Islam, and rose through ability to become Alauddin's most trusted commander and, in the sultan's last years, the effective ruler of the empire as naib (regent).

Campaigns in the Deccan and the South

Kafur led the great southern expeditions that first carried Delhi's arms deep into peninsular India: against the Yadavas of Devagiri (1308), the Kakatiyas of Warangal (1310), the Hoysalas of Dwarasamudra (1311), and the Pandyas of Madurai (1311), whose temple cities he plundered as far south as Rameswaram. These campaigns brought enormous treasure — gold, elephants, and horses — to Delhi and reduced the southern kingdoms to tributary status, though they did not annex the far south directly.

Rise and fall

Chroniclers such as Ziauddin Barani portray Kafur — nicknamed "Hazar Dinari" after the price reportedly paid for him — as the sultan's favourite, and, in Alauddin's final illness, as manipulating the succession, blinding rivals, and ruling through the child sultan Shihabuddin Omar. Within weeks of Alauddin's death in January 1316, Kafur was murdered by the palace guards, and Alauddin's son Qutbuddin Mubarak Shah took the throne.
