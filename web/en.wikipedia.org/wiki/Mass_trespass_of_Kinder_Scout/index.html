Mass trespass of Kinder Scout

The mass trespass of Kinder Scout was an act of wilful trespass by ramblers on 24 April 1932, intended to highlight the exclusion of walkers from areas of open countryside in England. Kinder Scout, the highest point in the Peak District of Derbyshire, was owned by the Duke of Devonshire and kept as a private grouse moor. The protest became a landmark event in the campaign for public access to the countryside and is credited with helping pave the way for national parks and statutory rights of way.

Background

In the early 20th century, large areas of moorland in northern England were closed to the public, reserved for grouse shooting for a few weeks each year. Rambling was a popular working-class pastime in industrial cities such as Manchester and Sheffield, and campaigners had long demanded a legal right to roam. A trespass on Winter Hill in 1896 had drawn some 10,000 people. By 1932 frustration had grown: an annual rally at Winnats Pass was addressed by campaigners, and access bills in Parliament had repeatedly failed.

The trespass

On 24 April 1932, around 400 ramblers, many of them members of the British Workers' Sports Federation, set out from Bowden Bridge quarry near Hayfield towards Kinder Scout. They were met on the mountain by gamekeepers; a scuffle broke out in which one keeper was slightly injured. The group reached the plateau and met a party from Sheffield before returning to Hayfield, where police arrested six men. The arrested men, including the organiser Benny Rothman, were tried at Derby Assizes and five were jailed for between two and six months.

Legacy

The harsh sentences provoked public sympathy. The trespass is widely seen as a catalyst for the National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949, the creation of the Peak District as Britain's first national park in 1951, the opening of the Pennine Way in 1965, and ultimately the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000, which established a statutory right to roam over much open country in England and Wales. Benny Rothman became a celebrated figure in the access movement, and the event is commemorated annually.
