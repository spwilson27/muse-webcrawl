Peltzman effect

The Peltzman effect is the hypothesised tendency for people to react to a safety regulation by increasing other risky behaviour, partially or wholly offsetting the regulation's benefit. It is named after Sam Peltzman, a professor of economics at the University of Chicago Booth School of Business, who set out the argument in a 1975 paper, "The Effects of Automobile Safety Regulation," published in the Journal of Political Economy.

The automobile study

Peltzman analysed the effect of United States automobile safety regulations introduced in the 1960s, including mandatory seat belts, padded dashboards, and collapsible steering columns. Standard engineering analysis predicted a substantial fall in occupant deaths per mile driven. Peltzman concluded instead that the regulations had no significant net effect on the total death rate: deaths among vehicle occupants fell, but this was offset by an increase in deaths among pedestrians and cyclists and by an increase in non-fatal accidents, which he attributed to drivers responding to greater safety by driving less carefully.

Interpretation and criticism

The effect is an application of the broader economic idea that people respond to incentives: when the perceived cost of an activity falls, they consume more of it. A related concept in safety research is risk homeostasis, associated with Gerald Wilde, which proposes that people maintain a target level of risk.

Peltzman's conclusions have been widely debated. Later studies generally find that seat belt laws and vehicle safety standards did produce large net reductions in fatalities, with behavioural offsetting being partial rather than complete. Nevertheless, the Peltzman effect remains a standard cautionary example in policy analysis: evaluations of safety interventions should account for behavioural adaptation rather than assuming behaviour stays fixed.

Other domains

Analogous offsetting behaviour has been investigated in contexts including bicycle and motorcycle helmets, protective equipment in contact sports, antiretroviral-based HIV prevention, and financial regulation, with mixed and contested results in each case.
