Oblique shock

An oblique shock wave is a shock wave that, unlike a normal shock, is inclined with respect to the direction of the incoming flow. Oblique shocks occur when a supersonic flow is turned into itself, most commonly when the flow encounters a wedge, a concave corner, or the nose of a body, and they are a central topic of compressible aerodynamics.

Behaviour

Across an oblique shock the flow velocity component normal to the shock drops to subsonic speed, while the tangential component passes through unchanged; the flow is deflected toward the shock and emerges slower, at higher pressure, density, and temperature. For a given deflection angle below a maximum value, two solutions exist — a strong shock (steep angle, usually with subsonic downstream flow) and a weak shock (shallow angle, usually remaining supersonic) — and most natural and engineering flows select the weak solution. If the required deflection exceeds the maximum for the upstream Mach number, no attached solution exists and the shock detaches, standing off the body as a curved bow shock. The governing relation between shock angle, deflection, and Mach number is the θ–β–M equation, often presented graphically as shock polar diagrams.

Applications

Oblique shocks are exploited in supersonic aircraft inlets, where a series of oblique shocks from ramps or a centrebody compresses the intake flow more efficiently and with less loss than a single normal shock; they shape the design of supersonic wings and nozzles, and they interact with boundary layers and expansion fans in phenomena such as shock–shock interaction and Mach reflection.
