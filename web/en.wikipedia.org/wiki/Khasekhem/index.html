Khasekhem was an ancient Egyptian king (pharaoh) of the Second Dynasty who reigned in the late 28th century BC. He is known almost exclusively from artefacts found at Hierakonpolis (Nekhen), including two seated statues and stone vessels, and is closely associated with — and very possibly the earlier name of — the last Second Dynasty king, Khasekhemwy.

Evidence

Khasekhem's name, written with the kha (rising sun) and sekhem (power sceptre) signs and surmounted by the falcon of Horus alone, appears on two seated statues of hard stone discovered by James Quibell at Hierakonpolis in 1898, as well as on stone vessel fragments. The bases of the statues are carved with contorted, slain figures of enemies, accompanied by tallies — one commonly read as recording tens of thousands of northern foes — suggesting commemoration of a victorious campaign.

Identity and Khasekhemwy

Most Egyptologists hold that Khasekhem and Khasekhemwy are the same king. Khasekhemwy's name uniquely bears both the Horus falcon and the Set animal, together with the epithet "the two powers have arisen; the two lords are at peace in him". The prevailing interpretation is that Khasekhem took the expanded name Khasekhemwy after suppressing a revolt — possibly a northern uprising associated with the Seth faction — thereby proclaiming the reconciliation of Horus and Set. A minority of scholars, however, regard Khasekhem and Khasekhemwy as two successive rulers.

Reign

If identical with Khasekhemwy, he ruled for roughly 17–18 years (some estimates give up to 30), built the great mudbrick enclosure known as the Shunet el-Zebib at Abydos and the Gisr el-Mudir at Saqqara, and was buried in Tomb V at Umm el-Qa'ab. His wife Nimaathap is remembered as an ancestress of the Third Dynasty, linking Khasekhem to the transition from the Early Dynastic Period to the Old Kingdom under Djoser.
