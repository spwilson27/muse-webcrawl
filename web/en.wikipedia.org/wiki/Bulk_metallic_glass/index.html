Bulk metallic glass
TypeAmorphous metal alloy
First produced1960 (thin ribbons); bulk forms 1990s
Notable exampleVitreloy (Zr-based)

A bulk metallic glass (BMG) is a metallic alloy with a disordered, non-crystalline (amorphous) atomic structure that can be cast in sections of at least millimetre thickness while retaining the glassy structure. Unlike ordinary metals, which crystallise on cooling, these alloys can be cooled slowly enough to avoid crystallisation because their multi-component compositions frustrate the formation of crystal nuclei.

History

The first metallic glass, an alloy of gold and silicon, was produced in 1960 at Caltech by W. Klement, R. H. Willens and Pol Duwez using extremely rapid quenching, yielding only thin foils. Through the 1990s, groups led by Akihisa Inoue in Japan and William L. Johnson at Caltech developed multi-component alloys — typically containing four or more elements of differing atomic sizes — with critical cooling rates low enough to cast bulk pieces. Johnson's group commercialised the zirconium-based alloy family known as Vitreloy.

Properties

Bulk metallic glasses combine very high strength (often roughly twice that of crystalline alloys of similar composition) with a high elastic limit of about 2%, good corrosion resistance and excellent soft-magnetic or wear properties in some systems. However, they tend to fail by localised shear banding with little macroscopic ductility in tension, and their cost and limited castable size (usually a few centimetres) restrict applications.

Applications

Uses include sporting goods such as golf club heads, watch and phone cases, medical and aerospace components, micro-gears, and transformer cores and other soft-magnetic parts for iron-based glasses. Their ability to be thermoplastically formed in the supercooled liquid region, like a plastic, enables precise micro- and nano-scale moulding.
