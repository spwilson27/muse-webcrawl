Eastern grey kangaroo

Scientific nameMacropus giganteus
FamilyMacropodidae
HeightUp to about 2 m (large males)
DistributionEastern Australia and Tasmania

The eastern grey kangaroo (Macropus giganteus), also known as the great grey kangaroo and the forester kangaroo, is a marsupial found in the eastern third of Australia, with a population of several million. It is the second largest and heaviest living marsupial in the world, exceeded only by its close relative the red kangaroo. Despite its scientific name giganteus, the eastern grey is slightly smaller than the red kangaroo of the arid interior.

Description and behaviour

Eastern greys have soft grey-brown fur, paler below, a finely haired muzzle, and large ears. Large males — "boomers" — may stand approaching two metres and weigh over 60 kg, while females ("flyers") are much smaller. They are mainly nocturnal and crepuscular grazers of open forest, woodland and pasture, resting in shade during the day in loose social groups called mobs. Like other kangaroos they move by hopping at speed and swimming when pressed, using the tail for balance and propulsion.

Reproduction

Females give birth to a single tiny joey after a gestation of about five weeks; the newborn climbs to the pouch and develops there for roughly nine months, continuing to suckle after leaving the pouch permanently. Females can support young of different ages with different milk from different teats simultaneously.

Relationship with humans

Abundant and adaptable, the eastern grey is listed as Least Concern. It is commercially harvested under quota in some states for meat and leather, and its numbers around farms, golf courses and Canberra's suburbs sometimes bring it into conflict with landholders.
