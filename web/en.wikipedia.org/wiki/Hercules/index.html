Hercules

Hercules (Latin: Herculēs) is the Roman equivalent of the Greek divine hero Heracles, son of Jupiter (Zeus) and the mortal Alcmena. In classical mythology, Hercules is famous for his strength and for his numerous far-ranging adventures, above all the Twelve Labours imposed on him by King Eurystheus. The Romans adapted the Greek stories, giving the hero a prominent place in their own literature and art, and the emperor Commodus even associated himself with the hero's image.

The Twelve Labours

Driven mad by the goddess Hera, Heracles killed his own wife and children; as penance he was ordered to perform twelve seemingly impossible tasks: slaying the Nemean lion and the Lernaean Hydra, capturing the Ceryneian Hind and the Erymanthian Boar, cleaning the Augean stables in a day, driving away the Stymphalian birds, capturing the Cretan Bull and the Mares of Diomedes, seizing the girdle of Hippolyta, stealing the cattle of Geryon, fetching the apples of the Hesperides, and bringing Cerberus, the hound of Hades, from the underworld.

Death and apotheosis

Hercules died from a shirt soaked in the poisoned blood of the centaur Nessus, given to him by his wife Deianira in the belief that it would restore his love. In agony he built his own funeral pyre on Mount Oeta; on death he was raised to Olympus and made immortal, reconciling with Hera and marrying her daughter Hebe.

Cult and legacy

Hercules was widely worshipped in the Roman world, with temples such as that of Hercules Victor in Rome's Forum Boarium. His club, knotted oak and lion skin became standard attributes in art. His name survives in the constellation Hercules, in phrases such as "Herculean task", and in innumerable works of literature, opera, film and comics.
