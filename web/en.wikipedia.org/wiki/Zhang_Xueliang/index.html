Zhang Xueliang (Chinese: 張學良; 3 June 1901 – 15 October 2001) was a Chinese warlord and general who ruled Manchuria and parts of northern China after the assassination of his father, Zhang Zuolin, in 1928. Nicknamed the "Young Marshal," he is best remembered for the Xi'an Incident of December 1936, in which he detained Chiang Kai-shek to force a united front against Japan — an act that changed the course of modern Chinese history and cost Zhang his freedom for more than half a century.

Ruler of Manchuria

Zhang succeeded his father after Japanese officers murdered the elder Zhang in the Huanggutun Incident. Defying Japanese expectations, he announced the Northeast Flag Replacement in December 1928, bringing Manchuria under the Nationalist government of Chiang Kai-shek. He intervened decisively in the Central Plains War of 1930 on Chiang's side, receiving high office in return. After the Mukden Incident of September 1931, Zhang's forces withdrew from Manchuria without fighting on Chiang's orders of non-resistance — a decision for which Zhang bore enduring criticism. He travelled to Europe, overcame his opium addiction, and returned to command armies against the Communists in the northwest.

The Xi'an Incident

Convinced that China must unite against Japan rather than fight a civil war, Zhang and General Yang Hucheng arrested Chiang Kai-shek at Xi'an on 12 December 1936. After two weeks of negotiation involving Zhou Enlai, Chiang was released, having verbally agreed to a united front. Zhang, in a gesture of loyalty, accompanied Chiang back to Nanjing — and was immediately court-martialled and placed under house arrest.

Later life

Zhang spent over fifty years in custody, first in mainland China and then in Taiwan, studying Ming history and Buddhism while denied any political role. He was fully released only in 1990, after the death of Chiang's son Chiang Ching-kuo, and emigrated to Hawaii in 1995. He died in Honolulu in 2001 at the age of 100, having outlived nearly all his contemporaries. In mainland China he is now honoured as a patriotic hero for the Xi'an Incident; historians continue to debate his choices of 1931 and 1936.
