Anderson shelter

An Anderson shelter, designed to be half-buried in a garden and covered with earth.

An Anderson shelter is a type of air-raid shelter used by British civilians during the Second World War. It was designed in 1938 by William Paterson and Oscar Carl Kerrison in response to a request from the Home Secretary, Sir John Anderson, after whom it was named. Anderson shelters were issued free to low-income households and sold at a modest price to others; about 3.6 million were built or distributed between February 1939 and the end of the war.

Design

The shelter consisted of fourteen panels of corrugated steel that bolted together to form an arch 1.95 m long, 1.35 m wide, and 1.8 m high (6 ft 6 in × 4 ft 6 in × 6 ft). Householders dug a pit about 1.2 m deep in their garden, assembled the shelter in it, and covered it with at least 40 cm of the excavated earth. The design could accommodate up to six people and protected against blast and debris from all but a direct hit. The steel's corrugation gave the structure considerable strength for its weight, and the curved profile deflected blast waves.

Use and reception

Anderson shelters performed well in the Blitz, and many lives were saved. However, they were cold and damp: because they were sunk into the ground they often flooded, and sleeping in them through the winter was uncomfortable. From 1941 the indoor Morrison shelter offered an alternative for those without gardens. After the war, many Anderson shelters were dug up for scrap, but others survived as garden sheds, and examples are preserved in museums across Britain.
