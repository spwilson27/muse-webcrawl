Amorality

Field: Ethics, moral philosophy
Related concepts: Immorality, moral nihilism, moral scepticism

Amorality is the absence of, indifference toward, or disregard for morality. An amoral agent either lacks a sense of right and wrong altogether — as with infants, animals, or certain natural processes — or acts without reference to moral considerations. The concept is distinguished from immorality, which involves knowingly doing what one takes to be wrong: the immoral person violates morality, whereas the amoral person stands outside it.

Uses of the concept

Philosophers describe nature as amoral: natural disasters are neither good nor evil because no moral agent is involved. In psychology, amoral behaviour is discussed in connection with developmental stages — young children are typically considered amoral before they internalise norms — and with personality disorders in which moral judgement is impaired. In political and business rhetoric, institutions such as markets or bureaucracies are sometimes called amoral when they pursue their functions without regard to moral outcomes, a usage critics regard as evasive when it masks choices made by responsible persons.

Philosophical positions

Some philosophical stances approach amorality deliberately: moral nihilists deny that moral facts exist, and Friedrich Nietzsche's call to move "beyond good and evil" proposed revaluing inherited morality rather than simply abandoning values. Anthropologists use "amoral" descriptively of social codes — for example, "amoral familism" — where obligations are held to stop at the boundary of a group. Whether a human adult can be fully amoral, rather than merely immoral or morally indifferent, remains contested.
