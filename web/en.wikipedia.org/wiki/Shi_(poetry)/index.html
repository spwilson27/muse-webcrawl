Shi (poetry)

Shi (詩) is the Chinese word for poetry, and by extension the name of the principal classical Chinese poetic genre. Shi poetry is characterised by lines of uniform length — most commonly four, five, or seven characters — with end rhyme and, in later regulated verse, strict tonal patterning. The term is also used in the names of the earliest Chinese poetry anthology, the Shijing (Classic of Poetry).

History

The earliest shi are the hymns and folk songs collected in the Shijing, compiled by around the 6th century BCE, in predominantly four-character lines. During the Han dynasty, five-character lines became dominant, producing the "ancient style" (gushi) associated with the Nineteen Old Poems. In the Tang dynasty the form reached its classical peak with regulated verse (lüshi) and quatrains (jueju), practised by poets such as Li Bai and Du Fu. Seven-character lines flourished alongside the five-character form.

Forms

  • Gushi ("ancient-style poetry") — freer in rhyme and length, five or seven characters per line.
  • Lüshi ("regulated verse") — eight lines of five or seven characters, with strict tonal parallelism and rhyme.
  • Jueju ("cut-off lines") — a four-line quatrain form.
  • Pailü — extended regulated verse of more than eight lines.

Distinction from ci and fu

Shi is distinguished from ci, song lyrics written to fixed tunes with lines of irregular length that rose to prominence in the Song dynasty, and from fu, rhymed prose-poems of the Han dynasty. Together the three forms make up the main tradition of classical Chinese verse.
