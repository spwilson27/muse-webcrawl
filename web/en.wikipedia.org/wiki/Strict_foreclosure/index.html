Strict foreclosure

Strict foreclosure is granted by court judgment rather than by a public sale.

Strict foreclosure is a method of mortgage foreclosure in which the lender (mortgagee), upon the borrower's default, obtains a court decree that vests title to the mortgaged property directly in the lender after a court-set redemption period expires — without the property being offered at a public foreclosure sale. It is one of the oldest forms of foreclosure, descending from the English equity practice of foreclosing the borrower's "equity of redemption."

How it works

The lender files suit and proves the debt and default. The court enters a judgment of strict foreclosure that fixes the amount owed and sets "law days" — a date (often staggered for junior lienholders and the owner in order of priority) by which each party may redeem by paying the debt plus interest and costs. If no one redeems by their law day, title becomes absolute in the lender by operation of the judgment. Junior lienholders who fail to redeem lose their interests.

Where used

Strict foreclosure survives as the standard mortgage foreclosure method in only a few U.S. states, notably Connecticut and Vermont, where it is the primary form of foreclosure. Most other states use foreclosure by sale (judicial sale or non-judicial power-of-sale under a deed of trust).

Advantages and criticism

For lenders, strict foreclosure is faster and cheaper than a sale, and avoids a low auction price that might leave a deficiency to chase. Critics object that the borrower forfeits any equity above the debt: if the property is worth more than what is owed, the lender receives a windfall and the borrower loses the surplus that a sale would have produced. Because of this harshness, Connecticut courts may instead order a foreclosure by sale when the property's value substantially exceeds the debt, and borrowers may seek protection of the automatic stay in bankruptcy. A lender in a strict foreclosure generally cannot obtain a deficiency judgment, since it has taken the property itself in satisfaction.

Related concepts

  • Deed in lieu of foreclosure — the borrower voluntarily conveys title to avoid foreclosure.
  • Equity of redemption — the borrower's right to reclaim the property by paying the debt before foreclosure is complete.
  • Foreclosure by sale — the property is sold at auction and proceeds applied to the debt.
