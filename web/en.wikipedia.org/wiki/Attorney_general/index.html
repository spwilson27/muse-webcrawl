Attorney general

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An attorney general is the chief legal officer of a government — in most jurisdictions the state's principal legal adviser and, in many, the head of its prosecution service or justice department. The office descends from the medieval English king's attorney. In England and Wales the Attorney General is the Crown's chief legal adviser, superintends the prosecution services (the Crown Prosecution Service and Serious Fraud Office), represents the government in major litigation and holds a set of constitutional functions (consents to certain prosecutions, references of unduly lenient sentences and points of law) while sitting in or attending cabinet — a dual legal and political role whose tensions are a standing constitutional debate (sharpened after the Iraq-war legal advice controversy of 2003).

In the United States the Attorney General heads the Department of Justice (created 1870; the office itself dates to the Judiciary Act of 1789), serves in the cabinet, and directs federal law enforcement and prosecution, nominally independent of the president in individual cases by post-Watergate norms that remain contested; each state also has an attorney general (in most states directly elected) with consumer-protection, antitrust and criminal jurisdiction that has made state AGs major national policy actors. Commonwealth systems (Canada, Australia, India, Kenya and others) adapt the model with variations in prosecutorial independence — several (Ireland; Israel, where the AG doubles as binding legal adviser and chief prosecutor) give the office distinctive constitutional weight.
