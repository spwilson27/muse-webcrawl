Phacoemulsification

Phacoemulsification is a modern cataract surgery method in which the eye's internal lens is emulsified with an ultrasonic handpiece and aspirated from the eye. Aspirated fluids are replaced with irrigation of balanced salt solution to maintain the anterior chamber.

History

The technique was developed by American ophthalmologist Charles Kelman, who introduced it in 1967. Kelman was inspired by his dentist's ultrasonic probe to find a way to remove cataracts through an incision small enough not to require an extended hospital stay. Early instruments were bulky and the technique difficult, but by the 1980s and 1990s improvements in machines, foldable intraocular lenses, and surgical training made phacoemulsification the standard method of cataract extraction in the developed world.

Technique

The procedure is typically performed under topical or local anaesthesia. Its main steps are:

  1. Incision — a small incision (about 2–3 mm) is made at the edge of the cornea, often clear-corneal and self-sealing.
  2. Capsulorhexis — a circular opening is torn in the anterior capsule of the lens.
  3. Hydrodissection — fluid is injected to free the lens nucleus from the cortex.
  4. Phacoemulsification — the ultrasonic tip emulsifies the nucleus, which is aspirated; techniques include "divide and conquer" and "phaco chop."
  5. Cortical removal — remaining soft lens matter is removed by irrigation and aspiration.
  6. Lens implantation — a foldable intraocular lens (IOL) is inserted into the capsular bag.

Equipment

The phaco machine delivers ultrasonic energy through a hollow titanium needle while simultaneously providing irrigation and aspiration. Modern systems modulate power (torsional and transversal ultrasound, micropulse) to reduce heat and energy delivered to the eye, protecting the corneal endothelium. Femtosecond laser–assisted cataract surgery can automate some steps, including the incision and capsulotomy.

Complications

Although generally safe and effective, possible complications include posterior capsule rupture with vitreous loss, corneal oedema, endophthalmitis (a rare but serious infection), cystoid macular oedema, and retinal detachment. Outcomes are usually excellent, with most patients achieving substantially improved vision.
