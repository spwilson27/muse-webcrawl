Kiezdeutsch (literally "neighbourhood German", from Berlin slang Kiez, "city block/neighbourhood") is a German urban youth dialect that emerged in multiethnic, working-class districts of large cities, particularly Berlin, since the 1990s. It is spoken chiefly by adolescents — both those with migrant backgrounds and those without — as an in-group vernacular, alongside standard German, which the same speakers use in formal contexts.

Features

Linguists describe Kiezdeutsch not as "broken German" but as a systematic variety with innovations in grammar, sound and vocabulary. Characteristic features include:

  • New word order options, such as placing adverbials in the prefield ("Morgen ich geh Arbeitsamt" — "Tomorrow I go job centre"), beyond standard German verb-second patterns.
  • Simplification of inflection: dropped articles and prepositions in fixed phrases ("ich geh Aldi", "I'm going [to] Aldi"), and generalised forms.
  • New particles and discourse markers such as musstu ("you gotta") and emphatic ischwör ("I swear").
  • Loanwords especially from Turkish and Arabic, e.g. lan ("dude/man"), wallah ("honestly, by God"), and yallah ("come on").
  • A distinctive staccato intonation and phonetic features such as the palatalisation of the "ich-Laut" to a [ʃ]-like sound (isch).

Classification and debate

Early public debate in Germany often stigmatised the variety as evidence of failed integration and declining language standards. Researchers — notably Heike Wiese of the University of Potsdam, whose 2012 book Kiezdeutsch: Ein neuer Dialekt entsteht popularised the term — argue instead that it is a legitimate new urban dialect arising from language contact, comparable to Multicultural London English or Rinkeby Swedish in Stockholm. They point out that its speakers command standard German as well, style-shifting between registers, and that some Kiezdeutsch innovations are already spreading into mainstream youth speech.

In media

The variety has been widely parodied and dramatised in German comedy and film, and the term Kanak Sprak — from Feridun Zaimoğlu's 1995 book of that name — was an earlier literary label for related styles of speech. Linguistic corpora collected in Berlin (the KiDKo corpus) and Hamburg have made Kiezdeutsch one of the best-studied youth languages in Europe.
