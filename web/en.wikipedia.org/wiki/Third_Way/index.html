Third Way

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Third Way is a political position that attempts to reconcile right-wing and left-wing politics by advocating a varying synthesis of centre-right economics and centre-left social policies. The term was revived in the 1990s by the sociologist Anthony Giddens (The Third Way, 1998) and adopted by Bill Clinton's New Democrats and, most influentially, Tony Blair's New Labour (in power 1997–2010) and Gerhard Schröder's Neue Mitte in Germany.

Its programme accepted markets, fiscal discipline and globalisation while promising social investment (education, welfare-to-work, minimum wage, public-service modernisation) — “tough on crime, tough on the causes of crime” in Blair's formula — and it underpinned the American and British centre-left governments' longevity. Critics from the left saw capitulation to neoliberalism; from the right, triangulation without conviction. After the 2008 financial crisis and the electoral collapses of European social democracy, the Third Way waned as a brand, though its policy DNA (activation, public-service reform, fiscal rules) persists across the mainstream.
