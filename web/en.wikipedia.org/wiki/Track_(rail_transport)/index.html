Track (rail transport)

The track on a railway or railroad, also known as the permanent way or simply line in the United Kingdom, is the structure consisting of the rails, fasteners, sleepers (ties) and ballast (or slab track), plus the underlying subgrade. It enables trains to move by providing a dependable surface for their wheels to roll upon. Early tracks used wooden rails and were also used for horse-drawn wagons; modern rails are made of steel.

Structure

A conventional track structure consists of two parallel steel rails, laid on crosswise sleepers (ties) embedded in ballast, which holds the track in position and transmits loads to the formation beneath. The distance between the inner sides of the two rails is the track gauge, of which the most common is standard gauge, 1,435 mm (4 ft 8½ in).

Rails are joined by fishplates or, in modern practice, welded into continuous lengths (continuous welded rail) to reduce wear and noise. Fastenings range from simple spikes hammered into wooden sleepers to elastic clips on concrete sleepers.

Geometry and maintenance

Track geometry — including alignment, cant (superelevation), cross level and gauge — must be maintained within tolerances for safe running at speed. Curves raise the outer rail (cant) to counteract centrifugal effects. Ballast must be tamped periodically to restore geometry, and rails are ground or replaced when worn. Slab track, in which rails are fixed directly to a concrete slab, is used in tunnels, on high-speed lines, and on some metro systems.
