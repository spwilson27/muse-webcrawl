Firth of Forth

Firth of Forth
Location: East coast of Scotland
Source: River Forth (plus the Almond, Avon, Esk and Leven)
Outflow: North Sea
Length: about 77 km (48 mi) from Stirling to the Isle of May
Notable crossings: Forth Bridge (rail, 1890), Forth Road Bridge (1964), Queensferry Crossing (2017)

The Firth of Forth (Scottish Gaelic: Linne Foirthe) is the estuary (firth) of several Scottish rivers including the River Forth. It meets the North Sea with Fife on its north coast and Lothian on its south. It was known to the Romans as Bodotria. The firth extends inland as far as Stirling, where the Forth becomes tidal, and seaward to a line roughly between Fife Ness and Dunbar, taking in islands including Inchkeith, Inchcolm, Craigleith, Fidra, the Lamb, and the Isle of May, with the Bass Rock at its mouth.

Geography and economy

Towns and ports on the firth include Leith (the port of Edinburgh), Grangemouth — site of a major petrochemical complex — Rosyth, where a naval dockyard was established in 1909, Burntisland, Kirkcaldy, and Methil. The firth was long a centre of herring and white-fish fishing and of coal export from Fife and Lothian pits; the islands of Inchcolm (with its medieval abbey) and the Isle of May (a national nature reserve famous for puffins and grey seals) are notable landmarks. The river is crossed at the Queensferry narrows by three bridges from three centuries: the Forth Bridge (1890, railway), the Forth Road Bridge (1964), and the Queensferry Crossing (2017); further west the firth is spanned at Kincardine and by the Clackmannanshire Bridge.

Wildlife

The Firth of Forth is designated a Special Protection Area and Ramsar site for its internationally important populations of wintering wildfowl and waders, including pink-footed geese, shelduck, redshank, and turnstone; its rocky islands support large seabird colonies, notably gannets on the Bass Rock — one of the largest northern gannet colonies in the world — and puffins on the Isle of May and Craigleith.

History

The firth formed the northern frontier of Roman Britain at times and the boundary between the Picts and the Angles; ferries at Queensferry (named for Queen Margaret in the 11th century) served pilgrims to St Andrews and Dunfermline for centuries. In both world wars the firth was defended as the approach to Rosyth naval base; the first air raid of the Second World War on Britain targeted warships in the firth in October 1939.
