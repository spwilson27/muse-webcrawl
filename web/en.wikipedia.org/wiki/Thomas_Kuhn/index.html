Thomas Kuhn

Thomas Samuel Kuhn (July 18, 1922 – June 17, 1996) was an American historian and philosopher of science whose 1962 book The Structure of Scientific Revolutions is one of the most influential academic works of the 20th century. It introduced the concept of the paradigm shift into wide currency and challenged the view that science progresses by steady accumulation of knowledge.

Career

Kuhn studied physics at Harvard, receiving his PhD in 1949, and turned to the history of science after a formative experience teaching science to humanities students. He taught at Harvard, UC Berkeley, Princeton, and finally MIT, where he spent the remainder of his career.

The Structure of Scientific Revolutions

Kuhn argued that science develops through alternating phases:

  • Normal science — research conducted under an accepted paradigm, a shared framework of theory, methods, and exemplars, largely devoted to puzzle-solving.
  • Anomalies and crisis — persistent failures of the paradigm accumulate until confidence in it collapses.
  • Scientific revolution — a new paradigm replaces the old in a shift Kuhn compared to a gestalt switch or religious conversion; rival paradigms are incommensurable, lacking a neutral measure for comparison.

The book provoked extensive debate among philosophers — including Karl Popper and Imre Lakatos — and influenced the social sciences, where "paradigm" became a ubiquitous, if loose, term. Kuhn later refined his views in essays collected in The Essential Tension (1977) and emphasized the taxonomic structure of scientific concepts.
