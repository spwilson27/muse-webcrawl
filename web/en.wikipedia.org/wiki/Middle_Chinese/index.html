Middle Chinese (formerly known as Ancient Chinese) is the historical variety of Chinese recorded in the Qieyun, a rhyme dictionary first published in 601 AD during the Sui dynasty, and reflected in the rhyme tables and dictionaries of the following centuries. It represents the prestige language of the Northern and Southern dynasties, Sui and Tang periods, and is the common ancestor — or close relative of the ancestor — of most modern Chinese varieties, except for Min Chinese, which split off earlier.

Sources

No direct records of Middle Chinese pronunciation survive; its phonology is reconstructed from the fanqie spellings and rhyme categories of the Qieyun and its expanded successor the Guangyun, from the rhyme tables of the Song dynasty, and from comparison with modern varieties and with Sino-Xenic borrowings into Vietnamese, Korean and Japanese.

Phonology

Reconstructions give Middle Chinese a rich syllable structure more complex than any modern standard variety: a large inventory of initial consonants (including voiced obstruents and three-way stop contrasts), final consonants -p, -t, -k, -m, -n and -ŋ, a system of medials and rhymes distinguished by division and rounding, and four tones (level, rising, departing and entering). From the late Tang period onward, devoicing of obstruents, loss of distinctions among initials and other changes gave rise to Late Middle Chinese and eventually the modern languages.

Legacy

Middle Chinese is the stage of the language in which classical Tang poetry was composed; its rhyme categories underlie regulated verse. Its readings were also borrowed wholesale into the vocabularies of Japanese (on'yomi), Korean and Vietnamese, preserving evidence of its sound system.
