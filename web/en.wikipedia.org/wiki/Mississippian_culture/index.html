Mississippian culture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Platform mounds around a plaza at Moundville, Alabama]

The Mississippian culture was a mound-building Native American civilisation that flourished in the eastern and midwestern United States from about AD 800 to 1600, from the Great Lakes to the Gulf of Mexico and from the Appalachians to the edge of the Plains. Its chiefdoms — Cahokia, Moundville, Etowah, Spiro, Angel Mounds among them — shared maize-intensive agriculture, platform mounds topped by temples and elite houses around plazas, shell-tempered pottery, and an iconographic system (the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex) of falcon warriors, crosses-in-circles and weeping eyes. European diseases and disruption shattered the Mississippian world in the 16th–17th centuries; its descendant peoples include the Muscogee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Cherokee and many others.

Society

Mississippian towns were ruled by chiefs who combined priestly and political authority, claimed descent from the sun in some cases (the Natchez Great Sun, surviving into the French colonial era, was the last), and redistributed food through great feasts and mound ceremonies. Maize — adopted intensively after AD 900 with beans and squash — fed dense populations; craft specialists worked copper from the Great Lakes, shells from the Gulf, and mica and flint into prestige goods exchanged across the continent.

Collapse and descendants

Many centres were already cycling or abandoned before Europeans arrived — Cahokia by 1350, others rising as it fell. De Soto's expedition (1539–1543) met populous Mississippian chiefdoms; within 150 years epidemics and slave-raiding had emptied much of the region (the 'shatter zone'), and the peoples reorganised as the historic tribes. Their mound-building ancestors were long denied by settler mythology (the 'Moundbuilder myth' credited the earthworks to a lost race) until Cyrus Thomas' Smithsonian investigations (1894) established Native authorship.
