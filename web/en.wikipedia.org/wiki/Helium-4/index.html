Helium-4

Helium-4 (4He) is the most abundant isotope of helium, accounting for about 99.99986 percent of helium on Earth. Its nucleus — two protons and two neutrons — is identical to an alpha particle and is exceptionally stable. Helium-4 was produced in large quantities in Big Bang nucleosynthesis and continues to be created by nuclear fusion in stars and by alpha decay of heavy elements.

Properties

Helium-4 is a boson, and liquid helium-4 undergoes a transition at the lambda point (2.17 K) to the superfluid phase called helium II, which flows without viscosity and has extraordinary thermal conductivity. It condenses at 4.2 K at atmospheric pressure and, unusually, does not solidify at atmospheric pressure even at absolute zero because of zero-point energy.

Formation and abundance

About a quarter of the ordinary matter mass produced in the Big Bang is helium-4. In stars, it is both the product of the proton–proton chain and the CNO cycle's net result, and the fuel for the triple-alpha process that makes carbon. Terrestrial helium comes mostly from alpha decay of uranium and thorium trapped in natural gas deposits.
