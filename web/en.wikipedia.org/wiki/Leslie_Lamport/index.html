Leslie B. Lamport (born February 7, 1941) is an American computer scientist and mathematician, best known for his foundational work in distributed systems and for creating the LaTeX document preparation system. He received the Turing Award in 2013 "for fundamental contributions to the theory and practice of distributed and concurrent systems, notably the invention of concepts such as causality and logical clocks, safety and liveness, replicated state machines and sequential consistency."

Education and career

Lamport was born in Brooklyn, New York, and graduated from the Bronx High School of Science. He received a B.S. in mathematics from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 1960 and an M.A. (1963) and PhD (1972) in mathematics from Brandeis University. He worked at MITRE Corporation, SRI International, and Digital Equipment Corporation/Compaq before joining Microsoft Research in 2001.

Contributions

Lamport's 1978 paper "Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System" introduced logical clocks (Lamport timestamps) and the "happened-before" relation, and remains one of the most cited works in computer science; his bakery algorithm models mutual exclusion. With Robert Shostak and Marshall Pease he formulated the Byzantine generals problem, establishing the theory of Byzantine fault tolerance. His Paxos family of protocols (described in "The Part-Time Parliament", 1998) provides consensus in unreliable networks and underlies many replicated systems. Lamport also created LaTeX (building on Donald Knuth's TeX), the temporal logic of actions (TLA) and the TLA+ specification language, the basis of his book Specifying Systems (2002), and early digital signature schemes (Lamport signatures).
