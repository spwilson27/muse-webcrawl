The Habsburg Monarchy (German: Habsburgermonarchie), also known as the Habsburg Empire or the Danubian Monarchy, was the composite monarchy ruled by the Austrian branch of the House of Habsburg from the late Middle Ages until 1918. It was never formally a single state but a dynastic union of crowns and territories — the Austrian hereditary lands, the Kingdoms of Bohemia and Hungary-Croatia, and at various times Milan, Naples, the Austrian Netherlands and other possessions — held together by the person of the monarch and common institutions centred on Vienna.

Rise

Rudolf I's acquisition of Austria in 1282 founded the dynasty's power base. Through strategic marriages — summed up in the motto Bella gerant alii, tu felix Austria nube ("Let others wage war; you, fortunate Austria, marry") — the Habsburgs acquired Burgundy, Spain and its empire, Bohemia and Hungary. After 1526 the Austrian line defended Central Europe against the Ottoman Empire while the Spanish branch ruled a global empire until 1700.

The Danubian great power

The Monarchy survived the Thirty Years' War, repelled the Ottoman siege of Vienna in 1683, and under Maria Theresa and Joseph II underwent centralising and enlightened reforms. In 1804 Francis II proclaimed the Austrian Empire in response to Napoleon's dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire (1806). After defeat by Prussia in 1866, the Compromise of 1867 refounded the Monarchy as the dual state of Austria-Hungary.

Dissolution

A multinational state of more than fifty million people by 1914, the Monarchy entered the First World War after the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand and collapsed in October–November 1918, succeeded by Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia and territories ceded to Poland, Romania and Italy.
