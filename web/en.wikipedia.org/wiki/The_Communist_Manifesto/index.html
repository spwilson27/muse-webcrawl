The Communist Manifesto

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Communist Manifesto (Manifest der Kommunistischen Partei) is an 1848 pamphlet by German philosophers Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, commissioned by the Communist League and published in London in February 1848, as revolutions swept Europe. It is among the world's most influential political documents and the founding text of Marxist communism.

Contents

The Manifesto opens with the famous spectre — "A spectre is haunting Europe — the spectre of communism" — and argues that history is the history of class struggles. It analyses the bourgeoisie's revolutionary transformation of production and its self-undermining crises, describes the proletariat's formation and predicted victory, distinguishes communism from other socialist currents, and closes with a ten-point transitional programme (including abolition of property in land, a heavy progressive income tax, and free public education) and the call: "Workers of the world, unite!" Later prefaces by Marx and Engels updated it as circumstances changed, and translations carried it worldwide as the labour movement grew.

Legacy

Its literary power, analytical ambition and political afterlife — as scripture for parties and states, target of liberal and social-democratic refutation, and object of endless reinterpretation — make it a canonical text of political modernity, studied both by adherents and by historians of ideas regardless of their verdict on its predictions.
