Chromatography

Chromatography is a family of techniques for separating mixtures: the mixture is carried by a mobile phase (liquid or gas) over a stationary phase, and its components travel at different rates because they partition unequally between the two — spreading into bands or peaks.

Mikhail Tsvet invented the method in 1900–1906, separating plant pigments on a chalk column (hence 'colour writing'). Modern descendants — paper and thin-layer chromatography, gas chromatography (GC) and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) — analyse everything from inks and drugs to air pollutants and proteins.

[Image: Paper chromatography strip separating ink into coloured bands]

How results are read

In planar methods the retardation factor R₁ compares distances travelled by spot and solvent front; in column methods each compound has a characteristic retention time, and a detector trace (chromatogram) of peaks gives both identification and quantity.

Applications

Forensic labs test inks and drugs of abuse; hospitals monitor blood drugs; environmental agencies measure pesticides at parts-per-billion; the fragrance and food industries check authenticity.
