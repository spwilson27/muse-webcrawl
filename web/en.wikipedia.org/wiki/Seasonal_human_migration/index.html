Seasonal human migration

Seasonal farm workers follow harvest cycles

Seasonal human migration is the temporary, recurring movement of people between places in response to seasonal variations in labour demand, climate, or resource availability. Unlike permanent migration, seasonal migrants return to their home communities when the season ends, and the pattern typically repeats annually.

Forms

Agricultural labour

The most widespread form is agricultural: workers travel to planting and harvest work that is intense but brief, following ripening crops across regions — fruit picking, sugarcane cutting, coffee and tea harvests, and grain threshing. Programmes such as Canada's Seasonal Agricultural Worker Program and the United States' H-2A visa channel this movement formally, while much of it remains informal or undocumented.

Pastoralism and transhumance

Pastoral communities have practised seasonal movement for millennia, moving herds between lowland winter pastures and highland summer grazing — a pattern known as transhumance in the Alps, Pyrenees and Mediterranean, and common across Central Asia, the Sahel and East Africa.

Tourism, construction and other sectors

Tourism generates seasonal migration of hospitality workers to beach resorts in summer and ski resorts in winter. Construction, fishing, forestry and food processing also draw seasonal workers, as do climate-driven movements: "snowbirds" retire to warmer regions for winter, while in hot countries some populations historically moved to cooler highlands in summer.

Causes and significance

Seasonal migration is driven by the uneven distribution of work and resources across the year, by climatic constraints on agriculture and herding, and by wage differences between regions. For sending households it provides essential income and a way to smooth consumption against seasonal hunger; remittances from seasonal work often finance schooling, housing and farm inputs.

Challenges

Seasonal migrants frequently face insecure housing, limited access to healthcare and schooling for accompanying children, wage theft, and legal vulnerability where their status depends on a single employer. Because censuses and services are organised around permanent residence, seasonal movers can be statistically invisible and politically marginal, despite numbering in the tens of millions worldwide.
