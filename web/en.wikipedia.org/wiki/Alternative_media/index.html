Alternative media

Alternative media are media sources that differ from established or dominant types of media — in content, production, or distribution — such as mainstream newspapers, television networks and large commercial publishers. Alternative media generally aim to give voice to perspectives excluded from or marginalised by mainstream coverage, challenge dominant ideologies, and enable more participatory forms of communication in which audiences become producers.

Characteristics

Scholars such as Chris Atton define alternative media less by any single format than by their practices: non-commercial or collectively owned organisation, horizontal decision-making, de-professionalised production ("ordinary people" as journalists), and content that privileges marginalised communities and social movements. Typical examples include independent and community radio, underground and dissident newspapers, fanzines, public access television, Indymedia-style open-publishing websites, podcasts and activist social media.

History

Alternative media have roots in the radical press of the 19th century — abolitionist, labour and suffragist papers — and in the samizdat publications of the Soviet bloc. The 1960s underground press (papers such as the Los Angeles Free Press and Oz) flourished alongside counterculture movements. Pirate and community radio expanded access to broadcasting in the 1970s–80s, and the 1999 Seattle WTO protests launched the Independent Media Center (Indymedia) network, an early model of participatory online journalism. The internet and social media dramatically lowered production costs, blurring the line between alternative and mainstream media and creating new forms — blogs, citizen journalism, and partisan digital outlets — that also raise questions about misinformation and echo chambers.

Theory and criticism

Media theorists connect alternative media to the idea of a plural public sphere in which counterpublics can form. Critics note that alternative outlets may lack professional standards of verification, depend on precarious volunteer labour, or replicate the biases they oppose; conversely, defenders argue they correct systematic blind spots of corporate media and have repeatedly broken stories later picked up by the mainstream. The term itself is contested, since "alternative" is defined relative to a moving mainstream.
