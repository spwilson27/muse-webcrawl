Racketeering

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Racketeering is a pattern of organised criminal activity — extortion, bribery, loan-sharking, fraud, murder and other predicate offences — conducted as part of an ongoing criminal enterprise. The word entered American English in the 1920s from the labour and protection "rackets" of Prohibition-era Chicago. Its legal embodiment is the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO, 1970), which criminalises conducting an enterprise's affairs through a "pattern of racketeering activity" (at least two listed predicate acts within ten years) and adds forfeiture and — explosively — a civil action for treble damages.

Use and spread

Designed against the Mafia (and vindicated in the Commission Trial and the dismantling of the Five Families' leadership), RICO's breadth migrated: corrupt police units, FIFA officials, tobacco companies (a civil RICO judgment in 2006), abortion protesters (decades of litigation ending in Scheidler's limits), crypto exchanges and celebrity defendants in civil suits of varying merit. State "little RICO" statutes and foreign analogues (enterprise and association offences in Italy's Article 416-bis, the heart of anti-mafia law) extend the model. The permanent debate is scope: enterprise liability fits syndicated crime precisely because it punishes organisation — and threatens ordinary business and protest when organisation itself is treated as conspiracy.
