The common ostrich is the largest living ratite.

A ratite is any of a group of mostly large, long-necked, flightless birds in the infraclass Palaeognathae. The living ratites include the ostriches of Africa, the rheas of South America, the emu and cassowaries of Australia and New Guinea, and the kiwis of New Zealand. Extinct groups include the moas of New Zealand and the elephant birds of Madagascar, the largest birds known to have lived.

The name derives from the Latin ratis, meaning "raft", referring to the flat sternum (breastbone) that lacks the keel, or carina, found in flying birds. In flying birds the keel anchors the powerful pectoral muscles used in flight; its absence in ratites is associated with the loss of flight.

Characteristics

Ratites share a distinctive structure of the palate (the palaeognathous palate) that distinguishes them from all other living birds (the neognaths). Their feathers lack the tiny hooks (barbules) that zip the barbs of flight feathers together, giving their plumage a loose, hair-like or fluffy texture. Most ratites are large and long-legged, adapted for running; the ostrich can reach speeds of about 70 km/h (43 mph) and is the fastest bird on land. Kiwis are the major exception, being small, nocturnal, and forest-dwelling.

Unlike most birds, in several ratite species the males take the primary or sole role in incubating the eggs and rearing the chicks; this is true of rheas, cassowaries, emus, and kiwis.

Evolution and biogeography

The distribution of ratites across the southern continents was long explained by vicariance: the group's ancestor was thought to have been present on the supercontinent Gondwana, with the modern lineages carried apart as the continents drifted. However, molecular phylogenetic studies have complicated this picture. They show that the flying tinamous of South America are nested within the ratites (as sister group to the extinct moas), implying that flight was lost independently several times among palaeognaths, and that the ancestors of some ratite lineages may have dispersed by flight after the breakup of Gondwana.

The extinct elephant birds of Madagascar, such as Vorombe titan, may have exceeded 800 kg in weight, while the giant moa of New Zealand stood up to 3.6 m tall. Both groups survived into the period of human settlement of their islands and were driven to extinction by hunting and habitat change; the last moas disappeared around the 15th century CE, and elephant birds probably by the end of the first millennium CE or shortly after.

Classification

Living ratites are usually placed in the following orders: Struthioniformes (ostriches), Rheiformes (rheas), Casuariiformes (emus and cassowaries), and Apterygiformes (kiwis). Extinct orders include Dinornithiformes (moas) and Aepyornithiformes (elephant birds). Together with the tinamous, these form the Palaeognathae, the sister group of all other living birds.

Relationship with humans

Ostriches are farmed worldwide for feathers, leather, meat, and eggs. The emu is farmed for meat and oil, and appears with the red kangaroo on the coat of arms of Australia. Ratite eggs — the largest of any living bird in the case of the ostrich — have been used as containers and ornaments for tens of thousands of years. Several ratite species are threatened, chiefly by habitat loss; all five kiwi species are in decline and are the focus of intensive conservation in New Zealand.
