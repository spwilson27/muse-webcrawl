Sol-gel

The sol–gel process is a method for producing solid materials from small molecules. The method is used for the fabrication of metal oxides, especially the oxides of silicon and titanium. The process involves conversion of monomers into a colloidal solution (sol) that acts as the precursor for an integrated network (gel) of either discrete particles or network polymers.

A silica gel formed by the sol–gel route

Process stages

  • Hydrolysis and condensation: metal alkoxide precursors such as tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) react with water, forming silanol groups that condense into Si–O–Si bridges.
  • Gelation: growing clusters link until a continuous network spans the liquid, sharply increasing viscosity.
  • Ageing: further condensation strengthens the network and expels liquid (syneresis).
  • Drying: removal of liquid gives a porous xerogel; supercritical drying preserves the pore structure as an aerogel.
  • Densification: heat treatment collapses pores to produce dense glass or ceramic.

Applications

Sol–gel chemistry yields thin optical coatings, protective and anti-reflective films, membranes, catalyst supports, optical fibres, monolithic glasses at temperatures far below melting, and bioactive glasses for implants. Dip coating and spin coating deposit sol films directly.

Advantages and drawbacks

The route offers molecular-level mixing, high purity and low processing temperatures; disadvantages include shrinkage and cracking during drying, precursor cost, and long processing times for bulk monoliths.
