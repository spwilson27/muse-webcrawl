Mevalonate pathway

The mevalonate pathway, also known as the isoprenoid pathway or HMG-CoA reductase pathway, is an essential metabolic pathway present in eukaryotes, archaea, and some bacteria. The pathway produces two five-carbon building blocks called isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP) and dimethylallyl pyrophosphate (DMAPP), which are used to make isoprenoids, a diverse class of over 30,000 biomolecules such as cholesterol, vitamin K, coenzyme Q10, and all steroid hormones.

The mevalonate pathway begins with acetyl-CoA and ends with the production of IPP and DMAPP. It is best known as the target of statins, a class of cholesterol-lowering drugs. Statins inhibit the enzyme HMG-CoA reductase, which catalyzes the first committed step of the pathway: the conversion of HMG-CoA to mevalonate.

Reactions

The pathway proceeds through the following key steps: two molecules of acetyl-CoA condense to form acetoacetyl-CoA (catalyzed by acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase), which reacts with a third acetyl-CoA to give 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA (HMG-CoA), catalyzed by HMG-CoA synthase. HMG-CoA reductase then reduces HMG-CoA to mevalonate, the rate-limiting and principal regulatory step of the pathway.

Mevalonate is phosphorylated twice by mevalonate kinase and phosphomevalonate kinase to form mevalonate-5-pyrophosphate (also called 5-pyrophosphomevalonate). Decarboxylation by mevalonate-5-pyrophosphate decarboxylase yields isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP), which is isomerized to dimethylallyl pyrophosphate (DMAPP) by IPP isomerase. IPP and DMAPP can then be combined into longer prenyl chains such as geranyl pyrophosphate (GPP, C10), farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP, C15), and geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGPP, C20).

Products and importance

Downstream products of the pathway include cholesterol (via squalene, formed from two molecules of FPP), dolichol (used in N-linked glycosylation), ubiquinone (coenzyme Q10), heme A, prenylated proteins, and the isoprenoid side chains of many natural products. In plants, the mevalonate pathway operates in the cytosol, while a second, independent route — the non-mevalonate or MEP pathway — operates in plastids. Most bacteria instead use the MEP pathway exclusively, which makes that pathway an attractive target for antibiotics and antimalarial drugs.

Regulation

The pathway is tightly regulated at the level of HMG-CoA reductase through feedback inhibition by cholesterol and other sterols, control of enzyme degradation via the sterol-sensing machinery of the endoplasmic reticulum (including Insig proteins and the SREBP transcription factors), and reversible phosphorylation by AMP-activated protein kinase.

Clinical significance

Defects in enzymes of the pathway cause metabolic disorders such as mevalonate kinase deficiency, which leads to mevalonic aciduria and hyperimmunoglobulinemia D syndrome (HIDS). Beyond cholesterol synthesis, inhibition of the pathway affects protein prenylation, which is relevant to cancer biology and to the mechanism of action of nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates, which inhibit farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase.
