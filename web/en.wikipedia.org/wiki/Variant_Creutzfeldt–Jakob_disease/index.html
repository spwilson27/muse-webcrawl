Variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease


Florid plaques in vCJD brain tissue

Variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (vCJD), commonly known as "mad cow disease" in popular reporting (a term properly belonging to the cattle disease), is a fatal degenerative brain disorder in humans. It is a transmissible spongiform encephalopathy caused by prions and is believed to be acquired by eating beef products contaminated with the agent of bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE).

History

vCJD was first described in the United Kingdom in 1996, in young patients whose illness differed from classical (sporadic) Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease in its early psychiatric symptoms, longer duration and distinctive brain pathology with "florid plaques". The announcement of a probable link to BSE, which had reached epidemic proportions in British cattle, triggered a major public-health and trade crisis, bans on British beef exports, and mass cattle culls. In total, 178 definite or probable cases were recorded in the UK, with smaller numbers in other countries, mostly France and Ireland; the epidemic peaked in 2000 and has since declined to near zero.

Symptoms and pathology

The disease typically begins with psychiatric or sensory symptoms—depression, anxiety, pain or numbness—followed after months by unsteady gait, involuntary movements, progressive dementia, mutism and death, on average about 13–14 months after onset (much longer than the ~4–5 months of sporadic CJD). The median age at death was about 28 years, far younger than for sporadic CJD. Unlike sporadic CJD, the infectious prion protein accumulates abundantly in lymphoid tissues such as tonsils and appendix, which both aids diagnosis by tonsil biopsy and raised concern about blood transfusion transmission; a few cases were indeed linked to transfusion of blood from donors who later developed vCJD.

Prevention

Controls that ended the BSE epidemic—bans on feeding ruminant-derived meat-and-bone meal to cattle, removal of specified risk materials (brain, spinal cord and other tissues) from the human food chain, and testing regimes—brought human cases down correspondingly. There is no effective treatment or cure for vCJD.
