Overpopulation in companion animals

Dogs at an animal shelter

Overpopulation in companion animals refers to the surplus of domesticated animals, principally cats and dogs, relative to the number of available homes, resulting in large numbers of unwanted, stray and surrendered animals being taken in by animal shelters and pounds, many of which are euthanised. It is a major animal welfare concern in many countries.

Causes

Contributing factors include failure to spay or neuter pets, unplanned litters, casual breeding, abandonment, and the surrender of animals by owners unable or unwilling to keep them. Free-roaming and feral cat populations reproduce rapidly: a single unspayed female cat and her offspring can theoretically produce very large numbers of descendants over a few years.

Responses

Common responses include subsidised spaying and neutering programmes, trap–neuter–return (TNR) programmes for feral cats, pet licensing and microchipping, adoption promotion, no-kill shelter policies, and legislation addressing puppy mills and pet shops. In the United States, shelter intake and euthanasia numbers have fallen substantially since the 1970s as sterilisation has become widespread, though overpopulation remains acute in some regions and in many other countries with large free-roaming dog populations.
