Tax haven

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tax haven is a jurisdiction — a country, territory or special zone — that offers foreign individuals and businesses minimal or zero tax liability in a politically and economically stable environment, typically combined with limited financial disclosure. There is no single agreed list; the OECD, the EU (which maintains a list of non-cooperative jurisdictions), the IMF and NGOs such as the Tax Justice Network identify havens by criteria including effective rates, secrecy and ring-fenced regimes for non-residents. Commonly named havens include Caribbean territories (Cayman Islands, British Virgin Islands, Bermuda, Bahamas), European microstates and financial centres (Liechtenstein, Monaco, and for some purposes Ireland, Luxembourg, the Netherlands and Switzerland), and Asian centres (Singapore, Hong Kong).

Havens hold a striking share of global wealth: economist Gabriel Zucman's widely cited estimate put about 8 per cent of the world's household financial wealth in tax havens (as of the mid-2010s), and studies attribute a large share of multinational foreign investment to conduit structures. Defenders argue havens provide neutral, efficient platforms for cross-border investment and protect assets from predatory governments; critics argue they facilitate evasion, avoidance and illicit finance and hollow out other states' tax bases. International pressure — automatic information exchange, beneficial-ownership registers, minimum taxes — has narrowed bank secrecy since 2009 without eliminating the system.
