Ukulele

The ukulele is a small, four-stringed member of the lute family, developed in Hawaii in the 1880s from Portuguese instruments — the machete and cavaquinho — brought to the islands by Madeiran immigrants. Its name is commonly translated as "jumping flea," said to evoke the player's quick-moving fingers, though Queen Liliʻuokalani explained it as "the gift that came here."

History

Hawaiian craftsmen such as Manuel Nunes, José do Espírito Santo, and Augusto Dias began building the instruments in Honolulu, and the ukulele won royal patronage from King Kalākaua, becoming inseparable from Hawaiian music. It swept the mainland United States during the Panama–Pacific International Exposition of 1915 and became an emblem of the Jazz Age, with stars such as Cliff Edwards ("Ukulele Ike"). Later revivals were led by virtuosos including Roy Smeck, George Formby in Britain, Tiny Tim, Israel Kamakawiwoʻole — whose medley of "Somewhere Over the Rainbow" became a global hit — and Jake Shimabukuro.

Types and tuning

Standard sizes are soprano (the classic size, about 53 cm), concert, tenor, and baritone. The most common tuning is reentrant g′–C–E–A, in which the fourth string is pitched higher than the third, giving the ukulele its bright, chiming chord voicings. Traditional instruments are built of koa wood; modern ones use mahogany, spruce, and laminates, and inexpensive molded plastic models have made the ukulele a popular beginner's instrument in school music programs worldwide.
