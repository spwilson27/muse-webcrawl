Tel Dan (Hebrew: תל דן) is an archaeological site and nature reserve in northern Israel, in the Hula Valley at the foot of Mount Hermon. The mound, roughly 20 hectares in area, is fed by the abundant springs of the Dan River, one of the principal sources of the Jordan River. In antiquity the city was known as Laish (also Leshem) before its conquest by the Israelite tribe of Dan, after which it became the northernmost city of Israel — the source of the biblical phrase "from Dan to Beersheba".

History

The site was occupied from the Neolithic period. Its major phases belong to the Bronze and Iron Ages: in the Middle Bronze Age (c. 18th century BCE) the Canaanite city of Laish was enclosed by massive earthen ramparts and a remarkable arched mudbrick city gate, often described as one of the earliest true arches in the world. According to the biblical Book of Judges, the Danites conquered Laish and renamed it Dan, and the city later became a cult centre of the northern Kingdom of Israel, where King Jeroboam I is said to have set up a golden calf. Archaeologists have uncovered a large cultic precinct (the "high place") with an altar platform consistent with this tradition.

The Tel Dan Stele

In 1993–94 excavators discovered fragments of a basalt victory inscription, the Tel Dan Stele, erected by an Aramean king — usually identified as Hazael of Damascus — in the 9th century BCE. The Aramaic text contains the phrase bytdwd, read by most scholars as "House of David", the first widely accepted extra-biblical reference to the Davidic dynasty. The stele is now displayed in the Israel Museum in Jerusalem.

Other finds

Excavations directed for decades by Avraham Biran of the Hebrew Union College revealed Iron Age fortifications, including a city gate complex with a paved plaza and a canopied structure where the king may have sat in judgement, Israelite and Phoenician pottery, a bilingual Greek–Aramaic dedication "to the god who is in Dan", and evidence of Assyrian destruction in 732 BCE, when Tiglath-Pileser III conquered the region. The site today lies within the Tel Dan Nature Reserve, noted for its lush streamside vegetation.
