Apep

Name in hieroglyphsꜥꜣp (Apep / Apophis)
SymbolSerpent
RoleEmbodiment of chaos (isfet), enemy of Ra and of Ma'at
Greek nameApophis (Ἄποφις)

Apep, also spelled Apophis (Ancient Egyptian: ꜥꜣpp; Coptic: Ⲁⲫⲱⲫ, Aphōph), is the ancient Egyptian deity of chaos, embodied as a gigantic serpent. Apep was the great enemy of the sun god Ra and of Ma'at (truth and cosmic order): each night, as Ra sailed through the underworld (Duat) in his solar barque, Apep attacked him, seeking to prevent the sunrise and plunge creation back into disorder.

Ra's defenders spear Apep beneath the solar barque, a scene from tomb paintings.

Mythology

Apep was said to dwell below the horizon or in the Duat, and his roar shook the underworld. Ra was defended by other deities — above all Seth, who stood at the prow of the barque and speared the serpent, and the cat-headed goddess Bastet, who in some accounts slew Apep. Because the sun rose each morning, Apep was always defeated but never destroyed, since chaos could not be permanently eliminated. Temporary victories by Apep were blamed for eclipses, storms, and earthquakes.

Rituals against Apep

Egyptian priests performed daily temple rituals and annual rites to aid Ra and repel Apep. The New Kingdom text known as the Book of Overthrowing Apep (preserved in the Papyrus Bremner-Rhind) collects spells for spitting on, trampling, binding, and burning wax or papyrus figures of the serpent, whose names were inscribed and then destroyed by fire. Unlike nearly all other Egyptian deities, Apep had no cult and no temples; he was worshipped only in the sense of being ritually opposed.

Origins

Mentions of a great hostile serpent appear from the First Intermediate Period and Middle Kingdom onward; Apep's fully developed mythology is a New Kingdom phenomenon. Some scholars link his emergence to the trauma of foreign (Hyksos) rule and to theological efforts to explain evil in a cosmos governed by Ma'at.
