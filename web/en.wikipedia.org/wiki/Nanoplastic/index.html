Nanoplastic

Nanoplastics are plastic particles smaller than 1 micrometre (1,000 nanometres) in size — below the range of microplastics. They arise largely from the breakdown of larger plastic debris by sunlight, abrasion and weathering, and may also be released directly from products and processes such as synthetic textiles, cosmetics, tyre wear and 3D printing.

Occurrence

Because of their tiny size, nanoplastics are difficult and expensive to detect with standard methods, and their concentrations in the environment remain poorly quantified. They have been reported in ocean water, Arctic and Antarctic ice, soils, drinking water and bottled water, where studies using stimulated Raman scattering microscopy have counted on the order of hundreds of thousands of particles per litre, most of them nanoplastics.

Effects

Particles in this size range can cross biological membranes and have been detected in human blood, lung tissue and placentas. Laboratory studies show they can be taken up by cells, provoke inflammation and oxidative stress, and act as carriers for adsorbed pollutants and additives; their long-term significance for human and ecosystem health is an active area of research, complicated by the lack of standardised measurement techniques.
