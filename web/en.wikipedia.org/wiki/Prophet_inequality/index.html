Prophet inequality

In probability theory, a prophet inequality is a bound on the performance of online algorithms for optimal stopping problems, relative to an offline "prophet" who knows all future outcomes. The classical result compares a gambler who must accept or reject a sequence of independent non-negative random variables as they arrive, irrevocably, against a prophet who observes the entire sequence and selects the maximum.

Classical result

Suppose X1, ..., Xn are independent non-negative random variables with known distributions. A prophet obtains E[maxi Xi], while a decision-maker using a stopping rule τ obtains E[Xτ]. The prophet inequality states that there exists a simple threshold policy guaranteeing:

E[Xτ] ≥ (1/2) E[maxi Xi]

The factor 1/2 is tight in general. The result was proved by Krengel and Sucheston in the 1970s, with a simple proof via threshold policies given by Samuel-Cahn (1984): set a threshold at half the prophet's expected maximum and accept the first variable exceeding it.

Variants and applications

Prophet inequalities have been extended to matroid constraints, knapsacks, and other combinatorial settings, with applications to mechanism design — where they underpin posted-price mechanisms that approximate optimal auctions — and to online resource allocation. In the i.i.d. setting the achievable ratio improves to approximately 0.745, a result of Correa and coauthors building on work by Hill and Kertz. The free-order and random-order variants, in which the decision-maker can choose the order of inspection or the order is random, admit better constants.
