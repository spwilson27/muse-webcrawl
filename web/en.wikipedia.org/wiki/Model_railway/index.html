Rail transport modelling

Rail transport modelling (also called model railroading in the United States) is a hobby in which rail transport systems are modelled at a reduced scale. It typically involves building a layout with miniature track, trains, scenery, buildings and landscapes representing a real or fictional place and era.

Scales and gauges

Models are built to standard scales. The most popular worldwide include:

  • HO scale (1:87) — the most common scale in North America and continental Europe.
  • OO scale (1:76) — dominant in the United Kingdom.
  • N scale (1:160, or 1:148 in Britain) — allows large layouts in small spaces; especially popular in Japan.
  • O scale (about 1:43–1:48), Z scale (1:220) and G scale for garden railways.

"Gauge" refers to the distance between the rails; in standard gauge modelling the track represents the prototype 1,435 mm gauge, while narrow-gauge prototypes have their own modelling conventions.

Control

Traditionally, trains were controlled by varying the DC voltage on the track. Modern Digital Command Control (DCC) sends digital signals through the rails, allowing each locomotive — fitted with a decoder — to be controlled independently for speed, direction, lights and sound. Computer control and automation are increasingly common.

The hobby

Modellers range from those running ready-to-run trains on simple track ovals to "fine scale" enthusiasts who hand-build historically accurate scenes. Notable manufacturers include Hornby, Bachmann, Märklin, Kato and Athearn. Famous exhibition layouts include Miniatur Wunderland in Hamburg, the world's largest model railway attraction.

History

Model trains began as clockwork and live-steam toys in the 19th century, with firms such as Märklin (founded 1859) and Lionel standardising gauges. Electric models spread in the early 20th century, and the hobby matured into scale modelling after the Second World War.
