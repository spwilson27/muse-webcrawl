A Tale of a Tub is a prose satire by Jonathan Swift, written between 1694 and 1697 and first published anonymously in 1704. Regarded as Swift's first major work and one of the most difficult and celebrated satires in English literature, it interweaves an allegorical story of three brothers — Peter, Martin, and Jack, representing Roman Catholicism, Anglicanism, and dissenting Protestantism — with a series of extravagant digressions mocking contemporary abuses in religion, learning, and criticism.

Title and frame

The title refers to the tub that sailors throw overboard to distract a whale from attacking their ship; in his preface Swift explains that the "whale" is Thomas Hobbes's Leviathan, which threatened the ship of state and church, and the Tale is the tub thrown to divert it. The book is presented by a fictional bookseller and an earnest, modern "Author" — a Grub Street hack whose voice continually undermines itself.

Content

The narrative proper concerns a father who leaves each of his three sons a coat (a faith), with a will (the Bible) stipulating that the coats be neither altered nor adorned. As fashion changes, the brothers find ingenious ways to reinterpret the will to justify shoulder-knots, lace, and embroidery — a satire on how doctrines and corruptions were added to primitive Christianity. Peter (Catholicism) eventually claims supremacy and locks his brothers out; Martin (Anglicanism, named for Martin Luther) reforms cautiously, while Jack (Calvinism/Dissent, named for John Calvin) tears his coat to pieces in a frenzy of reform.

Between narrative sections come the famous digressions — on critics, on madness, on digressions themselves — written in the voice of a modern enthusiast who praises his own era's superficiality. The "Digression concerning the Original, the Use, and Improvement of Madness in a Commonwealth" advances the mock theory that all great conquerors, philosophers, and religious founders were madmen whose vapours rose to the brain.

Publication and reception

The volume of 1704 also contained The Battle of the Books and A Discourse Concerning the Mechanical Operation of the Spirit. Published anonymously, the work was attributed to Swift's cousin Thomas Swift until Jonathan claimed it in the "Apology" added to the fifth edition of 1710. Queen Anne is said to have taken the satire as an attack on religion itself, which may have cost Swift an English bishopric; Samuel Johnson later praised its genius, remarking that he doubted whether Swift's later works equalled the Tale.
