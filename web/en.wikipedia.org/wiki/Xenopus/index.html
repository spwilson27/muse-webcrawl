Xenopus

Xenopus (from Greek xenos, "strange", and pous, "foot" — referring to the black claws on its hind feet) is a genus of highly aquatic frogs native to sub-Saharan Africa, in the family Pipidae. The best-known species, the African clawed frog Xenopus laevis, is one of the most important vertebrate model organisms in developmental biology, cell biology, and toxicology, because its large, robust eggs and embryos develop externally and are easily manipulated. Members of the genus are distinguished by flattened bodies, fully webbed hind feet with clawed toes, eyes on top of the head, and the absence of a tongue; they feed underwater using their forelimbs to shovel food into the mouth.

Use in research

X. laevis was first widely used in the 1930s–1960s for the Hogben pregnancy test: urine from a pregnant woman injected into a female frog triggered egg-laying. The frogs were exported worldwide for this purpose — a dispersal hypothesised to have spread the chytrid fungus Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis implicated in global amphibian declines, though the hypothesis is contested. John Gurdon used Xenopus for his landmark nuclear-transfer experiments (1958–1962), proving that a differentiated cell's nucleus retains the full genome — work recognised by the 2012 Nobel Prize. Oocytes of X. laevis remain a standard expression system for ion channels and membrane proteins, and the diploid relative X. tropicalis is preferred for genetics because of its shorter generation time.

As an invasive species

Escaped or released X. laevis have established feral populations in California, France, Italy, Chile, and elsewhere, where they prey on native amphibians and fish and act as disease reservoirs; the species is regulated in several jurisdictions.
