Fitness tracker

A fitness tracker or activity tracker is a wearable device, usually worn on the wrist, that monitors and records fitness-related metrics such as steps taken, distance walked or run, calories burned, heart rate, and sleep quality. Most trackers synchronise with a smartphone application, which stores the data, displays trends, and sets goals for the user.

Technology

Trackers rely on a set of sensors. A three-axis accelerometer detects movement and is used to count steps and estimate activity intensity. Many models add an optical heart-rate sensor using photoplethysmography, which shines green light into the skin and measures blood flow from the reflected signal. Higher-end devices include GPS for route tracking, altimeters for floors climbed, gyroscopes, skin-temperature sensors, and blood-oxygen (SpO2) sensors. Sleep tracking is inferred from movement and heart-rate patterns rather than measured directly.

History

The concept descends from the mechanical pedometer and from early heart-rate monitors used by athletes, such as Polar's wireless chest-strap monitors of the 1980s. The modern category took off in the late 2000s: Fitbit released its first clip-on tracker in 2009, and wristband models from Fitbit, Jawbone and the Nike+ FuelBand popularised the form factor in the early 2010s. Smartwatches, beginning with devices such as the Pebble and later the Apple Watch (2015), absorbed most tracker functions, and the market consolidated; Google acquired Fitbit in 2021.

Accuracy and criticism

Studies generally find that step counts from wrist trackers are reasonably accurate at walking pace but degrade during other activities, while calorie-expenditure estimates can be substantially in error. Heart-rate readings are accurate at rest but less reliable during vigorous exercise. Critics raise privacy concerns, because trackers collect continuous location, health and sleep data that may be shared with employers, insurers or data brokers. Fitness data has occasionally surfaced in legal proceedings, and aggregated public data has revealed sensitive locations, as in the 2018 Strava heat-map incident.

Health effects

Trackers are a common tool in behaviour-change programmes and are often credited with nudging users toward the widely promoted goal of 10,000 steps a day — a target that originated in 1960s Japanese pedometer marketing rather than in medical research. Evidence that trackers alone produce sustained weight loss or health improvements is mixed; effects tend to be modest and to fade when users stop wearing the device.
