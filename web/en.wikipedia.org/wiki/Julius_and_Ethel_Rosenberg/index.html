Julius Rosenberg (1918–1953) and Ethel Rosenberg (1915–1953) were an American married couple convicted of conspiring to pass secrets about the atomic bomb to the Soviet Union during and after the Second World War. They were executed in the electric chair at Sing Sing Prison on 19 June 1953, the first American civilians put to death for espionage in peacetime.

Background

Julius Rosenberg, an electrical engineer and Communist Party member, worked for the US Army Signal Corps before being dismissed in 1945 over his party membership. According to decrypted Soviet cables (the Venona intercepts, released in the 1990s), Julius ran a spy ring that recruited engineers and passed secrets including designs for the proximity fuze and information from the Manhattan Project.

Arrest and trial

The investigation followed the arrest of physicist Klaus Fuchs in Britain, which led to courier Harry Gold and then to Ethel's brother David Greenglass, a machinist at Los Alamos, who implicated the Rosenbergs. At their trial in March 1951, the Greenglasses testified that Julius had passed atomic secrets, including a sketch of an implosion lens, and that Ethel had typed up David's notes. Judge Irving Kaufman sentenced them to death, declaring their crime "worse than murder".

Executions and aftermath

Despite an international campaign for clemency, including appeals from the Pope and protests worldwide, President Dwight D. Eisenhower refused to intervene, and the couple were executed on 19 June 1953, leaving two young sons, Michael and Robert.

Historical debate

The case became an enduring controversy. The Venona decrypts and later Soviet sources confirmed Julius's extensive espionage, but evidence for Ethel's active involvement was far thinner — grand jury testimony released in 2015 showed her brother David had not then implicated her in typing, a key trial claim he later recanted partially. Many historians now conclude that Julius was guilty of a serious espionage conspiracy, while Ethel's culpability and the severity of both death sentences remain disputed.
