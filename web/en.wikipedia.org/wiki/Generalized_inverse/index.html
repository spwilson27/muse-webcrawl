Generalized inverse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, and in particular linear algebra, a generalized inverse (or g-inverse) of a matrix A is a matrix that has some, but not necessarily all, of the properties of an inverse matrix. Generalized inverses can be defined for any matrix, including rectangular matrices and singular square matrices, for which an ordinary inverse does not exist.

Motivation

Consider the linear system

Ax = y

where A is an m×n matrix. If A is square and nonsingular, the unique solution is x = A−1y. When A is rectangular or singular, the system may have no solution or infinitely many; a generalized inverse provides a systematic way to obtain a solution, or a least-squares solution, in every case: for a suitable generalized inverse Ag, the vector x = Agy solves the system whenever a solution exists.

Types

Various classes of generalized inverse are defined by which of the four Penrose conditions they satisfy:

  1. A Ag A = A
  2. Ag A Ag = Ag
  3. (A Ag)* = A Ag
  4. (Ag A)* = Ag A
  • A {1}-inverse (inner inverse) satisfies condition 1 only; such inverses always exist but are not unique.
  • A reflexive inverse satisfies conditions 1 and 2.
  • The Moore–Penrose inverse, denoted A+, satisfies all four conditions. It exists and is unique for every matrix. It can be constructed from the singular value decomposition: if A = UΣV*, then A+ = VΣ+U*, where Σ+ inverts the nonzero singular values and transposes the matrix.
  • The Drazin inverse is used for singular square matrices, commuting with A and satisfying a form of condition 2 together with a power condition.
  • The Bott–Duffin inverse arises in constrained problems, notably in electrical network theory.

History

The idea was introduced by E. H. Moore in 1920 and independently by Roger Penrose in 1955; Arne Bjerhammar had also arrived at the same object in 1951 in the context of least squares. Generalized inverses are now standard tools in statistics (estimation in linear models), numerical analysis, signal processing, and control theory.

Applications

The Moore–Penrose inverse gives the minimum-norm least-squares solution of a linear system, making it central to linear regression, pseudoinverse-based solvers, and the training of certain machine learning models. In inconsistent systems it yields the vector that minimises ||Ax − y||, and among all minimisers the one of smallest norm.
