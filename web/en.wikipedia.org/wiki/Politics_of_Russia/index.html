Politics of Russia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The politics of Russia take place in a federal semi-presidential republic under the Constitution of 1993, adopted after the Soviet collapse amid the violent October 1993 confrontation between President Boris Yeltsin and the parliament. The presidency — elected for six-year terms (four before 2012) — dominates: it appoints the prime minister (with Duma consent), controls the security services and much of the media environment, and, after constitutional amendments in 2020, was permitted to seek further terms. The Federal Assembly (State Duma and Federation Council) and the judiciary operate with limited independence in most scholarly assessments.

The 1990s' competitive chaos (oligarchic privatisation, regional defiance, the Chechen wars) gave way after 2000 to Vladimir Putin's “power vertical”: the party of power (United Russia) dominating elections that observers (OSCE reports on successive polls) found neither free nor fair, systemic opposition parties tolerated within limits, and independent media and NGOs progressively restricted (foreign-agent laws from 2012). Scholars classify the system variously as electoral or competitive authoritarianism, a personalist regime or a hybrid degenerating into full authoritarianism after the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, which brought wartime censorship and repression to levels unseen since the late Soviet period. Russia's politics cannot be separated from its resource economy and its security services' corporate role.
