The National Collegiate Athletic Association (NCAA) is a nonprofit organisation that regulates student athletics among about 1,100 schools in the United States, and one Canadian province. It also organises the athletic programmes of colleges and helps more than 500,000 college student-athletes who compete annually in college sports. The organisation is headquartered in Indianapolis, Indiana.

History

The NCAA was founded in 1906 as the Intercollegiate Athletic Association of the United States (IAAUS), following meetings convened by President Theodore Roosevelt in response to a spate of deaths and serious injuries in college football. It adopted its present name in 1910. For decades it functioned mainly as a rules-making and discussion forum; it began enforcing rules and staging championships in earnest after Walter Byers became its first executive director in 1951. In 1973 the membership split into three divisions — Division I, II, and III — and in 1982 women's championships were added across all divisions.

Structure

Member schools are divided into three divisions based largely on enrolment, resources, and whether athletic scholarships are offered. Division I schools, particularly those in the Football Bowl Subdivision (FBS), operate the largest programmes. The NCAA stages 92 championships in 26 sports, the most famous of which is the Division I men's basketball tournament, known as "March Madness".

Finance and media

The NCAA derives the great majority of its revenue from television and marketing rights for its championships, above all the long-term contracts with CBS and Turner (and later Warner Bros. Discovery) for the men's basketball tournament. Most revenue is distributed back to member conferences and schools.

Criticism and reform

The NCAA's model of amateurism — under which student-athletes historically could not be paid or profit from their name, image, and likeness (NIL) — drew decades of criticism and litigation, including the Supreme Court's 2021 decision in NCAA v. Alston. Since July 2021 athletes have been permitted to profit from NIL deals under state laws and NCAA interim policy, and subsequent legal settlements have moved the association toward direct revenue sharing with athletes.
