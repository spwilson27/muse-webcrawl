Choice overload

Choice overload, also called overchoice and the paradox of choice, is a cognitive phenomenon in which having too many options makes it harder for people to make a decision, and can leave them less satisfied with the decision they do make. The term "overchoice" was introduced by Alvin Toffler in his book Future Shock (1970), and the idea was popularised by psychologist Barry Schwartz in The Paradox of Choice: Why More Is Less (2004).

Research

The best-known demonstration is the "jam study" by Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper (2000). In a grocery store, shoppers who encountered a display of 24 varieties of jam were much more likely to stop and taste than those who saw a display of 6 varieties — but far less likely to buy: about 3% of the large-display group purchased jam, compared with about 30% of the small-display group. Similar effects have been reported for chocolates, retirement savings plans (where participation falls as the number of funds grows) and other domains.

Proposed mechanisms include decision paralysis and deferral, anticipated and experienced regret, heightened expectations, self-blame when outcomes disappoint, and the cognitive effort of comparing many similar alternatives. Schwartz argued that abundant choice in modern consumer societies contributes to dissatisfaction and even depression, and distinguished "maximisers", who seek the best option and suffer most from overload, from "satisficers", who settle for good enough.

Boundary conditions

Later research qualified the effect: a 2010 meta-analysis by Benjamin Scheibehenne and colleagues found an average effect near zero across studies, with large variation. Choice overload appears stronger when the decision is complex or important, when options are hard to compare, when the chooser lacks clear preferences or expertise, and when no default option is provided. Organising options into categories, providing defaults, and curating assortments can mitigate the effect — one reason large retailers and retirement plans often limit or structure their offerings.
