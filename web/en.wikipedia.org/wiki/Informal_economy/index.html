Informal economy

[Image: street vendors with stalls of goods in an informal market]

The informal economy (shadow or underground economy, in part) comprises economic activities and jobs that are — in law or in practice — not covered by formal arrangements: unregistered small businesses, casual day labour, street vending, domestic work and undeclared 'cash-in-hand' work. It is legal in substance in most cases, unlike the Black market, but escapes Taxation, regulation and official statistics.

Scale and significance

The International Labour Organization estimates about two billion workers — roughly 60% of the world's employed — work informally, the share exceeding 85% in much of Africa and South Asia and falling to under 20% in most of Europe. Hernando de Soto argued that excessive bureaucracy pushes the poor into informality and that formal property titles would unlock 'dead capital'; others emphasise that informal work lacks social protection, safety standards and credit access. Policy aims at gradual formalisation — simplified registration, microfinance, digital payments — without destroying livelihoods. Informality complicates measuring GDP and Unemployment and is a central topic of development economics.
