Xerography

Xerography is a dry photocopying technique, originally called electrophotography, invented by Chester Carlson, who filed his fundamental patents in the late 1930s and demonstrated the process in 1938 with the first photocopy, made in Astoria, Queens. The name derives from the Greek xēros ("dry") and graphein ("writing"), because the process uses no liquid chemicals.

Process

Xerography works in six steps: (1) a photoconductive drum or belt is uniformly electrostatically charged; (2) exposure to the image discharges the illuminated areas, leaving a latent electrostatic image; (3) charged toner particles are developed onto the image areas; (4) the toner is transferred to paper; (5) heat and pressure fuse the toner permanently; and (6) the photoreceptor is cleaned and recharged. The same basic cycle drives laser printers, which expose the photoreceptor with a scanning laser or LED array rather than a reflected image.

Commercialisation

After years of rejection by manufacturers, Carlson licensed the process to the Haloid Company, which renamed itself Xerox and launched the first automatic office copier, the Xerox 914, in 1959. The 914's success created the modern photocopying industry; xerography remains the dominant technology in copiers and laser printers.
