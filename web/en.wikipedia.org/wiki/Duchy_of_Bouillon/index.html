The Duchy of Bouillon was a small sovereign territory in the Ardennes, centred on the town and castle of Bouillon on the river Semois, in what is now the Belgian province of Luxembourg. Strategically placed between France, the Spanish Netherlands and the prince-bishopric of Liège, the duchy survived for centuries as a contested frontier state.

History

The lordship of Bouillon is associated with Godfrey of Bouillon, the first ruler of the Crusader Kingdom of Jerusalem, who mortgaged the castle to the bishop of Liège to finance his expedition in 1096. The bishops retained Bouillon for centuries, while the French La Tour d'Auvergne family, vicomtes of Turenne, advanced a rival claim. In 1678, under the Treaties of Nijmegen, the duchy passed to the La Tour d'Auvergne as sovereign dukes, though it remained under French protection.

End of the duchy

The duchy was swept away during the French Revolutionary Wars: it was briefly proclaimed a republic in 1794 and annexed to France in 1795. The Congress of Vienna assigned the territory to the Netherlands in 1815, and after the Belgian Revolution of 1830 Bouillon became part of Belgium.
