Trauma bonding

Trauma bonding is a psychological response to abuse in which the abused person forms a strong emotional attachment to their abuser, arising from a recurring cycle of abuse punctuated by intermittent reinforcement—periods of reward, affection, or calm following mistreatment. The concept was developed by Patrick Carnes and is used in the study of abusive relationships, hostage situations, and coercive control.

Characteristics

A trauma bond typically involves an imbalance of power, cycles of abuse and reconciliation ("honeymoon" phases), and intense emotional experiences that bind the victim to the perpetrator. Victims may defend the abuser, minimize the abuse, feel unable to leave despite harm, and experience symptoms resembling Stockholm syndrome. Intermittent reinforcement—the unpredictable alternation of cruelty and kindness—is considered a powerful condition for forming such bonds, similar to variable-ratio reinforcement schedules in behavioral psychology.

Contexts

Trauma bonding has been described in domestic violence, human trafficking, cults, child abuse, and hostage-taking. It is distinguished from ordinary attachment or dependency: clinicians caution that the term is sometimes used loosely in popular media for any difficult relationship, diluting its clinical meaning.

Treatment

Interventions emphasize safety planning, psychoeducation about abuse cycles, trauma-focused therapy, and rebuilding social support and self-trust. Breaking the bond is often gradual, as the victim processes grief for the intermittent positive aspects of the relationship as well as the harm.
