Silicone

Silicone is a synthetic polymer made up of siloxane (–R2Si–O–SiR2–, where R is an organic group). Silicones are mixed inorganic–organic polymers with the chemical formula [R2SiO]n. They are typically colourless oils or rubber-like substances.

Silicones are used in sealants, adhesives, lubricants, medicine, cooking utensils, thermal insulation, and electrical insulation. Common forms include silicone oil, silicone grease, silicone rubber, silicone resin, and silicone caulk.

History

The chemist Frederick Kipping coined the word "silicone" in 1901, by analogy with ketones, which he believed (incorrectly) had a similar structure. Commercial development followed in the 1930s and 1940s, notably through the work of Eugene G. Rochow at General Electric, who developed the direct process (the Müller–Rochow process) for producing the methylchlorosilane intermediates from silicon and methyl chloride over a copper catalyst. Dow Corning was founded in 1943 to exploit these materials.

Properties

Silicones exhibit low thermal conductivity, low chemical reactivity, low toxicity, thermal stability over a wide temperature range (roughly −100 to 250 °C), water repellency, and excellent electrical insulation. The Si–O backbone is much stronger and more flexible than the C–C backbone of organic polymers, giving silicone elastomers their characteristic resilience and weather resistance.

Uses

Applications include automotive and aerospace gaskets and lubricants, construction sealants and waterproofing, cookware and bakeware, medical devices and implants, personal care products (where cyclomethicones and dimethicone are common), and electronics potting and insulation.

Safety and environment

Silicones are generally regarded as low in toxicity, though certain low-molecular-weight cyclic siloxanes (D4 and D5) have attracted regulatory scrutiny for persistence and bioaccumulation in the environment.
