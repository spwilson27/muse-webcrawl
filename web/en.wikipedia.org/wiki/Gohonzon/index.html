Gohonzon

Gohonzon (Japanese: 御本尊) is the central object of devotion in Nichiren Buddhism. In its best-known form it is a calligraphic mandala — a hanging scroll inscribed with Chinese and Sanskrit characters — first created by the 13th-century monk Nichiren, who regarded it as the embodiment of the Lotus Sutra's teaching and of his own enlightenment. Practitioners enshrine the Gohonzon in a butsudan (household altar) and chant Namu Myōhō Renge Kyō before it in the practice of gongyo.

The mandala

Down the centre of the scroll runs the daimoku, Namu Myōhō Renge Kyō, flanked by the names of the Buddhas Shakyamuni and Tahō (Many Treasures) and by the bodhisattvas of the Lotus Sutra, protective deities of Indian, Chinese and Japanese tradition, and figures representing the ten worlds. Nichiren signed the mandala and, according to tradition, first inscribed it in 1271 while in exile on Sado Island.

Meaning

In Nichiren's writings the Gohonzon is not an idol but a "mirror" of the practitioner's own life: he taught that the mandala embodies the Mystic Law and the state of Buddhahood inherent in all people, and that faith in it, expressed through chanting, allows that Buddhahood to manifest. Different Nichiren schools differ over interpretation and over who may confer copies of the mandala on believers.

The Dai-Gohonzon

The Dai-Gohonzon, inscribed on wood in 1279 and enshrined at Taiseki-ji, head temple of Nichiren Shōshū near Mount Fuji, is regarded by that school as the supreme object of devotion, to which all other Gohonzon are transcriptions. Since the 1991 schism between Nichiren Shōshū and the Soka Gakkai, the lay organisation has issued its own transcribed Gohonzon, a point of continuing dispute.
