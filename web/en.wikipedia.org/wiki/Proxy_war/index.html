Proxy war

Proxy war
TypeIndirect armed conflict
ActorsExternal sponsors and local proxies
Notable eraCold War (1947–1991)

A proxy war is an armed conflict between two belligerents in which at least one side acts at the instigation, or on behalf, of a party that is not directly fighting. External sponsors typically supply money, weapons, training, intelligence or logistical support to local forces — states, insurgent movements or militias — whose own goals overlap with, but need not be identical to, those of the sponsor. Proxy warfare allows great powers to pursue strategic objectives while limiting the risk of direct confrontation, escalation or the domestic political costs of deploying their own troops.

Characteristics

Proxy relationships range from loose material sponsorship to near-total control. Analysts distinguish the proxy war from related concepts such as covert action, foreign military intervention and surrogate warfare; a defining feature is that the burden of fighting and dying falls overwhelmingly on the proxies and the population of the contested territory rather than on the sponsoring powers.

History

Indirect sponsorship of friendly belligerents is as old as statecraft, but the term "proxy war" became prominent during the Cold War, when the United States and the Soviet Union — deterred from direct war by nuclear weapons — competed through local allies. The Korean War, the Vietnam War, the Soviet–Afghan War and conflicts in Angola, Nicaragua, Ethiopia and El Salvador are standard examples: in each, one or both superpowers armed and financed opposing sides.

Motivations and risks

Sponsors may seek to weaken a rival, secure resources or bases, uphold credibility with allies, or test weapons and doctrines. For proxies, external support can be decisive in sustaining a war effort. The arrangement carries well-documented risks: sponsors exercise imperfect control over their proxies (the "principal–agent problem"), support can prolong conflicts and intensify their destructiveness, weapons can diffuse to unintended users, and proxy wars can escalate into direct confrontations. Humanitarian consequences for the host population are typically severe.

Contemporary examples

Scholars and journalists have applied the term to the civil wars in Syria and Yemen, where regional and global powers backed rival factions, and to aspects of the conflict in Libya. The applicability of the label to any given war is often contested, since local belligerents retain their own agendas and wars usually combine internal and external causes.
