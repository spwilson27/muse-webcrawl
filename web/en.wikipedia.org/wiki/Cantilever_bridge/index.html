Cantilever bridge

Cantilever bridge
AncestorTruss bridge, beam bridge
CarriesPedestrians, vehicles, trains, pipelines
Span rangeShort to long (up to ~550 m main span)
MaterialSteel, prestressed concrete, timber (historically)
Falsework requiredNo — built outward from piers
Famous examplesForth Bridge; Quebec Bridge; Minato Bridge

A cantilever bridge is a bridge built using structures that project horizontally into space, supported on only one end (cantilevers). For small footbridges, the cantilevers may be simple beams; however, large cantilever bridges designed to handle road or rail traffic use trusses built from structural steel, or box girders built from prestressed concrete.

The steel truss cantilever bridge was a major engineering breakthrough when first put into practice, because it can span distances of over 1,500 feet (460 m) and can be more easily constructed at difficult crossings by virtue of using little or no falsework (temporary supports during construction): each arm is built outward in balance from its pier, so no support is needed in the water or gorge below.

Structure

In a typical through-truss cantilever bridge, two cantilever arms project from opposite piers toward the centre. Often they do not meet but instead support a short central truss — a suspended span — which rests on the ends of the cantilever arms. The upper members of the cantilever are in tension and the lower members in compression. Anchor arms extend from the piers back to the abutments, counterbalancing the main span.

A balanced cantilever design, widely used for concrete box-girder bridges, builds segments symmetrically out from each pier so the structure remains in equilibrium throughout construction; the segments are either cast in place with travelling formwork or precast and lifted into position, then joined by post-tensioned cables.

History

Cantilever bridges were built in timber and stone in China and Tibet for centuries, with arms of corbelled logs projecting from the banks. The modern steel cantilever was pioneered in Germany by Heinrich Gerber, who patented the design and built the Hassfurt Bridge over the Main in 1867 with a hinged suspended span. In North America, C. Shaler Smith's High Bridge in Kentucky (1877) and the Niagara Cantilever Bridge (1883) demonstrated the type's power.

The Forth Bridge in Scotland (1890), designed by John Fowler and Benjamin Baker, became the archetypal cantilever bridge, with a main span of 521 m; Baker famously demonstrated the principle in a photograph, with two men seated on chairs representing the piers, their arms and sticks forming the cantilever arms and suspended span, and engineer Kaichi Watanabe sitting on the central "suspended" platform.

The Quebec Bridge, still the longest cantilever span in the world at 549 m (1,801 ft), collapsed twice during construction — in 1907, killing 75 workers, and in 1916, when the suspended span fell while being lifted, killing 13 more — before completion in 1919. The disaster led to major reforms in engineering education and professional licensing in Canada, commemorated by the Iron Ring worn by Canadian engineers.

Longest cantilever bridges

  • Quebec Bridge, Canada — 549 m (1919)
  • Forth Bridge, Scotland — 521 m (1890)
  • Minato Bridge, Osaka, Japan — 510 m (1974)
  • Commodore Barry Bridge, Pennsylvania, USA — 501 m (1974)
  • Crescent City Connection (two spans), Louisiana, USA — 480 m (1958/1988)

Advantages and disadvantages

Cantilever construction excels where falsework is impossible — deep water, navigation channels, steep gorges — and can achieve long spans in steel. Against this, the structures are heavier than comparable suspension or cable-stayed designs, use more material, and require careful maintenance of many truss members and connections, which is why large new cantilevers have become rare in favour of cable-stayed bridges.
