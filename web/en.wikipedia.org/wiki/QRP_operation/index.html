QRP operation

PracticeAmateur radio at very low power
Typical power≤ 5 W (CW); ≤ 10 W PEP (SSB)
Name originQ code "QRP" = "shall I reduce power?"

QRP operation is a pursuit within amateur radio in which operators make contacts using very low transmitter power — conventionally 5 watts or less for CW (Morse code) and 10 watts PEP or less for single sideband voice. The name comes from the Q code signal QRP, meaning "Shall I reduce transmitter power?", now used as a noun for low-power operating itself. Still lower power levels, below one watt, are often called QRPp.

Philosophy and practice

QRP enthusiasts argue that the challenge of communicating across continents with a few watts — "doing more with less" — restores skill and experimentation to a hobby in which legal-limit amplifiers of 1,000 watts or more are common. Successful QRP work depends on efficient antennas, good operating technique, and favourable propagation rather than raw power. Because the decibel penalty of 5 W relative to 100 W is only about 13 dB, and signals vary naturally by more than that, long-distance (DX) contacts at QRP levels are routine; operators have worked all continents with under one watt, and a celebrated measure of achievement is "miles per watt".

Equipment and community

QRP operation encourages home construction of simple, inexpensive transceivers — classic designs include the Pixie and the Rock-Mite — as well as portable operation from hilltops and parks, where light batteries suffice. Clubs such as the QRP Amateur Radio Club International and GQRP Club, dedicated magazines, contests and awards sustain the subculture. Weak-signal digital modes such as WSPR and FT8 have extended low-power techniques to extremely low power levels.
