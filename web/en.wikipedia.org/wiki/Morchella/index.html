Morchella

Morchella, the true morels, is a genus of edible sac fungi closely related to anatomically simpler cup fungi in the order Pezizales (division Ascomycota). These distinctive fungi have a honeycomb appearance due to the network of ridges with pits composing their caps. Morels are prized by gourmet cooks, particularly for French cuisine. Commercial farming of morels has proven difficult, making them expensive wild-harvested delicacies.

The genus was described by Persoon in 1794, with Morchella esculenta as the type species. Taxonomy of the group was historically confused, but modern phylogenetic studies have recognised several distinct lineages, broadly grouped into the "yellow morels" (Esculenta clade), "black morels" (Elata clade) and the early-diverging Rufobrunnea clade.

Morels fruit in spring in temperate forests of the Northern Hemisphere, often associated with trees such as ash, elm, oak and apple, and sometimes appearing prolifically after forest fires. They must be cooked before eating, as raw morels contain hydrazinic toxins. False morels (species of Gyromitra and Verpa) resemble true morels but may be poisonous; a true morel is hollow from cap to stem, whereas a false morel is not.

Morel hunting is a popular seasonal activity in North America and Europe, with festivals held in many towns. Efforts at indoor cultivation, particularly in China since the 2010s, have achieved partial success.
