Algebraic geometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A cubic curve and a torus drawn together, geometry meeting algebra]

Algebraic geometry is a branch of mathematics that studies geometric objects defined by polynomial equations — algebraic varieties — combining techniques from abstract algebra (commutative algebra) with the language of geometry. Its simplest objects are curves (elliptic curves) and surfaces cut out by polynomials in several variables.

Classical algebraic geometry grew from analytic geometry (Descartes) and the 19th-century Italian school; in the 20th century it was refounded by Oscar Zariski and comprehensively rebuilt by Alexander Grothendieck in the 1960s around schemes — spaces built from rings — enabling number theory and geometry to merge (arithmetic geometry).

Landmarks

Elliptic curves over finite fields underpin modern public-key cryptography (and are central to Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, via the modularity theorem). Grothendieck's scheme theory resolved the Weil conjectures (finished by Deligne, 1974), among the century's deepest results.

The subject's abstraction is notorious ('rising sea' philosophy — Grothendieck compared his style to letting problems dissolve as the sea rises) yet it continuously feeds applications from error-correcting codes to string theory.
