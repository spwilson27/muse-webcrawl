Trait theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In psychology, trait theory is an approach to the study of human personality that measures traits — habitual patterns of behaviour, thought, and emotion — which are considered relatively stable over time, differing among individuals and influencing behaviour across situations. Trait theory contrasts with state theories and with approaches that attribute behaviour mainly to social or situational factors.

History

Gordon Allport's lexical study of 1936 identified thousands of trait words in the English language and distinguished cardinal, central, and secondary traits. Raymond Cattell used factor analysis to reduce personality descriptors to sixteen factors (the 16PF model), while Hans Eysenck proposed a smaller set of superfactors — extraversion, neuroticism, and later psychoticism.

The Big Five

Modern research converged on the Big Five model: openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism (OCEAN). Derived from the lexical hypothesis and questionnaire factor analysis, the Big Five are widely used in research and show moderate heritability and cross-cultural recurrence. An influential extension, the HEXACO model, adds an honesty–humility dimension and reorganises some factors.

Criticism

Critics, notably Walter Mischel in Personality and Assessment (1968), argued that behaviour is less consistent across situations than trait theory predicts, sparking the person–situation debate. Later work resolved much of the dispute by noting that traits predict aggregated behaviour over time better than single acts, and that traits and situations interact.
