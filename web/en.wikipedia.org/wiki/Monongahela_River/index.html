The Monongahela River — often referred to locally as "the Mon" — is a 130-mile-long (210 km) river on the Allegheny Plateau in north-central West Virginia and southwestern Pennsylvania. It rises at Fairmont, West Virginia, where the Tygart Valley River and West Fork River join, and flows north to Pittsburgh, where it meets the Allegheny River to form the Ohio River. The name derives from a Lenape phrase meaning "falling banks," referring to the river's eroding bluffs.

History

The Monongahela valley was a corridor of early conflict in the French and Indian War: in 1754 George Washington fought the Battle of Jumonville Glen nearby, and in 1755 General Edward Braddock's expedition was destroyed at the Battle of the Monongahela near the river's mouth. In the nineteenth century the river became a major artery for the coal, coke and steel industries of southwestern Pennsylvania, and a system of locks and dams, completed by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, made it navigable along its whole length.

Industry and transportation

The river remains one of the busiest inland waterways in the United States, carrying coal, limestone, sand and gravel to steel mills and power plants. Barges moving through its nine locks carry millions of tons of cargo annually. The valley's steel towns — Homestead, Braddock, Duquesne and Clairton — were centres of the industry dramatized by Andrew Carnegie's mills, and the 1892 Homestead Strike occurred on its banks.
