Expressionism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The Scream (1893) by Edvard Munch: an androgynous figure on a bridge clutching its face beneath a blood-red sky

Expressionism is a modernist movement, initially in poetry and painting, originating in Northern Europe around the beginning of the 20th century. Its practitioners presented the world solely from a subjective perspective, distorting it radically for emotional effect in order to evoke moods or ideas, rather than depicting objective reality.

Key groups included Die Brücke (The Bridge), founded in Dresden in 1905 by Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, Fritz Bleyl, Erich Heckel and Karl Schmidt-Rottluff, and Der Blaue Reiter (The Blue Rider), formed in Munich in 1911 around Wassily Kandinsky and Franz Marc. Edvard Munch's The Scream (1893) is an important precursor and the movement's most famous image.

Spread and influence

Expressionism extended into literature, theatre, dance, film and architecture. German Expressionist cinema of the 1920s — The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920), Nosferatu (1922) — translated its distorted sets and psychological intensity to the screen. The style also fed into later movements such as Abstract Expressionism and Neo-expressionism.
