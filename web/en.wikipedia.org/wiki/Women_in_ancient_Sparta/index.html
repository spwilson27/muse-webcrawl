Women in ancient Sparta

Spartan women were famous in ancient Greece for having more freedom than women elsewhere in the Greek world. Unlike in Athens, Spartan women could legally own and inherit property, and were usually better educated. The primary duty of a Spartan woman was to bear and raise physically fit children to become Spartiate soldiers.

Childhood and education

Spartan girls received a public education provided by the state, unlike girls in most other Greek cities. Their schooling included athletics — running, wrestling, discus and javelin — intended to make them physically strong mothers. They also learned music, dance and poetry, and performed in public choruses; the lyric poet Alcman composed songs for girls' choruses in the seventh century BC.

Marriage and family life

Spartan women married later than other Greek women, typically around the age of eighteen or twenty, and to men of roughly their own age. Because Spartan men lived in barracks until the age of thirty, wives often ran the household. The reforms attributed to Lycurgus aimed marriage at producing healthy offspring.

Property and status

By the fourth century BC, women owned a substantial share of Spartan land — Aristotle complained that nearly two-fifths of Sparta was in women's hands — because inheritance passed through daughters when male lines died out in war. Spartan women could not hold political office, however, and were excluded from the assembly and magistracies.
