Environmental disaster

An environmental disaster is a catastrophic event, natural or human-caused, that severely damages ecosystems and the human communities depending on them. The term is most often applied to human-caused events such as industrial accidents, oil spills and deforestation, and sometimes to natural events whose damage is greatly worsened by human choices.

Environmental disasters can unfold in minutes, as at Bhopal, or over decades, as with the shrinking of the Aral Sea.

Notable examples

The Bhopal gas leak (India, 1984), in which methyl isocyanate escaped from a pesticide plant, killed thousands and injured far more, and remains the deadliest industrial disaster. The Chernobyl explosion (Ukraine, 1986) spread radioactive contamination across parts of Europe and created an exclusion zone. The Exxon Valdez (1989) and Deepwater Horizon (2010) oil spills devastated marine and coastal life in Alaska and the Gulf of Mexico respectively.

Slow-motion disasters include the diversion of rivers feeding the Aral Sea for cotton irrigation, which destroyed one of the world's largest lakes and its fisheries; the 1930s North American Dust Bowl, where drought met destructive farming practice; and the 1991 Kuwait oil fires set during the Gulf War. Minamata disease in Japan showed how industrial mercury poisoning could pass through seafood to people.

Causes and prevention

Common ingredients are hazardous technology operated with weak regulation, cost-cutting, poor maintenance and ignored warnings. Prevention rests on safety engineering and culture, land-use planning that keeps people and ecosystems out of harm's way, emergency preparedness, and liability rules that internalise risk.
