Ancient Mesopotamian religion refers to the religious beliefs (concerning the cosmos, the gods, humanity, and worship) and practices of the civilizations of ancient Mesopotamia, particularly Sumer, Akkad, Assyria, and Babylonia between circa 3500 BCE and 400 CE, when it largely died out through gradual decline and Christianization.

It was a polytheistic system in which each city-state had its patron deity, and humans existed to serve the gods, who controlled natural forces and destinies and were honoured in temples called ziggurats.

Deities

The pantheon was headed by Anu, god of the sky; Enlil, god of wind and chief executive of the gods; and Enki (Ea), god of fresh water, wisdom, and magic. Inanna (Ishtar), goddess of love and war, was among the most widely worshipped. In Babylon, Marduk rose to supremacy as the national god, celebrated in the creation epic Enuma Elish; in Assyria, Ashur held that role. Other major gods included Shamash (sun and justice), Sin (moon), and Nergal (plague and the underworld).

Mythology and worship

Mesopotamian myths, including the Epic of Gilgamesh and the flood story of Atrahasis, influenced later traditions including elements of the Hebrew Bible. Worship centred on temple households where priests fed and clothed cult statues; festivals such as the Babylonian New Year (Akitu) renewed the king's mandate. Divination—by omens, astrology, and extispicy (examining animal entrails)—guided state and personal decisions, and Mesopotamian astral science profoundly shaped later astronomy.

Afterlife

The dead were believed to descend to a bleak underworld, the Kur or House of Dust, regardless of their conduct in life—a pessimistic outlook contrasting with Egyptian and later traditions.
