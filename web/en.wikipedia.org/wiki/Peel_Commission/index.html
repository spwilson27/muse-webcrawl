Peel Commission

The Peel Commission, formally the Palestine Royal Commission, was a British Royal Commission of Inquiry headed by Lord Peel, appointed in 1936 to investigate the causes of unrest in Mandatory Palestine. Its report, published on 7 July 1937, was the first official proposal to partition Palestine into separate Jewish and Arab states — the first formal articulation of the two-state idea.

The partition plan proposed by the Peel Commission in 1937.

Background

The commission was appointed following the outbreak of the 1936–39 Arab revolt in Palestine, which began with a general strike protesting Jewish immigration and land purchases and British rule. The six-member commission, chaired by William Peel, 1st Earl Peel, arrived in Palestine in November 1936 and heard testimony from Arab, Jewish and British officials, including David Ben-Gurion and Chaim Weizmann for the Zionist side.

The report

The report concluded that the Mandate's twin obligations — to foster a Jewish national home and to protect the rights of the Arab population — had become irreconcilable: "an irrepressible conflict has arisen between two national communities within the narrow bounds of one small country." It recommended ending the Mandate and partitioning the country: a Jewish state in the Galilee and the coastal plain (about a fifth of the territory), an Arab state comprising the hill country and the Negev to be united with Transjordan, and a continued British mandate over Jerusalem, Bethlehem and a corridor to the sea, together with enclaves at Jaffa, Haifa and elsewhere. The report also contemplated an exchange of populations between the two states.

Reactions and aftermath

The Arab leadership rejected partition outright and the revolt resumed. The Zionist Congress of 1937 rejected the specific boundaries but authorised negotiations on partition in principle, a decision Ben-Gurion defended as a stepping stone. The British government initially endorsed the plan but then appointed the Woodhead Commission, whose 1938 report declared partition impracticable; Britain formally abandoned it in the White Paper of 1939. The Peel plan nevertheless set the frame for later proposals, including the 1947 UN Partition Plan.
