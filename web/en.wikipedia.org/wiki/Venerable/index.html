Venerable

Venerable (from Latin venerabilis, "worthy of reverence") is a title and style of address used in several Christian traditions, and in Buddhism, to denote a person recognised for holiness, seniority or learning. In ordinary English the word also serves as a general adjective meaning "accorded great respect because of age, wisdom or character".

Bede, the eighth-century English monk, is the best-known bearer of the title "the Venerable".

In the Roman Catholic Church

In the process of canonisation, Venerable is the second of four stages: a deceased person is first named a Servant of God, then declared Venerable by papal decree once the Pope recognises that they lived a life of "heroic virtue". Beatification (Blessed) and canonisation (Saint) follow. One of the oldest and most famous uses is that of the Venerable Bede (c. 673–735), the English monk and historian of Jarrow, author of the Ecclesiastical History of the English People; his title arose from tradition and was confirmed by the Church.

In the Anglican Communion

In the Church of England and other Anglican churches, The Venerable is the style of an archdeacon, a senior cleric who assists a bishop with the administration of a diocese. It is used as a prefix before the name (e.g. "The Venerable Jane Smith, Archdeacon of X") for the duration of the appointment.

In Buddhism

In Theravada and other Buddhist traditions, "Venerable" (often abbreviated "Ven.") is an honorific for ordained monks and nuns, especially in English-language usage, translating respectful forms of address such as the Pali āyasmā or the titles Bhante and Ajahn used in Thailand.

Other uses

The word is also used outside religion: some orders and fraternal organisations style senior officers "Venerable" (as in the Worshipful Company traditions), and in everyday speech anything old and respected — a "venerable institution" or "venerable oak" — may carry the adjective.
