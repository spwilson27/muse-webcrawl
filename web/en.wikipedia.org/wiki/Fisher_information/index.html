In mathematical statistics, the Fisher information is a way of measuring the amount of information that an observable random variable X carries about an unknown parameter θ of a distribution that models X. Formally, it is the variance of the score, or the expected value of the observed information.

The Fisher information was introduced by the statistician Ronald Fisher in the early twentieth century. Unlike the entropy of a distribution, which does not involve any unknown parameter, the Fisher information depends on the parameter to be estimated and quantifies how sensitively the data distribution responds to changes in that parameter.

Definition

If f(x; θ) is the probability density (or mass) function of X conditional on the value of θ, the score is the gradient of the log-likelihood with respect to θ. The Fisher information is the expected value of the squared score:

I(θ) = E[ (∂/∂θ ln f(X; θ))² | θ ]

Under regularity conditions it equals the negative expected value of the second derivative of the log-likelihood:

I(θ) = −E[ ∂²/∂θ² ln f(X; θ) | θ ]

Cramér–Rao bound

The Fisher information appears in the Cramér–Rao bound, which states that the variance of any unbiased estimator of θ is at least the reciprocal of the Fisher information. Estimators that attain the bound are called efficient. Thus a large Fisher information implies that the parameter can be estimated precisely, while a small Fisher information implies that the data are relatively uninformative about the parameter.

Matrix form

When the parameter is a vector, the Fisher information generalises to the Fisher information matrix, whose entries are covariances of the components of the score vector. The matrix is central to asymptotic theory, defining the covariance of maximum likelihood estimators in the large-sample limit, and it serves as a Riemannian metric on statistical manifolds in the field of information geometry.

Applications

Fisher information is used in experimental design, where designs may be chosen to maximise information about parameters of interest; in the theory of Jeffreys priors in Bayesian statistics; in information geometry; and in physics, where it has been applied to problems ranging from measurement theory to the derivation of physical laws. It is also related to the concept of observed information, the negative Hessian of the log-likelihood evaluated at the observed data and the maximum likelihood estimate.
