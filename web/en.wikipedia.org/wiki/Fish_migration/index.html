Fish migration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fish migration is the mass movement of fish from one area or body of water to another, undertaken regularly — daily, seasonally, or once in a lifetime — usually in connection with feeding or breeding. Many species of fish migrate on a regular basis, on time scales ranging from daily vertical movements to annual journeys spanning thousands of kilometres.

Classification

Following the terminology developed by George S. Myers, fish that migrate between fresh and salt water are diadromous. Anadromous fish, such as salmon, shad, and sea lampreys, live in the sea and migrate up rivers to spawn in fresh water. Catadromous fish, notably freshwater eels, do the reverse, migrating from rivers to oceanic spawning grounds such as the Sargasso Sea. Movements wholly within the sea are oceanodromous (e.g., tuna, herring, and the "sardine run" of southern Africa), and those wholly within fresh water are potamodromous (e.g., many carps and trout migrating upstream to spawn).

Mechanisms and cues

Migrating fish navigate using combinations of celestial cues, the Earth's magnetic field, water currents, temperature gradients, and olfaction — salmon famously imprint on the scent of their natal stream as juveniles and home to it as adults. Physiological changes, such as the smoltification of young salmon, prepare the body for switching between fresh and salt water.

Ecology and human impacts

Migrations move enormous quantities of nutrients between ecosystems and support major commercial fisheries. They also leave fish vulnerable: dams and weirs block river migrations, driving declines in salmon, sturgeon, and eel populations and prompting construction of fish ladders, lifts, and bypass channels, as well as dam removals. Overfishing on migration routes and climate-driven shifts in currents and temperature pose additional threats.
