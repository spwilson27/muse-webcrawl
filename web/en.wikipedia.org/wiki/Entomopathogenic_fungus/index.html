Entomopathogenic fungus

An entomopathogenic fungus is a fungus that infects and kills insects and other arthropods, acting as a parasite that is lethal to its host. Known from more than 700 species across several fungal phyla, these fungi play a major role in regulating insect populations in nature and are exploited commercially as biological pest-control agents, in some cases on an industrial scale.

Infection cycle

Unlike bacteria and viruses, which must usually be eaten, entomopathogenic fungi infect through the insect's cuticle. A spore landing on the host sticks to the cuticle, germinates, and produces a penetration structure (an appressorium) that breaches the exoskeleton using pressure and cuticle-degrading enzymes such as chitinases and proteases. Inside the body cavity the fungus proliferates in the haemolymph — species of Beauveria and Metarhizium often produce toxins (destruxins, beauvericin) that help kill the host within days. After death the cadaver mummifies, and under humid conditions fungal hyphae emerge through the cuticle and produce new spores on the surface, ready to spread to other insects.

Important genera

  • Beauveria bassiana — a broad-host-range species behind many commercial mycoinsecticides; the disease it causes is called white muscardine and was studied by Agostino Bassi in silkworms in the 1830s, a landmark in germ theory.
  • Metarhizium species — used against locusts, termites and ticks; the product "Green Muscle" targets locusts in Africa.
  • Ophiocordyceps — includes the "zombie-ant fungus" Ophiocordyceps unilateralis, which manipulates infected ants into climbing vegetation and biting leaves before death, positioning the fruiting body for spore dispersal. The related Ophiocordyceps sinensis (caterpillar fungus) is prized in traditional Chinese medicine.
  • Entomophthora and relatives — often highly host-specific, causing dramatic natural epizootics among flies and aphids.

Use in pest control

Mass-produced spores are sprayed like chemical insecticides against pests including whiteflies, thrips, locusts and malaria-transmitting mosquitoes. Advantages include safety to vertebrates and the ability to persist and spread in the pest population; disadvantages include slower kill than chemicals, and dependence on warm, humid conditions for the spores to germinate.
