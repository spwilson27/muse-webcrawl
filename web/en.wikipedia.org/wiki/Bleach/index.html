Bleach
Common typesSodium hypochlorite, hydrogen peroxide, calcium hypochlorite
UsesWhitening, disinfecting, stain removal
Active principleOxidation of chromophores and microbial proteins

Bleach is the generic name for any chemical product that is used industrially or domestically to remove colour (whitening) from fabric or other materials, or to disinfect surfaces. In particular, the term usually refers to an aqueous solution of sodium hypochlorite, the most common household bleach.

Chemistry

Most bleaches work by oxidation: the active agent breaks the chemical bonds of chromophores, the coloured parts of molecules, rendering them colourless. Chlorine-based bleaches, such as sodium hypochlorite (NaClO), release hypochlorous acid in water. Oxygen-based bleaches, such as hydrogen peroxide and sodium percarbonate, release reactive oxygen species and are generally gentler on coloured fabrics.

History

Sun bleaching of linen was practised for thousands of years. In 1774 the Swedish chemist Carl Wilhelm Scheele discovered chlorine, and Claude Berthollet in France recognised its bleaching properties in 1785, producing "Eau de Javel". Charles Tennant patented bleaching powder (calcium hypochlorite) in 1799, greatly accelerating the textile industry.

Household use and safety

Household bleach is typically a 3–8% solution of sodium hypochlorite. It is used to whiten laundry, remove stains, and disinfect surfaces and drinking water in emergencies. Bleach must never be mixed with acids or ammonia, as this releases toxic chlorine or chloramine gases. It is corrosive to skin and eyes and degrades many materials.

Environmental impact

Chlorine bleaching of paper pulp historically released dioxins; the industry has largely shifted to elemental chlorine-free processes. Domestic bleach breaks down into salt and water but can form chlorinated organic compounds in wastewater.
