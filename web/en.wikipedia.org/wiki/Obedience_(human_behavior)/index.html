Obedience (human behavior)

Obedience, in human behaviour, is a form of social influence in which a person yields to explicit instructions or orders from an authority figure. It differs from compliance, which involves requests from peers, and from conformity, in which behaviour matches that of a group without any explicit instruction. Obedience is generally regarded as necessary to the functioning of organizations and societies, yet its destructive potential is a central topic of social psychology.

The Milgram experiment

The most famous research on obedience is the series of experiments begun in 1961 by Stanley Milgram at Yale University. Volunteers, assigned the role of "teacher", were ordered by an experimenter to deliver what they believed were increasingly powerful electric shocks to a "learner" (an actor) for wrong answers. Despite the learner's cries of protest — and eventual silence — 65 percent of participants in the baseline version continued to the maximum 450 volts. Milgram interpreted the results in terms of an "agentic state" in which individuals surrender responsibility to the authority. Variations showed obedience fell when the victim was closer, when the authority was less legitimate or absent, and when peers refused to obey.

Discussion and interpretation

The experiments were partly motivated by the trial of Nazi war criminal Adolf Eichmann and the defence of "only following orders"; Hannah Arendt's phrase "the banality of evil" frames obedience as a bureaucratic rather than fanatical phenomenon. Milgram's methods drew ethical criticism for deceiving and distressing participants and helped spur modern research-ethics rules informed by documents such as the Belmont Report. Later analyses, including reinterpretations of the archived data and partial replications, have debated whether participants truly believed the shocks were real, and have proposed "engaged followership" — identification with the authority's cause — as an alternative to blind obedience. The related Stanford prison experiment (1971) addressed the related question of conformity to social roles.
