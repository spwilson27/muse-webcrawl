Treaty on European Union

The Treaty on European Union (TEU) is one of the two principal treaties forming the constitutional basis of the European Union, the other being the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU). The TEU was originally the Maastricht Treaty, signed on 7 February 1992 in Maastricht, Netherlands, and in force from 1 November 1993. It has since been amended by the treaties of Amsterdam (1997), Nice (2001) and Lisbon (2007).

The treaty established the European Union, introduced European citizenship, laid down the path to Economic and Monetary Union and the euro, and set out the Union's common provisions: its values, objectives, democratic principles and the competences of its institutions.

Structure

The TEU is divided into six titles: common provisions; provisions on democratic principles; provisions on the institutions; provisions on enhanced cooperation; general provisions on the Union's external action and specific provisions on the Common Foreign and Security Policy; and final provisions. Article 2 proclaims the Union's founding values of human dignity, freedom, democracy, equality, the rule of law and respect for human rights. Article 7 provides a mechanism for sanctioning a member state that seriously breaches those values, and Article 50 governs the voluntary withdrawal of a member state — the provision invoked by the United Kingdom in the Brexit process.

History

The Maastricht Treaty transformed the European Communities into the European Union, structuring it around three "pillars": the European Communities, the Common Foreign and Security Policy, and Justice and Home Affairs. The Treaty of Lisbon abolished the pillar structure, gave the EU a single legal personality, and made the TEU and TFEU equal in legal value.
