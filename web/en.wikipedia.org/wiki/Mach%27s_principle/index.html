Mach's principle

Mach's principle
FieldTheoretical physics, gravitation
Associated withErnst Mach; named by Albert Einstein (1918)

In theoretical physics, particularly in discussions of gravitation theories, Mach's principle (or Mach's conjecture) is the name given by Albert Einstein to an imprecise hypothesis often credited to the physicist and philosopher Ernst Mach. The hypothesis is that the existence of absolute rotation is determined by the large-scale distribution of matter, as exemplified by the rotating-bucket argument of Isaac Newton.

Background

Mach criticized Newton's notion of absolute space, arguing in his Science of Mechanics (1883) that the inertia of a body and the rotation of a system should be understood relative to the other matter in the universe rather than to an invisible absolute frame.

Relation to general relativity

Einstein hoped his general theory of relativity would fully embody Mach's principle, and he listed it among the theory's foundational ideas in 1918. Whether general relativity is truly "Machian" remains debated: the theory admits solutions, such as empty de Sitter space and Gödel's rotating universe, in which inertial frames exist without the expected matter distribution. Effects such as Lense–Thirring frame dragging, confirmed by Gravity Probe B, show that rotating matter does influence local inertial frames, a partial realization of Mach's idea.

Variants

Because Mach never stated the principle precisely, many distinct formulations exist — a catalogue by Barbour and Pfister lists 21 versions — ranging from the claim that constants of nature are determined by the universe's total mass to the requirement that the universe as a whole not rotate.
