Steam cracking

Steam cracking is a petrochemical process in which saturated hydrocarbons are broken down into smaller, often unsaturated, hydrocarbons. It is the principal industrial method for producing lighter alkenes (olefins), including ethylene and propylene, which are the most important building blocks of the modern chemical industry. The process takes place at very high temperatures (typically 750–950 °C) in the presence of steam, which acts as a diluent, lowers the hydrocarbon partial pressure, and reduces coke formation.

Process description

Hydrocarbon feedstock is diluted with steam (typically 0.3–1.0 kg of steam per kg of hydrocarbon, depending on the feed) and briefly heated in tubular reactors inside fired furnaces. Preheating occurs in the convection section; the mixture then enters the radiant section, where temperatures reach 750–950 °C and the residence time is only a few tenths of a second. The cracking reactions proceed mainly by a free-radical chain mechanism, initiated by the homolytic cleavage of carbon–carbon bonds.

To stop the reactions at the desired products and prevent degradation to coke and methane, the effluent is rapidly cooled in transfer-line heat exchangers, which generate high-pressure steam. The cracked gas is then compressed, treated to remove acid gases and water, and separated in a train of cryogenic distillation columns into ethylene, propylene, a C4 fraction rich in butadiene, and pyrolysis gasoline rich in aromatics such as benzene, toluene, and xylene.

Feedstocks and yields

Feedstock choice strongly affects the product slate. Ethane gives very high ethylene yields (around 75–80 wt%) but little propylene or C4 material. Naphtha cracking, long dominant in Europe and Asia, yields roughly 30% ethylene, 15% propylene, and significant amounts of butadiene and aromatics. Gas oil and heavier feeds give still lower olefin yields and more pyrolysis fuel oil. Since the development of shale gas in North America, ethane cracking has expanded rapidly there because of its cost advantage.

Coke formation and decoking

A major operational constraint is the deposition of coke on the inner walls of the reactor coils, which insulates the tubes and raises pressure drop. Furnaces must periodically be taken off-line—typically every 30 to 90 days—for decoking, in which the coke is burned off with a steam–air mixture. Metallurgy limits tube skin temperatures to about 1,100–1,150 °C for modern chromium–nickel alloys.

Industry

World ethylene capacity exceeds 200 million tonnes per year, making steam cracking one of the largest-scale processes in the chemical industry. The technology is licensed by firms such as Technip, Lummus Technology, and KBR. Because the process is energy-intensive, it is also a significant source of industrial carbon dioxide emissions, motivating research into electrically heated cracker furnaces.
