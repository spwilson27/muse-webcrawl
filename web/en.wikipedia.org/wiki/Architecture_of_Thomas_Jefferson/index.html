Architecture of Thomas Jefferson

Thomas Jefferson (1743–1826), third president of the United States and principal author of the Declaration of Independence, was also one of the most influential amateur architects in American history. Largely self-taught from books — above all Andrea Palladio's Four Books of Architecture — Jefferson championed the neoclassical style and introduced classical models into the young republic's civic and domestic building.

Monticello

Jefferson's own home, Monticello, on a mountaintop near Charlottesville, Virginia, occupied him for more than forty years. Begun in 1768 and remodelled after his residence in France (1784–1789) as minister to Paris, the house features an octagonal dome — the first on an American house — a portico modelled on a Roman temple, and numerous ingenious devices and labour-saving arrangements. It was built largely by enslaved labourers, whose quarters lined the adjacent Mulberry Row.

Public buildings

The Virginia State Capitol in Richmond (designed 1785, with Charles-Louis Clérisseau) was modelled on the Maison Carrée, a Roman temple at Nîmes, and was the first public building in the modern world designed in the form of a classical temple. As president, Jefferson influenced the design of Washington, D.C., promoting classical forms for federal architecture.

University of Virginia

Jefferson's last and most celebrated project was the University of Virginia (founded 1819). Its "Academical Village" arranges ten pavilions — each a miniature classical temple front in a different order, housing professors and classrooms — along a terraced lawn, closed at the head by the Rotunda, a half-scale adaptation of the Pantheon in Rome, which housed the library. The university and Monticello are jointly inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Other works

Jefferson also designed his retreat at Poplar Forest near Lynchburg, an octagonal villa begun in 1806, and provided designs and advice for friends' houses, courthouses and churches throughout Virginia.

Style and influence

Jefferson's architecture fused Palladian and Roman-revival forms with red brick and white-painted wood trim characteristic of Virginia building. His work helped define the Federal and Jeffersonian classical styles and established classicism as the visual language of American democracy.
