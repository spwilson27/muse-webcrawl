History of New York City

The history of New York City runs from the Lenape homeland and the Dutch colony of New Amsterdam through British rule, the American Revolution, its rise as America's greatest port and financial centre, and its role as a global city in the 20th and 21st centuries.

Native and colonial era

The area was inhabited by the Lenape. Giovanni da Verrazzano sighted the harbour in 1524, and Henry Hudson explored the river in 1609 for the Dutch. In 1626 Peter Minuit famously "purchased" Manhattan; the settlement of New Amsterdam became the capital of New Netherland. The English seized the colony in 1664 and renamed it New York. Under British rule the city grew as a trading port, though the slave trade and enslaved labour were integral to its economy.

Revolution and early republic

New York was a battleground of the American Revolution: the British occupied it from 1776 to 1783 after Washington's defeat in the Battle of Long Island. After the war it briefly served as the US capital (1785–1790); George Washington was inaugurated at Federal Hall in 1789. The Erie Canal (1825) linked the city to the Great Lakes and made it the nation's dominant port and commercial metropolis.

Growth, immigration and consolidation

Mass immigration — Irish, German, then southern and eastern European — swelled the population; the 1863 Draft Riots exposed its tensions. In 1898 the five boroughs (Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx and Staten Island) consolidated into Greater New York. Skyscrapers, the subway (1904) and Wall Street defined the 20th-century city; the 1929 crash began the Great Depression.

Postwar to the present

Postwar suburbanisation and deindustrialisation culminated in the 1975 fiscal crisis. A revival from the 1980s restored the city's finances and population. On 11 September 2001, terrorist attacks destroyed the World Trade Center, killing nearly 3,000 people. The city rebuilt, and the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic again tested its resilience; New York remains the largest US city and a world capital of finance, media and culture.
