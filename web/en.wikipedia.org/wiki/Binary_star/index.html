Binary star

A binary star system consists of two stars orbiting a common barycentre under their mutual gravity. Binary and multiple star systems are very common — a large fraction of stars, perhaps most Sun-like stars, have companions — and they are crucial for measuring stellar masses directly via Kepler's laws.

Binary star
Types: Visual, spectroscopic, eclipsing, astrometric
Examples: Sirius, Algol, Cygnus X-1
Importance: Direct stellar mass measurement

Classification

Visual binaries are resolvable by telescope; spectroscopic binaries are detected by Doppler shifts in spectral lines; eclipsing binaries vary in brightness as one star passes before the other; astrometric binaries show wobble in a visible star's motion. By separation, systems range from detached to semi-detached and contact binaries sharing an envelope.

Evolution and phenomena

Mass transfer in close binaries produces Algol systems, novae, Type Ia supernovae from accreting white dwarfs, X-ray binaries with neutron stars or black holes, and progenitors of gravitational-wave mergers.

Planets

Planets can orbit one star (S-type) or both stars (circumbinary, P-type), as first confirmed for Kepler-16b.
