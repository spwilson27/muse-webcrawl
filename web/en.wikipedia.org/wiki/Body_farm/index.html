A body farm is a research facility where decomposition of human remains can be studied in a variety of settings. The first was founded in 1981 by anthropologist William M. Bass at the University of Tennessee, Knoxville — formally the Anthropological Research Facility — after law enforcement repeatedly sought his help estimating time since death in criminal cases. The popular name comes from Patricia Cornwell's 1994 novel The Body Farm.

Research

Donated bodies are placed outdoors — on the surface, buried, submerged, in vehicles, in sun or shade — and scientists record the sequence and rate of decay, insect succession, scavenger activity, and the effects of climate and soil. The resulting data underpin methods for estimating the post-mortem interval, identifying remains, and training forensic teams and cadaver dogs. Body farms also maintain large skeletal collections for comparative study.

Facilities

Following Tennessee's example, facilities have opened at Western Carolina University, Texas State University (the Freeman Ranch, among the largest), Sam Houston State University, Southern Illinois University, Colorado Mesa University, and the University of South Florida, with counterparts in Australia (the Australian Facility for Taphonomic Experimental Research, opened 2016) and the Netherlands.

Donation and ethics

Bodies are obtained through willed-body donation programmes; donors consent in advance, and facilities enforce strict security, privacy, and respectful handling. Research is reviewed under institutional protocols, and remains are eventually curated as skeletons or returned per the donor's wishes.
