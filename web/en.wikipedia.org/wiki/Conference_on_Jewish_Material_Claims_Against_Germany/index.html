The Conference on Jewish Material Claims Against Germany, or Claims Conference, represents the world's Jews in negotiating for compensation and restitution for victims of Nazi persecution and their heirs. The Claims Conference administers compensation funds, recovers unclaimed Jewish property, and funds social welfare services for Jewish victims of Nazi persecution, allocating funds to social service agencies for home care, food programs, medical care and other assistance for elderly Holocaust survivors.

History

The Claims Conference was founded in 1951 by Nahum Goldmann and representatives of 23 major Jewish national and international organisations. In 1952, the Claims Conference participated in negotiations leading to the Luxembourg Agreements between the Federal Republic of Germany, Israel and the Claims Conference, under which West Germany agreed to pay DM 3 billion to Israel and DM 450 million to the Claims Conference for the relief, rehabilitation and resettlement of Jewish victims of Nazi persecution. Chancellor Konrad Adenauer's government ratified the agreements despite domestic opposition, regarding them as a moral obligation of the German state.

Programs

The Claims Conference negotiates and administers several compensation programs, including the Hardship Fund, the Article 2 Fund, and programs for child survivors and former slave and forced labourers. In 2000, it participated in establishing the German Foundation "Remembrance, Responsibility and Future", funded jointly by the German government and German industry, which made payments to former forced labourers. Annual negotiations with the German Federal Ministry of Finance have expanded eligibility and funding for home care for survivors. The organisation also pursues restitution of Jewish communal and individual property in Eastern Europe through the World Jewish Restitution Organization, and operates the Looted Art and Cultural Property Initiative, which maintains databases of art looted by the Nazis.

Criticism and controversy

The Claims Conference has faced criticism over the share of funds spent on administration and allocation policy. In 2013, former Claims Conference executive director of the Holocaust Victim Assets Litigation-era programs and others were convicted in a fraud scheme in which approximately $57 million in German compensation funds were diverted using falsified applications; the organisation cooperated with the FBI investigation and tightened controls.
