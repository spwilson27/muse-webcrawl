Forward osmosis

Forward osmosis (FO) is an osmotic process that, like reverse osmosis (RO), uses a semi-permeable membrane to effect separation of water from dissolved solutes. The driving force for this separation is an osmotic pressure gradient, such that a "draw" solution of high concentration (relative to that of the feed solution) is used to induce a net flow of water through the membrane into the draw solution, thus effectively separating the feed water from its solutes.

Principle

In contrast to the reverse osmosis process, which uses hydraulic pressure as the driving force for separation and can exhibit significant membrane fouling, forward osmosis uses the natural energy of osmotic pressure. Because no large hydraulic pressure is applied, fouling layers form loosely and can often be removed by simple flushing. After water passes into the draw solution, a second step is generally needed to recover the product water and regenerate the draw solute.

Applications

Forward osmosis has been studied for seawater and brackish water desalination, wastewater treatment and reuse, concentration of liquid foods and pharmaceuticals, osmotic power generation (pressure-retarded osmosis), and emergency hydration — for example, self-filtering hydration pouches that use a sugar or electrolyte draw solution to extract drinkable fluid from contaminated water.

Challenges

Key obstacles include internal concentration polarisation within the membrane support layer, which sharply reduces effective driving force; reverse solute flux from the draw solution into the feed; and the energy cost of draw-solution recovery, which limits FO's advantage over mature reverse-osmosis technology.
