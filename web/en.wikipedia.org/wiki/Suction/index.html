Suction

Suction is the colloquial term used to describe the effect produced when a region of lower pressure draws in surrounding fluid or objects. Strictly speaking, suction does not "pull"; rather, the higher pressure of the surrounding medium pushes material into the area of reduced pressure. Suction is produced by creating a partial vacuum and is fundamental to devices such as vacuum cleaners, suction cups, and medical suction apparatus.

Principles

When the pressure in a confined space is reduced below that of the surrounding atmosphere, the pressure difference exerts a net force. At sea level, atmospheric pressure of about 101 kilopascals sets the theoretical maximum pressure difference achievable by suction. This limits, for example, the height to which water can be lifted by a suction pump to roughly 10.3 metres.

Applications

Suction is used in medicine to clear airways and surgical fields, in industry for material handling with vacuum lifters, in dentistry, and in everyday devices including vacuum cleaners and plungers. Suction cups adhere to smooth surfaces by expelling air from beneath a flexible cup, allowing atmospheric pressure to hold them in place. Many animals, such as octopuses and remoras, use suction for adhesion and feeding; suction feeding is the predominant mode of prey capture among ray-finned fishes.
