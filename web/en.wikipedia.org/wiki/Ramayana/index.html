The Ramayana (Sanskrit: Rāmāyaṇa, "Rama's journey") is one of the two great Sanskrit epics of ancient India, the other being the Mahābhārata. Traditionally ascribed to the sage Valmiki, it narrates the life of Rama, prince of Ayodhya and seventh avatar of the god Vishnu, and is a foundational text of Hindu literature and culture.

Story

Rama, heir to the throne of Ayodhya, is exiled to the forest for fourteen years through the intrigues of his stepmother Kaikeyi. His wife Sita and brother Lakshmana accompany him. In the forest, Sita is abducted by Ravana, the ten-headed king of Lanka. Rama allies with the monkey-king Sugriva and is aided by the devoted Hanuman, who finds Sita imprisoned in Lanka. With an army of monkeys and bears, Rama builds a bridge (Rama Setu) across the sea to Lanka — traditionally identified with Sri Lanka — defeats and kills Ravana, and rescues Sita. Returning in triumph after fourteen years, Rama is crowned king; his reign, Rāma Rājya, is remembered as an ideal of righteous rule. A later book, the Uttara Kāṇḍa, adds Sita's banishment and the birth of their sons Lava and Kusha.

Structure and versions

The epic comprises about 24,000 couplets arranged in seven books, from the Bāla Kāṇḍa (Rama's childhood) to the Uttara Kāṇḍa. It exists in numerous regional recensions and retellings, most famously Kamban's Tamil Iramavataram (12th century) and Tulsidas's Hindi Ramcharitmanas (16th century). Versions spread across Southeast Asia with Hindu culture: the Thai Ramkien, Khmer Reamker, Laotian Phra Lak Phra Lam and Javanese and Malay versions remain living traditions in dance, theatre and shadow puppetry.

Cultural significance

The Ramayana's themes — dharma (duty), devotion, loyalty and the conflict between obligation and love — pervade South and Southeast Asian art, drama and religious life. The festival of Diwali celebrates Rama's return to Ayodhya, and Dussehra his victory over Ravana. The text is also a major source for the study of ancient Indian society, geography and kingship.
