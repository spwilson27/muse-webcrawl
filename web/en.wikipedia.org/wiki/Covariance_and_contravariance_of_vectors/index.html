Covariance and contravariance of vectors

In physics and multilinear algebra, covariance and contravariance describe how the components of a geometric object change when the basis of the space changes. A contravariant vector (such as a displacement or velocity) has components that transform oppositely to the transformation of the basis — for example, if the basis vectors are scaled up, the components shrink. A covariant vector (such as a gradient) has components that transform in the same way as the basis. The terms were introduced by James Joseph Sylvester in 1851 in the theory of algebraic forms, and the distinction is fundamental to the tensor calculus used in general relativity.

In an oblique basis, a vector has both contravariant components (along the basis) and covariant components (along the dual basis).

Introduction

A physical quantity such as a velocity does not depend on the coordinates used to describe it, but its list of components does. If a vector v is written as a combination of basis vectors, v = viei, the components vi are called contravariant: when the basis is changed, they transform with the inverse of the basis transformation, so that the vector itself is unchanged. Quantities that pair naturally with vectors — such as the gradient of a scalar field, whose components are partial derivatives — transform the other way and are called covariant. In Einstein notation, contravariant components carry superscript (upper) indices and covariant components subscript (lower) indices.

Transformation rules

Under a change of basis described by a matrix A, the components of a contravariant vector transform with A−1, while the components of a covariant vector transform with A itself. This is precisely why the gradient is covariant: rescaling a coordinate axis by a factor stretches the partial derivative along it by the same factor, whereas a displacement measured along the axis shrinks in numerical value.

Covariant and contravariant components

In orthogonal coordinate systems with unit basis vectors, such as ordinary Cartesian coordinates, the two kinds of components coincide, which is why the distinction is invisible in elementary treatments. In oblique or curvilinear coordinates they differ, and both are needed: the contravariant components are the projections of the vector parallel to the coordinate axes, while the covariant components are the projections perpendicular to the other axes, onto the directions given by the dual (reciprocal) basis.

Metric tensor

The metric tensor gij converts between the two forms: vi = gijvj ("lowering an index"), and its inverse raises an index. In Euclidean space with Cartesian coordinates the metric is the identity, so raising and lowering have no numerical effect; in curved spaces and in the spacetime of general relativity, the metric is nontrivial and the covariance/contravariance distinction is essential for writing physical laws in a coordinate-independent form.
