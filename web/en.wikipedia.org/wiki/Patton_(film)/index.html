Patton is a 1970 American epic biographical war film about U.S. General George S. Patton during World War II. It stars George C. Scott as Patton and Karl Malden as General Omar Bradley. The film was directed by Franklin J. Schaffner from a script by Francis Ford Coppola and Edmund H. North, who based their screenplay on Patton: Ordeal and Triumph by Ladislas Farago and Bradley's memoir A Soldier's Story. The film was shot in 65mm Dimension 150 and largely filmed in Spain, whose army supplied period vehicles and thousands of extras.

Plot

The film opens with Patton addressing his troops before an enormous American flag, a composite of speeches the general actually gave. It then follows his command in North Africa after the American defeat at Kasserine Pass, his rivalry with the British general Bernard Montgomery during the invasion of Sicily, the notorious incident in which he slapped a shell-shocked soldier, and his sidelining during the Normandy landings, where he served as a decoy commander of the fictitious First U.S. Army Group. Restored to command of the Third Army, Patton races across France, relieves Bastogne during the Battle of the Bulge, and drives into Germany, before being removed after controversial public remarks comparing American political parties to the Nazis.

Reception and awards

Patton was a critical and commercial success and won seven Academy Awards, including Best Picture, Best Director, Best Original Screenplay and Best Actor for Scott — who famously refused the Oscar, calling the awards ceremony a "meat parade," and became the first actor to decline the honour. Jerry Goldsmith's score, with its echoplexed trumpet calls evoking Patton's belief in reincarnation, is considered among his finest. In 2003 the film was selected for preservation in the United States National Film Registry as culturally, historically or aesthetically significant. Scott reprised the role in the 1986 television film The Last Days of Patton.
