Bell System

The Bell System was the system of companies led by the Bell Telephone Company and later by AT&T that provided telephone services to much of the United States and Canada from 1877 until the antitrust breakup that took effect on 1 January 1984. At its height, "Ma Bell" was the largest employer and one of the largest corporations in the world, operating a near-monopoly over American telecommunications.


The Bell System bell logo, used until the 1984 breakup

Structure

The system combined AT&T, the parent and long-distance operator; 22 (later 24) regional Bell Operating Companies providing local service; Western Electric, the manufacturing arm; and Bell Telephone Laboratories, the research organisation founded in 1925. Bell Labs produced landmark innovations — the transistor, the laser, information theory, the UNIX operating system and the C programming language among them — funded by monopoly revenues.

Breakup

After decades of antitrust scrutiny, the United States Department of Justice filed suit in 1974; the 1982 Modified Final Judgment required AT&T to divest its local operating companies. On 1 January 1984 these were reorganised into seven independent Regional Bell Operating Companies (the "Baby Bells"), while AT&T retained long-distance services, Western Electric and Bell Labs. Many of the divested companies later recombined, with several eventually forming the core of the modern AT&T and Verizon.

Legacy

The Bell System's standardised equipment, universal-service policies and research establishment shaped 20th-century telecommunications. Uniform devices such as the Western Electric Model 500 telephone and the familiar bell-in-a-circle trademark remain iconic.
