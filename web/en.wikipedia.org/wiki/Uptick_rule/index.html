Uptick rule

The uptick rule was a rule established by the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) that required every short sale transaction to be entered at a price that was higher than the price of the previous trade. The rule was adopted in 1938 as Rule 10a-1 under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, and it remained in effect until the SEC eliminated it in 2007. A modified version, known as the "alternative uptick rule" (Rule 201 of Regulation SHO), was adopted in 2010.

History

The original uptick rule was introduced in the aftermath of the stock market crash of 1929 and the ensuing investigations into bear raids, in which short sellers were suspected of driving down the prices of stocks to profit from their decline. Under Rule 10a-1, a short sale could only be executed on an "uptick" (a price higher than the immediately preceding trade) or on a "zero-plus tick" (a price equal to the preceding trade, provided the last price change was upward).

In 2004 the SEC began a pilot program suspending the rule for a sample of stocks, and after studies concluded that the rule had little effect on market quality in modern decimalized markets, the Commission voted to rescind it entirely, effective July 6, 2007.

Alternative uptick rule

Following the financial crisis of 2007–2008, pressure mounted to restore short-sale price restrictions. In February 2010 the SEC adopted Rule 201, which imposes a short-sale price test that is triggered only when a stock's price declines by 10 percent or more in a single trading day. Once triggered, short sales are permitted only at a price above the current national best bid for the remainder of that day and the following day.
