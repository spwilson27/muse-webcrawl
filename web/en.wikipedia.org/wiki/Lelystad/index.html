Lelystad is a Dutch municipality and the capital city of the province of Flevoland in the central Netherlands. It was founded in 1967 on land reclaimed from the IJsselmeer as part of the Zuiderzee Works, and is named after Cornelis Lely (1854–1929), the engineer and statesman who designed the plans for the closure and reclamation of the Zuiderzee. The first inhabitants arrived in 1967, and Lelystad became a separate municipality on 1 January 1980. Almost the whole city lies about three metres below sea level.

History

The land on which Lelystad stands was drained in the 1950s as part of the Eastern Flevoland polder, completed in 1957. The city was planned as the future capital of the new province and as a regional centre for the reclaimed IJsselmeer polders. Growth was slower than originally forecast, in part because Lelystad lacked a railway connection until the Flevolijn line to Almere and Amsterdam opened in 1988. In the twenty-first century the city has grown to roughly 83,000 inhabitants.

Geography

Lelystad lies on the shores of the IJsselmeer and the Markermeer and is bordered by the Oostvaardersplassen, a large nature reserve of marshland created accidentally when a planned industrial area was left undeveloped; it became famous for its populations of grazing animals and birds. The Houtribdijk dam, carrying the N302 road, connects Lelystad to Enkhuizen across the IJsselmeer.

Attractions and transport

Attractions include Batavia Stad, an outlet shopping centre built in the style of a seventeenth-century Dutch town; the Batavialand museum, with a full-scale reconstruction of the 1628 East Indiaman Batavia; and the Aviodrome aviation museum at Lelystad Airport, the largest general-aviation airport in the Netherlands. Lelystad Centrum station provides rail services to Amsterdam, Zwolle, and The Hague. Expansion of Lelystad Airport for commercial holiday flights was approved and the terminal built, but its opening has been repeatedly postponed amid political and environmental debate.
