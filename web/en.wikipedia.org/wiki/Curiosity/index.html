Curiosity

Placeholder image: Curiosity

Curiosity (from Latin curiosus, "careful, diligent, curious") is a quality related to inquisitive thinking such as exploration, investigation, and learning, evident in humans and many other animals. Curiosity is heavily associated with all aspects of human development, in which it derives from the process of learning and the desire to acquire knowledge and skill.

Overview

The term curiosity can also be used to denote the behavior or emotion of being curious in regard to the desire to gain knowledge or information. Curiosity as a behavior and emotion is the driving force behind human development, including advances in science, language, and industry.

Theories

Like other desires and appetites, curiosity has traditionally been linked with instinctual behavior. Early theories treated curiosity as an intrinsic drive to reduce uncertainty or to resolve an information gap. Daniel Berlyne distinguished perceptual curiosity, prompted by novel or surprising stimuli, from epistemic curiosity, the desire for knowledge. More recent accounts, such as George Loewenstein's information-gap theory (1994), describe curiosity as arising when attention focuses on a gap between what one knows and what one wants to know. Psychologists also distinguish trait curiosity, a stable individual difference, from state curiosity, a temporary condition triggered by circumstances.

Curiosity in development

Infants and young children display curiosity through exploration of objects and environments, and questioning behavior in childhood is a major driver of learning. Educational research suggests that curiosity enhances memory: studies have found that material encountered in a state of high curiosity is remembered better, including incidental information presented at the same time.

Neuroscience

Curiosity has been linked with the brain's reward circuitry, including dopaminergic pathways involving the substantia nigra, ventral tegmental area, and hippocampus. Anticipation of new information can itself be rewarding, which helps explain why people seek out knowledge even without external benefit.

Morbid curiosity

Morbid curiosity refers to an interest in death, violence, or other subjects traditionally considered taboo or frightening. It manifests in the popularity of horror films, true crime, and visits to sites of disaster, and has been studied as a way of exploring threatening topics from a safe distance.

Curiosity in animals

Many animals show exploratory behavior toward novel objects and environments, a tendency sometimes called neophilia, balanced against neophobia (avoidance of novelty). Exploration helps animals learn about food sources, predators, and territory.
