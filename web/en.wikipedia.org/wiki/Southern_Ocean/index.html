The Southern Ocean, also known as the Antarctic Ocean, comprises the southernmost waters of the world's oceans, generally taken to be those south of 60°S latitude encircling Antarctica. It is the world's fourth-largest ocean and the second-smallest only to the Arctic Ocean. Unlike the other oceans, it is defined not by land boundaries but by the Antarctic Convergence, where cold Antarctic waters meet the warmer subantarctic waters to the north.

Circulation

The ocean is dominated by the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, the largest ocean current on Earth, which flows clockwise around the continent driven by powerful westerly winds — the Roaring Forties, Furious Fifties and Screaming Sixties. The current transports roughly 130–150 million cubic metres of water per second and isolates Antarctica thermally, helping to keep the continent frigid.

Climate and sea ice

Sea ice cover fluctuates dramatically with the seasons, from about 2.6 million km² in March to over 18 million km² in September. The Southern Ocean is a major sink for anthropogenic carbon dioxide and heat, and its deep-water formation drives global thermohaline circulation.

Ecology

Despite near-freezing temperatures, the ocean supports rich ecosystems built on Antarctic krill, which feed penguins, seals, whales and fish. The Convention for the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources (CCAMLR, 1982) manages fisheries in the region.

Delimitation dispute

The International Hydrographic Organization recognised the Southern Ocean in its 2000 draft limits, but ratification stalled over the northern boundary; several countries, notably Australia, extend the term to waters directly south of their coasts. In 2021 National Geographic formally recognised it as the fifth ocean.
