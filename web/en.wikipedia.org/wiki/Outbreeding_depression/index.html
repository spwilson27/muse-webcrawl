Outbreeding depression

Outbreeding depression
FieldEvolutionary biology, conservation genetics
DefinitionReduced fitness in offspring of crosses between genetically distant populations
ContrastInbreeding depression; heterosis (hybrid vigour)

Outbreeding depression is the reduced biological fitness of offspring produced by mating individuals from genetically distinct populations of the same species. It is the counterpart of inbreeding depression: where mating between close relatives exposes harmful recessive genes, mating between overly distant partners can break up favourable genetic combinations and adaptations to local environments.

Mechanisms

Two main mechanisms are recognised. First, extrinsic (ecological) outbreeding depression arises when hybrids are intermediate between parents adapted to different environments and so fit neither—for example, fish crosses that lose population-specific traits. Second, intrinsic (genetic) incompatibility arises when genes that work well together within each population interact badly in hybrids (Dobzhansky–Muller incompatibilities). Because genes in each parent still function with their own genome, first-generation (F1) hybrids may appear vigorous, with breakdown appearing in the F2 generation when recombination separates co-adapted gene combinations. Fixed chromosomal differences between populations can also reduce hybrid fertility.

Conservation significance

Outbreeding depression complicates conservation management: translocating individuals to rescue small, inbred populations (genetic rescue) risks diluting local adaptation, yet refusing to do so risks inbreeding depression. Famous cautionary cases include the Tatra Mountains ibex, where crossing alpine ibex with animals from warmer-climate populations shifted the rutting season so calves were born in mid-winter and died. Modern guidelines attempt to predict risk from genetic and environmental distance between populations before mixing them.
