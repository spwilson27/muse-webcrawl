Lamarckism

Lamarckism, also known as Lamarckian inheritance or the theory of inheritance of acquired characteristics, is the notion that an organism can pass on to its offspring physical characteristics that the parent organism acquired through use or disuse during its lifetime. It is named after the French naturalist Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (1744–1829), who incorporated the idea into his theory of evolution, most fully in his Philosophie zoologique (1809), building on similar ideas from earlier thinkers.

Lamarck's theory

Lamarck proposed two laws. First, in every animal that has not passed the limit of its development, the more frequent and sustained use of any organ strengthens that organ and enlarges it, while permanent disuse weakens and eventually eliminates it. Second, all that nature has caused individuals to gain or lose by these influences is preserved by reproduction in the new individuals that derive from them, provided the changes are common to both parents or at least to the mother. His famous example was the giraffe: ancestors stretching to reach high leaves, Lamarck suggested, gradually lengthened their necks, and this acquired elongation was inherited by their descendants. Lamarck's wider theory also included an innate drive toward greater complexity and the generation of simple life forms that then evolved upward.

History and decline

The inheritance of acquired characteristics was widely accepted in the 19th century. Charles Darwin himself allowed a role for it in his provisional hypothesis of pangenesis. It lost scientific support after August Weismann's experiments in the 1890s — he cut off the tails of mice for many generations without any effect on the tails of their offspring — and with the rediscovery of Mendelian genetics and the chromosomal theory of inheritance, which distinguished the germline from the body (soma) and showed no mechanism by which bodily changes could rewrite hereditary material. By the modern synthesis of the 1930s–40s, Lamarckism was largely abandoned in mainstream biology. In the Soviet Union, a form of neo-Lamarckism championed by Trofim Lysenko was imposed as official doctrine from the 1930s to the 1960s, with disastrous consequences for Soviet genetics and agriculture.

Later discussion

Modern findings in epigenetics — heritable changes in gene expression not caused by changes in DNA sequence, some of which can be influenced by environment and transmitted across a few generations — are sometimes popularly compared to Lamarckism, as are phenomena such as cultural evolution and the Baldwin effect. Biologists generally note that these mechanisms operate within, and do not overturn, the framework of genetics and natural selection.
