Second Boer War

Date11 October 1899 – 31 May 1902
LocationSouthern Africa
BelligerentsBritish Empire vs. South African Republic (Transvaal) and Orange Free State
ResultBritish victory; Treaty of Vereeniging

The Second Boer War (Afrikaans: Tweede Vryheidsoorlog, "Second War of Independence") was a conflict fought between the British Empire and the two independent Boer republics, the South African Republic (Transvaal) and the Orange Free State, from 11 October 1899 to 31 May 1902. It ended with the annexation of both republics to the British Empire and was the largest and most costly war fought by Britain between the Napoleonic Wars and the First World War.

Causes

The underlying causes lay in the discovery of gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886, which transformed the poor Transvaal and flooded it with mostly British foreign workers (uitlanders), whom President Paul Kruger's government refused full political rights. Tensions were sharpened by the failed Jameson Raid of 1895–96, by British high commissioner Alfred Milner's determination to assert British supremacy in southern Africa, and by the strategic and imperial rivalry of the age. Negotiations over the uitlander franchise failed, British troop reinforcements arrived, and the republics issued an ultimatum in October 1899 and invaded Natal and the Cape Colony.

Course of the war

The war fell into three phases. First, Boer commandos besieged Ladysmith, Mafeking, and Kimberley and inflicted a series of defeats on British forces during "Black Week" of December 1899 (Stormberg, Magersfontein, Colenso). Second, under Lord Roberts, a huge British army relieved the sieges, captured Bloemfontein and Pretoria in 1900, and formally annexed both republics. Third, from late 1900 to 1902, Boer bittereinders under leaders such as Christiaan de Wet, Louis Botha, and Jan Smuts waged a guerrilla war. Lord Kitchener responded with a system of blockhouse lines, scorched-earth farm burnings, and the internment of Boer civilians — overwhelmingly women and children — and of Black Africans in separate camps. Deaths in the concentration camps, about 26,000–28,000 Boer civilians and at least 14,000 (probably more) Black inmates, mostly from disease, caused a scandal in Britain, where Emily Hobhouse's reports led the opposition leader to denounce "methods of barbarism".

Outcome

By the Treaty of Vereeniging (31 May 1902) the Boers accepted British sovereignty in return for reconstruction funds, an amnesty, and a promise of eventual self-government. The former republics became crown colonies, achieved responsible government within a few years, and joined the Cape and Natal in the Union of South Africa in 1910 — dominated by former Boer generals. Total British and imperial military deaths were about 22,000, the majority from disease; Boer combatant deaths were roughly 6,000–7,000. The war exposed the weaknesses of the British Army and drove a generation of military and social reform, while its memories shaped Afrikaner nationalism for a century.
