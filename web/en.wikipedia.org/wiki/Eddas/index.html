Eddas

The Eddas are two Old Norse literary works that together form the principal sources for Norse mythology and early Germanic heroic legend: the Poetic Edda and the Prose Edda. Both were written down in Iceland in the 13th century, though much of their content is considerably older.

The Poetic Edda

The Poetic Edda is a collection of anonymous Old Norse poems, preserved chiefly in the Icelandic manuscript known as the Codex Regius, written around 1270 and rediscovered in 1643. Its mythological poems include the Völuspá, a seeress's account of the creation and coming destruction of the world; Hávamál, the sayings of Odin; and Þrymskviða, in which Thor's hammer is stolen. Its heroic poems retell the legends of Sigurd, the Volsungs and the Burgundian kings, material shared with the German Nibelungenlied.

The Prose Edda

The Prose Edda, also called the Younger Edda or Snorri's Edda, was composed around 1220 by the Icelandic chieftain and historian Snorri Sturluson as a handbook for poets, explaining the myths behind traditional skaldic kennings. It consists of a prologue and three sections: Gylfaginning, a systematic retelling of Norse myth; Skáldskaparmál, a dialogue on poetic diction; and Háttatal, a catalogue of verse forms illustrated by Snorri's own poem.

Legacy

The origin of the word "edda" is uncertain. The Eddas shaped the modern understanding of Norse mythology and influenced writers including J. R. R. Tolkien and composers such as Richard Wagner, whose Ring cycle draws on the same legendary material.
