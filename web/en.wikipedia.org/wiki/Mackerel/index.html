Mackerel is a common name applied to a number of different species of pelagic fish, mostly from the family Scombridae — the same family as tunas and bonitos. True mackerels belong to the tribe Scombrini, including the Atlantic mackerel (Scomber scombrus) and chub mackerels (Scomber japonicus and relatives), while the larger Spanish and king mackerels belong to the genus Scomberomorus, and the Indian mackerel (Rastrelliger kanagurta) is a staple of South and Southeast Asian fisheries. Other fish, such as the horse mackerels (family Carangidae), borrow the name without close relation.

Characteristics

Mackerels are streamlined, fast-swimming schooling fish with deeply forked tails, small finlets behind the dorsal and anal fins, and — in the true mackerels — striking iridescent blue-green backs marked with dark wavy bars over silvery bellies. Most species are relatively small, around 30 cm (12 in), though king mackerel can exceed 1.6 m. Many lack a swim bladder or have a reduced one and must swim constantly. Some species, including the Atlantic mackerel, can partially regulate body temperature through countercurrent heat exchange, supporting their active predatory lifestyle feeding on small fish, krill, and squid.

Fisheries and food

Mackerel are among the world's most important food fish, caught in huge quantities with purse seines and midwater trawls. They are oily fish, exceptionally rich in omega-3 fatty acids, vitamin D, and vitamin B12, and are eaten grilled, smoked, pickled (as in Japanese shime saba), and canned. Because the flesh spoils quickly and accumulates histamine if poorly refrigerated, mackerel is associated with scombroid food poisoning. High mercury in large predatory species such as king mackerel leads health authorities to advise limited consumption, whereas smaller Atlantic and chub mackerels are low in mercury and frequently recommended as sustainable seafood choices — though Northeast Atlantic mackerel stocks have seen quota disputes among coastal states (the so-called "mackerel wars" involving Iceland and the Faroe Islands).
