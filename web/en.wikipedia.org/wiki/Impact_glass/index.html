Impact glass

Impact glass is a type of naturally occurring glass formed by the high-energy impact of a meteorite on the Earth's surface. When a meteorite strikes rock at hypervelocity, the intense heat and pressure can melt the target rock almost instantaneously; the melt is ejected into the atmosphere, cools rapidly in flight, and falls back to Earth as glassy objects. Impact glasses are a form of impactite, the class of materials created or modified by impact events.

Tektites

The best-known impact glasses are tektites — small, usually black, green or brown glassy bodies found in geographically restricted strewn fields. Major tektite strewn fields include the Australasian field (source crater not conclusively identified), the Central European field (associated with the Nördlinger Ries crater in Germany, whose glasses are known as moldavites), the Ivory Coast field (associated with Lake Bosumtwi in Ghana), and the North American field (associated with the Chesapeake Bay crater), which produced georgiaites and bediasites.

Other impact glasses

Not all impact glasses travel far from their crater. Libyan desert glass, found in the eastern Sahara, is a pale yellow-green glass of nearly pure silica, thought to have formed about 29 million years ago, possibly from the melting of sand by an impact or a near-surface airburst. Darwin glass, found in Tasmania, is associated with the Darwin Crater. Glass also occurs as melt fragments within impact breccias and as coatings and bombs near craters such as Wabar in Saudi Arabia, where the desert sand was fused by the impact of an iron meteorite.

Formation and composition

Impact glasses are typically derived from surface rocks or sediments at the impact site, and their chemistry reflects that target material. They are usually very poor in water and other volatiles, consistent with brief exposure to extremely high temperatures, and may contain lechatelierite (pure silica glass) and high-pressure mineral phases. Because tektites can be dated by radiometric methods, they are valuable for establishing the ages of impact events, and a global layer of tektite-like spherules marks the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary produced by the Chicxulub impact.
