State fair

A state fair is an annual competitive and recreational gathering of a U.S. state's population, usually held in late summer or early fall. It is a larger version of a county fair, often including only exhibits or competitors that have won in their categories at the more-local county fairs.

State fairs began in the nineteenth century for the purpose of promoting state agriculture, through competitive exhibitions of livestock and farm products. As the U.S. evolved from a predominantly agrarian to an industrial society in the twentieth century, and the more service- and technology-based economy of the twenty-first century, modern state fairs have grown into large events lasting up to two weeks, with midway rides, concerts, food vendors famous for deep-fried specialties, and commercial exhibitions alongside the traditional agricultural competitions.

History

The first state fair in the United States was held in Syracuse, New York, in 1841, organized by the New York State Agricultural Society; promoter Elkanah Watson is often credited with popularizing the format. Among the largest fairs today are the State Fair of Texas in Dallas, the Minnesota State Fair (the "Great Minnesota Get-Together"), and the Iowa State Fair, each drawing well over a million visitors. State fairs award ribbons for livestock, crops, baking, crafts, and art, and typically feature events such as demolition derbies, rodeo, and tractor pulls.
