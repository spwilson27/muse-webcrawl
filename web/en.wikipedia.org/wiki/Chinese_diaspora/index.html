Chinese diaspora

The Chinese diaspora refers to the tens of millions of ethnic Chinese living outside Greater China, known in Chinese as huáqiáo (華僑, "overseas Chinese") for citizens of China abroad, or ethnic Chinese of other nationalities. Numbering around 50 million worldwide, the diaspora is concentrated in Southeast Asia, with large communities also in North America, Oceania and Europe.

History

Chinese merchants settled in Southeast Asian ports from at least the Song and Ming dynasties, forming communities in places such as Malacca, Batavia and Manila. In the 19th century, large-scale emigration from Guangdong and Fujian provinces carried labourers — often through the "coolie" trade — to mines, plantations and railways in the Americas, Australia and Southeast Asia; gold rushes in California (from 1849) and Victoria drew tens of thousands. Exclusion laws in the United States (1882), Canada and Australia restricted Chinese immigration for much of the 20th century. After China's reform and opening in 1978, new waves of students, professionals and investors emigrated worldwide.

Communities

Ethnic Chinese form majorities in Singapore and substantial minorities in Malaysia (about a quarter of the population) and Thailand, and they have historically played prominent roles in regional commerce. Chinatowns in cities such as San Francisco, New York, London and Sydney act as cultural and commercial hubs, while newer suburbs — such as Flushing in New York and Richmond in British Columbia — reflect recent immigration.

Culture and identity

Diaspora communities maintain temples, clan and hometown associations, Chinese-language schools and newspapers, and celebrate festivals such as Lunar New Year and the Mid-Autumn Festival, while also assimilating to varying degrees into host societies. Relations with China — cultural, economic and political — remain a significant theme for many overseas Chinese communities.
