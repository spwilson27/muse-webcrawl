Interrupted projection


Field: Cartography
Purpose: World maps with reduced distortion

An interrupted projection is a map projection that divides the Earth's surface into separate lobes or sections, with gaps (interruptions) usually placed over the oceans, in order to reduce the distortion of land areas. Each lobe is projected with its own central meridian, so shapes and areas on land are represented more faithfully than in an uninterrupted world map, at the cost of discontinuity across the gaps.

Examples

Well-known interrupted projections include the Goode homolosine projection (1923), which combines the sinusoidal and Mollweide projections in interrupted form; the Boggs eumorphic projection; the interrupted forms of the sinusoidal projection itself; the butterfly-like Cahill–Keyes and Waterman butterfly projections based on polyhedral nets; and the Dymaxion map of Buckminster Fuller, which unfolds an icosahedron and can be arranged to show either land or oceans nearly contiguously.

Advantages and disadvantages

By centring each continent (or each ocean, in ocean-centred versions) on its own lobe, interrupted projections achieve low distortion where the mapped subject lies. They are therefore popular for world thematic maps of land phenomena. The disadvantages are the unfamiliar appearance, the impossibility of measuring across the interruptions, and the poor representation of whichever realm (land or ocean) is split.
