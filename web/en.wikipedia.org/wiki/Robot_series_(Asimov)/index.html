The Robot series is a series of science fiction short stories and novels by Isaac Asimov centred on positronic robots — androids and machines governed by the Three Laws of Robotics. Beginning with stories published from 1940 onward and collected in I, Robot (1950), the series grew into four detective novels featuring New York detective Elijah Baley and his robot partner R. Daneel Olivaw, and was eventually merged by Asimov into his Foundation universe.

The Three Laws

Asimov's robots are constrained by the Three Laws of Robotics, introduced in the 1942 story "Runaround": (1) A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm; (2) a robot must obey orders given by human beings except where such orders conflict with the First Law; (3) a robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law. Much of the series' drama arises from conflicts and loopholes among the laws; later works add a "Zeroth Law" putting humanity as a whole above individual humans.

Short stories

The early stories star robopsychologist Susan Calvin of U.S. Robots and Mechanical Men, Inc., and the field-testing team of Gregory Powell and Mike Donovan, who untangle robot misbehaviour caused by subtle interactions of the Three Laws. Stories such as "Liar!", "Evidence" and "The Bicentennial Man" explore robot psychology, machine personhood and the boundary between robot and human.

The Baley novels

The Caves of Steel (1954) introduces Elijah Baley, a detective in a future overpopulated Earth whose domed cities fear both robots and the "Spacers" of the colonised Outer Worlds, investigating a murder with the humaniform robot R. Daneel Olivaw. Sequels The Naked Sun (1957) and The Robots of Dawn (1983) take Baley to Spacer worlds, and Robots and Empire (1985) bridges the Robot and Foundation series, setting up psychohistory and the fate of Earth.

Influence

Asimov's robots — rule-bound, fundamentally benevolent machines whose puzzles are logical rather than monstrous — transformed science fiction's treatment of robots and still frame popular discussion of machine ethics and artificial intelligence safety.
