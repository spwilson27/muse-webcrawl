The Lotus 25 was a racing car designed by Colin Chapman for the 1962 Formula One season. It was revolutionary as the first fully stressed monocoque chassis to appear in Formula One, replacing the spaceframe construction used by every competitor. Driven principally by Jim Clark, the 25 dominated the 1963 season, carrying Clark to his first World Championship and Lotus to its first Constructors' title.

Design

Instead of a tubular spaceframe, Chapman designed a "bathtub" monocoque of folded aluminium sheet riveted into a rigid box structure, with the driver sitting inside it and the engine and suspension bolted directly to it. The monocoque was roughly three times stiffer than the spaceframe of the preceding Lotus 24 while being lighter. To keep the frontal area small, Clark reclined in a laid-back driving position, an unusual posture at the time that became standard in racing car design.

Power came from the Coventry Climax FWMV 1.5-litre V8, producing around 190 horsepower in later development. The car was raced by the works Team Lotus and also supplied to privateers including Reg Parnell Racing.

Racing history

The Lotus 25 made its debut at the 1962 Dutch Grand Prix at Zandvoort. Clark took the car's first win at Spa-Francorchamps in Belgium. In 1963 Clark won seven of the ten championship rounds — a record at the time — clinching the drivers' and constructors' championships with rounds to spare. The 25 continued to win races in 1964 and scored a final victory at the 1965 French Grand Prix before being succeeded by the Lotus 33 and, for the new 3-litre formula, the Lotus 43.

Legacy

The monocoque concept introduced by the Lotus 25 became the universal standard for Formula One chassis construction and remains, in carbon-fibre form, the basis of every modern Grand Prix car. The 25 is regularly cited among the most significant racing cars ever built.
