Parkinson's law

Formulated byCyril Northcote Parkinson (1909–1993)
First publishedThe Economist, 19 November 1955
BookParkinson's Law: The Pursuit of Progress (1957)
Statement"Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion."

Parkinson's law is the humorous adage that "work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion." It was formulated by the British historian and author Cyril Northcote Parkinson in a satirical essay published in The Economist in 1955 and developed in his book Parkinson's Law: The Pursuit of Progress (1957), which drew on his experience in the British civil service and colonial administration.

Parkinson's satire targeted the self-perpetuating growth of bureaucracy.

"Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion. Thus, an elderly lady of leisure can spend the entire day in writing and dispatching a postcard to her niece at Bognor Regis."

Corollaries

Parkinson proposed related laws about bureaucracy: officials multiply subordinates (not rivals), and officials make work for each other, so administrative staff grows at a predictable rate regardless of the amount of work to be done — his "law" of bureaucratic growth, supported in the essay by statistics showing the British Colonial Office staff expanding even as the Empire shrank. He also formulated a law about committees (the time spent on an item is inversely proportional to the sum of money involved — later dubbed the "law of triviality," illustrated by a committee approving a nuclear reactor quickly but debating a bicycle shed at length), and Parkinson's law of finance, concerning the point at which expenditure rises to meet income.

Influence and analogues

The adage entered general usage in management, project planning, and computing. Analogues include the saying that "data expands to fill the space available for storage" and Hofstadter's law ("It always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law"). In software development, Parkinson's law is cited alongside Brooks's law and in discussions of procrastination and student syndrome in project scheduling. Although framed satirically, the underlying observations about incentives in organisations have been taken seriously by management scholars.
