Benjamin Apthorp Gould (27 September 1824 – 26 November 1896) was a pioneering American astronomer, founder of the Astronomical Journal and of the Argentine National Observatory at Córdoba, whose catalogues of southern stars laid foundations for modern astronomy in the southern hemisphere.

Early career

Gould graduated from Harvard in 1844 and studied in Europe under Carl Friedrich Gauss at Göttingen, taking his doctorate there in 1848 — the first American to receive a PhD in astronomy. Returning home, he founded the Astronomical Journal in 1849, still a leading periodical. He worked for the U.S. Coast Survey on longitude determinations (pioneering the use of the telegraph for longitude), and became director of the Dudley Observatory in Albany, New York, where a dispute with the trustees ended his tenure in 1859. During the Civil War he helped organise the United States Sanitary Commission, and he was a founding member of the National Academy of Sciences.

Córdoba

Invited by President Domingo Faustino Sarmiento, Gould moved to Argentina in 1870 to establish a national observatory at Córdoba. There he pioneered astronomical photography in the south and produced the great visual and photographic catalogues of the southern sky: the Uranometría Argentina (1879), covering some 7,000 southern stars, and the Córdoba Durchmusterung, completed by his successors, which catalogued over 600,000 stars. His meteorological work also created Argentina's national weather service.

Gould Belt

In 1879 Gould noted that bright nearby stars appear arranged in a great ring inclined to the plane of the Milky Way — the structure still known as the Gould Belt, a local complex of young stars and gas some 3,000 light-years across in which the Sun is embedded.

He returned to the United States in 1885 and died at Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1896. The lunar crater Gould is named in his honour.
