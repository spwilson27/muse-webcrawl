Harry S. Truman

Harry S. Truman (May 8, 1884 – December 26, 1972) was the 33rd president of the United States, serving from 1945 to 1953. A plain-spoken Democrat from Missouri with little formal education beyond high school, he became vice president under Franklin D. Roosevelt in January 1945 and succeeded him on Roosevelt's death that April, in the closing weeks of World War II. Truman presided over the end of the war — including the decision to use atomic bombs against Japan — the founding of the United Nations, the onset of the Cold War with the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan, the creation of NATO, the recognition of Israel, and the Korean War, whose conduct brought him into conflict with General Douglas MacArthur, whom he dismissed in 1951. Expected to lose the election of 1948, he won the most famous upset in American political history. His presidency, unpopular when he left office, has been re-evaluated by historians as one of the most consequential of the twentieth century — the administration that built the architecture of the postwar Western order.

Early life and Senate

Truman grew up on farms in western Missouri, worked as a bank clerk and bookkeeper, served as an artillery officer in France in the First World War (rising to major and commanding a battery that never lost a man), and failed in business — the Kansas City haberdashery he co-owned went bankrupt in the 1921 recession, and he spent years paying off the debts. Backed by the Pendergast machine he entered county government, where he built a reputation for honest administration of road-building programmes, and was elected to the Senate in 1934. There he made his national name chairing the Truman Committee (1941–44), which investigated waste and profiteering in war contracts and was credited with saving billions — the work that made him an acceptable compromise vice-presidential nominee in 1944, when party leaders replaced Henry Wallace on the ticket.

Presidency

End of the war and the atomic decision

Truman, who had been told almost nothing of the Manhattan Project as vice president, took over a world war in its last act: Germany surrendered within a month; at Potsdam in July he sparred with Stalin, issued the ultimatum to Japan and learned the atomic test had succeeded. In August 1945 he authorised the atomic bombings of Hiroshima (6 August) and Nagasaki (9 August) — decisions he defended ever after as shortening the war and saving invasion casualties; Japan surrendered on 14–15 August. Domestically, the sudden end of the war brought reconversion chaos, a wave of strikes, and Republican victory in the 1946 midterms.

The Cold War settlement

In 1947, alarmed by Communist pressure on Greece and Turkey and by Britain's retreat, Truman proclaimed the Truman Doctrine — American support for peoples resisting subjugation — and his secretary of state George Marshall launched the Marshall Plan, the vast programme of aid that rebuilt Western Europe and anchored it to the United States. Truman recognised the state of Israel in 1948 against much official advice, backed the Berlin Airlift through the Soviet blockade of 1948–49, and in 1949 signed the North Atlantic Treaty creating NATO. At home he desegregated the armed forces by executive order (1948), and proposed a "Fair Deal" of social reforms mostly blocked by Congress.

1948 and Korea

Written off by every pollster — the Chicago Tribune famously printed "DEWEY DEFEATS TRUMAN" — Truman won the 1948 election outright after a whistle-stop campaign blaming a "do-nothing Congress". In June 1950, when North Korea invaded South Korea, he committed American forces under the UN flag without a congressional declaration; the war's course — Inchon triumph, Chinese intervention, stalemate — ruined his popularity. His dismissal of MacArthur in April 1951 for insubordination (the general had publicly challenged the policy of limited war) provoked a storm but affirmed civilian control; the war ground on to an armistice concluded after he left office. Beset also by McCarthyism, corruption scandals and a seizure of the steel mills struck down by the Supreme Court, Truman chose not to seek another term and retired to Independence, Missouri, in January 1953 with approval ratings among the lowest recorded.

Later life and legacy

Truman lived modestly in Independence — the presidential pension system was created partly in response to his straitened circumstances — wrote his memoirs, campaigned for Democratic successors, and watched his reputation steadily climb. Historians now generally rank him among the near-great presidents: the decisions of 1945–49 (containment, the Marshall Plan, NATO, Berlin) structured the Western world for two generations, and his personal signature — blunt, decisive, "The buck stops here", as the sign on his desk read — became a byword for presidential responsibility.
