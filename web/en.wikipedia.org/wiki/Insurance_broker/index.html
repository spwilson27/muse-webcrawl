Insurance broker

An insurance broker's office, where brokers meet clients to arrange coverage.

An insurance broker is an intermediary who sells, solicits, or negotiates insurance on behalf of a client for compensation. An insurance broker's primary duty is to provide unbiased advice and represent the interests of the insured, rather than those of an insurer. Brokers help clients assess their risks, identify appropriate policies, and obtain coverage from insurance companies.

Unlike an insurance agent, who typically represents one or more specific insurers, a broker generally works with many insurers and owes a duty of care to the client. In many jurisdictions, brokers must be licensed and are subject to regulatory oversight.

History

The modern insurance market developed in London in the late 17th century, centred on Lloyd's of London, where brokers acted on behalf of merchants seeking marine insurance. The role of the broker expanded during the 19th and 20th centuries as insurance products diversified into fire, life, liability, and commercial lines. Large international brokerages such as Marsh McLennan, Aon, and Willis Towers Watson emerged through consolidation in the late 20th century.

Roles and responsibilities

Brokers analyse a client's risk exposures, recommend coverage, negotiate terms and premiums with insurers, assist with policy placement and renewals, and often help clients with claims. Commercial brokers may design complex programmes combining property, casualty, marine, aviation, and specialty coverages across many countries.

Remuneration

Brokers are typically paid by commission from the insurer, calculated as a percentage of the premium, or by fees agreed with the client. Disclosure rules vary by jurisdiction; some regulators require brokers to reveal commissions on request or in all cases.

Regulation

In the United States, brokers are licensed at the state level. In the United Kingdom, insurance intermediaries are regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority. Brokers owe clients duties of reasonable care and, in some markets, fiduciary duties regarding the handling of client money.
