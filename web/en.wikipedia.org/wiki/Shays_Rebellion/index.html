Shays's Rebellion

Shays's Rebellion was an armed uprising in Western Massachusetts in 1786–1787, named for Daniel Shays, a former captain in the Continental Army who led a large contingent of protesters. The rebellion grew out of the post-Revolutionary economic crisis: heavy state taxes demanded in scarce hard currency, farm debts, and court-ordered foreclosures and imprisonment for debt fell heavily on rural farmers, many of them war veterans.

Course of the rebellion

Beginning in the summer of 1786, "Regulators" modelled on Revolutionary-era protest shut down county courts at Northampton, Worcester, and elsewhere to stop debt and tax cases. The state government, hampered by the weakness of the Articles of Confederation, raised a privately financed militia under Benjamin Lincoln. On January 25, 1787, Shays's men were repulsed by cannon fire when they tried to seize the federal Springfield Armory; four rebels were killed. Lincoln's force surprised and scattered the remaining insurgents at Petersham in February, and the rebellion collapsed. Most participants were pardoned; two men were hanged for burglary, and Shays himself was pardoned in 1788.

Consequences

The rebellion shocked American leaders, convinced many that the Articles of Confederation were too weak to preserve order, and helped spur the calling of the Philadelphia Convention of 1787, which drafted the United States Constitution with a stronger federal government. It also contributed to political change in Massachusetts itself, where the more moderate John Hancock replaced Governor James Bowdoin and pursued debt relief.
