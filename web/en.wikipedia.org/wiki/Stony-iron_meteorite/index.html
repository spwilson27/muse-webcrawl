Stony-iron meteorite

Stony-iron meteorites are meteorites composed of roughly equal proportions of nickel–iron metal and silicate minerals. Together with stony meteorites (chondrites and achondrites) and iron meteorites, they form one of the three main divisions in the traditional classification of meteorites by composition. Stony-irons are rare, making up only a few percent of known meteorite falls and finds.

Classes

Two principal groups are recognised. Pallasites consist of centimetre-sized crystals of olivine embedded in a continuous nickel–iron matrix; when cut and polished they are among the most beautiful of all meteorites. They are thought to come from the core–mantle boundary region of differentiated asteroids. Mesosiderites are breccias in which fragments of silicate rock — similar to eucrites and diogenites — are mixed with molten metal, probably by a violent impact that mixed crustal material with a molten core.

Origin

Stony-irons sample the interiors of planetesimals that melted and differentiated early in Solar System history. Their metal and silicate phases cooled slowly, allowing large olivine crystals to grow in the pallasites; oxygen-isotope studies link the main-group pallasites to particular parent bodies, possibly including the same asteroid sampled by other meteorite groups.

Notable specimens

Famous pallasites include the Krasnojarsk meteorite (the first pallasite described, brought to scientific attention by Peter Simon Pallas in the 1770s), the Esquel and Fukang pallasites, and the Brenham finds in Kansas. The Hoba meteorite is an iron, but large mesosiderites such as Vaca Muerta (Chile) and Estherville (Iowa) are well-known stony-irons.
