Ex nihilo

Ex nihilo is a Latin phrase meaning "out of nothing." It often appears in conjunction with the concept of creation, as in creatio ex nihilo, "creation out of nothing" — the doctrine that God created the universe from nothing, without pre-existing matter. The phrase contrasts with creatio ex materia, creation out of pre-existing material, and with the ancient maxim ex nihil nihil fit, "nothing comes from nothing."

Theological usage

The doctrine of creation ex nihilo became standard in Jewish, Christian, and Islamic theology. An early statement appears in 2 Maccabees 7:28, where a mother exhorts her son to recognise that God made heaven and earth "out of things that did not exist." In the second century AD, Christian writers such as Theophilus of Antioch and Irenaeus defended creation from nothing against Gnostic and Platonic views that God merely shaped eternal matter, as the demiurge does in Plato's Timaeus.

The doctrine was taken to imply the absolute dependence of the world on God, divine omnipotence, and the goodness of material creation. It remains a point of discussion in contemporary philosophy of religion and in debates over cosmology and the origin of the universe.

Other usages

In art history, the term has been applied to works created spontaneously or from no preconceived plan. In everyday usage, ex nihilo describes anything produced seemingly from no resources at all. Philosophers also use it in discussions of whether something can begin to exist without a material cause.
