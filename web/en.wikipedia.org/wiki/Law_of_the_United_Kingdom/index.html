Law of the United Kingdom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland has three distinct legal systems: the law of England and Wales (common law), Scots law (a mixed system) and Northern Ireland law (common law, similar to England's). There is no single “British law”: the Acts of Union (1707) preserved Scotland's courts and private law, and since devolution (1998) the Scottish Parliament, the Senedd and the Northern Ireland Assembly legislate within their competences, while the UK Parliament legislates UK-wide on reserved matters and for England.

Across the systems sit UK-wide institutions: the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom (established 2009, replacing the Appellate Committee of the House of Lords) as final appeal court for civil cases UK-wide and criminal cases from England, Wales and Northern Ireland (Scottish criminal appeals end at the High Court of Justiciary); the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council for certain Commonwealth appeals; and common frameworks such as the Human Rights Act 1998 (applying the European Convention across the UK) and, formerly, EU law (1973–2020). Sources of law in each system rank statute above precedent above custom, with equity, Roman-law learning (stronger in Scotland) and academic writing in supporting roles.
