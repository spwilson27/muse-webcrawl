H.M.S. Pinafore

H.M.S. Pinafore
LibrettistW. S. Gilbert
ComposerArthur Sullivan
Premiere25 May 1878, Opera Comique, London
SubtitleThe Lass That Loved a Sailor

H.M.S. Pinafore; or, The Lass That Loved a Sailor is a comic opera in two acts, with music by Arthur Sullivan and a libretto by W. S. Gilbert. It opened at the Opera Comique in London on 25 May 1878 and ran for 571 performances, becoming an international sensation and one of the most popular of the Gilbert and Sullivan Savoy operas. Its unauthorized productions in the United States sparked a craze — and the copyright battles that followed led to the creation of the D'Oyly Carte Opera Company as the partners' own production vehicle.

Plot

Aboard HMS Pinafore at Portsmouth, the captain's daughter Josephine is loved by the humble sailor Ralph Rackstraw but promised by her father, Captain Corcoran, to Sir Joseph Porter, the First Lord of the Admiralty — a pompous politician who has risen "from office boy to ruler of the Queen's navee" without ever going to sea ("When I was a lad"). Sir Joseph teaches that love levels all ranks, unintentionally encouraging the couple. They plan to elope, but the villainous Dick Deadeye betrays them. In the finale, the nurse Little Buttercup reveals that she mixed up two babies in infancy: Ralph is really of high birth and Corcoran a common sailor, so the ranks are reversed, the marriages rearranged, and all ends happily.

Music and satire

Famous numbers include "We sail the ocean blue", "I'm called Little Buttercup", "When I was a lad", "I am the captain of the Pinafore" and "He is an Englishman!". The opera satirises the British class system, party politics, jingoistic patriotism and the appointment of the unqualified to high office — W. H. Smith, the newsagent magnate who became First Lord of the Admiralty, was widely taken as the model for Sir Joseph Porter.

Legacy

Pinafore established the template of the Savoy operas and remains in constant performance worldwide. It influenced the development of the modern musical, and its songs have been quoted and parodied endlessly in popular culture.
