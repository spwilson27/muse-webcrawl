Air compressor

An air compressor is a machine that converts power (using an electric motor, diesel or gasoline engine, etc.) into potential energy stored in pressurized air (i.e., compressed air). By one of several methods, an air compressor forces more and more air into a storage tank, increasing the pressure. When tank pressure reaches its engineered upper limit, the air compressor shuts off. The compressed air, then, is held in the tank until called into use. The energy contained in the compressed air can be used for a variety of applications, utilizing the kinetic energy of the air as it is released and the tank depressurizes. When tank pressure reaches its lower limit, the air compressor turns on again and re-pressurizes the tank.

A typical single-stage reciprocating air compressor with receiver tank.

Types

Compressors may be classified by their working principle:

  • Positive displacement compressors force air into a chamber whose volume is then decreased. They include reciprocating (piston) compressors, rotary screw compressors, rotary vane compressors, and scroll compressors.
  • Dynamic (turbo) compressors impart velocity to the air with rapidly rotating impellers and convert the velocity into pressure in diffuser passages. Centrifugal and axial compressors used in large industrial plants and gas turbines are of this type.

Compressors are also described by the number of stages (single-stage or multi-stage, the latter giving higher pressures more efficiently) and by whether the compressed air is lubricated or oil-free.

Applications

Compressed air is sometimes called industry's "fourth utility". Applications include driving pneumatic tools (impact wrenches, nail guns, spray guns, sandblasters), inflating tires, supplying breathing air for diving and firefighting, starting large diesel engines, operating air brakes on trains and heavy vehicles, cleaning with blow guns, and providing process air in manufacturing, food, and pharmaceutical plants. Larger systems feed entire factories from central compressor rooms, with dryers and filters to remove water and oil vapour from the air.

Safety and maintenance

Receiver tanks are pressure vessels and are subject to inspection regimes because corrosion or overpressure can cause catastrophic rupture. Routine maintenance includes draining condensed water from the tank, checking pressure-relief valves, replacing intake filters, and monitoring lubricant condition.
