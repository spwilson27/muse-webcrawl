Clostridium

Clostridium is a genus of anaerobic, Gram-positive bacteria. Species of Clostridium inhabit soils and the intestinal tracts of animals, including humans. They form highly resistant endospores that allow them to survive desiccation, heat, and disinfectants. The genus includes several major human pathogens as well as species of industrial importance.

Characteristics

The name derives from the Greek klōstēr (κλωστήρ), "spindle," reflecting the shape cells take when spores form. Members are obligate anaerobes, killed by exposure to oxygen in their vegetative state, though their spores are aerotolerant. Many species ferment a wide range of substrates, producing organic acids, alcohols, and gases such as hydrogen and carbon dioxide.

Clostridioides difficile infection is a leading cause of healthcare-associated diarrhea, arising when antibiotics disrupt the normal gut flora. C. botulinum produces the most potent known biological toxin, used in medicine and cosmetics as Botox, while C. tetani causes the spastic paralysis of tetanus, preventable by vaccination.

Industrial uses

Clostridium acetobutylicum was used in the acetone-butanol-ethanol fermentation developed by Chaim Weizmann during the First World War to produce acetone for cordite. Other species are studied for biofuel production and for their ability to fix nitrogen or degrade cellulose.

Taxonomy

Genomic studies have split the historically broad genus; many species have been moved to new genera such as Clostridioides, leaving Clostridium in a narrower sense around the type species C. butyricum.
