National Grid

Term: Electricity transmission network
Examples: Great Britain, other national systems
Function: Balancing supply and demand, bulk power transfer

A national grid is a national electricity transmission network that interconnects power stations, substations and consumers across a country, allowing electricity to be generated, balanced and delivered at national scale.

Function

High-voltage transmission lines carry bulk power from generators to regional distribution networks, which deliver it to homes and businesses. A system operator continuously balances supply and demand to maintain a stable frequency — 50 Hz in Great Britain and much of the world, 60 Hz in North America — and manages reserves against plant failures.

The British grid

The term is strongly associated with Great Britain's network, first unified under the Central Electricity Board from 1926 and developed into a fully integrated grid by the 1960s. It connects offshore wind farms, interconnectors to neighbouring countries, nuclear, gas and other generators. In Great Britain the transmission system operator role passed to the publicly owned National Energy System Operator (NESO) in 2024, while National Grid plc owns much of the transmission infrastructure in England and Wales and also operates networks in the northeastern United States.
