Philip Levine (10 August 1900 – 18 October 1987) was a Russian-born American immunohematologist who played a central role in the discovery of the Rh blood group system and in explaining hemolytic disease of the newborn (erythroblastosis fetalis).

Career and discoveries

Born in Kremenchug in the Russian Empire, Levine emigrated to the United States as a child and earned his medical degree from Cornell Medical College in 1923. Working with Karl Landsteiner at the Rockefeller Institute, he co-discovered the M, N and P blood group systems in 1927. In 1939, with Rufus Stetson, he published the case of a woman who produced an antibody after delivering a stillborn infant and reacting to a transfusion of her husband's blood — the first recognised evidence of the Rh factor, named shortly afterwards from the rhesus monkey experiments of Landsteiner and Alexander Wiener. In 1941 Levine showed that maternal–fetal Rh incompatibility caused erythroblastosis fetalis, explaining a major cause of infant death and laying the groundwork for preventive anti-D immunoglobulin therapy.

Levine spent much of his later career at the Ortho Research Foundation in Raritan, New Jersey. With Landsteiner and Wiener, he shared the 1946 Albert Lasker Award for clinical medical research for work on the Rh factor.
