Pulp (paper)

Pulp is a lignocellulosic fibrous material prepared by chemically, semi-chemically, or mechanically separating cellulose fibres from wood, fiber crops, waste paper, or rags. Pulp is one of the most abundant raw materials worldwide and is the principal input of papermaking; it is also used to manufacture rayon, cellophane, cellulose acetate, and other regenerated cellulose products.

Production

In chemical pulping, wood chips are cooked with chemicals that dissolve the lignin binding the fibres together. The dominant process is the kraft process, which uses sodium hydroxide and sodium sulfide and accounts for the great majority of chemical pulp because it produces strong fibres and recovers its cooking chemicals. The older sulfite process yields brighter, weaker pulp.

Mechanical pulping grinds or refines wood against abrasive surfaces, retaining most of the lignin and giving high yields but weaker, yellowing paper; it is used for newsprint and magazines. Semi-chemical and chemi-mechanical processes combine a mild chemical treatment with mechanical refining. Recycled pulp is produced by re-pulping and de-inking waste paper.

Market pulp

Pulp produced for sale to other mills, rather than for a mill's own paper production, is dried, pressed into sheets, and baled as market pulp. Softwood kraft pulp (often called northern bleached softwood kraft, NBSK) serves as a benchmark grade in world markets. Major producing countries include Brazil, Canada, the United States, Chile, and Indonesia, with eucalyptus hardwood pulp a fast-growing segment.

Uses and environment

Beyond printing and writing papers, pulp goes into tissue, packaging board, specialty papers, diapers (fluff pulp), and dissolving grades for textiles. Pulping is energy- and water-intensive, and its environmental management — recovery of chemicals, treatment of effluents, and sustainable forestry — is a central concern of the industry.
