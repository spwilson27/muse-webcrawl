History of Sheffield

The history of Sheffield, a city in South Yorkshire, England, can be traced back to the founding of a settlement in a clearing beside the River Sheaf in the second half of the 1st millennium AD. The area had seen human occupation since at least the last ice age, but significant growth did not occur until the Industrial Revolution.

Early history

The earliest evidence of human occupation in the Sheffield area is two flints discovered in the Creswell Crags area, dated to around 12,800–11,500 years ago. During the Iron Age the area was part of the territory of the Brigantes tribe. Sheffield takes its name from the River Sheaf, which itself may derive from a word meaning a boundary or a stream.

Medieval Sheffield

After the departure of the Romans, the Sheffield area may have been part of the Celtic kingdom of Elmet. The Anglo-Saxons expanded into the area in the late 6th century. A settlement grew around a cross erected by Saxon Christians, and Sheffield Castle was built after the Norman Conquest. A market charter was granted in 1297, establishing Sheffield as a market town.

Cutlery and steel

Sheffield had become known for the production of knives by the 14th century: Geoffrey Chaucer mentions a "Sheffield thwitel" in The Reeve's Tale. By the early 1600s it was the main centre of cutlery production in England outside London, overseen from 1624 by the Company of Cutlers in Hallamshire. The crucible steel process, developed by Benjamin Huntsman in the 1740s, and the later invention of stainless steel by Harry Brearley in 1913, reinforced the city's reputation. The phrase "Made in Sheffield" became a mark of quality.

Industrial Revolution

During the 19th century Sheffield grew rapidly with the steel industry, its population increasing from about 60,000 in 1801 to over 450,000 by 1901. Steel works, mills and workshops lined the river valleys, powered first by water and then by steam. The city was incorporated as a borough in 1843 and granted city status in 1893. The Great Sheffield Flood of 1864, caused by the collapse of the Dale Dyke Dam, killed over 240 people.

20th century

Sheffield suffered heavy bombing in the Sheffield Blitz of December 1940. After the Second World War the steel industry initially prospered, but from the 1970s international competition and recession led to widespread closures and job losses. The city hosted the World Student Games in 1991, for which facilities such as Ponds Forge and the Don Valley Stadium were built.

Modern era

Since the 1990s Sheffield has undergone extensive regeneration, including the Winter Garden, Millennium Square and the redeveloped station gateway. Its two universities, the University of Sheffield and Sheffield Hallam University, are major employers. The city is also noted for its green spaces, with an estimated two million trees.
