William Marbury

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

William Marbury (7 November 1762 – 13 March 1835) was a Georgetown businessman and Federalist Party operative whose failed commission gave rise to Marbury v. Madison (1803), the foundational case of judicial review. In the last days of John Adams's presidency, Marbury was appointed a justice of the peace for the District of Columbia under the Organic Act of 27 February 1801 — one of the “midnight judges”. His commission, signed and sealed, was not delivered before Jefferson took office; the new secretary of state, James Madison, declined to deliver it, and Marbury petitioned the Supreme Court for a writ of mandamus under § 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789.

Chief Justice Marshall's opinion held that Marbury was entitled to the commission, that the laws afforded a remedy — but that § 13 unconstitutionally expanded the Court's original jurisdiction, and so, in the act of denying itself the power to help him, the Court asserted the greater power to declare acts of Congress unconstitutional. Marbury never received the judgeship; he returned to banking and finance in Georgetown, prospered as president of the Bank of Columbia, and is remembered almost entirely for the case that bears his name — history's most consequential unsuccessful litigant.
