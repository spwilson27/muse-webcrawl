The Eight Banners (Manchu: ᠵᠠᡴᡡᠨ ᡤᡡᠰᠠ; Chinese: 八旗) were administrative and military divisions under the Later Jin and the Qing dynasty of China into which all Manchu households were placed. In war, the Eight Banners functioned as armies, but the banner system was also the basic organizational framework of all Manchu society. Created in the early 17th century by Nurhaci, the banner armies played an instrumental role in his unification of the fragmented Jurchen people and in the Qing conquest of Ming China.

Organization

The original banners were organized by color: Yellow, White, Red, and Blue, each with a plain banner; bordered versions of each color were added to create eight. Each banner was subdivided into companies (niru) of nominally 300 households. As Mongol and Han Chinese forces were incorporated, parallel Mongol Eight Banners and Han (Hanjun) Eight Banners were created, bringing the total to twenty-four banners, though the system retained the name "Eight Banners".

Bannermen received land and stipends, were registered hereditarily, and were garrisoned first in Manchuria and then, after 1644, in Beijing and strategic provincial garrisons across China. Banner identity shaped legal status, marriage, dress (including the queue hairstyle imposed on Han men), and privilege under the Qing.

Decline

Over the 18th and 19th centuries the banner forces declined in military effectiveness as stipends failed to keep pace with growing banner populations, and impoverishment of bannermen became a persistent problem. Banner armies performed poorly in the Opium Wars and the Taiping Rebellion, during which the Qing relied increasingly on Han-led forces such as the Xiang Army. The banner system was formally abolished after the fall of the Qing in 1912, though banner communities and identities persisted.
