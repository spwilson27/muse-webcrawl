Drug withdrawal

Drug withdrawal is the group of symptoms that occur upon the abrupt discontinuation or reduction in intake of medications or recreational drugs after a period of sustained use that has produced physical dependence. The body, having adapted to the drug's presence (through receptor down-regulation and other neuroadaptations), is thrown out of equilibrium when it is removed. Withdrawal symptoms are frequently the opposite of the drug's acute effects, and their severity ranges from mild discomfort to life-threatening states such as delirium tremens and withdrawal seizures.

Mechanism

Chronic drug exposure shifts the nervous system's set-points: depressants such as alcohol and benzodiazepines reduce GABAergic inhibition and increase glutamate excitability in compensation, so sudden removal leaves the brain hyperexcitable. Opioid dependence involves adaptations in the locus coeruleus and noradrenergic systems; withdrawal produces a noradrenergic storm. These adaptations, together called allostasis, explain why withdrawal effects typically mirror-image the drug's actions.

Management

Management combines monitoring, supportive care, and pharmacotherapy. Alcohol and sedative withdrawal is usually treated with a tapering benzodiazepine regimen guided by symptom scales such as CIWA-Ar, plus thiamine to prevent Wernicke encephalopathy. Opioid withdrawal is managed with substitution agonists (methadone, buprenorphine) or alpha-2 agonists such as clonidine or lofexidine, and transition to maintenance treatment reduces relapse and mortality. Gradual dose tapering is generally preferred to abrupt cessation whenever dependence is suspected.
