Physical dependence is a physiological state of adaptation to a substance, drug, or toxin, manifested by a withdrawal syndrome produced by abrupt cessation of exposure, rapid dose reduction, or administration of an antagonist. It results from the body's compensatory changes—such as receptor down-regulation, altered neurotransmitter production, and changes in gene expression—that counteract the drug's effects while it is present and are left unopposed when it is removed.

Characteristics

Physical dependence is closely associated with tolerance, the need for increasing doses to achieve the same effect, because both arise from the same homeostatic adaptations. Crucially, physical dependence is not synonymous with addiction: patients taking opioids, antidepressants, corticosteroids, or beta-blockers exactly as prescribed routinely develop physical dependence without compulsive drug-seeking. Modern diagnostic manuals such as the DSM-5 therefore exclude tolerance and withdrawal from the criteria for substance use disorder when the substance is taken under appropriate medical supervision.

Examples

Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can be life-threatening, producing seizures and delirium tremens because both drug classes act on the GABA system. Opioid withdrawal, while intensely unpleasant—influenza-like symptoms, muscle aches, diarrhea, insomnia—is rarely fatal in healthy adults. Caffeine produces a mild, well-documented withdrawal syndrome (headache, fatigue, irritability) recognized in the DSM-5. Abrupt discontinuation of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) causes antidepressant discontinuation syndrome, with dizziness and "brain zap" sensations; nicotine withdrawal drives relapse in tobacco use.

Mechanisms

At the cellular level, chronic agonist exposure leads receptors to desensitize, internalize, or decrease in number, while antagonist exposure can up-regulate them. In opioid dependence, superactivation of adenylyl cyclase in the locus coeruleus is a classic model of the rebound noradrenergic storm seen in withdrawal. These adaptations are forms of neuroplasticity and may persist long after detoxification, contributing to protracted withdrawal symptoms.

Management

Physical dependence is managed by gradual dose reduction (tapering), substitution with a longer-acting agent of the same class (as in methadone or buprenorphine maintenance for opioids, or diazepam tapers for benzodiazepines), and symptomatic medications such as clonidine for autonomic symptoms. Recognizing dependence as an expected pharmacological phenomenon rather than a moral failing is central to contemporary addiction medicine.
