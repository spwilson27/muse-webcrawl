Rebound effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rebound effect
DefinitionReturn of symptoms, often worse than baseline, after a drug is stopped
MechanismReceptor up-regulation / compensatory adaptation
Classic examplesRhinitis medicamentosa, rebound acid hypersecretion, rebound insomnia

The rebound effect, or rebound phenomenon, is the emergence or re-emergence of symptoms that were either absent or controlled while taking a medication, but appear when that same medication is discontinued or reduced in dosage. In the case of a re-emergence, the symptoms are often more severe than they were before treatment began. The effect typically follows prolonged use and reflects the body's compensatory adaptations: for example, if a drug blocks a receptor, the body may increase the number or sensitivity of those receptors, so that removing the drug leaves the system over-responsive to its natural signals.

Examples

Rhinitis medicamentosa is the classic case: topical decongestant sprays such as oxymetazoline, used for more than a few days, produce rebound nasal congestion worse than the original symptom, driving a cycle of escalating use. Rebound acid hypersecretion occurs after stopping proton pump inhibitors, as elevated gastrin levels have stimulated acid-producing capacity. Rebound insomnia and anxiety follow abrupt cessation of benzodiazepines and Z-drugs used as hypnotics. Rebound hypertension can follow sudden withdrawal of clonidine or beta-blockers—the latter also risking tachycardia and angina because of up-regulated beta receptors. Rebound headaches (medication-overuse headache) result from frequent use of analgesics and triptans. Topical corticosteroid withdrawal can produce rebound flares of eczema or psoriasis, and rebound congestion is also described for some migraine and ADHD medications.

Rebound vs. relapse and withdrawal

Rebound is distinguished from relapse (the return of the underlying disease at its natural severity) by its timing—typically within hours to days of stopping—and by symptoms that temporarily exceed the original baseline. It is one component of the broader withdrawal syndrome but refers specifically to the overshoot of the treated symptom, whereas other withdrawal symptoms may be entirely new. Tapering the dose gradually allows compensatory adaptations to reverse slowly and is the standard strategy for avoiding rebound.
