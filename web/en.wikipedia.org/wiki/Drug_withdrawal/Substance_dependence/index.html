Substance dependence, in medical and psychological terminology, is an adaptive state that develops from repeated drug administration, characterized by a cluster of cognitive, behavioral, and physiological symptoms indicating that the individual continues using the substance despite significant substance-related problems. The World Health Organization's ICD-10 defines a dependence syndrome by features including a strong desire or compulsion to take the substance, difficulties controlling its use, a physiological withdrawal state, tolerance, progressive neglect of alternative pleasures and interests, and persistence despite clear evidence of harm.

Terminology and diagnosis

The DSM-IV (1994) distinguished "substance dependence" (the more severe diagnosis, requiring three or more of seven criteria) from "substance abuse." Because "dependence" was often confused with ordinary physical dependence—an expected pharmacological adaptation—the DSM-5 (2013) merged the two into a single diagnosis of substance use disorder, graded mild, moderate, or severe by the number of criteria met, and explicitly excluded tolerance and withdrawal for substances taken as medically prescribed. The term "addiction" is generally reserved for the severe end of this spectrum.

Neurobiology

Dependent states involve lasting changes in the brain's reward circuitry, especially dopaminergic pathways from the ventral tegmental area to the nucleus accumbens. With repeated use, natural rewards lose salience while drug cues gain compulsive motivational power (incentive sensitization), the prefrontal systems governing self-control weaken, and stress systems (including corticotropin-releasing factor) are recruited so that use is maintained as much by relief of negative states—"the dark side" of addiction—as by pleasure.

Risk factors and course

Twin studies suggest heritability of roughly 40–60 percent for many substance dependencies. Additional risks include early initiation, co-occurring mental disorders, trauma, availability, and social environment. Dependence is now generally framed as a chronic, relapsing condition comparable to other chronic diseases, with relapse rates similar to those of hypertension or asthma management.

Treatment

Effective treatments combine pharmacotherapy—methadone, buprenorphine, or naltrexone for opioid dependence; naltrexone, acamprosate, or disulfiram for alcohol dependence; varenicline or nicotine replacement for tobacco—with behavioral therapies such as cognitive behavioral therapy, contingency management, motivational interviewing, and mutual-help groups like Alcoholics Anonymous and Narcotics Anonymous.
