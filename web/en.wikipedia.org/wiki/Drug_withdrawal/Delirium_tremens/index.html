Delirium tremens (Latin for "shaking frenzy"), often abbreviated DTs, is the most severe form of alcohol withdrawal, characterized by sudden and severe mental and nervous-system changes: profound confusion (delirium), agitation, fever, hallucinations, and coarse tremor. It is a medical emergency that occurs in a minority of people with alcohol dependence—roughly 3–5 percent of those who stop drinking abruptly after sustained heavy consumption.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms usually begin two to four days after the last drink. They include global confusion and disorientation, vivid hallucinations (classically visual—seeing insects or animals, called zoopsia—and tactile sensations of crawling skin, or formication), extreme agitation and fear, fever, rapid heartbeat, high blood pressure, sweating, and severe tremor. Withdrawal seizures may precede or accompany the syndrome. Without treatment the agitation and autonomic instability can progress to cardiovascular collapse.

Pathophysiology

Chronic alcohol exposure enhances the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA and suppresses the excitatory glutamate (NMDA) system; the brain compensates by down-regulating GABA receptors and up-regulating NMDA receptors. When alcohol is suddenly removed, the unopposed excitatory state produces central nervous system hyperexcitability and sympathetic overdrive—the basis of the tremor, seizures, and autonomic storm of withdrawal. Repeated withdrawal episodes appear to "kindle" progressively more severe symptoms.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by a history of heavy drinking and abrupt cessation; the CIWA-Ar scale is used to grade withdrawal severity and guide dosing. Clinicians must exclude other causes of delirium such as infection, hypoglycemia, head injury, and Wernicke encephalopathy, which is prevented and treated with thiamine given before glucose.

Treatment and prognosis

The mainstay is benzodiazepines (such as diazepam, lorazepam, or chlordiazepoxide), which substitute for alcohol at the GABA receptor; symptom-triggered or loading-dose regimens are used, with intensive-care monitoring for severe cases. Phenobarbital or propofol may be added for refractory cases. Supportive care includes fluids, electrolyte correction (especially magnesium, potassium, phosphate), and thiamine. With modern treatment, mortality is about 1–5 percent, compared with historical figures of 15 percent or more; the term was first described in its modern sense by Thomas Sutton in 1813.
