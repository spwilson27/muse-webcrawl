Familial Mediterranean fever (FMF) is a hereditary autoinflammatory disorder characterised by recurrent brief episodes of fever and serosal inflammation causing pain in the abdomen, chest, joints, or skin. It is the most common of the hereditary periodic fever syndromes.

FMF occurs most frequently among people originating from the Mediterranean region — including Sephardic Jews, Armenians, Arabs, Turks, Greeks, and Italians — though it is recognised worldwide. In some of these populations the carrier rate for mutations is remarkably high, reaching 1 in 3 or more.

Cause

FMF is caused by mutations in the MEFV gene on chromosome 16, which encodes pyrin, a protein that regulates the inflammasome and the inflammatory cytokine interleukin-1β. The disease is classically inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern, though some mutations behave in a dominant fashion. The most common mutations are M694V, V726A, M680I, and E148Q.

Signs and symptoms

Attacks typically last 12 to 72 hours and include fever; severe abdominal pain from sterile peritonitis (sometimes leading to unnecessary appendectomy); pleuritic chest pain; arthritis, most often of a single large joint of the leg; and an erysipelas-like rash on the lower leg. Between attacks most patients feel well. The most serious complication is secondary (AA) amyloidosis, which can lead to kidney failure.

Treatment

Daily oral colchicine, introduced in the 1970s, prevents attacks in most patients and markedly reduces the risk of amyloidosis. For the minority who are colchicine-resistant or intolerant, interleukin-1 blockers such as anakinra and canakinumab are effective.
