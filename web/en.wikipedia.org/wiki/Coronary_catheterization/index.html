Coronary catheterization

Coronary catheterization is a minimally invasive procedure used to access the coronary circulation and chambers of the heart using a catheter. It is performed in a cardiac catheterisation laboratory ("cath lab") and encompasses both diagnostic studies — above all coronary angiography — and therapeutic interventions collectively known as percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).

Technique

After local anaesthesia, a sheath is placed in a peripheral artery, most often the radial or femoral artery. Specially shaped catheters (such as the Judkins left and right) are advanced over a guidewire to the coronary ostia. Contrast injections image the arteries; pressure tracings, ventriculography, and adjunct tools such as intravascular ultrasound (IVUS), optical coherence tomography (OCT) and fractional flow reserve (FFR) wires may be used. If a significant blockage is treated, balloon angioplasty and usually a drug-eluting stent follow in the same session.

Indications

Coronary catheterisation is indicated for acute coronary syndromes — most urgently ST-elevation myocardial infarction, where rapid "primary PCI" restores blood flow — and for stable chest pain syndromes where non-invasive tests suggest significant coronary artery disease, as well as for evaluating heart failure, valvular disease and congenital heart disease.

Complications

Bleeding or haematoma at the puncture site is the most common problem; the shift from femoral to radial access has reduced major bleeding and mortality. Stroke, myocardial infarction, coronary dissection, contrast-induced kidney injury and death are uncommon; mortality for purely diagnostic procedures is well under one in a thousand in most registries.

History

The field descends from Werner Forssmann's 1929 self-experiment, the right-heart catheterisation of Cournand and Richards in the 1940s, Mason Sones's selective coronary angiography, and Andreas Gruentzig's first coronary balloon angioplasty in 1977.
