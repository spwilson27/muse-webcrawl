Cuisine of Iceland

Icelandic cuisine
StaplesFish, lamb, dairy (skyr), potatoes, rye bread
Traditional dishesHákarl, hangikjöt, plokkfiskur, svið

The cuisine of Iceland has a long history shaped by the island's harsh climate, scarce arable land, and reliance on fishing and sheep farming. Dairy products, seafood, and lamb dominate, and traditional preservation methods — fermenting, smoking, drying, and curing in whey — remain visible in the national diet.

Traditional foods

The midwinter Þorrablót festival features þorramatur, a selection of traditional foods including hákarl (fermented Greenland shark), svið (singed sheep's head), hangikjöt (smoked lamb), and sour ram's testicles. Everyday dishes include plokkfiskur (a fish stew of mashed fish and potatoes), kjötsúpa (lamb soup), and fish served fresh, dried (harðfiskur), or salted. Skyr, a cultured dairy product similar to strained yoghurt, has been made since the Viking Age settlement and is now exported worldwide.

Breads and modern cuisine

Rye bread is traditionally baked slowly in geothermal heat (hverabrauð). Since the late twentieth century, imported ingredients and greenhouse produce grown with geothermal energy have diversified Icelandic cooking, and Reykjavík has developed a noted restaurant scene, while coffee is deeply embedded in daily life.
