Motion (legal)

In legal terminology, a motion is a procedural device to bring a limited, contested issue before a court for a decision. It is a request made to a judge for an order, usually on matters arising during or before litigation, rather than a request for final judgment on the merits of a case. Motions may be written or oral, though major motions are ordinarily submitted in writing with supporting memoranda of law.

Common types

Frequently used motions include motions to dismiss for failure to state a claim, motions for summary judgment, motions to suppress evidence, motions in limine to exclude evidence at trial, motions for a new trial, and motions for judgment notwithstanding the verdict. In criminal cases, defendants commonly move to suppress evidence obtained in violation of constitutional rights.

Procedure

The moving party (the movant) files the motion and serves it on the opposing party, who may file a response; the movant may then reply. Courts may decide motions on the papers or hold a hearing with oral argument. Rulings on most pretrial motions are interlocutory and appealable only after final judgment, with some exceptions.

See also

Courts generally encourage motions as a means of narrowing disputes and resolving cases efficiently without trial where the law permits.
