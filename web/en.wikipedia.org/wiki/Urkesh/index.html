Urkesh

Urkesh, modern Tell Mozan, was a major Hurrian city of ancient northern Mesopotamia, located in the foothills of the Taurus Mountains in present-day northeastern Syria. It flourished in the third millennium BC as the capital of a Hurrian kingdom and a religious centre associated with the god Kumarbi. The site lies about 5 km from the modern city of Amuda and roughly 60 km west of Qamishli.

History

Occupation at Tell Mozan reaches back to the Halaf period, but Urkesh rose to prominence in the Early Bronze Age, when it controlled routes between the Anatolian highlands — a source of metals — and the Mesopotamian plain. Inscriptions of its king Tupkish and queen Uqnitum, written in Akkadian but naming Hurrian rulers, date to around 2250 BC. The city maintained relations, sometimes marital, with the Akkadian Empire: a daughter of Naram-Sin, Tar'am-Agade, appears in seal impressions at the site. Urkesh declined in the early second millennium BC and was eventually abandoned.

Excavations

Systematic excavations began in 1984 under Giorgio Buccellati and Marilyn Kelly-Buccellati for UCLA and the IIMAS institute. Work revealed a royal palace (Palace AP) with extensive archives of sealings, a monumental temple terrace with a wide staircase, and an underground structure (the abi) interpreted as a ritual pit connected to necromantic rites for ancestors and underworld deities. Excavations were suspended after 2010 because of the Syrian civil war; a local site-protection programme guarded the exposed remains during the conflict.

Significance

Urkesh is the first extensively excavated Hurrian capital and provides key evidence for the early urbanisation of the Hurrians, their language and religion, and their interactions with Akkad. The site pioneered "inter-site" digital publication and community archaeology approaches in the region.
