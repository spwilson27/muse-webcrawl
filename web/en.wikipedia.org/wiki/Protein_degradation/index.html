Protein degradation

Protein degradation is the breakdown of proteins into smaller polypeptides or amino acids within cells. Cells continuously synthesise and degrade proteins; degradation removes damaged, misfolded, or unneeded proteins and allows the cell to regulate the levels of enzymes and regulatory proteins rapidly. Balanced protein turnover is essential, and its failure is implicated in diseases including cancer and neurodegeneration.

The ubiquitin–proteasome system

In eukaryotes, most regulated protein degradation occurs through the ubiquitin–proteasome system. Proteins destined for destruction are tagged with chains of the small protein ubiquitin by a cascade of enzymes (E1 activating, E2 conjugating, and E3 ligase enzymes, the last conferring substrate specificity). The tagged protein is recognised by the proteasome, a large barrel-shaped protease complex (the 26S proteasome), which unfolds the substrate, removes the ubiquitin for recycling, and cleaves the protein into short peptides. The discovery of this system earned Aaron Ciechanover, Avram Hershko, and Irwin Rose the 2004 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Lysosomal degradation and autophagy

Long-lived proteins, membrane proteins, and whole organelles are degraded in lysosomes, acidic compartments containing proteases such as cathepsins. Autophagy delivers cytoplasmic material to lysosomes in double-membraned vesicles; it is upregulated by starvation and mTOR inhibition and clears aggregates that the proteasome cannot handle.

Regulation and disease

Protein half-lives range from minutes (many transcription factors, whose rapid turnover permits quick responses) to the lifetime of the organism (lens crystallins). Signals for degradation include N-terminal residues (the N-end rule), specific sequence motifs such as PEST sequences, and misfolded regions. Proteasome inhibitors such as bortezomib are used to treat multiple myeloma, and hijacking the ubiquitin system with PROTAC molecules is an emerging therapeutic strategy.
