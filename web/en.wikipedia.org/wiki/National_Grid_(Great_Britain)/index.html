National Grid (Great Britain)

The National Grid is the high-voltage electric power transmission network serving Great Britain, connecting power stations and major substations so that electricity generated anywhere can be used to satisfy demand elsewhere. The grid operates at a synchronous frequency of 50 hertz.

History

The grid was conceived following the Electricity (Supply) Act 1926, which created the Central Electricity Board to build a national network linking selected power stations. Construction proceeded through the 1930s, and the regional grids were interconnected by 1938. The industry was nationalised in 1948 under the British Electricity Authority and later the Central Electricity Generating Board. At privatisation in 1990, the transmission network in England and Wales passed to the National Grid Company.

Operation

The system is balanced in real time so that generation matches demand plus losses, with system frequency held close to 50 Hz. The grid is connected to the networks of France, the Netherlands, Ireland, Belgium, Norway, and Denmark by high-voltage direct-current interconnectors. Grid-scale control for Great Britain is exercised by the electricity system operator.
