Amino acid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Amino acids are organic compounds that serve as the building blocks of proteins. Each has an amino group, a carboxyl group and a distinctive side chain (R group) that determines its chemistry. Living things build proteins from a standard set of 20 amino acids (21–22 counting selenocysteine and pyrrolysine), specified by the genetic code in DNA and assembled by ribosomes in sequence dictated by messenger RNA.

Humans can synthesise 11 of the 20; the other nine are 'essential' — they must come from dietary protein, which is why protein quality (amino-acid completeness) matters in Nutrition. Beyond proteins, amino acids serve as neurotransmitters (glutamate, GABA), hormone precursors and metabolic intermediates.

Properties

Side chains classify amino acids as nonpolar, polar, acidic or basic; the sequence of them folds a protein into its shape — the central dogma-to-structure chain that modern AI (AlphaFold) learned to predict. A single amino-acid swap can transform function, as in haemoglobin S.

Origins

Amino acids form abiotically — the 1953 Miller–Urey experiment produced them from simple gases and sparks — and are found in meteorites, supporting the idea that life's ingredients are cosmically common.
