Glacial Lake Missoula was a prehistoric proglacial lake in western Montana that existed periodically during the last ice age, between about 18,000 and 13,000 years ago. The lake formed when a lobe of the Cordilleran Ice Sheet dammed the Clark Fork River near the Idaho panhandle. Its repeated catastrophic draining produced the Missoula Floods, which scoured the landscape of the Pacific Northwest.

Formation

The Purcell lobe of the ice sheet advanced south across the Clark Fork valley, creating an ice dam up to 610 m (2,000 ft) high. Behind it, water ponded into a lake roughly the size of Lake Erie and Lake Ontario combined, covering some 7,770 km2 (3,000 sq mi) of western Montana. Ancient shorelines are still visible as horizontal terraces on hillsides around Missoula.

The floods

When the rising water floated or undermined the ice dam, the lake drained catastrophically — in some cases within days — releasing flows estimated to be many times the combined flow of all modern rivers. Geologist J Harlen Bretz identified the resulting features, including the Channeled Scablands of eastern Washington, giant current ripples, and erratics, in the 1920s; his flood theory was controversial for decades before being accepted. Geologists now believe the lake filled and emptied dozens of times.
