The House of Representatives is the lower house of the bicameral Parliament of Australia, the upper house being the Senate. Its composition and powers are established in Chapter I of the Constitution of Australia.

The term of members of the House is a maximum of three years from the first sitting of the House, but on many occasions the House is dissolved earlier. The House is almost always dissolved by the Governor-General on the advice of the Prime Minister, even when not at the end of its term. Elections for members of the House have always been held in conjunction with elections for the Senate since the 1970s, and double dissolutions — in which both houses are dissolved simultaneously — have occurred several times.

Origin and role

The House follows many of the conventions of the Westminster system. The party, or coalition of parties, that enjoys the support of the majority of members forms the government, and its leader becomes Prime Minister. Members are popularly known as MPs (Members of Parliament), and each represents a single electoral division of roughly equal population, in accordance with the constitutional "nexus" provision linking the size of the House to that of the Senate.

Composition

Since the 2022 federal election the House has had 151 members, each elected from a single-member electoral division using full preferential instant-runoff voting. The allocation of seats among the states and territories is determined by population, with each original state guaranteed a minimum of five seats.

Functions

Most legislation is initiated in the House of Representatives, and money bills must originate there. The House scrutinises the executive through question time, debates, and its committee system. A government must maintain the confidence of the House to remain in office; loss of supply or an adverse vote on a motion of no confidence can precipitate an election or a change of government, as occurred during the constitutional crisis of 1975.
