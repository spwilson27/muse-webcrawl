The cuisine of Hawaii is a fusion of many cuisines brought to the Hawaiian Islands by immigrants and settlers from Polynesia, East and Southeast Asia, Europe, and the Americas. In the history of Hawaii, the successive waves of immigration are reflected in its food: the original Polynesian voyagers brought taro, coconut, breadfruit, sweet potato, and pigs; later arrivals added rice, soy sauce, tropical fruits, and European and American ingredients, producing both a traditional Native Hawaiian cuisine and a distinct modern "local food" culture.

Traditional cuisine

Traditional staples include poi (pounded taro root paste), kālua pig cooked in an underground earth oven (imu), laulau (pork and fish wrapped in taro leaves), lomi-lomi salmon (in fact introduced from the Pacific Northwest trade), pipikaula (dried beef), and seafood such as opihi (limpets) and raw fish. Feasts called ʻahaʻaina, later lūʻau, marked celebrations, with strict kapu rules separating men's and women's eating until the system was abolished in 1819.

Modern local food and regional cuisine

Plantation-era immigration for the sugar and pineapple industries produced the plate lunch — two scoops of rice, macaroni salad, and an entrée such as chicken katsu, teriyaki beef, or mahi mahi — and items like saimin noodle soup, Spam musubi, loco moco (rice, hamburger patty, fried egg, and gravy), and poke (seasoned raw fish), which has spread worldwide. In 1991 a group of twelve chefs, including Sam Choy, Roy Yamaguchi, and Alan Wong, founded Hawaii Regional Cuisine, promoting locally grown produce and a refined Pacific Rim fusion style.
