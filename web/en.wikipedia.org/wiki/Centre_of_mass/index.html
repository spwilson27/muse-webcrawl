Centre of mass

In physics, the centre of mass of a distribution of mass in space is the unique point at which the weighted relative position of the distributed mass sums to zero. It is the point where a force may be applied to cause linear acceleration without angular acceleration; the object's motion can be described as the translation of this point plus rotation about it.

Definition

For a system of particles with masses mi at positions ri, the centre of mass R is the mass-weighted average of the positions: R = (Σ mi ri) / (Σ mi). For a continuous body the sum becomes an integral over the volume weighted by density.

Barycentre

For two orbiting bodies, the centre of mass of the system is called the barycentre. Both bodies orbit around it; for the Earth–Moon system it lies about 4,670 km from Earth's centre, beneath the surface.

Applications

The concept is used throughout mechanics and engineering: stability of vehicles and structures, the design of aircraft (where the centre of mass must lie within certified limits relative to the centre of pressure), sports technique, and animation. In a uniform gravitational field the centre of mass coincides with the centre of gravity.
