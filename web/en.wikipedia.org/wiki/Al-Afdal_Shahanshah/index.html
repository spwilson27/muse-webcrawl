Al-Afdal Shahanshah

Al-Afdal ruled Fatimid Egypt in all but name for over twenty-five years.

Al-Afdal Shahanshah (Arabic: الأفضل شاهنشاه, al-Afḍal Shāhanshāh; 1066 – 11 December 1121), born Abu al-Qasim Shahanshah ibn Badr al-Jamali, was an Armenian-born vizier of the Fatimid caliphs of Egypt from 1094 until his assassination in 1121. One of the most powerful statesmen of the Crusader era, he effectively ruled the Fatimid state, restored its administration and armies, and directed Egypt's long struggle against the Crusader states of the Levant.

Rise to power

Al-Afdal's father, Badr al-Jamali, an Armenian former slave-soldier, had been summoned in 1073 to rescue the Fatimid state from military anarchy and famine, ruling as vizier with near-dictatorial powers. On Badr's death in 1094, al-Afdal succeeded him smoothly — an unprecedented hereditary succession — and when Caliph al-Mustansir died months later, al-Afdal installed the youngest son, al-Musta'li, as caliph, provoking a schism with supporters of the elder son Nizar that split Isma'ili Shi'ism into Musta'li and Nizari branches permanently.

Rule and the Crusades

Al-Afdal reformed taxation, restored prosperity and irrigation in Egypt, and rebuilt the Fatimid army. When the First Crusade arrived in Syria in 1097, he initially treated the Franks as useful counterweights to the Seljuk Turks, attempting diplomacy with them; after the Crusaders stormed Jerusalem — which al-Afdal's forces had just wrested from the Artuqids — in July 1099, he marched north and was defeated at the Battle of Ascalon. Thereafter he launched near-annual campaigns against the Kingdom of Jerusalem, winning at Ramla in 1101 and 1102 but losing others, while Fatimid land power slowly ceded the Palestinian coast to the Crusaders, though Ascalon itself held out until 1153.

Death and legacy

Al-Afdal was assassinated in Cairo in December 1121, during the Eid al-Adha festivities — by conspirators whose instigator was blamed variously on the young caliph al-Amir, whom he had kept a cipher, or on Nizari agents. His vast treasure was seized by the caliph. His son Abu Ali Ahmad, known as Kutayfat, briefly seized the vizierate a decade later during the succession crisis after al-Amir's murder.
