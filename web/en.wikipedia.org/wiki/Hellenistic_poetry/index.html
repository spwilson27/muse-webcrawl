Hellenistic poetry

Hellenistic poetry is the poetry written in Greek during the Hellenistic period, roughly from the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BC to the Roman conquest of the Greek world. Centred on the great library and Mouseion of Alexandria in Egypt, it is characterised by learning, refinement, and self-conscious artistry: poets were often scholar-poets who edited the classics by day and wrote allusive, polished verse for an educated court audience.

Characteristics

Hellenistic poets reacted against the grand scale of classical epic. Callimachus, the most influential figure, championed the short, exquisitely wrought poem — "a big book is a big evil" — and produced hymns, epigrams, the learned catalogue poem Aetia, and the miniature epic Hecale. Apollonius of Rhodes, by contrast, wrote the Argonautica, a full-scale epic about Jason and the Argonauts, noted for its psychological portrayal of Medea. Theocritus invented the pastoral idyll, depicting shepherds and country life in Sicily and Alexandria; his poems became the model for Virgil's Eclogues and for the whole European pastoral tradition.

Genres and influence

The period flourished in epigram (collected later in the Greek Anthology), didactic poetry such as Aratus's astronomical Phaenomena and Nicander's works on poisons, and mime and bucolic verse. Hellenistic poetry profoundly influenced Roman poets — Catullus, Virgil, Horace, Propertius, and Ovid all worked in dialogue with Callimachus, Theocritus, and Apollonius.
