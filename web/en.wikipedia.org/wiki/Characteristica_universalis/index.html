Characteristica universalis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The characteristica universalis (Latin: "universal characteristic") is a concept developed by the German philosopher and polymath Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. It is a hypothetical universal language or formal system in which every concept could be represented by characters, and every truth could be established by calculation. Combined with a calculus ratiocinator — a formal method of reasoning — disputes could, in Leibniz's famous phrase, be resolved by saying Calculemus: "Let us calculate."

Leibniz's project

Leibniz began thinking about a universal characteristic as a young man, in his De Arte Combinatoria (1666), and returned to it throughout his life. The system was to rest on an "alphabet of human thought": primitive, indefinable concepts from which all others could be composed. Each primitive would receive a character or number, and complex ideas would be formed by combining them, so that the correctness of reasoning could be checked mechanically, much as arithmetic can be checked.

Relation to later work

Although Leibniz never completed the project — the prerequisites, an encyclopaedia of all knowledge analysed into primitives, were beyond one lifetime — the idea anticipated important developments in modern logic. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century thinkers such as George Boole, Gottlob Frege and Bertrand Russell developed formal languages in which propositions could be manipulated symbolically. Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) explicitly acknowledged Leibniz's project as a forerunner. The characteristica universalis is also frequently cited as a precursor of computing and of universal formal languages in philosophy of language.
