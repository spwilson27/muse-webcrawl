Turbo code

In information theory, turbo codes are a class of high-performance forward error correction (FEC) codes that were the first practical codes to approach the theoretical maximum channel capacity, the Shannon limit. They were introduced by Claude Berrou, Alain Glavieux and Punya Thitimajshima of Télécom Bretagne in 1993 and revolutionised coding theory, earning the nickname from the feedback loop in their decoder, analogous to a turbocharger.

Construction and decoding

A turbo code is formed by combining two (or more) relatively simple recursive systematic convolutional encoders in parallel, separated by an interleaver that shuffles the input bits so the two encoders produce different parity sequences for the same message. At the receiver, two soft-in/soft-out decoders work iteratively, exchanging probabilistic ("extrinsic") information about each bit through the interleaver and de-interleaver — the "turbo" feedback — and converging on a highly reliable decision after typically 6–18 iterations. The random-like interleaver gives the code its exceptional performance at low signal-to-noise ratios.

Applications

Because they come within a fraction of a decibel of the Shannon limit, turbo codes were adopted in deep-space communications and in the third-generation (3G UMTS) and fourth-generation (LTE) mobile phone standards, where they carried much of the world's mobile data traffic. In 5G standards they were largely superseded in the data channel by LDPC codes, which offer lower decoding latency, while polar codes took over control channels — the three code families together representing the modern culmination of error-correction research stimulated by the turbo breakthrough.
