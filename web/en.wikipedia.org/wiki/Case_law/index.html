Case law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Case law, also known as precedent or common law in the relevant sense, is the body of law created by judges and similar quasi-judicial tribunals through written opinions deciding individual cases. In common-law systems, the decisions of appellate courts bind future courts under Stare decisis, so the law develops incrementally through analogies and distinctions drawn between cases, alongside statutes which prevail in case of conflict.

A judgment typically separates the ratio decidendi — the legal reasoning necessary to the decision, which binds — from obiter dicta, remarks by the way, which are persuasive only. Law reports collect significant decisions; historically the Year Books and nominate reports, today official and commercial series and databases. Case law fills the interstices of codes and constitutions, supplies whole fields (much of tort and contract), and is the medium in which constitutional doctrines such as judicial review were articulated.
