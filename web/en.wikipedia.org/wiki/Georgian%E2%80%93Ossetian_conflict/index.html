Georgian–Ossetian conflict

Date1989 – present (phases of armed conflict)
LocationSouth Ossetia, Georgia
StatusFrozen conflict; South Ossetia recognised by Russia and a few other states

The Georgian–Ossetian conflict is an ethno-political conflict over Georgia's former autonomous region of South Ossetia, which evolved in 1989 and developed into the 1991–92 South Ossetia War. Despite a declared ceasefire and numerous peace efforts, the dispute remained unresolved, and in August 2008 diplomatic tensions and clashes between Georgian and Ossetian forces escalated into the Russo-Georgian War.

Background

South Ossetia, an autonomous oblast within the Georgian Soviet Socialist Republic, was home to both ethnic Georgians and Ossetians. As the Soviet Union weakened in the late 1980s, rising Georgian nationalism and Ossetian fears of assimilation set the two communities on a collision course. In November 1989 the South Ossetian regional council voted to upgrade the region's status to an autonomous republic, a move Tbilisi declared unconstitutional.

1991–92 war

Georgia declared independence in April 1991 under President Zviad Gamsakhurdia, whose government abolished South Ossetia's autonomy. Fighting between Georgian forces and Ossetian militias, supported by former Soviet units, continued until June 1992, when the Sochi agreement established a ceasefire and a tripartite peacekeeping force of Georgian, Russian and Ossetian battalions.

2004 and 2008 escalations

President Mikheil Saakashvili, elected in 2004, vowed to restore Georgia's territorial integrity. After a brief flare-up in 2004, tensions rose through the summer of 2008; on the night of 7–8 August Georgia launched an operation against Tskhinvali, prompting a large-scale Russian military intervention. After five days of fighting, Russia recognised South Ossetia (and Abkhazia) as independent states, a step condemned by most of the international community.

Aftermath

Since 2008 Russian forces have remained stationed in the region, and the administrative boundary line has been progressively fenced in a process Georgians call "borderisation". Geneva International Discussions, co-chaired by the EU, OSCE and UN, remain the main negotiation format.
