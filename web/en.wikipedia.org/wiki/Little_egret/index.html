Little egret

Little egret (Egretta garzetta) foraging in shallow water

The little egret (Egretta garzetta) is a species of small heron in the family Ardeidae. It is a white bird with a slender black beak, long black legs, and — in the western population — yellow feet. In breeding plumage it develops two long nape plumes and filamentous feathers (aigrettes) on its back, for which it was once heavily hunted.

Description

The little egret is 55–65 cm long with a wingspan of 88–106 cm and weighs 350–550 g. Its plumage is entirely white. Breeding adults have two long, pointed nape plumes and lacy aigrettes on the back and breast. Outside the breeding season the plumes are absent and the bare skin between the eyes and bill (the lores) becomes greyish or greenish-yellow. The subspecies E. g. garzetta (Europe, Africa, Asia) has black legs with yellow feet; the Australasian E. g. nigripes has yellowish feet, and the eastern reef-associated dimorphic forms are sometimes split as separate species.

Distribution and habitat

The species breeds in wetlands across temperate Europe, Africa, Asia, and Australia, favouring marshes, lagoons, rice fields, riverbanks, and mangroves. Northern populations are migratory. Once rare in northern Europe because of plume hunting and later pesticide-era declines, the little egret has expanded markedly since the late twentieth century: it first bred in Britain in 1996 and is now a familiar sight on estuaries and gravel pits. It has also colonised the Caribbean and reached the Americas as a vagrant.

Behaviour

Little egrets feed in shallow water on fish, frogs, crustaceans, insects, and small mammals. They hunt actively — walking, running, and stirring the water with one foot to flush prey ("foot-stirring") — or stand and wait. They breed colonially, often with other herons and egrets, building stick nests in trees or reed beds and laying three to five pale blue-green eggs. The species is generally silent away from colonies.

Conservation

The little egret is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN. In the nineteenth century its aigrettes were so prized in the millinery trade that the species was nearly exterminated in parts of its range; the campaign against the plume trade contributed to the founding of bird-protection societies such as the RSPB.
