The Yellowstone Caldera, sometimes referred to as the Yellowstone supervolcano, is a volcanic caldera and supervolcano in Yellowstone National Park in the Western United States. The caldera and most of the park are located in the northwest corner of Wyoming. The caldera measures about 72 by 55 kilometres (45 by 34 miles) and was formed during the last of three supereruptions over the past 2.1 million years.

Geology

Yellowstone lies above a hotspot, where a plume of hot material rises from deep within the Earth and melts through the crust. As the North American Plate has moved southwest over the relatively stationary hotspot, a track of caldera-forming eruptions has been left across the Snake River Plain of Idaho towards the present location of Yellowstone.

The three caldera-forming supereruptions occurred 2.1 million, 1.3 million, and 640,000 years ago, ejecting roughly 1,000 to 2,450 cubic kilometres of material in the largest events — enough to class them among the largest known eruptions on Earth, rated VEI 8. The most recent caldera was partly filled by later rhyolitic lava flows, the last of which erupted about 70,000 years ago.

Magma system and hydrothermal features

Seismic imaging has revealed a large magma reservoir beneath the caldera, only a fraction of which is molten, together with a deeper, larger reservoir of partially molten material. The heat from this system drives Yellowstone's famous geothermal features — including Old Faithful and the Grand Prismatic Spring — which together comprise about half of the world's geysers and hydrothermal features.

The caldera floor undergoes continual deformation: episodes of uplift and subsidence of several centimetres per year have been measured over decades, driven by magmatic and hydrothermal processes. Small earthquakes are common, with 1,500 to 2,500 locatable events in a typical year.

Hazards and monitoring

A future caldera-forming eruption would have severe regional and global consequences, but scientists of the U.S. Geological Survey consider such an event unlikely in the foreseeable future; the annual probability has been estimated at roughly 0.00014 percent (1 in 730,000). More probable hazards include hydrothermal explosions, large earthquakes, and lava flows. The Yellowstone Volcano Observatory monitors the system continuously with seismometers, GPS stations, and satellite radar interferometry.
