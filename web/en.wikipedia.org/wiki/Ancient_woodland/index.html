Ancient woodland

Ancient woodland is a term used in the United Kingdom for woodland that has existed continuously since 1600 or earlier. In England and Wales, the date 1600 was chosen by the historian and ecologist Oliver Rackham because deliberate tree planting was rare before that time, so a wood present on maps of that era is likely to have arisen naturally. In Scotland the threshold date is 1750, corresponding to the earliest reliable survey maps. Ancient woodland is regarded as irreplaceable habitat and one of the richest terrestrial environments for wildlife in the UK.

Categories

  • Ancient semi-natural woodland (ASNW) — composed predominantly of native trees and shrubs that have not been planted, often managed historically by coppicing.
  • Plantations on ancient woodland sites (PAWS) — sites where the original woodland was felled and replanted, often with non-native conifers, mainly in the twentieth century. The soil, seed bank, and some species may survive, and restoration by gradual removal of the plantation trees is a conservation priority.

Ecology

Because ancient woods have developed over centuries, they support species that colonise new ground only very slowly — "ancient woodland indicator species" such as bluebell, wood anemone, wild service tree, herb-paris, and many lichens, fungi, and invertebrates. Their soils are undisturbed by ploughing and retain characteristic profiles, archaeological features such as wood banks and saw pits, and rich fungal networks. Ancient woods cover only about 2.5 percent of the United Kingdom's land area.

Protection

English planning policy treats ancient woodland as an irreplaceable habitat whose loss cannot be compensated, and development resulting in its loss or deterioration should be refused except in wholly exceptional circumstances. Even so, ancient woods face threats from development, deer overgrazing, invasive species such as rhododendron, and diseases including ash dieback.
