Tolbooth

A tolbooth or tollbooth is a municipal building found in Scottish burghs from the medieval period onward. Originally a place where tolls and customs were collected and market dues paid, the tolbooth developed into the principal civic building of the burgh, housing the council chamber, court house, and prison. The word derives from the Middle English tolbothe, a booth where tolls were taken.

The tolbooth at Kirkcudbright, built in the 17th century

Functions

Tolbooths typically stood on the burgh's main street or market place. Their ground floors often contained prison cells ("the booth"), while upper floors held the council and court rooms. Many featured a steeple with a clock and a bell used to summon the council, announce executions, and mark market hours. Public punishment and the display of offenders — at the mercat cross or the tolbooth door — were associated with the building.

Notable examples

Surviving tolbooths include those at Kirkcudbright, Crail, Pittenweem, Stonehaven, and Musselburgh. Edinburgh's Old Tolbooth, demolished in 1817, stood on the High Street beside St Giles' Cathedral and served as the seat of the Scottish Parliament and town council as well as the city's main prison; its site is marked by the Heart of Midlothian mosaic. The Canongate Tolbooth in Edinburgh, built in 1591, now houses The People's Story Museum. See also burgh and mercat cross.

Decline

As civic administration professionalized in the 19th century, tolbooths lost their functions to purpose-built town halls, police stations, and courthouses. Many were demolished; others survive as museums, arts centres, or local landmarks.
