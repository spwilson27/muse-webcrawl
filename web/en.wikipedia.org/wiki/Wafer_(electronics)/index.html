Wafer (electronics)

Material: Monocrystalline silicon (most common)

Standard diameters: 150 mm, 200 mm, 300 mm

Use: Substrate for integrated circuits and solar cells

In electronics, a wafer (also called a slice or substrate) is a thin slice of semiconductor material, most commonly crystalline silicon, used as the substrate on which integrated circuits are fabricated. Photovoltaic wafers of silicon are likewise the basis of most solar cells. A wafer serves as the foundation for microelectronic devices built in and on it through processes such as doping, ion implantation, etching, thin-film deposition and photolithography.

Production

Wafers are sliced from large cylindrical single-crystal ingots grown by the Czochralski process, in which a seed crystal is slowly withdrawn from molten silicon while rotating, pulling a near-perfect crystal with it. The ingot is ground to the target diameter, sliced with diamond saws, then lapped, etched and polished to a mirror finish. A flat or notch ground into the wafer's edge indicates its crystal orientation and doping type.

Sizes

Wafer diameters have grown steadily to increase the number of dies per wafer and reduce cost per chip: from 25 mm in the 1960s through 100 mm, 150 mm and 200 mm, to today's mainstream 300 mm (12-inch) wafers; a transition to 450 mm has been researched but repeatedly deferred. Standard silicon wafer thickness for 300 mm wafers is about 775 micrometres.

Other materials

Compound semiconductors such as gallium arsenide and indium phosphide are used for high-frequency and optoelectronic devices, while silicon carbide and gallium nitride wafers serve power electronics. Sapphire and silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafers are used in specialised applications. After fabrication, the wafer is diced into individual dies, which are then packaged as chips.
