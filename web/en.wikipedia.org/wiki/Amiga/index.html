The Amiga is a family of personal computers introduced by Commodore in 1985. The original model was part of a wave of 16/32-bit computers that emerged in the mid-1980s, but the Amiga was distinguished by custom coprocessor chips (including Agnus, Denise and Paula) that offloaded graphics, animation and sound from the CPU, giving it multimedia capabilities far ahead of contemporary rivals such as the IBM PC and Apple Macintosh.

History

The machine was designed by a team led by Jay Miner at the Amiga Corporation, originally founded to build a games console, and acquired by Commodore in 1984. The Amiga 1000 launched in July 1985; the cheaper, integrated Amiga 500 and the expandable Amiga 2000 followed in 1987, and the A500 became a huge seller in Europe, especially for games. Later models such as the Amiga 1200 (1992) introduced the AGA graphics chipset. Commodore's bankruptcy in April 1994 ended production, though the platform retained a devoted following and successor systems and AmigaOS derivatives continue in niche form.

Legacy

The Amiga is remembered as a pioneering multimedia and video-production machine — NewTek's Video Toaster made it a staple of low-budget television graphics — and as the centre of a vibrant demoscene. Landmark games such as Lemmings, Sensible Soccer and Worms originated on the platform.
