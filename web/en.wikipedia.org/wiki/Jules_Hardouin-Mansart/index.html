Jules Hardouin-Mansart (16 April 1646 – 11 May 1708) was a French Baroque architect and builder whose major works included the Place des Victoires (1684–1690), the Place Vendôme (1690), the domed chapel of Les Invalides (1690–1706), and the Palace of Versailles. Among the most important architects of the reign of Louis XIV, he helped shape the classical French Baroque style that expressed the power and glory of the French monarchy.

Biography

Born Jules Hardouin in Paris, he studied under his great-uncle François Mansart, one of the originators of French classical architecture, from whom he inherited plans and drawings as well as the name Mansart. He also learned from Libéral Bruant, architect of the Hôtel des Invalides. Hardouin-Mansart first gained favour working for the king's brother before coming to the attention of Louis XIV through his design of the Château de Clagny.

In 1678 he became responsible for the enlargement of the Palace of Versailles, the project that dominated the rest of his career. At Versailles he built the Hall of Mirrors (Galerie des Glaces, with Charles Le Brun), the Grand Trianon, the palace chapel, and the great orangery, and enlarged the palace wings. He was appointed the king's chief architect (premier architecte du Roi) in 1681 and superintendent of royal buildings in 1699, and was raised to the nobility as comte de Sagonne in 1702.

Major works

  • Château de Clagny, Versailles (1674–1680, destroyed)
  • Palace of Versailles: Hall of Mirrors, Grand Trianon, Royal Chapel, North and South Wings
  • Church of Saint-Louis des Invalides with its great dome, Paris
  • Place des Victoires and Place Vendôme, Paris
  • Château de Marly (largely destroyed)
  • Château de Meudon (new château)

Style and legacy

Hardouin-Mansart's work combined the disciplined classicism of his great-uncle with a grander scale suited to royal propaganda. His dome of Les Invalides, with its nested interior shells, became one of the landmarks of Paris. The mansard roof, popularised by his great-uncle François, bears the family name. Hardouin-Mansart ran a large and efficient office that trained many architects of the next generation, including Robert de Cotte, his brother-in-law and successor.
