Moral psychology

Moral psychology is the study of human thought and behaviour in ethical contexts — how people make moral judgments, how those judgments motivate action, and how morality develops. It spans psychology, philosophy, and cognitive science.

Major theories

Lawrence Kohlberg proposed a stage theory of moral development, from obedience-based reasoning in childhood to principled conscience, based on responses to dilemmas such as the Heinz dilemma. Carol Gilligan criticised Kohlberg's justice-centred model and proposed an "ethic of care." Jonathan Haidt's social intuitionist model (2001) argues that moral judgments are typically fast, intuitive, and emotional — like a rider (reasoning) on an elephant (intuition), with reasoning often serving as post-hoc justification — and his moral foundations theory proposes evolved foundations such as care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and sanctity. Joshua Greene's dual-process theory links characteristically deontological judgments to emotion and consequentialist judgments to cognitive control, supported by trolley-problem neuroimaging studies.

Topics

The field also studies moral emotions (guilt, shame, empathy, disgust, moral elevation), altruism and cooperation, moral licensing, moral disengagement, psychopathy and moral deficits, and cross-cultural variation in moral norms.
