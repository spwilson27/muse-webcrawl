Traction battery

A traction battery is a rechargeable battery designed to supply power to the electric motors of a battery electric vehicle, hybrid electric vehicle, or other electrically propelled machinery such as forklifts, locomotives, and boats. Unlike starter batteries, which deliver short bursts of high current, traction batteries are designed for deep discharge over sustained periods and for many charge–discharge cycles.

An electric-car traction battery pack integrated into the vehicle floor.

Chemistries

Early electric vehicles used lead–acid traction batteries, which remain common in forklifts and golf carts because of their low cost and weight tolerance. Nickel–metal hydride batteries powered the first generations of hybrid vehicles. Modern battery electric cars overwhelmingly use lithium-ion chemistries — principally nickel manganese cobalt oxide (NMC), nickel cobalt aluminium oxide (NCA), and lithium iron phosphate (LFP), the last being cheaper, longer-lived, and thermally stable at the cost of lower energy density.

Design

A traction battery pack consists of many individual cells grouped into modules and a pack, together with a battery management system (BMS) that monitors cell voltages and temperatures, cooling (and sometimes heating) systems, contactors, and safety devices. Pack capacities in passenger cars range from about 20 kWh in small city cars to over 100 kWh in large vehicles, with voltages typically between 300 and 800 volts; higher-voltage architectures allow faster DC charging.

Performance and degradation

Key properties include energy density (which determines range), power density, cycle life, cost per kilowatt-hour, and safety. Batteries degrade through calendar ageing and cycling; manufacturers commonly warrant automotive packs to retain at least 70% of their original capacity for eight years. Degraded packs may find second-life use in stationary storage before being recycled to recover lithium, nickel, cobalt, and other materials.

Cost trends

Average lithium-ion pack prices fell from over $1,000 per kWh in 2010 to well under $150 per kWh in the early 2020s, a decline widely credited with making mass-market electric vehicles feasible.
