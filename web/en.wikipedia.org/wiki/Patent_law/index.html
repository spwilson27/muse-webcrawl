Patent law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Patent law is the branch of Intellectual property law that grants inventors exclusive rights over inventions for a limited time (generally 20 years from filing, per TRIPS Agreement) in exchange for public disclosure of how the invention works. A patentable invention must be novel (see Prior art), involve an inventive step (non-obviousness) and be capable of industrial application (useful), within eligible subject matter — abstractions, natural phenomena and (in Europe) computer programs “as such” being excluded or contested categories.

Law developed from the Venetian statute of 1474 and the English Statute of Monopolies 1624 through the US constitutional clause (1787, “to promote the Progress of Science and useful Arts”) to the harmonised modern system (Paris Convention 1883; Patent Cooperation Treaty 1970; TRIPS 1994). Grant requires examination (in most systems) of claims that define the monopoly; infringement is remedied by injunctions and damages, validity is litigated in parallel (and, since 2011, in US post-grant review). Recurring controversies: patent quality and “trolls” (non-practising entities), evergreening of pharmaceuticals, standard-essential patents and FRAND licensing, software and business-method eligibility (Alice, 2014), and the COVID-era waiver debates.
