Galaxy morphological classification is a system used by astronomers to divide galaxies into groups based on their visual appearance. There are several schemes in use by which galaxies can be classified according to their morphologies, the most famous being the Hubble sequence, devised by Edwin Hubble and later expanded by Gérard de Vaucouleurs and Allan Sandage.

Hubble sequence

The Hubble sequence is a morphological classification scheme invented by Edwin Hubble in 1926. It is often represented in the form of a "tuning fork" diagram. Elliptical galaxies lie on the handle, graded E0 (nearly spherical) to E7 (highly elongated) by apparent ellipticity. Lenticular (S0) galaxies form the junction, from which two arms branch: normal spirals (S) and barred spirals (SB), each graded a, b, c by the tightness of the spiral arms and the relative size of the central bulge. Irregular galaxies, lacking a regular shape, fall outside the scheme.

Despite the suggestive "early" and "late" terminology, the sequence is not an evolutionary sequence; Hubble himself emphasised this late in his career.

De Vaucouleurs system

The de Vaucouleurs system, introduced in 1959, extends Hubble's scheme with detailed stages ( Roman numerals and lowercase letters), family (SA, SAB, SB) describing the bar strength, and variety (r, s) describing whether inner rings or pure spiral structure dominate, as well as outer rings and pseudo-rings. It allows classification of features such as rings and lenses.

Yerkes scheme

The Yerkes (or Morgan) scheme classifies galaxies by the spectra of their stars and their concentration toward the centre, using spectral types a through k and inclination numbers 1 to 7.
