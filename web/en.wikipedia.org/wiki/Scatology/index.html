Scatology

In medicine and biology, scatology or coprology is the study of faeces (scats). Analysis of faecal samples can yield information about an organism's diet, digestive health, parasites, and — in wildlife ecology — the presence, diet, genetics, and movements of elusive animal species. In a broader cultural sense, the term also denotes an interest in excrement or obscene material, notably in literature and humour.

Scientific uses

Scatological studies are a non-invasive tool in ecology: researchers collect droppings of animals such as wolves, snow leopards, and bats to extract DNA, identify prey remains, and estimate population size and range without capturing the animals. In palaeontology, fossilised faeces are called coprolites and can reveal the diets of extinct species. Clinically, stool examination aids diagnosis of infections, malabsorption, and colorectal disease.

In culture

Scatological humour is among the oldest forms of comedy, appearing in Aristophanes and in the works of Chaucer, Rabelais, and Swift, whose "scatological poems" mocked conventions of Augustan verse. Writers such as James Joyce and Samuel Beckett used excremental imagery seriously, and psychoanalytic criticism has devoted considerable attention to the theme.
