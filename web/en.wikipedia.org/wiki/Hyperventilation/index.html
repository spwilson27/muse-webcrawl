Hyperventilation

Hyperventilation is breathing in excess of the body's metabolic needs.

Hyperventilation is irregular breathing that occurs when the rate or tidal volume of breathing eliminates more carbon dioxide than the body can produce. This leads to hypocapnia, a reduced concentration of carbon dioxide dissolved in the blood. The body normally attempts to compensate for this homeostatically, but if this fails or is overridden, the blood pH will rise, leading to respiratory alkalosis.

Hyperventilation can occur during anxiety attacks, intense exercise, or illness, and it may also be deliberately induced, for example before free diving. It is distinct from hyperpnea, which is an appropriately increased rate of breathing in response to increased metabolic demand.

Symptoms

Symptoms of hyperventilation include light-headedness, dizziness, numbness or tingling in the hands, feet and lips (paresthesia), rapid heartbeat, chest pain, shortness of breath, headache, and in severe cases loss of consciousness. The reduction in carbon dioxide causes cerebral vasoconstriction, reducing blood flow to the brain and contributing to the neurological symptoms.

Causes

Common causes include anxiety and panic attacks, asthma attacks, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), pneumonia, heart failure, metabolic acidosis (as a compensatory mechanism), high altitude, fever, pain, and certain medications. Hyperventilation syndrome is a pattern of breathing in which a person breathes more than the body requires, often associated with anxiety disorders.

Mechanism

During hyperventilation, excess carbon dioxide is exhaled. Because carbon dioxide in the blood exists in equilibrium with carbonic acid and bicarbonate, removing CO2 shifts the equilibrium and raises blood pH. Ionised calcium levels also fall, increasing nerve excitability and producing tingling sensations and, occasionally, carpopedal spasm.

Treatment

Treatment addresses the underlying cause. During an acute anxiety-related episode, reassurance and slow, controlled breathing may help. The traditional advice of breathing into a paper bag has fallen out of favour because it can cause dangerous hypoxia if the diagnosis is wrong. Persistent or unexplained hyperventilation warrants medical evaluation to exclude organic causes such as pulmonary embolism.
