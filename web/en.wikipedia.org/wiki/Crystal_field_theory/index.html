Crystal field theory

Crystal field theory (CFT) describes the breaking of degeneracies of electron orbital states, usually d or f orbitals, due to a static electric field produced by a surrounding charge distribution (anion neighbours). The theory was developed by physicists Hans Bethe and John Hasbrouck van Vleck in the 1930s and later combined with molecular orbital theory to form the more complete ligand field theory.

Overview

In a free transition-metal ion, the five d orbitals are degenerate — they have the same energy. When ligands approach the metal centre, the negatively charged ligand electrons repel the metal's d electrons. Orbitals pointing directly at the ligands (in an octahedral complex, the dz² and dx²−y² orbitals, the eg set) are raised in energy, while the orbitals pointing between ligands (dxy, dxz, dyz, the t2g set) are lowered. The energy difference between the two sets is denoted Δo (or 10 Dq).

Spectrochemical series and spin states

The magnitude of the splitting depends on the ligands. Weak-field ligands such as I−, Br− and Cl− produce a small Δo, while strong-field ligands such as CN− and CO produce a large Δo; the ordering is known as the spectrochemical series. If Δo is smaller than the electron-pairing energy, electrons occupy the higher orbitals singly, giving a high-spin complex; if Δo is larger, electrons pair up in the lower set, giving a low-spin complex. The difference in energy between configurations, the crystal field stabilization energy (CFSE), helps explain the colours, magnetism, and thermodynamic stability of coordination compounds.

Colour and limitations

Absorption of visible light promotes electrons between the split d levels (d–d transitions); the observed colour is complementary to the colour absorbed, which explains, for example, the deep blue of [Cu(NH3)4]2+. Pure crystal field theory treats ligands as point charges and cannot account for covalent bonding or the nephelauxetic effect; ligand field theory and molecular orbital theory address these shortcomings.
