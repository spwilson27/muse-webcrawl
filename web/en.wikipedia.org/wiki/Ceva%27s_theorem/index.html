Ceva's theorem

Three cevians of a triangle meeting at a single point.

Ceva's theorem is a theorem about triangles in plane geometry, published by the Italian mathematician Giovanni Ceva in his 1678 work De lineis rectis. It gives a necessary and sufficient condition for three cevians of a triangle — line segments from each vertex to the opposite side — to be concurrent, that is, to pass through a single common point.

Statement

Let ABC be a triangle, and let D, E and F be points on the sides BC, CA and AB respectively. The three lines AD, BE and CF are concurrent if and only if

(BD / DC) · (CE / EA) · (AF / FB) = 1

with signed lengths used when a point falls on the extension of a side. The theorem reverses as well: if the product condition holds for points on the three sides, the cevians meet at one point.

Proof and applications

The standard proof compares areas: for concurrent cevians, the ratio BD/DC equals the ratio of the areas of triangles ABD and ACD, and also of PBD and PCD where P is the concurrency point; subtracting and multiplying the three analogous ratios gives 1. The converse follows by contradiction, re-drawing one cevian through the intersection of the other two.

Ceva's theorem provides one-line proofs that the medians, angle bisectors and altitudes of a triangle are each concurrent, and it is a basic tool in olympiad and advanced Euclidean geometry. A trigonometric form states that the cevians are concurrent if and only if the product of the sines of the divided angles at the three vertices is 1. The theorem is closely related to Menelaus's theorem, which gives the analogous condition for three points on the sides to be collinear.
