Hearsay in United States law

Hearsay (US law)
Governing rulesFederal Rules of Evidence, Rules 801–807
DefinitionOut-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted (Rule 801(c))
General ruleInadmissible unless an exception or exclusion applies (Rule 802)

Hearsay objections are a staple of American trial practice.

Hearsay, in United States law, is an out-of-court statement offered in evidence to prove the truth of the matter asserted. Under Rule 802 of the Federal Rules of Evidence, hearsay is generally inadmissible, reflecting concerns that such statements cannot be tested by cross-examination of the declarant under oath before the trier of fact.

Definitions and exclusions

Rule 801 defines hearsay and carves out statements that are not treated as hearsay at all, notably a declarant-witness's prior inconsistent or consistent statements given under oath, and statements offered against an opposing party (often called admissions by a party-opponent), including those of agents, co-conspirators, and adoptive admissions. Statements offered for a purpose other than their truth — such as to show notice, motive, or effect on the listener — are likewise not hearsay.

Exceptions

Rules 803 and 804 list numerous exceptions. Rule 803 exceptions apply regardless of the declarant's availability and include present sense impressions, excited utterances, statements of then-existing state of mind, statements made for medical diagnosis or treatment, recorded recollections, business and public records, and learned treatises. Rule 804 exceptions require the declarant to be unavailable and include former testimony, statements against interest, and dying declarations. Rule 807 supplies a residual exception for trustworthy statements not covered elsewhere.

Confrontation Clause

In criminal cases the Sixth Amendment's Confrontation Clause independently bars "testimonial" hearsay from an unavailable declarant whom the accused had no prior opportunity to cross-examine, as held in Crawford v. Washington (2004).
