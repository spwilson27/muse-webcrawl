Redemptioner

Redemptioners were European immigrants, mainly from Germany, who came to the British North American colonies in the 18th and early 19th centuries under a system similar to indentured servitude. Unlike indentured servants, who signed their contracts before departure, redemptioners negotiated passage on credit and, upon arrival, had a limited period — often about two weeks — in which relatives or friends could "redeem" them by paying their passage.

The system

If no one paid the fare within the allotted time, the ship's captain could sell the redemptioner's labour to the highest bidder for a term of service, typically three to six years, sufficient to cover the debt. Whole families often emigrated under this system, arriving principally at Philadelphia. Children might be bound out separately from their parents.

History

The redemptioner system developed around the 1720s–1740s as German emigration to Pennsylvania grew, and ship captains and merchants sought ways to finance passengers who could not prepay. It exposed immigrants to harsh conditions during the voyage and the risk of exploitation on arrival, and it declined in the early 19th century as enforcement of passenger laws improved and conditions of emigration changed.
