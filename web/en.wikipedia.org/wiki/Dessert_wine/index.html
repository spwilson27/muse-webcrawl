Dessert wine

Dessert wines, sometimes called pudding wines in the United Kingdom, are sweet wines typically served with dessert. There is no simple definition of a dessert wine; in the United States, the term legally denotes fortified wines with alcohol content above 14%, a legacy of tax law. In practice the category covers some of the world's most prized wines, whose sweetness is balanced by acidity and achieved by concentrating grape sugars before or during fermentation.

Production methods

  • Noble rot: grapes are infected by the fungus Botrytis cinerea under alternating humid and dry conditions, shriveling them and concentrating sugar and flavor (Sauternes, Tokaji Aszú, German Trockenbeerenauslese).
  • Late harvest: grapes are left on the vine to ripen further and accumulate sugar.
  • Drying (passito/appassimento): harvested grapes are dried on mats or racks for weeks, as in Italian Vin Santo and Recioto della Valpolicella.
  • Freezing: grapes freeze on the vine and are pressed frozen, yielding ice wine (Eiswein) from Germany and Canada.
  • Fortification: grape spirit is added to arrest fermentation and retain sweetness, as in Port, Madeira, and the French vins doux naturels such as Banyuls and Muscat de Beaumes-de-Venise.
  • Chaptalization and arrested fermentation are used for some lighter sweet wines such as Moscato d'Asti.

Famous styles

Sauternes (notably Château d'Yquem), Hungarian Tokaji, German Beerenauslese and Trockenbeerenauslese, Canadian and German ice wines, vintage Port, Pedro Ximénez sherry, and Italian Vin Santo are benchmark dessert wines. Many can age for decades thanks to their sugar, acid, and (where fortified) alcohol.

Serving

Dessert wines are served chilled in small glasses, often in half-bottles, and pair classically with foie gras (Sauternes), blue cheese (Port), fruit tarts, or chocolate — the rule of thumb being that the wine should be at least as sweet as the dish.
