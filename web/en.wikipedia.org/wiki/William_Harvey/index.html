William Harvey

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

William Harvey (1578–1657) was an English physician who first described, completely and in detail, the systemic circulation and properties of blood being pumped around the body by the heart, in Exercitatio Anatomica de Motu Cordis et Sanguinis in Animalibus (De Motu Cordis, 1628). Trained at Cambridge and Padua under Fabricius, he became physician to James I and Charles I.

Harvey's argument was quantitative: the heart expels far more blood in an hour than the body could possibly manufacture or consume — therefore blood must recirculate. Ligature experiments on arms and the one-way valves of veins (his teacher's discovery) completed the proof. He could not see the capillaries (Malpighi found them with the microscope in 1661) but correctly inferred them.

Reception

The doctrine demolished Galen's ebb-and-flow model but met resistance for decades; by Harvey's death it was broadly accepted in advanced circles. His later De Generatione (1651) advanced embryology with the maxim that all life begins from an egg.

Legacy

Harvey is a founder of modern Physiology and of experimental medicine generally — his method (measure, test, publish the evidence) is the template. The Harveian Oration at the Royal College of Physicians, endowed by Harvey himself, continues annually.
