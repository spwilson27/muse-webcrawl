Poincaré duality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, the Poincaré duality theorem, named after Henri Poincaré, is a fundamental result on the structure of the homology and cohomology groups of manifolds. It states that if M is an n-dimensional closed (compact, without boundary) orientable manifold, then the k-th cohomology group of M is isomorphic to the (n − k)-th homology group of M, for all integers k.

Statement and mechanism

The isomorphism is given by the cap product with the fundamental class [M], the generator of the top-dimensional homology group determined by the orientation. As an immediate consequence, the Betti numbers in complementary degrees are equal: for a closed orientable surface of genus g, for example, b0 = b2 = 1. The pairing induced by duality is non-degenerate, making the middle-dimensional cup product a symplectic or symmetric form, depending on dimension.

History

Poincaré stated the duality in 1893 and published a proof in his landmark 1895 paper Analysis Situs, working with the intersection theory of submanifolds and the symmetry of Betti numbers. The modern formulation via cap products and cellular homology came with the development of algebraic topology in the 1920s–30s.

Generalisations

Versions exist for non-orientable manifolds (using mod-2 coefficients or twisted coefficients), for manifolds with boundary (Lefschetz duality, pairing absolute with relative groups), and in sheaf-theoretic form as Verdier duality. Poincaré duality is a cornerstone of manifold theory: it underlies invariants such as the signature, constrains which groups can arise as manifold homology, and features in classification problems and gauge theory.
