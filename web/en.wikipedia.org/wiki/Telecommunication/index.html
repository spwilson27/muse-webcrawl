Telecommunication

Telecommunication, often used in its plural form telecommunications, is the transmission of information over a distance using electrical, electromagnetic, or optical signals, for the purpose of communication. In modern practice, it encompasses the telephone, radio, television, and computer networks, including the Internet. The word derives from the Greek prefix tele- (τῆλε), meaning "far off", combined with the Latin communicare, "to share".

History

Early telecommunication included visual systems such as smoke signals, signal fires, semaphore telegraphs, and signal flags. The invention of the electrical telegraph in the 1830s and 1840s, associated with Samuel Morse in the United States and William Cooke and Charles Wheatstone in England, began the era of electrical communication. Alexander Graham Bell patented the telephone in 1876, and telephone networks spread rapidly through cities in the following decades.

Guglielmo Marconi's experiments with wireless telegraphy in the 1890s led to transatlantic radio signalling in 1901 and to broadcast radio in the 1920s. Television broadcasting began in the 1930s. The launch of communications satellites, beginning with Telstar in 1962, enabled near-instantaneous global links. Optical fibre transmission, developed from the 1970s, greatly increased the capacity of long-distance networks, while mobile telephony, beginning with first-generation analogue systems in the 1980s, made personal telecommunications ubiquitous.

Basic elements

A telecommunication system consists of a transmitter, which encodes information into a signal; a transmission medium (channel), such as copper wire, optical fibre, or free space; and a receiver, which recovers the information. Signals may be analogue, varying continuously, or digital, encoded as discrete symbols. Key impairments include attenuation, distortion, interference, and noise; Claude Shannon's information theory (1948) established fundamental limits on reliable transmission over noisy channels.

Networks and services

Telecommunication networks include the public switched telephone network, mobile cellular networks, the Internet, broadcasting networks, and private enterprise networks. Modern systems are largely built on packet switching and digital transmission standards. Regulation of spectrum and services is coordinated internationally through bodies such as the International Telecommunication Union (ITU), founded in 1865.

Societal impact

Telecommunications has transformed commerce, media, and social life, shrinking the effective distance between people and places. The widespread availability of broadband Internet and smartphones in the twenty-first century has made instant communication a routine feature of daily life, while raising questions about privacy, access, and the digital divide.
