Macclesfield Canal

The Macclesfield Canal is a canal in Cheshire, England, opened in 1831. It was engineered by Thomas Telford and runs 26 miles (42 km) from Marple Junction, where it joins the Peak Forest Canal, southwards through Macclesfield and Congleton to Hall Green near Kidsgrove, where a stop lock connects it with the Trent and Mersey Canal. It formed part of a through route between Manchester and the Midlands.

Route and features

The canal is unusual in concentrating almost all of its locks into a single flight: the twelve Bosley Locks raise the canal by 118 feet (36 m) just south of Macclesfield; apart from the stop lock at Hall Green, the remainder of the canal is level. Telford's late design is noted for its elegant cuttings and embankments, stone roving bridges ("snake bridges") that let towpath horses change sides without unhitching, and a high aqueduct at Bollington, famous for its silk mills nearby.

History

Authorized by Act of Parliament in 1826 after earlier schemes failed, the canal was built between 1826 and 1831. It carried coal, stone, silk goods and other cargo, but competition from the railways — the canal was bought by a railway company in 1846 — led to decline of freight traffic; the last regular commercial carrying ended in the 1950s. Today it is a cruising waterway managed by the Canal & River Trust, and forms part of the Cheshire Ring leisure route.
