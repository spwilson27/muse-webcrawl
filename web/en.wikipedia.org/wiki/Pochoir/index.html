Pochoir

Pochoir (French for "stencil") is a highly refined technique of making prints with stencils. The method reached its height of use in France in the early 20th century, particularly in the production of luxurious illustrated books and fashion plates during the Art Deco period.

Technique

In the pochoir process, an artist's original work is analysed and separated into individual colours. A stencil is cut by hand for each colour — sometimes as many as 250 stencils for a single image — from thin sheets of metal (such as zinc or copper) or celluloid. Colour is then applied by hand through each stencil in succession using special brushes called pompons, building up layers of vibrant, flat colour, often using gouache. Because each print is coloured by hand, slight variations between impressions are normal.

History

Pochoir was used from around 1900 to the 1930s by Parisian fashion publications such as La Gazette du Bon Ton to produce vivid fashion plates by artists including Paul Iribe, Georges Lepape and Charles Martin. The technique was also applied to decorative-arts portfolios, wallpaper and textile designs, and fine-art prints.

Famous pochoir productions include Sonia Delaunay's Tableaux vivants, the fashion albums of Paul Poiret, and portfolios documenting the designs shown at the 1925 Paris Exposition Internationale des Arts Décoratifs, from which the term Art Deco derives. The labour-intensive process declined as cheaper photomechanical reproduction improved.

Characteristics

Pochoir prints are noted for their saturated, opaque colour, crisp edges, and subtle gradations achieved by hand-brushing. Unlike most reproductive printing of the period, the images retain a painterly surface quality close to the original gouache artwork.
