Antidumping duty

An antidumping duty is a protectionist tariff that a domestic government imposes on foreign imports that it believes are priced below fair market value — that is, "dumped" into its market. Antidumping measures are a standard instrument of trade remedy law, permitted under the rules of the World Trade Organization when dumping causes or threatens material injury to a domestic industry.

Legal framework

Article VI of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) and the WTO's Anti-Dumping Agreement allow members to levy duties up to the "dumping margin": the difference between the export price of a product and its normal value, usually the price in the exporter's home market. To impose a duty, an investigating authority must normally establish dumping, material injury to the domestic industry, and a causal link between the two, typically after a petition by domestic producers.

Procedure and effects

Investigations may result in provisional measures, definitive duties (commonly lasting five years, subject to sunset review), or price undertakings from exporters. Duties are specific to exporting companies and countries. Critics argue that antidumping law is frequently used as ordinary protectionism, since international price discrimination is common and not necessarily harmful; supporters regard it as a necessary safeguard against predatory pricing and unfair trade.
