Salvator Mundi (Latin for "Saviour of the World") is a painting attributed in whole or in part to the Italian Renaissance artist Leonardo da Vinci, dated to c. 1499–1510. It depicts Jesus Christ in an anachronistic blue Renaissance dress, making the sign of the cross with his right hand while holding a transparent crystal orb in his left, representing his role as Saviour of the World and master of the cosmos.

History

The painting was long thought lost. A panel matching its composition was recorded in the collection of Charles I of England and was sold off after his execution; it subsequently passed through several hands and was eventually bought at auction in 1958 for £45, catalogued as a work by Leonardo's follower Giovanni Antonio Boltraffio. In 2005 it was acquired for under $10,000 at an estate sale in Louisiana by a consortium of art dealers, who had it conserved and researched. Following restoration, several specialists endorsed an attribution to Leonardo himself, and the work was exhibited as such at the National Gallery in London in 2011.

The 2017 sale

After passing to Russian billionaire Dmitry Rybolovlev, the painting was offered by Christie's in New York on 15 November 2017. It sold for US$450.3 million (including buyer's premium), breaking the record for the most expensive painting ever sold at public auction. The buyer was later reported to be acting on behalf of Saudi crown prince Mohammed bin Salman. The painting has rarely been seen in public since the sale.

Attribution debate

The attribution remains contested. Supporters cite the quality of the sfumato, the rendering of the hand and the orb, and comparison with Leonardo's preparatory drawings for the drapery. Sceptics note the painting's poor condition, extensive repainting, and passages they consider weak, and suggest a workshop origin or a primary role for an assistant such as Boltraffio or Bernardino Luini. In 2021 the Prado Museum downgraded the work in a catalogue from an autograph Leonardo to a workshop attribution, reflecting the continuing scholarly dispute.
