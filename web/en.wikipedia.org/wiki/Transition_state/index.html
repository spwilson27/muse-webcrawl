Transition state

The transition state of a chemical reaction is a particular configuration along the reaction coordinate. It is defined as the state corresponding to the highest potential energy along this reaction coordinate, and is typically denoted by the double-dagger symbol ‡. At this configuration, reacting species are in a structure intermediate between reactants and products, with bonds partially broken and partially formed. The transition state is not a chemical intermediate: it exists only fleetingly (on the order of a molecular vibration, about 10−13 s) and cannot be isolated.

Transition state theory

Transition state theory (TST), developed in 1935 by Henry Eyring, Meredith Evans and Michael Polanyi, explains reaction rates by assuming a quasi-equilibrium between reactants and the activated complex at the transition state. The free energy difference between reactants and the transition state — the activation free energy — determines the rate through the Eyring equation. The Hammond postulate states that the transition state resembles the reactant or product to which it is closer in energy, and is widely used to reason about reaction mechanisms.

Observation and catalysis

Transition states cannot be observed directly by ordinary spectroscopy because of their extremely short lifetimes, but femtochemistry (pioneered by Ahmed Zewail) probes species near the transition region, and transition-state structures are routinely computed with quantum chemistry. Enzymes and other catalysts work by binding and stabilising the transition state more strongly than the substrate, lowering the activation barrier; deliberately designed transition-state analogues are among the most potent enzyme inhibitors.
