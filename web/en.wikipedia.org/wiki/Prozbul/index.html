Prozbul

The prozbul (also spelled prosbul; Hebrew: פרוזבול) is a legal instrument in Jewish law, instituted by Hillel the Elder in the first century BCE, that allows a lender to collect a private loan even after the sabbatical year (shemitah), during which the Torah commands that debts be remitted.

Background

Deuteronomy 15 ordains that debts be cancelled in the seventh, sabbatical year, and warns lenders not to refuse loans as that year approaches. Hillel observed that the wealthy were in fact refusing to lend to the poor, contrary to the Torah's intent. He therefore ordained the prozbul: before the sabbatical year, the lender deposits the bond of debt with a court (beit din) and declares that the debt is assigned to the court. Because the remission applied to debts between individuals, a debt formally owed to a court remained collectable.

Form and practice

The text of the prozbul is preserved in Mishnah Shevi'it 10. Its name is usually explained as a contraction of Greek pros boulē — "before the council/court". Traditionally the borrower needed to own some land for the instrument to be fully valid, and even a small plot could be lent for the purpose. Prozbul documents are still executed today, commonly in the weeks before Rosh Hashanah of a sabbatical year, and printed forms appear in many prayer books and synagogues.

The prozbul is frequently cited in discussions of legal fictions and of rabbinic enactments (takkanot) that adapt the law to economic realities while formally preserving its letter.
