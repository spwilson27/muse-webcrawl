Artesian aquifer

Artesian aquifer
TypeConfined aquifer under pressure
Named forArtois, France
Notable exampleGreat Artesian Basin, Australia

An artesian aquifer is a confined aquifer containing groundwater under positive pressure. Water in a well penetrating such an aquifer rises above the top of the aquifer, and may flow at the surface without pumping if the pressure surface (potentiometric surface) lies above ground level — a flowing artesian well.

Geology

An artesian system forms when a permeable rock layer (such as sandstone) is sandwiched between impermeable confining layers (such as shale or clay) and is tilted or folded so that its recharge area, where rain enters, sits at a higher elevation than the well site. The weight of water in the elevated part pressurises the whole confined body. The name comes from the province of Artois in northern France, where such wells were famously bored by Carthusian monks from the 12th century; the Lillers well of 1126 is often cited as Europe's oldest.

Examples

The largest known artesian system is Australia's Great Artesian Basin, underlying about 1.7 million km² (roughly a fifth of the continent) and vital to the pastoral industry of the outback; uncontrolled flowing bores wasted much of its pressure in the 20th century, prompting bore-capping programmes. Other major artesian basins include the Dakota aquifer in the northern United States and the aquifers beneath Paris and London (where pressure has partly recovered as abstraction declined).
