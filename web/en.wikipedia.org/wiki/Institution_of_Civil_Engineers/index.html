Institution of Civil Engineers

The Institution of Civil Engineers (ICE) is an independent professional association for civil engineers, headquartered in London. Founded in 1818 and granted a royal charter in 1828, it is generally regarded as the world's first professional engineering institution. The ICE qualifies civil engineers in the United Kingdom and internationally, awarding the chartered title CEng and the post-nominals MICE and FICE, publishes research, and advises government on infrastructure policy. Its membership numbers around 95,000 in more than 150 countries.

History

The institution began in 1818 when eight young engineers met at the Kendal Coffee House in Fleet Street to form a society for "facilitating the acquirement of knowledge" in civil engineering — the term then covering all non-military engineering. In 1820 the engineer Thomas Telford, builder of roads, canals, and the Pontcysyllte Aqueduct, became its first president; his prestige helped secure the royal charter of 1828, which famously defined civil engineering as "the art of directing the great sources of power in nature for the use and convenience of man." The institution's membership grew with the railway age; specialist bodies later split from it — mechanical engineers in 1847 and electrical engineers in 1871 — leaving the ICE focused on civil works.

Activities

The ICE is the qualifying body for civil engineers in the UK under licence from the Engineering Council, operating grades from graduate and technician membership through incorporated engineer to chartered civil engineer (CEng MICE) and fellow (FICE). It publishes the Proceedings of the Institution of Civil Engineers journals and the long-running New Civil Engineer magazine, holds one of the largest engineering libraries in the world, issues the annual State of the Nation infrastructure reports, and hosts awards, lectures, and schools outreach. Its headquarters, One Great George Street, built in 1910–13 adjacent to Parliament Square, also serves as a major conference venue.

Notable presidents

Past presidents include many of the profession's most famous figures: Thomas Telford, George and Robert Stephenson, Isambard Kingdom Brunel, Joseph Bazalgette, and John Wolfe Barry, as well as, in the modern era, engineers from the water, energy, and transport sectors. The presidency is held for a one-year term, inaugurated each November with a presidential address.
