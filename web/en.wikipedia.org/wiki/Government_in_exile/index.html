Government-in-exile

A government-in-exile is a political group which claims to be the legitimate government of a sovereign state or semi-sovereign state, but is unable to exercise legal power and instead resides in a foreign country. Governments-in-exile usually undertake to return to their home country and regain power, and often form after occupation or revolution.

Historical examples

During the Second World War, London hosted numerous governments-in-exile, including those of Poland, Czechoslovakia, Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium, and Yugoslavia, as well as Charles de Gaulle's Free French leadership. These governments maintained armed forces, diplomatic recognition, and legal continuity despite the occupation of their countries.

Status in international law

The effectiveness of a government-in-exile depends on recognition by foreign states. Some governments-in-exile retain embassies and conduct international relations; others are primarily symbolic. With the passage of time, the claims of some exiled governments fade, while others persist for decades, such as the Central Tibetan Administration in India.
