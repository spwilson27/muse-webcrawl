Splenectomy

A splenectomy is a surgical procedure that partially or completely removes the spleen. The spleen is an organ in the upper left abdomen that filters blood, removes old red blood cells, and plays a role in immune defense. Splenectomy may be performed by open surgery or laparoscopically.

Indications

Common indications include traumatic rupture of the spleen, which can cause life-threatening internal bleeding; hematologic disorders such as immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP), hereditary spherocytosis, and some hemolytic anemias; splenic enlargement (splenomegaly) causing cytopenias; certain lymphomas and leukemias for diagnosis or treatment; and splenic cysts, abscesses, or tumors. Where possible in trauma, splenic salvage or partial splenectomy is preferred to preserve immune function, particularly in children.

Consequences and risks

People without a spleen (asplenia) have a lifelong increased risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI), particularly from encapsulated bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and Neisseria meningitidis. Patients therefore receive vaccinations against these organisms, education about fever, and sometimes prophylactic antibiotics. Blood counts typically show raised platelets and the appearance of Howell–Jolly bodies in red cells after removal.

History

Early splenectomies were performed in the 19th century with high mortality; the operation became safer with transfusion and antibiotics. Recognition of the spleen's immunological role, highlighted by King and Shumacker's 1952 report of fatal infections in splenectomized infants, shifted practice toward spleen preservation when feasible.
