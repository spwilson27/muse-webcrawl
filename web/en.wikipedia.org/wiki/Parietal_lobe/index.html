The parietal lobe is one of the four major lobes of the cerebral cortex in the brain of mammals. It is positioned above the temporal lobe and behind the frontal lobe and central sulcus. The name comes from the parietal bone, which is named from the Latin paries, meaning "wall".

The parietal lobe integrates sensory information from different modalities, particularly determining spatial sense and navigation (proprioception), the main sensory receptive area for the sense of touch (somatosensation) in the somatosensory cortex, and the dorsal stream of the visual system. This enables regions of the parietal cortex to map objects perceived visually into body coordinate positions.

Structure

The parietal lobe is defined by three anatomical boundaries: the central sulcus separates it from the frontal lobe; the lateral sulcus separates it from the temporal lobe; the parieto-occipital sulcus separates it from the occipital lobe. It comprises the postcentral gyrus (containing the primary somatosensory cortex, Brodmann areas 3, 1 and 2), the superior parietal lobule, the inferior parietal lobule (including the supramarginal and angular gyri), and the precuneus on the medial surface.

Function

The parietal cortex is involved in somatosensation, spatial awareness and attention, praxis (skilled movement), aspects of language and reading (notably the angular gyrus), and mathematics and number processing. The posterior parietal cortex is often described in terms of a dorsal "where/how" pathway that guides action.

Clinical significance

Damage to the right parietal lobe commonly produces hemispatial neglect, in which patients fail to attend to the left side of space. Damage to the inferior parietal lobule of the dominant hemisphere may cause elements of Gerstmann syndrome — agraphia, acalculia, finger agnosia and left–right disorientation. Lesions of the superior parietal and intraparietal regions can cause Balint syndrome, with simultanagnosia, optic ataxia and ocular apraxia.
