Masonic lodge

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Masonic lodge, often termed a private lodge or constituent lodge, is the basic organisational unit of Freemasonry. Every new lodge must be warranted or chartered by a Grand Lodge, and no lodge may confer the degrees of Freemasonry without such authority. The word "lodge" also refers to the room or building in which Masons meet, and by extension to the meeting itself.

Organisation

A lodge is presided over by a Master (in England, the Worshipful Master) and assisted by a Senior Warden, a Junior Warden, a Treasurer, a Secretary and other officers including Deacons, a Tyler who guards the door, and an Inner Guard in some jurisdictions. Officers are usually elected or appointed annually. Lodges meet regularly — monthly in most jurisdictions — to conduct business, admit new members and confer the three degrees of Entered Apprentice, Fellow Craft and Master Mason.

Lodges are numbered according to their seniority within their Grand Lodge's register, and many also bear distinctive names, often derived from a place, a virtue, a founding member or a local landmark. A Mason is said to "belong" to the lodge in which he was initiated, his mother lodge, though he may join others.

The lodge room

The lodge room is traditionally oblong, oriented east to west. Its furnishings include an altar or pedestal in the centre on which rest the Volume of the Sacred Law (such as the Bible), the square and compasses; three lesser lights; a mosaic or checkered pavement; and chairs for the Master in the east and the Wardens in the west and south. The room symbolically represents the Temple of King Solomon and, more broadly, the universe.

Types of lodges

Beyond regular constituent lodges, there are research lodges devoted to Masonic scholarship, lodges of instruction where ritual is rehearsed, and in some jurisdictions "table lodges" at which formal dining is combined with ceremony. Military travelling lodges historically held warrants allowing them to meet wherever their regiment was stationed.

History

The lodge system developed from the gatherings of operative stonemasons in the later Middle Ages. When speculative Freemasonry emerged in the seventeenth century, lodges became independent bodies of accepted masons. Four London lodges formed the first Grand Lodge in 1717, establishing the model by which local lodges are chartered and regulated that persists today.
