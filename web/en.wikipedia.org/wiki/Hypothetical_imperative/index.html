Hypothetical imperative

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A hypothetical imperative (German: hypothetischer Imperativ) is a command of reason that applies only conditionally, in the service of some desired end: it tells us what to do if we want to achieve a particular goal. The concept was introduced by Immanuel Kant in his Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785), where it is contrasted with the categorical imperative, which commands actions unconditionally, as ends in themselves.

Kant's classification

Kant divides hypothetical imperatives into two kinds. Rules of skill (problematic imperatives) prescribe means to merely possible ends — for example, a doctor's prescription for a patient who wants to be cured. Counsels of prudence (assertoric imperatives) prescribe means to happiness, an end all humans actually have by nature, but one too indeterminate for precise universal rules. Neither kind, Kant argues, can ground morality, because their force depends on the agent's contingent desires; only the categorical imperative commands with necessity, since it derives from reason itself rather than from any end given to it.

The distinction remains influential in ethical theory, where hypothetical imperatives are associated with instrumental rationality — "oughts" of the form if you want X, you ought to do Y — while philosophers debate whether moral requirements are instead categorical.
