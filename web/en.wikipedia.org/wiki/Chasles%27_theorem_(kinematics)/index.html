Chasles' theorem (kinematics)

A general rigid-body displacement decomposed into a screw motion.

Chasles' theorem in kinematics states that any displacement of a rigid body in three-dimensional space can be produced by a translation along a line (axis) together with a rotation about that same line. Such a combined motion is called a screw displacement, and the axis is the screw axis of the displacement. The theorem is named after the French mathematician Michel Chasles, who presented it in 1830; the corresponding result for infinitesimal motions had been anticipated by Giulio Mozzi in 1763, and the screw axis is consequently also known as the Mozzi–Chasles axis.

Statement and consequences

Chasles' theorem
Field: Kinematics, rigid-body dynamics
Named after: Michel Chasles (1830)
Anticipated by: Giulio Mozzi (1763)
Result: Every rigid displacement is a screw displacement

Given a rotation and an arbitrary translation, the translational component perpendicular to the rotation axis can be absorbed by shifting the axis sideways, leaving only the component parallel to the axis. What remains is a rotation about the shifted axis plus a sliding along it — exactly the motion of a screw. Pure translations and pure rotations appear as limiting cases (zero pitch and infinite pitch).

For continuous motion, the theorem implies that the instantaneous velocity field of a rigid body is at every moment a twist: rotation about an instantaneous screw axis combined with translation along it. This is a foundation of screw theory, developed by Robert Stawell Ball, which represents forces as wrenches and velocities as twists on screws, and is widely used in robotics, mechanism analysis and computer graphics to describe spatial motion.

Planar analogue

The corresponding theorem in plane kinematics is simpler: every displacement of a rigid body in the plane is either a pure translation or a rotation about some point, the instantaneous centre of rotation.
