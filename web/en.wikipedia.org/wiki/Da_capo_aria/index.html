The da capo aria (Italian for "from the head aria") is a musical form dominant in Baroque opera and oratorio, in which a singer performs an opening section (A), a contrasting middle section (B), and then repeats the first section da capo — from the beginning. The repeat was conventionally decorated with improvised ornamentation displaying the singer's virtuosity.

Structure

The A section, usually framed by orchestral ritornellos, presents one principal affection or mood; the shorter B section contrasts in key, texture or sentiment. In performance the da capo repeat was not written out in full — the score simply marked "D.C." — and the singer was expected to embellish the vocal line, especially in the works of Handel, Vivaldi and Hasse. A shortened variant, the dal segno aria, repeats from a sign partway through.

History

The form crystallised in the late 17th century in the operas of Alessandro Scarlatti and became the standard aria type of opera seria, structuring librettos by Metastasio and the music of Handel, whose operas and oratorios contain many famous examples, such as "Lascia ch'io pianga". By the mid-18th century reformers including Gluck criticised the form's dramatic implausibility — characters repeating music and text verbatim — and it declined in favour of more flexible forms, though it survived in adapted guises.
