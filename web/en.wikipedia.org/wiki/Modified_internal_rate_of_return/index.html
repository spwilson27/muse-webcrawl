Modified internal rate of return

The modified internal rate of return (MIRR) is a financial measure of an investment's attractiveness. It is used in capital budgeting to rank alternative investments of equal size. As the name implies, MIRR is a modification of the internal rate of return (IRR) and aims to resolve some problems with the IRR.

Problems addressed

The standard IRR assumes that positive cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself — often implausibly high — and that negative cash flows are financed at the IRR as well. It can also yield multiple solutions (or none) when a project's cash flows change sign more than once. MIRR replaces these assumptions with explicit ones: positive cash flows are compounded forward to the end of the project at a realistic reinvestment rate (often the cost of capital), and negative cash flows are discounted back to the present at a finance rate. The MIRR is then the single discount rate that equates the present value of the (discounted) costs with the future value of the (compounded) benefits over the project's life.

Calculation

MIRR = (FV of positive cash flows at the reinvestment rate ÷ PV of negative cash flows at the finance rate)1/n − 1, where n is the number of periods. Because all inflows are aggregated into a single future value and all outflows into a single present value, MIRR always produces one unique answer for a given set of assumptions, and it is generally lower — and more conservative — than the IRR for profitable projects.

Use and limitations

Spreadsheet functions (Excel's MIRR) implement the measure directly. Although MIRR resolves the multiple-root and reinvestment-assumption problems, managers still debate the choice of reinvestment and finance rates, and MIRR, like IRR, can conflict with net present value when ranking mutually exclusive projects of different scale or duration — in which case NPV remains the theoretically preferred criterion.
