Cushing's syndrome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cushing's syndrome
SpecialtyEndocrinology
SymptomsCentral weight gain, moon face, buffalo hump, purple striae, hypertension, easy bruising, muscle weakness
CausesProlonged exposure to cortisol: glucocorticoid medication; pituitary or adrenal tumors; ectopic ACTH
Named afterHarvey Cushing

Cushing's syndrome is a collection of signs and symptoms due to prolonged exposure to glucocorticoids such as cortisol. Signs include high blood pressure, central obesity with a characteristic rounded "moon face" and fatty "buffalo hump" between the shoulders, reddish-purple stretch marks, weak muscles, fragile skin that bruises easily, and, in women, excess hair growth and irregular periods.

Causes

The most common cause overall is the use of prescribed glucocorticoid drugs (exogenous Cushing's syndrome). Endogenous cases arise from excess cortisol production: a pituitary adenoma secreting ACTH (termed Cushing's disease, the most common endogenous cause), adrenal tumors secreting cortisol directly, or ectopic ACTH production by tumors elsewhere, such as small-cell lung cancer. The syndrome is named for neurosurgeon Harvey Cushing, who described it in 1912.

Diagnosis

Screening tests include the overnight dexamethasone suppression test, 24-hour urinary free cortisol, and late-night salivary cortisol. Blood ACTH levels and imaging then localize the source.

Treatment

Treatment aims to reduce cortisol to normal: tapering offending medication where possible, or surgery — transsphenoidal removal of a pituitary adenoma, or adrenalectomy for adrenal tumors. Medicines such as ketoconazole, metyrapone, and mifepristone can control cortisol when surgery is impossible or incomplete.
