The Uzbeks are a Turkic ethnic group native to Central Asia, the largest ethnic group of Uzbekistan and one of the largest Turkic peoples overall. They speak Uzbek, a Karluk Turkic language, and are predominantly Sunni Muslims of the Hanafi school. Significant Uzbek populations live in northern Afghanistan and in the Ferghana Valley regions of Tajikistan and Kyrgyzstan.

Origins and history

The Uzbek ethnogenesis is complex. The name derives from Öz Beg Khan (Uzbek Khan), the 14th-century ruler of the Golden Horde who promoted Islam among his people. In the late 15th and early 16th centuries, nomadic Uzbek tribal confederations under Muhammad Shaybani swept south from the Kazakh steppe and conquered the settled oases of Transoxiana from the Timurids, establishing the khanates of Bukhara and Khiva. These nomadic groups merged over centuries with the much older sedentary, largely Iranian- and Turkic-speaking population of the oasis cities (historically called Sarts), producing the modern Uzbek people.

Culture

Uzbek culture reflects both its nomadic steppe heritage and the sophisticated urban civilisation of Samarkand and Bukhara. Distinctive elements include the doppi skullcap, colourful atlas silk woven in the Ferghana Valley, elaborate suzani embroidery, and a cuisine centred on plov (osh), the rice pilaf cooked with meat, carrots and spices that functions as a national symbol. The mahalla (neighbourhood community) remains an important social institution.

Soviet and modern history

The Uzbek Soviet Socialist Republic was created in the 1924 national delimitation of Central Asia, fixing modern Uzbekistan's borders. After independence in 1991, Uzbeks became the politically and demographically dominant group in the new state. Large diaspora communities have since formed through labour migration, particularly in Russia and Kazakhstan.
