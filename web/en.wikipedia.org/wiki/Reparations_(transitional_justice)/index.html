Reparations (transitional justice)

Reparations are measures taken by a state or other actor to redress gross violations of international human rights law or serious violations of international humanitarian law. In the field of transitional justice, reparations are one of the principal mechanisms by which societies emerging from conflict or authoritarian rule address the harms suffered by victims, alongside truth-telling, prosecutions, and institutional reform.

Forms of reparation

The UN Basic Principles and Guidelines on the Right to a Remedy and Reparation (adopted by the General Assembly in 2005) identify five forms:

  • Restitution — restoring the victim to their original situation, e.g. return of property or restoration of rights.
  • Compensation — payment for economically assessable damage.
  • Rehabilitation — medical and psychological care, and legal and social services.
  • Satisfaction — truth-telling, official apologies, commemorations, and restoration of dignity.
  • Guarantees of non-repetition — reforms to prevent recurrence of violations.

Examples

Programmes have included West German compensation to Holocaust survivors under the 1952 Luxembourg Agreement; payments and an official apology to Japanese Americans interned during the Second World War under the U.S. Civil Liberties Act of 1988; reparations recommended by South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission; and extensive administrative programmes in countries such as Chile, Argentina, Peru, Colombia, and Morocco. Courts including the International Criminal Court may also order reparations to victims.

Debates

Reparations raise difficult questions: how to define eligible victims, how to value harm, how to fund programmes in poor post-conflict states, and whether reparations should be individual or collective. Advocates argue that reparations distinctively centre victims and acknowledge responsibility in a way that trials or truth commissions alone do not.
