Tropic of Cancer is a novel by the American writer Henry Miller, first published in Paris in 1934 by the Obelisk Press. Largely autobiographical, it describes the impoverished, bohemian life of an expatriate writer in Paris in the late 1920s and early 1930s, mixing narrative with philosophical meditation in a free-flowing, often explicitly sexual style that made the book notorious—and banned in the United States and Britain for decades.

Content and style

The first-person narrator, "Henry Miller", drifts through Parisian hotels, bars, and brothels, cadging meals and sleeping on friends' floors, while reflecting on art, hunger, sex, and spiritual rebirth. Characters are drawn from Miller's circle, including his friend Carl (based on Alfred Perlès) and the figure of his wife June, called Mona. Anaïs Nin, who helped support Miller and wrote the preface to the first edition, championed the book. Its prose—"This is not a book. This is libel, slander, defamation of character", Miller declares—broke with conventional narrative, influencing the Beat Generation; George Orwell praised it as a remarkable prose work by an American expatriate.

Banning and publication battle

U.S. customs seized imported copies for obscenity, and the book could not legally be published in the United States until Grove Press issued it in 1961, provoking dozens of obscenity prosecutions across the country. The legal battles contributed to the liberalisation of American publishing law; in 1964 the Supreme Court's decision in Grove Press, Inc. v. Gerstein effectively cleared the book. A companion volume, Tropic of Capricorn (1939), covered Miller's New York years.

Legacy

Tropic of Cancer is regularly listed among the great twentieth-century novels—Modern Library placed it among its 100 best English-language novels of the century—and remains a landmark of confessional writing and of the fight against literary censorship. It was loosely adapted into a film in 1970.
