Computer simulation

Computer simulation is the process of mathematical modelling, performed on a computer, which is designed to predict the behaviour of, or the outcome of, a real-world or physical system. The reliability of some mathematical models can be determined by comparing their results to the real-world outcomes they aim to predict. Computer simulations have become a useful tool for the mathematical modelling of many natural systems in physics (computational physics), astrophysics, climatology, chemistry, biology and manufacturing, as well as human systems in economics, psychology, social science, health care and engineering.

Types of simulation

  • Continuous simulation models systems described by differential equations, such as fluid flow and climate.
  • Discrete-event simulation models systems whose state changes at discrete points in time, such as queues, factories, and networks.
  • Agent-based modelling simulates the actions and interactions of autonomous agents to assess their effects on the system as a whole.
  • Monte Carlo simulation uses repeated random sampling to explore systems with significant uncertainty.

History

Computer simulation was pioneered during the Second World War, when John von Neumann, Stanislaw Ulam, and Nicholas Metropolis developed Monte Carlo methods at Los Alamos to study neutron diffusion. The ENIAC was used for weather-prediction experiments by 1950. Through the later twentieth century, simulation became central to aircraft and spacecraft design, nuclear stockpile stewardship, and forecasting, and flight simulators became standard for pilot training.

Validation and limitations

Because simulations are models, their fidelity depends on the validity of their assumptions, the quality of input data, and the resolution at which processes are represented. Verification (solving the equations correctly) and validation (solving the correct equations) are distinguished in the field; chaotic systems such as weather can only be simulated probabilistically beyond a limited horizon.
