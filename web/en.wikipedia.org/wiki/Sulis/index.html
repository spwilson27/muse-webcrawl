Sulis

TypeGoddess of healing waters
Major cult centreAquae Sulis (Bath, England)
Syncretised withMinerva
Sacred siteThe hot springs at Bath

Sulis was a Celtic goddess worshipped at the thermal springs of Aquae Sulis — modern Bath in Somerset, England — during the Romano-British period. The Romans identified her with their own goddess Minerva, and the combined deity Sulis Minerva presided over one of the most important healing sanctuaries in the province of Britannia.

The sanctuary at Bath

The hot springs at Bath, which produce over a million litres of water a day at around 46 °C, were sacred long before the Roman conquest. Around AD 60–70 the Romans built an elaborate temple and bathing complex on the site. The temple, dedicated to Sulis Minerva, was unusual in Britain for its classical pediment, which carried a striking carved head — often interpreted as a Gorgon, though with male, watery features that suggest a fusion of classical and Celtic imagery.

Curse tablets

Sulis is especially known from the nearly 130 lead defixiones (curse tablets) recovered from the Sacred Spring. Petitioners inscribed complaints — frequently about stolen clothing at the baths — onto small sheets of lead or pewter, rolled them up, and threw them into the spring for the goddess to act upon. The tablets, written in Latin and in the cursive hand of ordinary provincials, are among the most vivid surviving records of everyday life and grievance in Roman Britain. Some are written in a British Celtic language rendered in Latin letters, making them rare evidence for the spoken Celtic of the period.

Worship and healing

As a goddess of the healing waters, Sulis combined roles of healer, guardian of the spring, and dispenser of justice. The great altar and the temple precinct drew pilgrims from across the province and beyond. An inscription records a dedication by a haruspex — a priest who read omens from entrails — suggesting the cult had an organised priesthood blending Roman and local practice.

Legacy

The name of the Roman town, Aquae Sulis ("the waters of Sulis"), preserves the goddess's name. Excavations at the Roman Baths museum continue to recover offerings from the spring reservoir, and the gilt-bronze head of Sulis Minerva, found in 1727, remains one of the finest pieces of Romano-British sculpture.
