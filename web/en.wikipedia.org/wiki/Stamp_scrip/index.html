Stamp scrip

Stamp scrip is a type of local or emergency currency (scrip) that carries a built-in carrying cost, or demurrage: to remain valid, the note must periodically have a stamp affixed to it, purchased from the issuing authority, so that holding the money costs money. The device was intended to discourage hoarding and increase the velocity of circulation, especially during depressions when cash was scarce and spending had collapsed.

Theory

The idea derives from the monetary theories of the German-Argentine economist Silvio Gesell, whose concept of Freigeld ("free money") proposed currency that loses value over time unless maintained by stamps. The economist Irving Fisher became an advocate during the Great Depression and published the handbook Stamp Scrip in 1933, promoting the device as a self-liquidating stimulus: a one-dollar note requiring a two-cent stamp each week, for example, would fully fund its own redemption after a year while changing hands dozens of times.

Historical experiments

The most famous experiment took place in the Austrian town of Wörgl in 1932–1933, where the mayor issued "certified compensation bills" bearing monthly stamps of 1% of face value. Local accounts reported rapid circulation, falling unemployment, and completed public works before the Austrian central bank suppressed the scheme. In the United States, hundreds of communities, chambers of commerce, and municipalities issued stamp scrip in 1933, and bills were introduced in Congress to issue it nationally; the movement faded as federal relief and banking reforms took effect. Similar notes appeared in Germany (the Wära of Schwanenkirchen) before being banned.
