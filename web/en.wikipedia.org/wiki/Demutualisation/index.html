Demutualisation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Demutualisation is the process by which a member-owned mutual organisation — such as a building society, mutual insurer, or savings bank — changes its legal structure to become a shareholder-owned company, typically a public limited company. Members' ownership rights are converted into shares, cash, or other benefits, and the converted institution can raise capital on stock markets and is exposed to takeover like any other company.

Reasons

Institutions demutualise chiefly to gain access to external capital for expansion, acquisitions, or technological investment, since mutuals can raise funds only from retained earnings and wholesale borrowing. Managers may also seek the higher remuneration available in the corporate sector. Members who vote for conversion typically receive windfall payments or free shares, which can make demutualisation popular in the short term; speculators who opened accounts in anticipation of windfalls were known in Britain as "carpetbaggers".

United Kingdom

The Building Societies Act 1986 allowed British building societies to convert to banks. Abbey National converted first, in 1989. A wave followed in the mid-1990s: Cheltenham & Gloucester (taken over by Lloyds in 1995), the Halifax, Woolwich, Alliance & Leicester, and Northern Rock in 1997, and others thereafter. Millions of members received windfalls, and the mutual sector shrank dramatically. The long-term results were debated: several demutualised institutions — most spectacularly Northern Rock, nationalised in 2008, and HBOS (formed from Halifax and Bank of Scotland), rescued by Lloyds and the state in 2008–09 — failed during the financial crisis, prompting arguments that the mutual model had provided greater stability. The largest remaining British building society, Nationwide, has repeatedly affirmed its mutual status.

Elsewhere

Mutual insurers demutualised in the United States (MetLife, Prudential), Canada, South Africa (Sanlam, Old Mutual), and Australia. Stock exchanges themselves also demutualised worldwide from the 1990s onward, converting from member-owned associations into listed companies.

Criticism

Critics argue that demutualisation transfers wealth accumulated over generations from future members to current ones, shortens institutional time horizons, and removes the member-focused ethos that distinguished mutuals. Supporters reply that conversion brings capital, efficiency, and market discipline.
