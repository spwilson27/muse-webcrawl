Sickle cell disease

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sickle cell disease is a group of inherited blood disorders caused by a point Mutation in the beta-globin gene (a single base change, swapping glutamate for valine) that produces haemoglobin S. In low oxygen, haemoglobin S polymerises, deforming red blood cells into rigid sickles that block small vessels (causing pain crises, organ damage and strokes) and are destroyed early (causing anaemia). It was the first disease understood at the molecular level — Pauling's 'molecular disease' (1949).

The disease is commonest where malaria is or was endemic — sub-Saharan Africa, the Mediterranean, the Middle East and India — because carriers of one copy (sickle cell trait) are protected against severe malaria: the textbook example of balancing selection maintaining an allele. Inheriting two copies causes the disease, which affects roughly 300,000–400,000 births a year.

Course and care

Complications include pain episodes, infections (the spleen fails early), acute chest syndrome, stroke and shortened life expectancy, though modern care — newborn screening, penicillin prophylaxis, vaccination, hydroxyurea, transfusion — has transformed survival in wealthy countries. Stem-cell (bone-marrow) transplant can cure; in 2023 the first CRISPR gene therapy was approved, making sickle cell the first CRISPR-treated disease.

Research significance

From Pauling's protein chemistry to a single-base diagnosis and now gene editing, sickle cell disease has repeatedly been the proving ground for molecular medicine — while its global treatment gap remains a sharp equity issue, since most births are in low-resource settings.
