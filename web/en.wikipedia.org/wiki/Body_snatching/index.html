Body snatching

Body snatching is the illicit removal of corpses from burial sites, most notoriously for sale to medical schools for dissection and anatomical study. The practice flourished in the 18th and early 19th centuries, when the rapid growth of anatomy teaching collided with a legal supply limited to the bodies of executed murderers. Practitioners were known in Britain as "resurrectionists" or "resurrection men."

History

The Murder Act 1752 allowed the bodies of executed murderers to be dissected, but demand far outstripped supply as private anatomy schools multiplied. Gangs of body snatchers exhumed freshly buried corpses at night and sold them to anatomists, often at high prices. Fear of the practice led to countermeasures: iron coffins, mortsafes, watch houses, and cemetery watchmen. Grave robbing for dissection was not, strictly, theft of the corpse under common law—property could not exist in a dead body—so offenders were usually prosecuted for lesser offences, fuelling public anger.

Public outrage peaked with the Burke and Hare murders of 1828 in Edinburgh, in which sixteen people were murdered for sale to anatomist Robert Knox, and the similar "London Burkers" case of 1831. Parliament responded with the Anatomy Act 1832, which allowed unclaimed bodies from workhouses and hospitals to be used for dissection, largely destroying the economic basis of body snatching.

In culture

Body snatching furnished material for gothic fiction, including Robert Louis Stevenson's story "The Body Snatcher" (1884), and survives in the phrase "body snatcher," later popularized by Jack Finney's 1955 novel The Body Snatchers and its film adaptations.
