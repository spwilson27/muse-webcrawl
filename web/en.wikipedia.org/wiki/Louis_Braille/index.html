Louis Braille (/breɪl/; French: [lwi bʁaj]; 4 January 1809 – 6 January 1852) was a French educator and inventor of a reading and writing system for use by people who are visually impaired. His system is known worldwide simply as braille.

Blinded in both eyes as a result of childhood accidents, Braille mastered his disability while a child. He excelled in his education and received a scholarship to France's Royal Institute for Blind Youth. While still a student there, he began developing a system of tactile code that could allow blind people to read and write quickly and efficiently. Inspired by a military shorthand system presented by Charles Barbier, the young Braille constructed a new method in 1824 that was far more compact and flexible. He presented his work for the first time in 1829, and a second edition was published in 1837, adding symbols for mathematics and music.

Early life

Braille was born in Coupvray, a small town east of Paris. At the age of three, he injured one eye with an awl in his father's harness-making workshop; infection spread to the other eye, and by age five he was blind in both eyes. His family supported his education, and at ten he won admission to the Royal Institute for Blind Youth in Paris, where students were taught to read using embossed print books — a slow, cumbersome system developed by the institute's founder, Valentin Haüy.

The braille system

Braille's innovation was a cell of six raised dots arranged in two columns of three, from whose 63 combinations he derived letters, punctuation, and contractions. Unlike Barbier's 12-dot "night writing", the six-dot cell could fit under a fingertip at a single touch, allowing fluent reading. Braille also devised ways to write the code using a slate and stylus, and later extended the system to musical notation and mathematics. The school's administration long resisted adopting it — students embraced it eagerly, but official recognition came only slowly, and braille was not formally taught at the institute until after Braille's death. He became a respected teacher at the institute, though much of his adult life was marked by ill health; he died of tuberculosis in 1852, at age 43.

Legacy

Within two decades of his death, braille had spread across Europe and North America and was on its way to becoming the worldwide standard for tactile literacy. Braille's remains were transferred to the Panthéon in Paris in 1952, on the centenary of his death, in honour of his contribution to the lives of blind people. His birthplace in Coupvray is preserved as a museum. 4 January, his birthday, is observed as World Braille Day.
