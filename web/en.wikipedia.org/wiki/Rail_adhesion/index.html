Rail adhesion

The adhesion of a railway is the frictional grip between the steel wheels of a train and the steel rails, on which locomotives depend to move the train and to brake. Because both surfaces are smooth steel, the coefficient of friction is low compared with rubber tyres on roads, but the contact does not rely on high friction alone: heavy axle loads produce the adhesive weight that makes traction possible.

Adhesion can be sharply reduced by contamination of the railhead. In autumn, fallen leaves crushed by wheels form a slippery layer of pectin and iron compounds ("leaves on the line"), and rain, frost, grease, and crushed insects can have similar effects. Low adhesion causes wheelslip under acceleration and wheel slide under braking, lengthening stopping distances and causing wheel flats and delays.

Improvement techniques

Railways apply sand from locomotive sandboxes to increase grip, clean the railhead with water jets and laser or plasma systems, and run special railhead treatment trains. Traction control systems on modern locomotives regulate power to limit slip, sometimes exploiting controlled "creep" of the wheelset to maximize adhesion. Rail operators also trim lineside vegetation and adjust timetables in the leaf-fall season.
