Targeted killing

Targeted killing is a form of assassination carried out by governments against a specific individual identified in advance, outside of a formal judicial procedure. In contemporary usage the term is most often associated with strikes — frequently by unmanned aerial vehicles (drones) or special forces — against suspected members of terrorist or militant organisations. It is distinguished from collateral killing and from assassinations conducted by non-state actors, and its legality and effectiveness are matters of continuing international debate.

Armed drones such as the MQ-9 Reaper are the platforms most associated with modern targeted killing campaigns.

History

State-directed killing of named individuals has ancient precedents, but the modern doctrine emerged in the late 20th century. Israel publicly adopted a policy of "targeted assassinations" during the Second Intifada beginning in 2000, directed at militants it accused of planning attacks. After the September 11 attacks, the United States greatly expanded the practice under the 2001 Authorization for Use of Military Force, conducting strikes in Afghanistan, Pakistan, Yemen, Somalia, and Libya, especially during the Obama administration, which formalised procedures through a "disposition matrix" and stated legal criteria.

Methods

  • Drone strikes — missile attacks by remotely piloted aircraft, based on signals and human intelligence and surveillance.
  • Special operations raids — such as the 2011 raid that killed Osama bin Laden in Pakistan.
  • Air strikes and cruise missiles against identified individuals.
  • Personality strikes against named, identified persons, contrasted with "signature strikes" against groups whose behaviour matches a pattern associated with militancy.

Legal debate

Supporters argue targeted killing is lawful self-defence and an application of the laws of armed conflict against combatants in a non-international armed conflict, and that it is more discriminate than conventional warfare. Critics — including UN special rapporteurs, human rights organisations, and some legal scholars — contend that outside active battlefields the practice amounts to extrajudicial execution, violates the right to life under international human rights law, lacks transparency and due process, and causes civilian casualties that are systematically undercounted. Israel's Supreme Court (in the 2006 Targeted Killings case) held the practice could be lawful case-by-case under strict conditions of necessity, proportionality, and reliable identification; the United States government has maintained that its strikes comply with domestic and international law.

Effectiveness and criticism

Debate continues over whether targeted killing degrades militant organisations or instead fuels recruitment and radicalisation through civilian harm and perceptions of injustice. Studies of "leadership decapitation" reach mixed conclusions depending on the group's size, age, and structure.

Notable cases

  • Killing of Osama bin Laden (2011) and Ayman al-Zawahiri (2022) by the United States.
  • Killing of Iranian general Qasem Soleimani by a US drone strike in Baghdad (2020), which raised distinct questions because it targeted a serving state official.
  • Israeli strikes against leaders of Hamas and Hezbollah, including Yahya Sinwar and Hassan Nasrallah (2024).
