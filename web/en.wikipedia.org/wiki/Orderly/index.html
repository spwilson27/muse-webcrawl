Orderly

Roles: Military orderly; hospital orderly

An orderly is a person who carries out support duties in an institution, historically in two main settings: in the military, a soldier assigned to attend an officer or to carry messages and perform headquarters duties; and in hospitals, an attendant (now more often called a healthcare assistant, patient care assistant or porter) who performs non-clinical tasks supporting nursing staff and patients.

Military orderly

The military orderly originated as a soldier detailed to wait upon an officer — the related term batman was used in the British Army for an officer's personal servant-soldier. Orderlies carried orders and dispatches (hence "orderly room" for the unit office, and "orderly officer" or "orderly sergeant" for staff on daily duty rosters). The orderly room became the administrative centre of a regiment or battalion, where daily orders were published and routine discipline administered. First sergeants in the United States Army were historically known as orderly sergeants. With professionalisation and mechanisation the personal-servant role largely disappeared during the 20th century, though orderly-room staff duties persist in terminology.

Hospital orderly

In hospitals, orderlies have traditionally transported patients between wards, operating theatres and departments, moved equipment and supplies, assisted with lifting and repositioning patients, cleaned rooms, and helped with basic patient care under nursing supervision. The occupation grew out of 19th-century hospital staffing, when male attendants were often recruited for heavy work. In modern healthcare systems the title has largely been replaced by more specific roles — nursing assistant, healthcare support worker, porter — with defined training and certification, though "orderly" remains in use in some countries and institutions.

In popular culture

The hospital orderly and the military orderly appear as stock figures in literature and film set in barracks and wards, from the orderlies of Catch-22 to the ward attendants of asylum dramas; the term also survives in phrases such as "orderly officer of the day".
