Chalukya dynasty

The Virupaksha temple at Pattadakal, built under the Chalukyas in the 8th century.

The Chalukya dynasty was a classical Indian dynasty that ruled large parts of southern and central India between the 6th and the 12th centuries. During this period they ruled as three related but distinct dynasties: the Chalukyas of Badami (c. 543–753), the Eastern Chalukyas of Vengi (c. 624–1070), and the Western Chalukyas of Kalyani (c. 973–1189). The name is also spelled Chalukya, Cālukya, or Solanki in different traditions. Their rule marks a defining period in the history of the Deccan, in architecture, literature and administration.

Chalukyas of Badami

Chalukya dynasty
Badami Chalukyas: c. 543–753, capital Badami
Eastern Chalukyas: c. 624–1070, capital Vengi
Western Chalukyas: c. 973–1189, capital Kalyani
Region: Deccan, South India
Languages: Kannada, Sanskrit, Telugu

The dynasty was founded by Pulakeshin I, who fortified Badami (Vatapi) around 543. His grandson Pulakeshin II (reigned c. 610–642) was the most famous of the early Chalukyas: he defeated the north Indian emperor Harsha on the banks of the Narmada, checking Harsha's expansion into the Deccan, and received an embassy from the Persian king Khosrow II, an exchange recorded in the paintings of Ajanta. Pulakeshin II was eventually defeated and killed by the Pallava king Narasimhavarman I, who captured Badami in 642. The Chalukyas recovered under Vikramaditya I, and the dynasty continued until the Rashtrakutas overthrew Kirtivarman II in 753.

Eastern and Western Chalukyas

The Eastern Chalukyas, founded by Pulakeshin II's brother Kubja Vishnuvardhana in the coastal Andhra country, ruled from Vengi for over four centuries and became closely intertwined with the Cholas. The Western Chalukyas emerged in 973 when Tailapa II overthrew the Rashtrakutas and claimed descent from the Badami line; from Kalyani they dominated the western Deccan, fighting the Cholas in celebrated campaigns under Vikramaditya VI (reigned 1076–1126), whose reign also introduced the Chalukya-Vikrama era.

Legacy

The Chalukyas pioneered the Vesara style of temple architecture, blending northern Nagara and southern Dravida forms, seen at Aihole, Badami, Mahakuta and Pattadakal — the last now a UNESCO World Heritage site. Kannada literature flourished under their patronage, and their administrative system influenced the later kingdoms of the Deccan, including the Hoysalas and the empire of Vijayanagara, which claimed inheritance of Chalukya traditions.
