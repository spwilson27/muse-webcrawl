Jinete

Spanish jinetes, light cavalrymen of the Reconquista era.

Jinetes (from Spanish jinete, "horseman" or "rider") were Spanish light cavalry troops of the medieval and early modern periods, particularly prominent during the Reconquista and the Italian Wars. They were famed for their speed, mobility, and skill in skirmishing, and they took their tactics in part from the light horsemen of the Muslim states of Iberia and North Africa.

Equipment and tactics

The jinete typically wore light armor — a helmet, a mail shirt or padded jacket, and an adarga, a distinctive heart-shaped or oval leather shield of Moorish origin. His principal weapons were javelins, which he hurled before closing with a light sword; some also carried a short lance. Riding "a la jineta" — with short stirrups and bent knees, in the style from which the name derives — gave the rider close control of the horse, suiting hit-and-run tactics, reconnaissance, ambushes, and the pursuit of broken enemies rather than the frontal charges of heavy men-at-arms.

History

Jinetes were a mainstay of Castilian and Aragonese armies from the later Middle Ages. Gonzalo Fernández de Córdoba employed them effectively in the Italian Wars, and Spanish light cavalry influenced cavalry practice elsewhere in Europe. With the increasing professionalization of armies in the 16th century, the traditional jinete gradually disappeared as a distinct troop type, though light cavalry traditions associated with the term persisted.
