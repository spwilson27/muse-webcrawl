Jan Harold Brunvand (born 23 March 1933) is an American retired professor, anthropologist and folklorist, widely credited with bringing the study of urban legends to popular attention. His books catalogue hundreds of contemporary legends — stories told as true by people who attribute them to a "friend of a friend" (his coinage, often abbreviated FOAF).

Early life and education

Brunvand was born in Cadillac, Michigan, and raised in Lansing. He studied at Michigan State University and earned his PhD in folklore from Indiana University in 1961, where the folklore programme founded by Stith Thompson emphasised comparative tale-type analysis.

Career

After teaching at the University of Idaho and Southern Illinois University, Brunvand joined the University of Utah in 1966 and taught English and folklore there until his retirement in 1996. His 1981 bestseller The Vanishing Hitchhiker: American Urban Legends and Their Meanings examined tales such as the vanishing hitchhiker, the Kentucky Fried rat and the stolen kidney, analysing both their narrative structure and their social anxieties. It was followed by sequels including The Choking Doberman (1984), The Mexican Pet (1986), Curses! Broiled Again! (1989), The Baby Train (1993) and Too Good to Be True (1999).

Brunvand also wrote a long-running syndicated newspaper column, "Urban Legends", and edited the scholarly Encyclopedia of Urban Legends (2001). He frequently appeared as a sceptical commentator on television and radio programmes investigating viral stories.

Approach and legacy

Brunvand treats urban legends as a living branch of folklore transmitted orally and, increasingly, by email and social media. Critics have noted that his collections sometimes flatten regional variation, but his work established the field for general readers and influenced both academic folkloristics and popular fact-checking culture, including websites devoted to legend verification.
