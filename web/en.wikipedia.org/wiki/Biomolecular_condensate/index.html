Biomolecular condensate

Biomolecular condensates are a class of membrane-less organelles and organelle subdomains formed by the concentration of proteins and nucleic acids through the physical process of phase separation, typically liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS). Unlike classical organelles such as the nucleus or mitochondria, condensates are not enclosed by a lipid membrane; instead, their components are held together by multivalent, weak, transient interactions, and they behave as a distinct liquid phase within the cytoplasm or nucleoplasm.

History

Structures now recognised as condensates — including the nucleolus, Cajal bodies, and germ granules — were described by microscopists in the 19th and early 20th centuries, but their physical basis was not understood. A turning point came in 2009, when Brangwynne, Hyman and colleagues showed that P granules in Caenorhabditis elegans embryos behave like liquid droplets that flow, drip, and fuse. In 2011–2012, work on the nucleolus and on proteins containing low-complexity domains (such as FUS and hnRNPA1) established LLPS as a general mechanism. The term "biomolecular condensate" was proposed by Banani and colleagues in a 2017 review to unify the terminology for these structures.

Mechanism

Condensate formation is driven by multivalent interactions between "scaffold" molecules, often involving intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) of proteins, modular interaction domains, and RNA. When the concentration of components exceeds a saturation threshold, the solution demixes into a dense phase (the condensate) and a dilute phase. Condensates can fuse, wet surfaces, and exchange molecules rapidly with their surroundings; over time some "mature" from liquids into gels or solid-like aggregates, a transition implicated in neurodegenerative diseases such as ALS and Alzheimer's disease, where proteins like FUS, TDP-43, and tau form pathological aggregates.

Examples

  • Nucleolus — site of ribosome biogenesis
  • Stress granules — form in the cytoplasm under cellular stress
  • P-bodies — involved in mRNA storage and decay
  • Germ granules (P granules, nuage) — in germ cells
  • Cajal bodies and nuclear speckles — in the nucleus
  • Transcriptional condensates — clusters at enhancers and super-enhancers
  • Heterochromatin domains associated with HP1 proteins

Functions

Proposed functions include concentrating reactants to accelerate biochemical reactions, sequestering molecules to buffer or suppress reactions, organising the genome, sensing environmental changes, and providing mechanical or structural roles in the cell.
