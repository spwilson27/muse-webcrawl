Paris Is Burning is a 1990 American documentary film directed by Jennie Livingston. Filmed in the mid-to-late 1980s, it chronicles the ball culture of New York City and the African-American, Latino, gay, and transgender communities involved in it. The film takes its title from the Paris Is Burning ball held annually by the House of Xtravaganza's namesake drag ball tradition.

Content

The documentary explores the structure of "houses" — surrogate families led by "mothers" such as Pepper LaBeija, Dorian Corey, Angie Xtravaganza, and Willi Ninja — and the balls at which houses compete for trophies in categories such as "executive realness" and "evening gown". Participants explain the vocabulary of the scene, including voguing, reading, shade, and mopping. Through interviews, the film addresses poverty, racism, homophobia, AIDS, and the performers' aspirations for fame and acceptance. One of its central figures, Venus Xtravaganza, was murdered during filming; her death is addressed in the film's closing passages.

Reception and legacy

The film won the Grand Jury Prize at the 1991 Sundance Film Festival and numerous other awards. It is credited with bringing ball culture and voguing to mainstream attention and remains a landmark of queer cinema, though it has also been debated critically — notably by bell hooks — over questions of exploitation and the director's position as an outsider. In 2016 it was selected for preservation in the United States National Film Registry by the Library of Congress as "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant".
