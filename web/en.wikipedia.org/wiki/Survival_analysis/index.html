Survival analysis

Survival analysis is a branch of statistics for analysing the expected duration of time until one or more events occur — originally death in biological organisms, but in modern usage any event of interest, such as mechanical failure, relapse of a disease, recovery, or a customer cancelling a subscription. It is also called reliability analysis in engineering and duration modelling in economics. The field's distinguishing feature is its handling of censored data, where the event has not yet occurred (or is not observed) for some subjects by the end of the study.

Field: Statistics, biostatistics
Key methods: Kaplan–Meier estimator, Cox proportional hazards model
Central concepts: Survival function, hazard function, censoring

Basic concepts

The survival function S(t) gives the probability that the event has not occurred by time t, while the hazard function describes the instantaneous event rate among those still at risk. The standard non-parametric estimate of S(t) is the Kaplan–Meier (product-limit) estimator, published by Edward Kaplan and Paul Meier in 1958; groups can be compared with the log-rank test.

Models

The most widely used regression model is the Cox proportional hazards model (1972), which estimates covariate effects as hazard ratios without specifying the baseline hazard. Parametric alternatives assume a distribution for event times — such as the exponential, Weibull, log-normal or Gompertz — and include accelerated failure time models. Extensions handle competing risks, recurrent events, time-varying covariates and frailty (unobserved heterogeneity).

Applications

Survival analysis underpins clinical trials and epidemiology, where it measures treatment effects on survival, and is also used in engineering reliability testing, churn prediction, credit risk and unemployment-duration studies.
