Endogenous retrovirus

Endogenous retroviruses (ERVs) are endogenous viral elements in the genome that closely resemble and can be derived from retroviruses. They are abundant in the genomes of jawed vertebrates, and they comprise up to 5–8% of the human genome (lower estimates of ~1%).

Formation

ERVs arise when a retrovirus infects a germ cell — a sperm or egg cell, or their precursors — and its RNA genome is reverse-transcribed into DNA and integrated into the host chromosome. If that germ cell participates in fertilisation, the viral sequence is inherited by all cells of the offspring in a Mendelian fashion. Over millions of years, mutations, deletions and recombination typically inactivate most ERVs, leaving them as genomic fossils, although some retain open reading frames and can produce proteins.

Classification

ERVs are classified by similarity to exogenous retroviruses. Class I ERVs resemble gammaretroviruses, Class II resemble betaretroviruses, and Class III resemble spumaviruses. In humans they are collectively called human endogenous retroviruses (HERVs), named often by the tRNA that primes reverse transcription, e.g. HERV-K (lysine), HERV-W (tryptophan).

Roles in biology

Most ERVs are neutral or deleterious relics, but some have been co-opted (exapted) by the host. The classic example is syncytin, derived from an ERV envelope gene, which is essential for formation of the syncytiotrophoblast layer of the placenta in primates; analogous syncytins evolved independently in other mammals. ERV long terminal repeats (LTRs) also act as promoters and enhancers shaping gene expression, especially in early embryos and immune cells.

Disease associations

Abnormal activation of HERVs has been associated in studies with multiple sclerosis, certain cancers, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis and autoimmune conditions, though causation remains debated. In other animals, some ERVs remain infectious — koala retrovirus (KoRV) is currently endogenising in koala populations, providing a real-time view of the process.
