Hedge (finance)

Hedging
PurposeReduce exposure to adverse price movements
Common instrumentsFutures, forwards, options, swaps
Related conceptsRisk management, basis risk, hedge accounting

A hedge offsets gains and losses between an exposure and a derivative position.

A hedge is an investment position intended to offset potential losses or gains that may be incurred by a companion investment. In simple terms, a hedge is used to reduce any substantial losses or gains suffered by an individual or an organization. A hedge can be constructed from many types of financial instruments, including stocks, exchange-traded funds, insurance, forward contracts, swaps, options, and futures contracts.

How hedging works

A producer exposed to a commodity price — for example, a wheat farmer or an airline buying jet fuel — can lock in a price by selling futures contracts or buying put options, sacrificing some upside in exchange for certainty. The effectiveness of a hedge depends on the correlation between the hedged item and the hedging instrument; imperfect correlation creates basis risk. The ratio of the hedge position to the exposure is the hedge ratio, and delta hedging in options markets adjusts this ratio as prices move.

Hedge accounting and regulation

Under accounting standards such as IFRS 9 and US GAAP, qualifying hedges (fair value, cash flow, and net investment hedges) may receive special accounting treatment that aligns the recognition of gains and losses on the derivative with those on the hedged item, provided the relationship is documented and effective.

Hedgers and hedge funds

Commercial hedgers contrast with speculators in futures markets. Despite their name, modern hedge funds often employ leveraged, speculative strategies rather than pure hedging, though the original 1949 Alfred Winslow Jones fund was a genuinely hedged long/short equity vehicle.
