Nucleic acid denaturation

Nucleic acid denaturation is the separation of the two strands of a double-stranded nucleic acid such as DNA, breaking the hydrogen bonds between complementary bases. It occurs when DNA is exposed to high temperature, extremes of pH, or chemical agents such as formamide and urea. The reverse process, in which complementary single strands re-form a double helix, is called renaturation or annealing (hybridisation).

Melting temperature

Heating a DNA solution causes a cooperative transition: absorbance at 260 nm increases by roughly 40 percent (the hyperchromic effect) as the strands separate. The temperature at which half the molecules are denatured is the melting temperature, Tm. Because G–C pairs are held by three hydrogen bonds and stack more strongly, Tm rises with GC content; salt concentration also stabilises the helix by shielding phosphate repulsion.

Applications

Thermal denaturation is the first step of every polymerase chain reaction (PCR) cycle, typically 94–98 °C, separating the template strands so primers can anneal. Denaturation is also exploited in Southern and Northern blotting, fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH), DNA microarrays and sequencing library preparation. Alkaline denaturation is used in plasmid minipreparations, where chromosomal and plasmid DNA behave differently on neutralisation.

Denaturation in the cell

In vivo, strand separation is achieved enzymatically at near-constant temperature: helicases unwind DNA at replication forks and in transcription, while single-strand binding proteins stabilise the separated strands.
