Athenian democracy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Athenian democracy was the system of direct democracy developed in the Greek city-state of Athens from the late sixth century BC. It is the earliest known democracy and a reference point for democratic theory ever since. Under it, the Assembly (ekklesia) of all adult male citizens met on the Pnyx some forty times a year to decide policy by majority vote; a Council of 500, chosen annually by lot from the demes, prepared business; and large popular courts (dikasteria) of hundreds of jurors, also allotted, judged cases and reviewed laws.

The system's architects were Solon (594 BC, economic and legal reform), Cleisthenes (508/7 BC, who reorganised citizens into ten tribes and 139 demes), Ephialtes and Pericles (who introduced pay for jurors and transferred powers from the Areopagus). It was twice overthrown by oligarchic coups (411 and 404 BC) and twice restored, and lasted, with interruptions, until Macedonian domination after 322 BC.

Limits and legacy

Athenian citizenship excluded women, slaves and resident foreigners (metics), who together formed the large majority of the population; the democracy rested on slave labour and, in the fifth century, on empire. Critics ancient and modern — Plato, whose Republic ranks democracy just above tyranny; Aristotle, more sympathetic — debated its tendency to demagoguery and mob decision (the execution of Socrates in 399 BC; the Sicilian Expedition vote). Its institutions — sortition, rotation, ostracism, public pay for office — remain a standing repertoire for modern democratic reformers seeking to deepen participation beyond elections.
