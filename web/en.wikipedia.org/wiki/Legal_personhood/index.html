Legal personhood

Legal personhood is the quality of being a legal person — a bearer of legal rights and duties capable of taking part in legal relations: owning property, entering contracts, suing and being sued, and claiming the protection of courts. All adult human beings possess legal personhood, as do artificial entities such as corporations; the boundaries of the concept, particularly for fetuses, animals, and natural features, remain contested.

Nature of the concept

Philosophers of law distinguish several incidents of personhood: the capacity to bear rights, the capacity to bear duties, and the standing to enforce them. The bundle conferred on non-human persons is usually narrower than that of humans — a corporation, for example, holds property and speech rights in many jurisdictions but cannot vote or marry. Moral personhood, the status of being an object of moral concern, is a related but distinct idea; some theorists argue the law should track it, others that legal personhood is a pragmatic device only.

Historical development

Roman law recognized the persona as the entity capable of rights, while slaves were largely treated as property — a reminder that legal personhood has not always extended to all humans. In English law, married women long lacked independent legal personality under coverture, a disability removed by the Married Women's Property Acts of the nineteenth century. The recognition of the corporation as a person distinct from its members, settled in Salomon v Salomon (1897), underpins modern company law.

Contested cases

Animals. Litigants including the Nonhuman Rights Project have sought habeas corpus for chimpanzees and elephants, arguing that autonomous, cognitively complex beings are persons for the purpose of bodily liberty. Courts in the United States have so far rejected the claim, the New York Court of Appeals ruling in 2022 that the elephant Happy is not a person under habeas law.

Nature. Ecuador's 2008 constitution grants rights to nature (Pachamama); New Zealand has vested legal personality in the Whanganui River and in Te Urewera; India's Uttarakhand High Court declared the Ganges and Yamuna legal persons in 2017, a ruling later stayed. These measures typically appoint guardians to defend the feature's interests.

Beginning of life. Jurisdictions differ on when human personhood begins; in many, full legal personality begins at live birth, while some constitutions and recent statutes protect life from conception, with major consequences for abortion law, as illustrated by the U.S. Supreme Court's decision in Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022).

Artificial intelligence. Proposals to grant electronic personhood to advanced AI systems were debated in the European Parliament in 2017 but widely opposed by experts; no major jurisdiction has adopted them.
