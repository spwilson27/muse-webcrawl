Passing route

A route is a pattern or path that a receiver, tight end, or running back in gridiron football runs in order to get open for a forward pass. Routes are usually run in combination and in coordination with the other receivers on the field, forming a passing concept designed to attack particular coverages.

A route tree diagram showing common receiver routes.

Common routes

RouteDescription
FlatThe receiver runs to the flat area near the sideline, usually only a few yards downfield.
SlantA quick, shallow route in which the receiver takes a few steps forward, then cuts sharply at roughly 45 degrees toward the middle of the field.
Hitch (stop/curl)The receiver runs downfield, then stops and turns back toward the quarterback; a curl continues slightly inside.
OutThe receiver runs downfield and cuts at 90 degrees toward the sideline.
In (dig)A deeper version cutting across the middle of the field, typically 12–15 yards downfield.
CornerThe receiver threatens a post, then breaks toward the corner of the end zone.
PostThe receiver runs downfield and cuts at an angle toward the goal post, attacking the deep middle.
Go (fly/fade/streak)A straight deep route intended to outrun coverage down the sideline.
WheelUsually run from the backfield: the player flares toward the flat, then turns up the sideline.
ScreenThe receiver catches the ball behind the line of scrimmage behind a wall of blockers.

Route tree

Coaches often organise routes into a "route tree", a numbered or named diagram from the perspective of an outside receiver, with even numbers historically used for routes breaking inside and odd numbers for routes breaking outside in many systems. Receivers adjust routes based on the coverage they see — so-called "option routes" or "choice routes" — which requires the receiver and quarterback to read the defence identically.
