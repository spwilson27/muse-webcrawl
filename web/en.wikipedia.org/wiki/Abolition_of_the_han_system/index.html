Abolition of the han system

Haihan-chiken (廃藩置県)
Date29 August 1871 (14th day, 7th month, Meiji 4)
Issued byGovernment of the Emperor Meiji, Empire of Japan
EffectSome 260 feudal domains (han) replaced by centrally appointed prefectures
Key figuresŌkubo Toshimichi, Kido Takayoshi, Saigō Takamori, Ōkuma Shigenobu

The abolition of the han system (Japanese: 廃藩置県, haihan-chiken, "abolishing the domains and establishing prefectures") was the Imperial Japanese government's edict of 1871 that replaced the feudal domains (han) of the Tokugawa order, each ruled by a daimyo, with prefectures governed by officials appointed by the central government in Tokyo. Together with the return of the land registers (hanseki hōkan) in 1869, it marks the decisive step in the creation of a unitary Japanese state during the Meiji Restoration.

Background

After the Boshin War and the fall of the Tokugawa shogunate in 1868, Japan remained divided into more than 260 semi-autonomous domains. In 1869 the daimyo of Satsuma, Chōshū, Tosa and Hizen led the way in "returning" their registers to the emperor, and the other lords were ordered to follow; the daimyo stayed on as imperial governors of their domains (chihanji), so dual authority persisted and government finances and policy remained fragmented.

The edict

Reformers including Kido Takayoshi and Ōkubo Toshimichi, backed by troops assembled from Satsuma, Chōshū and Tosa, persuaded the court to act. On 29 August 1871 an imperial edict abolished the domains outright. The roughly 260 former daimyo-governors were dismissed, pensioned with stipends, and ordered to reside in Tokyo; in their place some 300 prefectures (soon consolidated to 72, and eventually 47) were created under appointed governors.

Consequences

The reform eliminated the last institutional basis of feudal particularism: the central government now controlled taxation, law and administration nationwide, clearing the way for the land-tax reform of 1873, conscription, and a unified legal system. Discontent among the former samurai class at the loss of status fed the shizoku rebellions of the 1870s, culminating in the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877, but the prefectural system itself endures in Japan today.
