Special drawing rights

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Special drawing rights (SDRs, ISO code XDR) are an international reserve asset created by the International Monetary Fund in 1969 to supplement member countries' official reserves. The SDR is not a currency but a claim on the freely usable currencies of IMF members; its value is based on a basket of the US dollar, the euro, the Chinese renminbi (added 2016), the Japanese yen and the pound sterling, weighted by trade and financial criteria and reviewed every five years.

SDRs are allocated to members in proportion to their IMF quotas, most notably in 2009 (during the global financial crisis) and in 2021, when an allocation of about US$650 billion — the largest ever — was made to support pandemic recovery; poorer countries received proportionally little because allocations follow quotas, prompting schemes to “rechannel” rich countries' SDRs to development and climate funds. Proposals to expand the SDR's role — as an embryonic world currency in Keynes's original bancor spirit — recur after crises but face the objection that the IMF lacks a treasury and taxing power behind it.
