Siege of Candia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Siege of Candia (1648–1669) was a conflict during the Cretan War (1645–1669) in which Ottoman forces besieged the Venetian-held city of Candia (modern Heraklion) on the island of Crete. Lasting more than twenty-one years, it is among the longest sieges in recorded history.

Background

The Ottoman invasion of Crete began in 1645, and by 1648 most of the island had fallen except for Candia, protected by massive bastioned fortifications designed by Michele Sanmicheli. The city was defended by Venetian troops under captains-general including Francesco Morosini, reinforced over the years by volunteers and contingents from France, the Papal States, Malta and other Christian powers.

The siege

The Ottomans maintained a blockade and conducted repeated assaults, mining operations and artillery bombardments over two decades, while Venetian fleets tried to sever Ottoman supply lines by attacking the Dardanelles. A major French expedition under the Duke of Beaufort in 1669 ended in disaster when Beaufort was killed and the French withdrew. With supplies exhausted and no relief in prospect, Morosini negotiated an honourable surrender.

Surrender and aftermath

By the treaty of 27 September 1669, the garrison and any inhabitants who wished to leave were evacuated with their property to Venetian territories. Venice retained only three small island fortresses off Crete — Gramvousa, Souda and Spinalonga. The loss of Crete ended Venice's era as a major eastern Mediterranean power, while the siege had cost the Ottomans, by contemporary estimates, well over 100,000 men.
