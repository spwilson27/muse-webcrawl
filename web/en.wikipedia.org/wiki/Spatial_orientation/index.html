Spatial orientation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Spatial orientation is the ability of an organism to maintain its body orientation and posture in relation to the surrounding environment — physical space — at rest and during motion. It depends on the integration of sensory information from the visual system, the vestibular system of the inner ear, and proprioceptors in muscles and joints.

Mechanisms

The vestibular apparatus detects angular and linear acceleration of the head through the semicircular canals and otolith organs. The brain combines these signals with visual cues and proprioceptive feedback to estimate the direction of gravity and the body's position in space. When these signals conflict, as in motion sickness or in the spatial disorientation experienced by pilots, orientation can fail, sometimes with fatal consequences in aviation.

In animals

Many animals possess specialised orientation abilities, including magnetoreception in migratory birds and path integration in desert ants, which allows them to return to their nest in a straight line after a winding search for food.
