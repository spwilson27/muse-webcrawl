Henry Cowell

Henry Dixon Cowell (March 11, 1897 – December 10, 1965) was an American composer, music theorist, pianist, teacher, and publisher. A leading figure of musical modernism, he pioneered techniques including tone clusters, playing directly on piano strings, and polyrhythms derived from the harmonic series.

Music

Cowell's piano pieces such as The Tides of Manaunaun (1917) and Dynamic Motion (1916) introduced forearm tone clusters that shocked early audiences. Works like The Banshee (1925) are played entirely inside the piano, sweeping and plucking the strings. His book New Musical Resources (1930) theorised rhythm in analogy to harmony and influenced generations of composers, including his student John Cage. The Rhythmicon, built with Léon Theremin, was an early drum machine based on these ideas.

Career

Deeply interested in the world's music, Cowell studied and promoted non-Western traditions, founded the journal New Music to publish ultramodern scores, taught at the New School, and later wrote hymns and fuguing tunes in a more accessible American idiom, as well as works for koto, shakuhachi, and other instruments. His career was interrupted by a 1936 morals conviction and imprisonment at San Quentin, where he continued composing and teaching before a pardon in 1942.
