Sirenia, commonly referred to as sea cows or sirenians, is an order of fully aquatic, herbivorous mammals that inhabit swamps, rivers, estuaries, marine wetlands and coastal marine waters. The four living species comprise the dugong (Dugong dugon) and three manatees (Trichechus spp.). Sirenians are the only herbivorous marine mammals and are classified within the clade Afrotheria, together with elephants — their closest living relatives — and hyraxes.

Anatomy and behaviour

Sirenians have stout, fusiform bodies with dense, heavy bones that act as ballast, valved nostrils, and no external hind limbs; propulsion comes from a horizontal tail fluke — paddle-shaped in manatees, notched and whale-like in the dugong. They surface to breathe and graze on seagrasses and aquatic plants, consuming up to 10–15% of their body weight daily. Their lungs are elongated and positioned along the backbone, aiding horizontal buoyancy control. Manatees also possess unique ever-replacing "marching molars".

Distribution and species

The dugong ranges across the Indo-Pacific from East Africa to Australia. The West Indian manatee inhabits the Caribbean and the Gulf of Mexico and Amazon estuaries' Atlantic coasts; the Amazonian manatee lives in the Amazon basin; and the West African manatee ranges along West African coasts and rivers. All four species are listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN.

Evolution and extinct forms

Sirenians evolved about 50 million years ago from four-legged shore-dwelling ancestors related to the ancestors of elephants; early forms such as Prorastomus and Pezosiren from Jamaica were amphibious. The order was once far more diverse. The largest sirenian, Steller's sea cow of the North Pacific, reached 9 metres but was hunted to extinction by 1768, only 27 years after its scientific discovery.

Conservation

Threats include habitat loss of seagrass meadows, boat strikes (the leading cause of death for Florida manatees), entanglement, and hunting. Sirenians are protected internationally, and their gentle behaviour and supposed resemblance to human figures at sea are thought to underlie mermaid legends — reflected in the order's name, after the sirens of Greek myth.
