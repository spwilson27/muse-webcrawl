Leukemia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Leukemia (leukaemia in British English) is a group of cancers of the blood-forming tissues, in which abnormal white blood cells multiply uncontrollably in the bone marrow, crowding out normal blood production. The name means 'white blood', after the pale, white-cell-rich blood of early described patients. Leukemias are classed as acute or chronic, and lymphoid or myeloid.

Types and symptoms

The four main types are acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL — the commonest childhood cancer), acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL) and chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML). Symptoms arise from too few normal cells: anaemia and fatigue, infections from lack of working white cells, and bleeding from low platelets. Diagnosis is by blood counts and bone marrow examination, refined by genetic tests that now guide treatment.

Treatment

Treatment includes Chemotherapy, targeted drugs, immunotherapy and stem cell (bone marrow) transplantation. CML was transformed by imatinib (from 2001), a targeted inhibitor of its characteristic BCR-ABL fusion protein, converting a fatal disease into a manageable chronic one for most patients; childhood ALL cure rates now exceed 85–90% in well-resourced health systems.
