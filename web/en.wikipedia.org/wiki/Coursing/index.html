Coursing

Coursing is the pursuit of game animals by sighthounds — dogs that hunt primarily by sight and speed rather than by scent. In its traditional form, two or more hounds chase a live quarry, most famously a hare, across open countryside while judges assess the dogs' speed, agility, and endurance. Coursing is one of the oldest of all canine sports and was historically practised by nobility throughout Europe, the Middle East, and Central Asia.

History

Depictions of long, slender hunting dogs appear in ancient Egyptian and Greek art, and the Roman writer Arrian described coursing in detail in the second century CE. In medieval and early modern England, coursing hares with greyhounds was a privilege regulated by the forest laws. In the sixteenth century, Thomas Howard, Duke of Norfolk, drew up rules for competitive coursing, and the first recorded coursing meetings took place under Elizabeth I. The National Coursing Club was founded in Britain in 1858, and the Waterloo Cup, first run in 1836 at Altcar near Liverpool, became the sport's most famous event.

Competitive coursing

In formal competition, hounds are slipped in pairs and judged on the "course": points are awarded for speed in running up to the hare, for turning it, for wrenching and killing it, and for general agility. A judge on horseback follows the course and decides the winner of each pairing, and meetings proceed on a knockout basis. In Ireland, coursing traditionally took place in enclosed park courses, where hares were netted in advance and released for each course.

Regulation and controversy

Hare coursing with live quarry has been controversial because of concerns for the welfare of the hares. In England and Wales, the Hunting Act 2004 made hare coursing illegal, and the Waterloo Cup was last run in 2005. Coursing remains legal under licence in Ireland, where hares are muzzled-dog coursed in parks and hares are released after events. Illegal hare coursing by trespassers continues to be a policing problem in parts of rural Britain. Lure coursing, in which dogs chase a mechanical lure, is a widely practised non-live alternative.
