Reintroduction

Species reintroduction
FieldConservation biology
GoalRe-establish a self-sustaining population within a species' former range
GuidelinesIUCN Guidelines for Reintroductions and Other Conservation Translocations (2013)

Species reintroduction is the deliberate release of a species into the wild, from captivity or other areas where the organism is capable of survival. It is a conservation strategy applied when a species has become locally extinct (extirpated) in part of its range but survives elsewhere. The goal of species reintroduction is to establish a healthy, genetically diverse, self-sustaining population in an area where it has been extirpated, or to augment an existing population.

Planning and methods

Successful reintroductions require that the original causes of decline — such as habitat loss, hunting, or introduced predators — have been removed or sufficiently reduced. Candidate release sites are assessed for habitat quality, food availability, and risks. Source animals may come from captive breeding programmes or be translocated from thriving wild populations. Animals are often released using a "soft release", being held in acclimatisation enclosures at the release site, or a "hard release" directly into the wild. Post-release monitoring, sometimes using radio tracking, is considered essential.

Notable examples

  • Grey wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park in 1995, with wide-ranging effects on the ecosystem.
  • The Arabian oryx, extinct in the wild by the early 1970s, was reintroduced to Oman and other Arabian states from zoo stock.
  • The California condor was brought back from a population of just 27 individuals through captive breeding and release.
  • European bison were reintroduced across parts of Europe after the species survived only in captivity.
  • Eurasian beavers have been reintroduced to many European countries, including Britain after an absence of roughly 400 years.
  • The Przewalski's horse was reintroduced to Mongolia, its native habitat.

Challenges

Reintroduced animals may lack survival skills learned in the wild, suffer high mortality from predation or starvation, or come into conflict with human activities such as livestock farming. Genetic considerations matter: small founder populations risk inbreeding depression. Reintroduction is also expensive and may fail if habitat quality has changed since the species disappeared. The IUCN's 2013 guidelines stress feasibility studies, risk assessment, stakeholder engagement and long-term monitoring.

Related concepts

Reinforcement (or augmentation) adds individuals to an existing population of the same species. Assisted colonisation moves a species outside its historic range in response to threats such as climate change, and is more controversial. Rewilding projects sometimes use reintroduction of keystone species as a central tool.
