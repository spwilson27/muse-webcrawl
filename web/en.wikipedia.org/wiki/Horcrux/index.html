A Horcrux is a fictional object in J. K. Rowling's Harry Potter series. It is a receptacle in which a Dark wizard or witch has hidden a fragment of their soul, created by committing murder — "the supreme act of evil", which splits the soul — and then performing a further, horrific spell. As long as a Horcrux survives, its creator cannot truly die, even if their body is destroyed. The concept is introduced and explained by Professor Horace Slughorn in Harry Potter and the Half-Blood Prince (2005) and forms the central quest of Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows (2007).

Voldemort's Horcruxes

Lord Voldemort, intending to split his soul seven ways, created six Horcruxes deliberately and one accidentally:

  • Tom Riddle's diary — destroyed by Harry Potter with a basilisk fang (Chamber of Secrets).
  • Marvolo Gaunt's ring — destroyed by Albus Dumbledore with the Sword of Gryffindor, at grave cost to his hand.
  • Salazar Slytherin's locket — destroyed by Ron Weasley with the Sword of Gryffindor.
  • Helga Hufflepuff's cup — destroyed by Hermione Granger with a basilisk fang.
  • Rowena Ravenclaw's diadem — destroyed by Fiendfyre in the Room of Requirement.
  • Nagini, his snake — killed by Neville Longbottom with the Sword of Gryffindor.
  • Harry Potter himself — an unintended Horcrux, created when Voldemort's killing curse rebounded on Halloween 1981; the soul fragment in Harry was destroyed when Voldemort struck him with the Killing Curse in the Forbidden Forest.

Destruction

Because a Horcrux is the opposite of a human being — the container depends on the soul fragment rather than vice versa — it can only be destroyed by things so destructive that the vessel is put "beyond magical repair", such as basilisk venom (with which the Sword of Gryffindor becomes impregnated) or Fiendfyre. Remorse is the only way to reintegrate a split soul, at excruciating pain and possible mortal risk.

Themes and origins

The word combines resonances of "horror" and crux ("cross"/"torment"). Critics have compared the Horcrux with the phylactery of the lich in fantasy gaming and with older folk motifs of the soul hidden outside the body (as in "The Giant Who Had No Heart in His Body"). Within the novels, creating Horcruxes embodies Voldemort's fear of death and mutilation of his own humanity, contrasting with the series' treatment of death as "the next great adventure".
