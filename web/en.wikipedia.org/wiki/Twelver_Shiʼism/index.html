Twelver Shi'ism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Twelver Shi'ism

Type: Branch of Shia Islam

Adherents: The majority of Shia Muslims; large populations in Iran, Iraq, Azerbaijan, and Bahrain, with communities in Lebanon, Pakistan, India, and elsewhere

Key doctrine: Belief in twelve divinely ordained Imams, the last of whom, Muhammad al-Mahdi, is in occultation

Scripture: The Quran and the hadith of the Prophet and the Imams

Twelver Shi'ism (Arabic: ithnā ʿashariyya), also known as Imami Shi'ism or Ja'fari Shi'ism, is the largest branch of Shia Islam, comprising roughly 85 percent of all Shia Muslims. Twelvers believe that the Islamic prophet Muhammad designated Ali ibn Abi Talib and his descendants — twelve in total — as his successors and as the legitimate leaders (Imams) of the Muslim community. Each Imam is believed to be divinely appointed, infallible (ma'ṣūm), and possessed of special knowledge of the inner meaning of revelation.

Twelver Shi'ism is the state religion of Iran and the faith of the majority in Iraq, Azerbaijan, and Bahrain.

The twelfth and final Imam, Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Mahdi, is believed to have entered a state of occultation (ghayba) in the ninth century and to remain alive but hidden; Twelvers await his return as the Mahdi, who will establish justice on earth before the Day of Judgment. In his absence, religious authority is exercised by qualified jurists, the mujtahids, whose rulings believers follow through the practice of taqlīd (emulation). Twelver Shi'ism is the official state religion of Iran and the majority faith in Iraq, Azerbaijan, and Bahrain, with significant minorities in Lebanon, Pakistan, India, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province, and a worldwide diaspora.

The Twelve Imams

The doctrine takes its name from the twelve Imams recognized as Muhammad's successors:

  1. Ali ibn Abi Talib (d. 661) — cousin and son-in-law of Muhammad; regarded by Shias as the first Imam and rightful caliph.
  2. Hasan ibn Ali (d. 670) — elder son of Ali and Fatimah.
  3. Husayn ibn Ali (d. 680) — killed with his companions at the Battle of Karbala; his martyrdom is commemorated annually during Muharram, culminating in Ashura.
  4. Ali ibn Husayn Zayn al-Abidin (d. c. 713)
  5. Muhammad ibn Ali al-Baqir (d. c. 732)
  6. Ja'far ibn Muhammad al-Sadiq (d. 765) — systematizer of Twelver law; the Twelver school of jurisprudence (fiqh) is named Ja'fari after him.
  7. Musa ibn Ja'far al-Kazim (d. 799)
  8. Ali ibn Musa al-Rida (d. 818) — buried in Mashhad, Iran, one of the holiest Shia shrines.
  9. Muhammad ibn Ali al-Jawad (d. 835)
  10. Ali ibn Muhammad al-Hadi (d. 868)
  11. Hasan ibn Ali al-Askari (d. 874)
  12. Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Mahdi — the Hidden Imam, believed to be in occultation since 874 and awaited as the Mahdi.

History

Origins and early development

The Shia movement began as support for Ali's claim to leadership after Muhammad's death in 632, a claim Twelvers ground in Muhammad's designation of Ali at Ghadir Khumm. The martyrdom of Husayn at Karbala in 680 became the defining event of Shia identity. The specifically Twelver doctrine crystallized after the death of the eleventh Imam, Hasan al-Askari, in 874, when his young son and successor disappeared. The community entered the period of the Minor Occultation (874–941), during which four successive deputies (safīrs) were believed to mediate between the Hidden Imam and his followers. With the death of the fourth deputy in 941 and a letter attributed to the Imam declaring that no further deputy would be appointed, the Major Occultation began — the era, continuing to the present, in which the Imam is inaccessible and scholars must derive guidance from the Quran and transmitted traditions.

Medieval consolidation

Under the Shia Buyid dynasty (934–1062), which ruled much of Iraq and western Iran, Twelver scholarship flourished in Baghdad: scholars such as al-Shaykh al-Mufid (d. 1022) and Sharif al-Murtada (d. 1044) gave Twelver theology its classical rationalist form. In the following centuries the centers of learning shifted to Hilla in Iraq and later to Jabal Amil in Lebanon and Isfahan. Twelver jurisprudence divided into two methodological camps — the rationalist Usuli school, which endorses independent legal reasoning (ijtihād) by living jurists, and the traditionalist Akhbari school, which restricts reliance to the transmitted reports of the Imams. The Usuli position ultimately prevailed, laying the institutional groundwork for the modern clerical hierarchy.

Safavid era and modern period

In 1501 the Safavid dynasty proclaimed Twelver Shi'ism the state religion of Persia, transforming Iran into a predominantly Shia society and sharply defining the sectarian geography of the Middle East. The Safavids imported Arab Shia scholars to staff the new religious establishment, and under the later Safavids the Usuli clerisy gained institutional independence, supported by religious taxes administered by the scholars themselves. Twelver scholarship also produced a rich philosophical tradition, culminating in figures such as Mulla Sadra (d. 1640).

In the twentieth century, the Iranian cleric Ruhollah Khomeini articulated the doctrine of velāyat-e faqīh ("guardianship of the jurist"), arguing that in the Imam's absence a qualified jurist should hold supreme political authority. This doctrine became the constitutional foundation of the Islamic Republic of Iran after the revolution of 1979. Other leading Twelver authorities, including Grand Ayatollah Ali al-Sistani in Najaf, have maintained a quieter, more traditionally limited view of clerical political involvement, and the scope of juristic authority remains debated within the tradition.

Beliefs

Principles of religion

Twelvers enumerate five principles of religion (uṣūl al-dīn):

  • Tawhid — the oneness of God.
  • 'Adl — divine justice; unlike Ash'ari theology, Twelver (Mu'tazili-influenced) thought holds that God necessarily acts justly and that human beings possess free will and moral responsibility.
  • Nubuwwa — prophethood.
  • Imama — the Imamate: divinely appointed leadership by the Imams after Muhammad.
  • Ma'ad — the Day of Resurrection and judgment.

Theology and law

Twelver theology is strongly rationalist: reason (ʿaql) is itself a source of law alongside the Quran, the Sunna of the Prophet and the Imams, and scholarly consensus. The Ja'fari school permits temporary marriage (mutʿa), differs from Sunni schools in certain rules of inheritance, divorce, ablution, and the call to prayer, and authorizes combining the noon and afternoon and the evening and night prayers. Twelvers maintain their own hadith corpus, centered on the Four Books — al-Kāfī, Man lā yaḥḍuruhu al-faqīh, Tahdhīb al-aḥkām, and al-Istibṣār — which transmit reports from the Prophet and the Imams.

Practices

Beyond the standard Islamic obligations of prayer, fasting, almsgiving, and pilgrimage, distinctive Twelver practices include the commemoration of Husayn's martyrdom in Muharram (with processions, elegies, and in some communities ritual self-flagellation, which many senior clerics discourage or forbid), the celebration of the Prophet's and Imams' birthdays, and pilgrimage (ziyāra) to the shrines of the Imams in Karbala, Najaf, Mashhad, Qom, and Samarra. Twelvers also pay khums, a one-fifth tax on certain gains, half of which (the "share of the Imam") is administered by the jurists and underpins the financial independence of the seminaries (hawzas) of Qom and Najaf.

Clerical hierarchy

In the Usuli system, every lay believer chooses a living jurist to emulate — a marjaʿ al-taqlīd ("source of emulation") — whose rulings on the details of religious practice are binding on his followers. Senior mujtahids hold the title of ayatollah, and the most widely followed that of grand ayatollah. The seminaries of Qom and Najaf remain the principal centers of Twelver learning. This decentralized, follower-funded structure historically gave the Twelver clergy an independence from the state unusual in the Islamic world.
