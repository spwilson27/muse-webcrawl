James I (late July 1394 – 21 February 1437) was King of Scots from 1406 until his assassination in 1437. The youngest of three sons of King Robert III, he was captured by the English while travelling to France as a boy in 1406 and spent 18 years as a prisoner of Henry IV and Henry V of England. Released in 1424 after a ransom was agreed, he returned to a Scotland governed in his absence by the Albany Stewarts, and set about restoring royal authority with energy — and ruthlessness — until his murder at Perth.

Captivity

James's elder brother David, Duke of Rothesay, had died in custody in 1402, and in 1406 Robert III sent the 11-year-old James to France for safety. His ship, the Maryenknyght of Danzig, was taken by English pirates off Flamborough Head, and the prince was delivered to Henry IV. Robert III died weeks later, leaving James king in captivity, while his uncle Robert Stewart, Duke of Albany, ruled as governor and showed little haste to ransom him. James received an excellent education at the English court, observed Henry V's methods of kingship, and campaigned with the English in France. In 1424 he married Joan Beaufort, a niece of Henry IV whom he had met at court, and was released after a ransom of £40,000 was agreed, nominally as "expenses" for his upkeep.

Reign

James moved quickly against the Albany Stewarts: in 1425 the Duke of Albany's son Murdoch and his family were arrested and executed after a show trial at Stirling. James reformed taxation and the administration of justice, held regular parliaments, coined money, patronised the University of St Andrews, and attempted to curb the power of the great magnates and impose order in the Highlands, arresting Highland chiefs en masse at Inverness in 1428. His foreign policy kept the Auld Alliance with France while avoiding open war with England.

The Kingis Quair

James is traditionally credited as the author of The Kingis Quair ("The King's Book"), a long dream-vision poem in rhyme royal recounting his captivity and his love for Joan Beaufort; it is among the finest works of early Scots literature, though its authorship has been debated.

Assassination

Discontented nobles, led by Sir Robert Graham and abetted by the king's uncle Walter Stewart, Earl of Atholl, who hoped for the throne, broke into the Dominican friary at Perth on the night of 20–21 February 1437. James hid in a privy vault but was discovered and stabbed to death; Queen Joan was wounded but escaped. The conspirators were hunted down and executed with exceptional severity. James was succeeded by his six-year-old son, James II.
