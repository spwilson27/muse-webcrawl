Middle High German (abbreviated MHG; German: Mittelhochdeutsch) is the term for the form of German spoken in the High Middle Ages, conventionally dated from about 1050 to 1350. It succeeded Old High German and preceded Early New High German, and was spoken in the southern and central parts of the German-speaking area (the contemporary northern language being Middle Low German).

Characteristics

Middle High German is distinguished from Old High German principally by the weakening (reduction) of full vowels in unstressed syllables to schwa (e), and from modern German by features including its fuller system of diphthongs (later monophthongised and lengthened in the transition to New High German) and a still richly inflected system of four cases and strong/weak adjective and noun declensions. Scholarly "normalised" Middle High German, used in editions and grammars, is an artificial standard based mainly on the courtly literary language of the period around 1200.

Literature

The Middle High German period produced the classical age of medieval German literature, patronised by the Hohenstaufen court. Its monuments include the heroic epic the Nibelungenlied (c. 1200), the courtly romances of Hartmann von Aue, Wolfram von Eschenbach's Parzival and Gottfried von Strassburg's Tristan, and the lyric poetry of the Minnesang, above all Walther von der Vogelweide. After about 1250 the flourishing declined, and the fourteenth century saw growing use of prose and chancery languages that fed into Early New High German.
