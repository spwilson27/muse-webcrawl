Plastid
Found inPlant and algal cells
TypesChloroplasts, chromoplasts, leucoplasts (amyloplasts, elaioplasts, proteinoplasts)

A plastid is a membrane-bound organelle found in the cells of plants, algae, and some other eukaryotic organisms. Plastids are the site of manufacture and storage of important chemical compounds used by the cell, most famously the chloroplast, where photosynthesis takes place. They are believed to have originated when an early eukaryotic cell engulfed a photosynthetic cyanobacterium — a primary endosymbiosis — and they retain their own small genome and double membrane as evidence of that origin.

Types

All plastids develop from undifferentiated proplastids in meristematic tissue and can interconvert depending on the cell's needs. Chloroplasts are green, photosynthetic plastids containing chlorophyll and internal thylakoid membranes. Chromoplasts synthesize and store carotenoid pigments, giving the yellow, orange, and red colours of many flowers, fruits, and autumn leaves. Colourless leucoplasts store food reserves: amyloplasts store starch (and sense gravity in roots and shoots), elaioplasts store lipids, and proteinoplasts store proteins. Etiolated shoots grown in darkness contain etioplasts, which green into chloroplasts on exposure to light.

Structure and genetics

Plastids are surrounded by two membranes and contain a fluid stroma in which floats a circular DNA molecule and bacterial-type (70S) ribosomes. Most plastid proteins are now encoded in the nucleus and imported; the plastid genome in land plants retains about 100 genes, mainly for photosynthesis and gene expression. Plastids are usually inherited maternally in flowering plants. Secondary endosymbiosis — a eukaryote engulfing a red or green alga — produced plastids with three or four membranes in many algal groups such as diatoms, dinoflagellates, and kelps.

Other functions

Beyond photosynthesis, plastids carry out the synthesis of fatty acids, amino acids, and many hormones and vitamins, and are a major site of nitrogen and sulfur assimilation — making them metabolic hubs of the plant cell, and targets for much genetic engineering of crops.
