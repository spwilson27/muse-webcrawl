Body camera

A body camera or body-worn camera (BWC) is a wearable audio, video, or photographic recording system. Body cameras are used in law enforcement worldwide to record interactions between police officers and the public, collect evidence, and increase accountability. Their use expanded rapidly in the 2010s following high-profile incidents involving police use of force, notably in the United States, where they became a central reform measure adopted by federal, state and local agencies.

History

Early experiments with officer-worn cameras took place in the United Kingdom in the mid-2000s. A frequently cited study in Rialto, California (2012–13) reported sharp drops in use-of-force incidents and civilian complaints when officers wore cameras. Following the 2014 shooting of Michael Brown in Ferguson, Missouri, the US Department of Justice funded national rollout programmes. By the early 2020s most large US police departments had adopted body cameras, though policies on activation, retention and public release vary widely.

Evidence and debate

Research on effectiveness is mixed: some studies find significant reductions in complaints and force, while a large 2017 Washington, D.C. randomised trial found no statistically significant average effects on documented uses of force or complaints. Proponents argue cameras deter misconduct and provide objective evidence; critics raise privacy concerns for victims and bystanders, the costs of data storage, and officers' discretion over when to record.

Other uses

Beyond policing, body-worn recording is used by corrections officers, parking enforcement, healthcare staff facing workplace violence, retail workers, and in sports broadcasting to provide first-person viewpoints.
