Sahul Shelf

The Sahul Shelf is the continental shelf underlying the Arafura Sea and the Timor Sea, extending from the north coast of Australia to New Guinea. It is part of the Australian continental plate and supports the geological concept of Sahul, the single landmass that joined Australia, New Guinea and Tasmania during the low sea levels of the Pleistocene ice ages.

Geography

The shelf is broad and shallow, generally less than 200 metres deep, and includes features such as the Sahul Banks and the Ashmore and Cartier reef systems. Its northwestern edge drops steeply into the Timor Trough, a deepwater boundary that figured in the maritime-boundary negotiations between Australia and Timor-Leste. The shelf is rich in natural gas fields, including the Greater Sunrise deposits.

Human prehistory

During the Last Glacial Maximum, sea levels stood about 120 metres lower, and the exposed shelf formed dry land crossed by the first peoples to reach Sahul at least 50,000–65,000 years ago. Even at the lowest stands, reaching Sahul from Sunda (Southeast Asia's exposed shelf) required sea crossings of up to 90 km across the gaps of Wallacea — among the earliest evidence of seafaring by modern humans. Rising post-glacial seas drowned the shelf, separating New Guinea and Tasmania from mainland Australia.
