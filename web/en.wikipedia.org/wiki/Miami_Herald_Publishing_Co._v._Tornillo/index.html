Citation: 418 U.S. 241 (1974)

Court: Supreme Court of the United States
Holding: Florida's right-of-reply statute for newspapers is unconstitutional

Miami Herald Publishing Co. v. Tornillo, 418 U.S. 241 (1974), is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States concerning freedom of the press. A unanimous Court held that a Florida "right of reply" statute — which required newspapers that criticised a political candidate to print the candidate's reply free of charge — violated the First Amendment by compelling editors to publish material they would otherwise omit.

Background

Pat Tornillo, a candidate for the Florida House of Representatives and executive director of a teachers' union, was criticised in two editorials in the Miami Herald during the 1972 campaign. When the paper refused to print his replies, he invoked Florida Statute § 104.38, dating to 1913, which made it a misdemeanour for a newspaper to refuse a candidate's reply to an attack on his record or character. The Florida courts divided: the circuit court held the statute unconstitutional, while the Florida Supreme Court upheld it, reasoning that it promoted fairness in an era of concentrated media ownership.

Decision

Chief Justice Warren E. Burger's unanimous opinion reversed. The Court held that the choice of material to include in a newspaper is an exercise of editorial control and judgment protected by the First Amendment, and that government compulsion of publication functions as a penalty on the content of the press: editors might respond to the statute by avoiding controversy altogether. The Court distinguished the "fairness doctrine" upheld for broadcasters in Red Lion Broadcasting Co. v. FCC (1969), resting the difference on the scarcity rationale applicable to licensed broadcasting and the absence of any licensing of print. A concurring opinion by Justice Byron White stressed the importance of a press free from governmental intrusion into the editorial process.

Legacy

Tornillo remains the leading precedent for the editorial-discretion principle in American press law: the government may not compel print media to publish what reason tells them not to publish. It is frequently cited in debates about access to media, net neutrality, and state laws regulating content moderation by online platforms.
