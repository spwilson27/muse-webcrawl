Shockley–Queisser limit

The Shockley–Queisser limit is the maximum theoretical efficiency of a solar cell made from a single p–n junction, calculated by William Shockley and Hans-Joachim Queisser in 1961. Under the standard AM1.5 solar spectrum, the limit is about 33.7% for an optimal band gap of around 1.34 eV; for unconcentrated sunlight it is commonly quoted as roughly 33%. It is a fundamental benchmark of photovoltaics, analogous to a Carnot limit for a single-junction device.

Loss mechanisms

The calculation assumes a single junction, one electron–hole pair per absorbed photon, and radiative recombination only. Four unavoidable losses cap the efficiency:

  • Below-band-gap photons are not absorbed at all (about 19% of solar power for silicon).
  • Thermalisation: photons with energy above the band gap lose their excess energy as heat.
  • Radiative recombination: the cell, as a black body, re-emits some absorbed energy.
  • Voltage and fill-factor losses: the operating voltage is necessarily below the band gap potential.

For silicon (band gap 1.1 eV) the limit is around 32%; the best laboratory silicon cells exceed 26%, approaching the practical Auger limit. Gallium arsenide (1.42 eV) sits nearer the optimum.

Exceeding the limit

The limit applies only to a single junction under unconcentrated light. Multi-junction (tandem) cells stack band gaps to capture more of the spectrum and have exceeded 47% under concentration; hot-carrier cells, multiple exciton generation, intermediate bands, and up- and down-conversion are other proposed routes beyond the limit. Concentrating sunlight raises the single-junction limit to about 40.7%.
