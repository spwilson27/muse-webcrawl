Putrefaction

Putrefaction
Stage ofDecomposition (third of five stages)
Typical onset2–3 days after death (temperature-dependent)
Main agentsAnaerobic bacteria (e.g. Clostridium, gut flora)

Putrefaction is the third of the five stages of decomposition, in which the proteins and soft tissues of a dead organism are broken down by bacteria and fungi. It follows fresh and bloat-adjacent autolysis processes and precedes advanced decay and skeletonisation. The term is especially used in forensic pathology, where its signs help estimate the post-mortem interval.

Process

After death, the body's own enzymes digest cells (autolysis), and anaerobic bacteria normally resident in the gut — species of Clostridium, coliforms and others — proliferate and spread through the tissues. Their metabolism of proteins produces gases (hydrogen sulfide, methane, carbon dioxide, ammonia) and the foul-smelling diamines putrescine and cadaverine, named for this process.

Signs

Characteristic changes include a greenish discoloration of the abdomen (from sulfhaemoglobin formation, usually first over the right iliac fossa), venous "marbling", bloating of the face and abdomen with gas, blistering and slippage of the skin, and eventual rupture and purging of fluids. Rate depends heavily on temperature, moisture, body size, cause of death and whether the body is buried, submerged or exposed; heat accelerates putrefaction and cold or freezing delays it.

Forensic significance

Forensic pathologists and entomologists use the progression of putrefactive changes, together with insect colonisation, to estimate time since death, though with wide margins of error.
