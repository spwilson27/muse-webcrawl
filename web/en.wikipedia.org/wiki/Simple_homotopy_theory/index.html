In mathematics, simple homotopy theory is a refinement of homotopy theory developed by J. H. C. Whitehead in the 1930s and 1940s. It distinguishes between spaces that are merely homotopy equivalent and those related by simple homotopy equivalence — a homotopy equivalence that can be decomposed into elementary expansions and collapses of cell complexes. The obstruction to improving a homotopy equivalence to a simple one is measured by the Whitehead torsion, an element of the Whitehead group of the fundamental group.

An elementary expansion adds a pair of cells; its inverse, an elementary collapse, is the basic move of simple homotopy theory.

Elementary expansions and collapses

A finite CW complex can be enlarged by attaching two cells in consecutive dimensions whose attaching data cancel; this is an elementary expansion, and its reverse is an elementary collapse. Two complexes are simple-homotopy equivalent if one can be obtained from the other by a finite sequence of such moves (together with subdivision). Because the moves are purely combinatorial, simple homotopy equivalence is a much more rigid relation than ordinary homotopy equivalence.

Whitehead torsion

Whitehead defined an abelian group Wh(π) associated with a group π — the quotient of the group ring's K1 by trivial units — and showed that every homotopy equivalence f : X → Y between finite complexes carries a torsion τ(f) in Wh(π1Y). The equivalence is simple if and only if its torsion vanishes. The theory resolved subtle questions raised by Reidemeister torsion and the classification of lens spaces, which are homotopy equivalent but not always simple-homotopy equivalent.

Applications

Simple homotopy theory is a central tool in geometric topology: the s-cobordism theorem of Barden, Mazur, and Stallings characterises when an h-cobordism is a product precisely in terms of Whitehead torsion, and the theory underlies surgery-theoretic classification of manifolds. The Whitehead group vanishes for many groups (trivially for free abelian groups), which by the s-cobordism theorem has strong consequences in high-dimensional manifold theory.
