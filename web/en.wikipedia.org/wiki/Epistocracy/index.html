Epistocracy

Epistocracy is a hypothetical form of government in which political power is restricted to, or weighted toward, citizens who possess political knowledge or competence. The term derives from the Greek epistēmē (knowledge) and kratos (power, rule). It is most closely associated with the philosopher Jason Brennan, whose 2016 book Against Democracy presents the modern case for the concept, although similar ideas appear throughout the history of political thought.

Proposals

Epistocratic proposals discussed in the literature include:

  • Restricted suffrage — only citizens who pass a knowledge examination may vote.
  • Plural voting — all may vote, but knowledgeable citizens receive extra votes, an idea earlier advocated by John Stuart Mill.
  • Epistocratic veto — a body of qualified citizens may veto legislation passed by a democratically elected legislature.
  • Simulated oracle — votes are weighted statistically using demographic and survey data to predict what a fully informed electorate would choose, a proposal by Bryan Caplan.
  • Government by "epistemic councils" or enfranchisement lotteries, in which a randomly selected body is given time and resources to become informed before deciding.

Arguments

Supporters argue that voting is an exercise of power over others and should meet a competence standard, as other consequential activities (such as practicing medicine) do. Brennan divides voters into "hobbits" (apathetic and uninformed), "hooligans" (partisan and biased) and "vulcans" (informed and dispassionate), contending that real electorates contain few vulcans. Plato's critique of democracy in the Republic — rule by those who know, compared to letting passengers steer a ship — is a classical antecedent, as is the historical use of literacy tests and educational qualifications, though those were often instruments of racial and class exclusion.

Criticism

Critics, notably David Estlund, raise the "demographic objection": any knowledge-based franchise would in practice privilege already-advantaged groups by sex, race and class, undermining the political equality and public justification that legitimate government requires. Others argue there is no agreed, neutral test of political competence, that disputes about who counts as knowledgeable are themselves political, and that inclusive participation has epistemic and stability benefits that epistocracy sacrifices. Empirical critics also note that high-information voters are themselves subject to motivated reasoning.
