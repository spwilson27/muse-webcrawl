Atmospheric escape

MechanismsThermal (Jeans, hydrodynamic), non-thermal (sputtering, charge exchange), impact erosion

Atmospheric escape is the loss of planetary atmospheric gases to outer space. Several mechanisms can operate, and escape is a major factor in how planetary atmospheres evolve over geological time. It explains, for example, why Mars lost most of its early atmosphere and why Earth's atmosphere contains almost no primordial hydrogen or helium.

Thermal escape

In Jeans escape, individual molecules in the high-energy tail of the thermal distribution at the exobase exceed escape velocity and leave. This process efficiently removes light gases such as hydrogen. When heating is strong — as on early Earth or close-in exoplanets — the upper atmosphere can flow outward wholesale as a hydrodynamic wind.

Non-thermal escape

Non-thermal processes include photochemical escape (energetic neutrals produced by chemical reactions), ion pickup and sputtering by the solar wind, and polar wind outflows along magnetic field lines. Planets without an intrinsic magnetic field, such as Mars and Venus, are more exposed to solar-wind stripping, as measured by missions including MAVEN.

Significance

Escape shapes planetary habitability: Venus's extreme deuterium enrichment records the loss of an ocean's worth of hydrogen, while Titan retains a thick nitrogen atmosphere thanks to its cold temperature and Saturn's distance from the Sun. Atmospheric escape is also a key observable in exoplanet studies, detected through extended hydrogen and helium absorption during transits.
