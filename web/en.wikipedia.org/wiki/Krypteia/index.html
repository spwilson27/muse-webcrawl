Krypteia

The Krypteia (Greek: κρυπτεία, also spelled crypteia) was an institution of ancient Sparta in which young Spartan men (the kryptai) were sent into the countryside, where they lived by stealth and were licensed to kill helots. It formed part of the Spartan upbringing system, the agoge, and functioned at once as military training, a rite of passage and an instrument of control over Sparta's large enslaved helot population.


The kryptai ranged the Laconian countryside by night.

Sources and description

Plutarch, in his Life of Lycurgus, describes how the ephors periodically declared war on the helots so that they could be killed without religious pollution; the ablest young men were sent out armed only with a dagger and basic rations, hiding by day and by night killing any helot they caught on the roads or working in the fields. Plato mentions the krypteia in the Laws as a gruelling endurance exercise involving barefoot travel in winter, sleeping rough and going without attendants. Aristotle was critical of the institution.

Interpretation

Scholars debate the krypteia's primary purpose: terrorising the helots into submission, training youths in stealth and self-reliance, or serving as a secret police and initiation rite analogous to institutions in other societies. Thucydides records a related episode in 424 BC in which some 2,000 helots, invited to claim their freedom, disappeared — an event often discussed alongside the krypteia.
