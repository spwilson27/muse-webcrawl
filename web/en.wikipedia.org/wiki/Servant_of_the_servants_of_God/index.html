Servant of the servants of God

The title Servus servorum Dei heads papal bulls.

Servant of the servants of God (Latin: Servus servorum Dei) is one of the titles of the Pope, the bishop of Rome. It expresses the humility of the papal office: the holder of supreme authority in the Catholic Church styles himself the servant of God's servants.

History

The title was used occasionally by earlier bishops, but it was Pope Gregory I (Gregory the Great, r. 590–604) who adopted it systematically. Gregory took it up in part to rebuke the Patriarch of Constantinople, John the Faster, who had assumed the title "Ecumenical Patriarch", which Gregory regarded as presumptuous. In correspondence Gregory contrasted his own humble style with what he saw as a title of pride.

Since Gregory, Servus servorum Dei has been a regular element of papal protocol. It appears in the formal intitulatio of papal bulls, where the pope signs his name followed by "bishop, servant of the servants of God" (episcopus, servus servorum Dei). A related but distinct usage is "Servant of God" (Servus Dei), the first formal stage in a Catholic cause for canonisation.
