Operating theater

An operating theater (American English) or operating theatre (British English) is a facility within a hospital where surgical operations are carried out in an aseptic environment. Historically, the term referred to a non-sterile, tiered theater or amphitheater in which students and other spectators could watch surgeons perform surgery; a famous surviving example is the Old Operating Theatre in London, in the roof space of St Thomas's Church.

Design

Modern operating theaters are generally windowless, controlled-environment rooms. They are maintained at positive air pressure relative to surrounding corridors, with filtered, often laminar, airflow over the operating table to reduce airborne contamination. Temperature and humidity are controlled, and surfaces are smooth and washable. An operating suite typically includes one or more theaters together with a scrub-up area, anaesthetic room, preparation room, recovery area and storage for sterile supplies and equipment such as the operating table, surgical lights, anaesthetic machine, diathermy and monitoring devices.

Personnel and practice

During an operation the theater houses the surgical team: the operating surgeon and assistants, the anaesthetist, scrub nurses or operating department practitioners who handle sterile instruments, a circulating nurse, and technicians. All staff wear dedicated theater clothing, caps and masks, observe scrubbing and gowning procedures, and maintain a sterile field around the patient. "Hybrid" operating theaters combine a surgical theater with advanced medical imaging systems such as angiography or intraoperative MRI for minimally invasive and image-guided procedures.

History

The earliest operating theaters were teaching amphitheatres in which speed was prized because anaesthesia did not exist; the introduction of ether anaesthesia in 1846 and of antiseptic and later aseptic technique transformed the theater from a public spectacle into a strictly controlled clinical space, and viewing galleries disappeared.
