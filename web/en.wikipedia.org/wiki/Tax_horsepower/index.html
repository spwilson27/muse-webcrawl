Tax horsepower

Fiscal ratings classified cars by formula, not by actual power output

Tax horsepower, or fiscal horsepower, is a rating historically used in several European countries to determine the level of tax or licence fee payable on a motor vehicle. The rating was computed from engine dimensions — usually cylinder bore and number of cylinders — by a simple formula, rather than measured from the engine's actual power output. Fiscal ratings strongly influenced engine design in the countries that used them for much of the 20th century.

Britain: the RAC rating

In the United Kingdom, the Royal Automobile Club (RAC) horsepower formula, adopted for taxation in 1910, was hp = (D² × n) / 2.5, where D is the cylinder bore in inches and n the number of cylinders. Because the formula ignored stroke length, British manufacturers favoured long-stroke, small-bore engines to minimise tax for a given capacity, a characteristic that persisted in British engine design until the horsepower tax was abolished in 1948, when a flat rate replaced it.

France: chevaux fiscaux

France used chevaux fiscaux (CV, "fiscal horses"), calculated from engine capacity and later from more elaborate formulas involving power and, in the modern version, CO2 emissions. The rating famously gave their names to cars such as the Citroën 2CV ("deux chevaux") and the Citroën Traction Avant models (7CV, 11CV, 15CV). Fiscal horsepower continues in France in modified form for registration fees (carte grise).

Other countries

Germany, Italy, Spain, Belgium, Japan and other countries also used fiscal formulas at various times. Italy's cavalli fiscali and Spain's caballos fiscales were based on cylinder volume. In Japan, tax classes based on engine displacement continue to shape the kei car category and ordinary vehicle taxation. Such systems generally encouraged small engines and, in some markets, long-stroke designs or forced-induction engines that achieved high real power within a low fiscal class.
