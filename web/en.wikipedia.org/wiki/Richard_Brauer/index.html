Richard Dagobert Brauer (10 February 1901 – 17 April 1977) was a leading German and American mathematician. He worked mainly in abstract algebra, but made important contributions to number theory as well. He was the founder of modular representation theory.

Biography

Brauer was born in Berlin and studied at the University of Berlin, completing his doctorate in 1926 under Issai Schur; his brother Alfred Brauer was also a noted mathematician and his doctoral advisor's other student. In 1927 he took a position at the University of Königsberg. Dismissed under the Nazi civil-service laws of 1933 because of his Jewish ancestry, he emigrated to the United States, holding positions at the University of Kentucky and the Institute for Advanced Study (as assistant to Hermann Weyl) before moving to the University of Michigan in 1935 and to Harvard University in 1952, where he remained until his retirement in 1971.

Mathematical work

Several mathematical concepts bear Brauer's name. The Brauer group, introduced with Hasse and Emmy Noether in the Brauer–Hasse–Noether theorem on central simple algebras over number fields, became a cornerstone of algebraic number theory and later of algebraic geometry. In finite group theory he founded the modular representation theory of groups — the study of representations over fields of prime characteristic — introducing Brauer characters, blocks and defect groups. His theorems on induced characters (1945–46) underpin much of character theory, and with Michio Suzuki he proved the Brauer–Suzuki theorem that finite simple groups of even order have a non-cyclic generalized quaternion Sylow 2-subgroup. The Brauer–Fowler theorem's bound on group order in terms of an involution's centralizer helped launch the classification programme for finite simple groups. Brauer edited the collected works of his teacher Issai Schur and influenced a generation of algebraists, including many doctoral students.

Recognition

Brauer received the Cole Prize in Algebra in 1949 and the United States National Medal of Science in 1970. He was a member of the National Academy of Sciences and served as president of the American Mathematical Society (1957–58).
