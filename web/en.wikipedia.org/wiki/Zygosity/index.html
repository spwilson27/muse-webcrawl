Zygosity (from Greek zygotos, "yoked") is the degree to which the copies of a gene — the alleles — in an organism or cell are identical. It describes the genetic state at a particular locus and is fundamental to Mendelian inheritance and the prediction of phenotypes.

Types

An organism is homozygous at a locus when its two copies of a gene are identical — either homozygous dominant (two dominant alleles, AA) or homozygous recessive (aa). It is heterozygous when the two copies differ (Aa). Males of species with XY sex determination are hemizygous for genes on the X chromosome, having only one copy. An individual lacking any copy of a gene at a locus is nullizygous.

Significance

Zygosity determines whether recessive traits are expressed: recessive disorders such as cystic fibrosis appear only in homozygous recessive individuals, while heterozygous carriers are usually unaffected. In breeding and agriculture, homozygosity is increased by inbreeding and self-pollination to fix desired traits, whereas heterozygosity underlies hybrid vigour (heterosis). In twin research, zygosity distinguishes monozygotic (identical) from dizygotic (fraternal) twins, enabling heritability studies.

Testing

Zygosity is determined by DNA sequencing, genotyping arrays, or, for twins, by comparing multiple genetic markers. In medicine, zygosity testing informs diagnosis of inherited conditions and pharmacogenetic dosing.
