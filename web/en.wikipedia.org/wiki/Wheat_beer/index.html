Wheat beer

Wheat beer is a top-fermented beer brewed with a large proportion of wheat relative to malted barley — commonly 30 to 70 percent of the grist. It comes in two principal traditional forms: German Weissbier ("white beer"), based on a mixture of barley and wheat malts, and Belgian witbier ("white beer"), made with unmalted wheat and flavoured with coriander and orange peel. Both are typically pale and cloudy from suspended yeast and wheat proteins.

German Weissbier

Bavarian Weissbier, also called Weizenbier, is brewed by law with at least 50 percent wheat malt and fermented with a distinctive yeast that produces banana-like esters and clove-like phenols. Variants include pale Hefeweizen ("yeast wheat", served cloudy), filtered Kristallweizen, dark Dunkelweizen, and the stronger Weizenbock. Historically a monopoly of the Bavarian ducal court and the Degenberg family, wheat brewing in Bavaria was opened to others in the 19th century, after which the style spread worldwide. Munich breweries such as Paulaner, Franziskaner, and Weihenstephan (the world's oldest brewery, founded 1040) are noted producers.

Other styles

Belgian witbier, nearly extinct by the 1950s, was revived by Pierre Celis at Hoegaarden in 1966. Berliner Weisse is a low-alcohol, sharply sour wheat beer of Berlin, traditionally served with raspberry or woodruff syrup. Lambic and gueuze also use unmalted wheat but are spontaneously fermented. American craft brewers make broadly defined wheat beers — often clean, lightly hopped ales served with a lemon wedge — plus the stronger wheat wines.

Serving

Weissbier is traditionally poured into a tall, vase-shaped Weizen glass that supports its large foam head; the bottle is often swirled at the end to rouse the yeast sediment.
