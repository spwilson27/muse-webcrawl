Russia in the European energy sector describes the role played by Russia as a major supplier of fossil fuels and nuclear technology to Europe, and the political and economic relationship built around that trade from the Soviet era until the sharp rupture that followed Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. For decades Russia was the European Union's largest external supplier of natural gas, crude oil and coal, a dependence that shaped European energy policy, diplomacy and debates about energy security.

Development of the trade

Gas trade between the USSR and Western Europe began in 1968, when Austria became the first Western country to import Soviet gas, and expanded during the Cold War despite American misgivings: in the 1980s large-diameter pipelines from western Siberia reached Germany, France and Italy. After 1991 the state companies Gazprom (gas), Rosneft and Lukoil (oil) inherited and extended these networks, adding the Yamal–Europe pipeline through Belarus and Poland, the Blue Stream line to Turkey, and the Nord Stream pipelines under the Baltic Sea directly to Germany, the second of which, Nord Stream 2, was completed in 2021 but never entered service.

Dependence and disputes

By 2021 Russia supplied roughly 40 per cent of the EU's imported natural gas, about a quarter of its crude oil and around half of its hard coal imports; some member states, especially in central and eastern Europe, depended on Russian gas for the great majority of their supply. Pricing and transit disputes between Gazprom and Ukraine in 2006 and 2009 briefly cut deliveries to Europe and pushed the EU towards diversification, storage obligations and interconnectors. Critics argued that Moscow used energy as geopolitical leverage; Russia countered that it had been a reliable supplier for fifty years.

The 2022 rupture

Following the invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, the EU adopted successive sanctions packages banning imports of Russian coal and most crude oil and petroleum products, while Russia progressively cut pipeline gas deliveries, including through Nord Stream, which was later damaged by sabotage explosions in September 2022. The EU's REPowerEU plan accelerated diversification to liquefied natural gas from the United States and others, pipeline gas from Norway and Azerbaijan, energy savings and renewables. Russian pipeline gas fell to a small fraction of EU imports within two years, at the cost of a severe price shock in 2022 that drove inflation and industrial curtailment across the continent.

Nuclear energy

Russia, through Rosatom, has also supplied enriched uranium, fuel assemblies for Soviet-designed VVER reactors in several EU states, and new-build nuclear projects, most prominently the Paks II plant in Hungary. Nuclear fuel was not initially covered by EU sanctions, though member states have sought alternative suppliers.
