Apokatastasis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Apokatastasis (Greek: ἀποκατάστασις, apokatástasis) is the concept of the ultimate restitution or restoration of all things to their original state. In theology, it most often refers to the doctrine — associated especially with the early Church theologians Origen of Alexandria and Gregory of Nyssa — that all rational creatures, including the damned and (in Origen's case, in some interpretations) even the Devil, will ultimately be reconciled to God and saved. It is closely related to the doctrine of universal salvation, or universalism.

Etymology and background

The word derives from the Greek verb apokathistemi, "to restore" or "to re-establish", and appears in the New Testament in Acts 3:21, which speaks of the "times of restoration of all things" (Greek: χρόνων ἀποκαταστάσεως πάντων). In Hellenistic astronomy and Stoic philosophy, the term referred to the cyclical return of the heavenly bodies to their original positions, and to the periodic renewal of the cosmos after its conflagration (ekpyrosis).

Origen

The Alexandrian theologian Origen (c. 185 – c. 253) developed the doctrine most influentially in his work On First Principles (De Principiis). Origen held that rational creatures were created equal and contemplative, but fell away from God through satiety and negligence, becoming embodied as angels, human beings, or demons according to the degree of their fall. Through a long process of purification and education across successive ages, all souls would eventually be restored to their original unity with God, so that God would be "all in all" (1 Corinthians 15:28). Origen's speculative system was later condemned: in 543 the emperor Justinian issued an edict against Origen's teachings, and the anathemas traditionally associated with the Second Council of Constantinople (553) condemned the doctrine of universal restoration in its Origenist form.

Gregory of Nyssa

Gregory of Nyssa (c. 335 – c. 395), one of the Cappadocian Fathers, taught a form of universal restoration grounded in his understanding of evil as a finite privation of the good. In works such as On the Soul and the Resurrection and the Great Catechism, Gregory argued that after a purifying fire proportioned to each soul's condition, all human beings — and in some passages seemingly all rational nature — would be restored, since evil cannot persist infinitely against the infinite Good. Unlike Origen, Gregory was never condemned and was later honoured as a saint and a "Father of Fathers".

Later history

The doctrine reappeared periodically in Christian thought: in the Syrian tradition associated with Isaac of Nineveh; in the works of John Scotus Eriugena; among some Radical Reformers; and in modern universalist theology. Many contemporary theologians, including David Bentley Hart in That All Shall Be Saved (2019), have argued for the doctrine, while others hold that eternal damnation is the historic and scriptural teaching of the Church. Some theologians distinguish between a dogmatic universalism (asserting all will certainly be saved) and a "hopeful universalism" associated with Hans Urs von Balthasar, who argued Christians may hope and pray that all be saved without claiming certainty.
