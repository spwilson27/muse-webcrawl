Enactivism is a position in cognitive science and the philosophy of mind arguing that cognition arises through the dynamic interaction between an acting organism and its environment. Rather than treating the mind as a computer that builds internal representations of an external world, enactivists hold that organisms enact, or "bring forth," a world of significance through their sensorimotor activity. The approach is closely related to embodied cognition, embedded cognition, and ecological psychology, and is often grouped with them under the umbrella of "4E cognition" (embodied, embedded, enacted, extended).

History

The term was introduced by biologist Humberto Maturana and developed most influentially by Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, and Eleanor Rosch in their 1991 book The Embodied Mind: Cognitive Science and Human Experience. Drawing on phenomenology (especially Maurice Merleau-Ponty), the biology of autopoiesis, and Buddhist philosophy, they proposed that cognition is not the recovery of a pregiven world by a pregiven mind but the enactment of a world through a history of structural coupling.

Principles

Central notions include autopoiesis — the self-producing organization of living systems — and sense-making, the idea that an autonomous organism generates meaning by regulating its coupling with its surroundings in accordance with its own norms of survival and flourishing. Perception, on this view, is a mode of action: to perceive is to master sensorimotor contingencies, the lawful ways sensory stimulation changes with movement. Later developments include radical enactivism (Hutto and Myin), which denies that basic cognition involves content-bearing representations at all, and sensorimotor enactivism associated with Alva Noë and J. Kevin O'Regan.

Criticism

Critics argue that enactivism struggles to account for "representation-hungry" cognition such as abstract reasoning, planning about absent situations, and mathematics, and that its central claims can be difficult to test empirically. Defenders reply that such higher cognition is scaffolded by language and cultural practices that are themselves enacted.
