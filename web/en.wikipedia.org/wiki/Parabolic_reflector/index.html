A parabolic reflector (or paraboloid reflector) is a reflective surface used to collect or project energy such as light, sound or radio waves. Its shape is part of a circular paraboloid — the surface created by a parabola rotating around its axis. The reflector's defining property is that it transforms an incoming collimated beam (parallel rays) travelling along its axis into a spherical wave converging at the focal point; conversely, a point source at the focus is reflected into a parallel beam.

Theory

The geometry follows from the definition of the parabola: every point on the curve is equidistant from the focus and the directrix, so all axis-parallel rays travel equal total path lengths to the focal point and arrive in phase. This makes the parabolic shape ideal wherever rays must be concentrated or collimated without spherical aberration on axis. Off-axis aberrations such as coma limit the field of view of simple parabolic mirrors, which is why precision telescope designs add corrective elements.

History

The concentrating property of burning mirrors was known in antiquity and attached by legend to Archimedes at the siege of Syracuse. James Gregory and Isaac Newton used paraboloidal mirrors in their 17th-century reflecting telescope designs; in the 1880s Heinrich Hertz used parabolic reflectors in his experiments demonstrating radio waves, and the shape became fundamental to radar in the Second World War.

Applications

Parabolic reflectors are used in satellite television dishes and deep-space communication antennas, radio telescopes such as the Lovell Telescope, solar furnaces and parabolic-trough solar power plants, vehicle headlights and searchlights, flashlights, directional (shotgun and parabolic) microphones, and solar cookers. Large solar concentrators exploit the same geometry to raise steam or melt material at temperatures above 3,000 °C.
