Free people of color

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Free people of color (French: gens de couleur libres) were people of mixed African, European, and sometimes Native American ancestry in the Americas who were not enslaved. The term was used particularly in French and Spanish colonies in the Caribbean and Louisiana, and in the antebellum United States, where free people of color formed a distinct social stratum — neither enslaved nor accorded the full rights of white citizens, but often educated, property-owning, and organised in their own communities, churches, and militia units.

Origins and status

Free people of color arose through manumission, self-purchase, birth to free mothers, or emancipation by colonial authorities. Under French rule, the Code Noir (1685) set terms for manumission and granted freed people nominal legal equality, though later regulations increasingly restricted them. In Spanish colonies, the practice of coartación (self-purchase in instalments) and a more fluid racial order produced large free colored populations. In Saint-Domingue (present-day Haiti), gens de couleur numbered some 28,000 by 1789 — roughly equal to the white population — and owned significant land and slaves, yet were barred from public office and many professions, grievances that fed into the Haitian Revolution.

In the United States

In the United States, the free Black population grew from about 59,000 in 1790 to nearly 490,000 by 1860, roughly 11 percent of the African American population. The largest concentrations were in the Upper South and in New Orleans, whose Creole of color community — descended from French and Spanish colonial society — was relatively wealthy, French-speaking, and Catholic, with its own institutions and, before the Civil War, militia units that served in the Battle of New Orleans in 1815. Southern states progressively restricted free people's rights: limiting manumission, requiring registration and passes, barring testimony against whites, and in some cases requiring freed people to leave the state.

Communities and contributions

Free people of color founded schools, mutual-aid societies, newspapers, and churches, and were prominent among early abolitionists. Institutions such as New Orleans's Catholic schools and benevolent societies, and figures including the educator and activist Frances E. W. Harper and the Louisiana Creole writer Victor Séjour, illustrate the community's cultural influence. After emancipation in 1865, free people of color were disproportionately represented among the first Black officeholders of Reconstruction, particularly in Louisiana.
