Wood carving

Wood carving is a form of woodworking by means of a cutting tool (knife) in one hand or a chisel by two hands, resulting in a wooden figure or figurine, or in the sculptural ornamentation of a wooden object. The phrase may also refer to the finished product, from individual sculptures to hand-worked mouldings forming part of a tracery.

Techniques

Carving techniques include chip carving, in which geometric patterns are cut with a knife; relief carving, in which a design stands out from a flat background panel; and carving in the round, producing fully three-dimensional figures. Tools range from knives and gouges — chisels with curved cutting edges — to mallets, rasps and abrasives used for finishing.

Woods used

Carvers favour woods that are soft, fine-grained and even in texture, such as basswood (lime), butternut, pine and balsa for beginners, while fine detail work may use boxwood, walnut or fruitwoods. Hardwoods such as oak were used extensively in medieval church carving.

History

Making wooden sculptures dates to prehistory; one of the oldest surviving wooden artefacts is the Shigir Idol, over 11,000 years old. Wood carving flourished in medieval church decoration, in the figureheads of ships, in African and Oceanic ritual art, and in the elaborate netsuke of Japan. The English carver Grinling Gibbons (1648–1721) was famed for naturalistic limewood carvings of flowers and foliage.
