Symposium

Symposium (Greek: συμπόσιον)
Context: Ancient Greece
Literary works: Plato, Xenophon

In ancient Greece, the symposium (Greek: συμπόσιον, symposion, "to drink together") was a convivial drinking party, a key social institution where men reclined, drank wine mixed with water, conversed, recited poetry and were entertained.

Practice

Held in the andrōn (men's room), symposia followed a meal. A symposiarch set the wine's strength and topics; guests played games such as kottabos, sang drinking songs (skolia), and debated philosophy and politics. Hetairai and musicians might attend.

In literature

Plato's Symposium presents speeches on love (eros) by Phaedrus, Pausanias, Aristophanes, Agathon, Socrates (reporting Diotima) and a drunken Alcibiades. Xenophon wrote a work of the same name. In modern usage, "symposium" means an academic conference or collection of essays on a topic.
