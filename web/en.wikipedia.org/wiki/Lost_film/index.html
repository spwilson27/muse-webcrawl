Lost film

A lost film is a feature film or short film that is no longer known to exist in any studio archive, private collection, or public archive such as the Library of Congress. The term is also applied to films of which only fragments survive.

Scale of the loss

The great majority of early cinema is lost. A 2013 study for the Library of Congress estimated that about 70 per cent of American silent feature films made between 1912 and 1929 are completely lost, and only a minority survive in their original 35 mm domestic-release form. Comparable losses occurred worldwide, and many early sound films, television broadcasts (routinely wiped), and regional cinemas survive only partially.

Causes

  • Nitrate film stock: until the early 1950s film was printed on highly flammable, chemically unstable cellulose nitrate, which decomposes, can spontaneously combust, and destroyed whole vaults in fires (for example the 1937 Fox vault fire).
  • Deliberate destruction: studios junked silent films after the sound transition, regarding them as worthless; prints were also melted down to recover silver.
  • Neglect and wiping: broadcasters wiped videotapes for reuse, notably the BBC in the 1960s–70s.
  • Decay of later media: acetate "vinegar syndrome" and obsolete video formats continue the problem.

Famous lost films and rediscoveries

Notable wholly or largely lost films include London After Midnight (1927) with Lon Chaney, the original release of The Magnificent Ambersons footage cut by RKO, and most of Theda Bara's work, including Cleopatra (1917). Occasionally treasures resurface: a near-complete print of Metropolis's lost footage was found in Buenos Aires in 2008, and the Dawson Film Find (1978) recovered hundreds of reels buried in the Yukon permafrost.

Preservation

Archives such as the Library of Congress, the BFI National Archive, and the George Eastman Museum, and initiatives like Martin Scorsese's Film Foundation, work to locate, restore, and digitise surviving material, while organisations such as the Internet Archive preserve orphan works.
