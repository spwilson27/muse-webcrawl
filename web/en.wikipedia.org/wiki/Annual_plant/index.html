Annual plant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An annual plant is a plant that completes its life cycle — germinating, growing, flowering, setting seed, and dying — within a single growing season. Annuals contrast with biennials, which take two years, and perennials, which live for multiple years. Many of the world's most important food crops and garden flowers are annuals.

Life cycle

Summer annuals germinate in spring or early summer and mature by autumn of the same year; examples include maize, beans, and most garden vegetables. Winter annuals germinate in autumn, overwinter as seedlings or rosettes, and flower and set seed the following spring or summer — a pattern common among wild grasses and weeds of Mediterranean and temperate climates. Desert annuals ("ephemerals") may complete their whole cycle in a few weeks after rain. After flowering and seed set, the plant undergoes senescence and dies; the species survives only through its seeds.

Importance

The staple cereals wheat, rice, and maize are annuals, as are legumes such as peas, beans, and soybeans, and oilseeds such as sunflower. In horticulture, annuals like petunias, marigolds, and zinnias provide a full season of flowers from a single sowing and are widely used in bedding displays. Because annuals invest heavily in seed production rather than long-lived structures, they often grow fast and exploit disturbed ground, which is why many agricultural weeds are annuals. Some normally perennial plants, such as tomato and cotton, are grown as annuals in cold climates where frost kills them.
