Algebraic graph theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Algebraic graph theory is a branch of mathematics in which algebraic methods are applied to problems about graphs, in contrast to geometric, combinatoric or algorithmic approaches. It has three main strands: the use of linear algebra, the use of group theory (especially automorphism groups), and the study of algebraic graph invariants.

Linear algebra and spectral graph theory

The oldest and most developed strand associates matrices with a graph — principally the adjacency matrix and the Laplacian matrix — and studies their eigenvalues (the graph's spectrum). Eigenvalues bound chromatic number, independence number and expansion; cospectral non-isomorphic graphs show the limits of the method. Spectral techniques underlie expander graphs, spectral clustering, and the design of networks, and they connect to random walks through the Laplacian's second-smallest eigenvalue (algebraic connectivity), which measures how well-connected a graph is.

Group theory

The automorphism group of a graph captures its symmetries. Highlights include Frucht's theorem (every finite group is the automorphism group of some graph) and the study of vertex-transitive and Cayley graphs, which link graph theory to geometric group theory and to the construction of large, highly connected networks. Highly symmetric graphs such as the Petersen graph serve as test cases and occasional counterexamples.

Graph invariants

Polynomial invariants such as the chromatic polynomial (counting proper colourings), the Tutte polynomial and the characteristic and matching polynomials pack combinatorial information into algebraic objects that can be studied with tools from algebra and topology. Knot-theoretic connections arise through the Tutte polynomial's relation to the Jones polynomial.

Applications

Algebraic methods contribute to chemistry (molecular spectra in Hückel theory), physics, coding theory, computer science — including spectral algorithms and network analysis — and the theory of expander graphs central to combinatorics and theoretical computing.
