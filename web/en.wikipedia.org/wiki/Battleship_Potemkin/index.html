Battleship Potemkin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Odessa steps sequence: crowds fleeing down the monumental staircase as Cossacks advance, the baby carriage at the brink]

Battleship Potemkin (Russian: Броненосец Потёмкин) is a 1925 Soviet silent film directed by Sergei Eisenstein, dramatising the mutiny aboard the Russian battleship Potemkin during the 1905 revolution. Produced by Mosfilm for the revolution's twentieth anniversary, it is one of the most influential films ever made — its 'Odessa steps' sequence the most famous passage in silent cinema.

The film is structured in five acts: the crew's protest at maggoty meat, the mutiny and Vakulinchuk's death, the funeral in Odessa's harbour, the massacre on the steps, and the squadron's refusal to fire on the rebels — history compressed and invented (the steps massacre did not occur) into revolutionary myth of unmatched formal power.

Technique and impact

Eisenstein's montage — some 1,500 shots in a 75-minute film, against the era's norm of a few hundred — intercuts stone lions seemingly rising, a nurse's cracked pince-nez, the runaway pram. Banned or cut in many countries (Britain until 1954), Potemkin was voted the greatest film ever made at the 1958 Brussels World's Fair critics' poll, and generations of directors studied it frame by frame. Goebbels, infamously, admired it and wished for a National Socialist equivalent — a compliment Eisenstein's admirers record with discomfort.

Restorations

The film survives in multiple versions; the 2005 restoration reinstated censored frames and Edmund Meisel's original 1925 score, and the Pet Shop Boys' 2004 live score at Trafalgar Square is among its modern afterlives. Its images — above all the pram — are embedded in world visual culture far beyond film history.
