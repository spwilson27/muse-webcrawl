Fundamental theorem of algebra

Fundamental theorem of algebra
FieldMathematics (algebra, complex analysis)
StatementEvery non-constant polynomial with complex coefficients has a complex root
First statedPeter Roth (1608); Albert Girard (1629)
First proofCarl Friedrich Gauss (1799)

The fundamental theorem of algebra, also called d'Alembert's theorem or the d'Alembert–Gauss theorem, states that every non-constant single-variable polynomial with complex coefficients has at least one complex root. This includes polynomials with real coefficients, since every real number is a complex number with its imaginary part equal to zero.

Equivalently (by definition), the theorem states that the field of complex numbers is algebraically closed. The theorem is also stated as follows: every non-zero, single-variable, degree n polynomial with complex coefficients has, counted with multiplicity, exactly n complex roots.

History

Peter Roth first stated a version of the theorem in 1608, and Albert Girard gave a fuller statement in 1629. Jean le Rond d'Alembert attempted a proof in 1746. Carl Friedrich Gauss published the first substantially rigorous proof in his 1799 doctoral dissertation, and produced several further proofs over his lifetime.

Proofs

All proofs of the fundamental theorem require some amount of mathematical analysis, or at least the topological concept of continuity of real or complex functions. Standard approaches include:

  • Complex analysis: using Liouville's theorem, which says a bounded entire function must be constant; if a polynomial had no root, its reciprocal would be a bounded entire function.
  • Topology: using winding numbers or degree arguments applied to the map induced by the polynomial on a large circle.
  • Algebra: proofs based on Galois theory and Sylow theorems reduce the result to elementary facts about real polynomials.

Consequences

Because the complex numbers are algebraically closed, every polynomial factors completely into linear factors over the complex numbers. This makes the complex numbers fundamental in algebra, number theory, and many areas of applied mathematics.
