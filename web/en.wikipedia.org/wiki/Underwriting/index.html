Underwriting

Underwriting is the process by which an individual or institution takes on financial risk for a fee. The term derives from the practice of marine insurance at Lloyd's coffee house in London, where risk-takers accepted a share of a voyage's risk by literally writing their names under the description of the risk (the policy) — hence "under-writing."

Insurance underwriting

In insurance, underwriters evaluate applications for coverage, classify and select risks, determine premiums, and set policy terms and exclusions. Life and health underwriting considers factors such as age, medical history, occupation, and lifestyle; property and casualty underwriting examines the characteristics of the property, hazards, and loss history. The goal is to assemble a pool of risks whose actual claims can be kept near expected levels, so that premiums remain adequate and competitive. Modern insurers supplement human underwriters with actuarial models and automated underwriting systems.

Securities underwriting

In investment banking, underwriting is the process by which a bank or syndicate helps an issuer sell new securities — typically shares in an initial public offering or bonds — to investors. In a firm commitment underwriting, the underwriter buys the entire issue from the issuer and resells it, taking on the risk of unsold inventory; in a best efforts offering, the bank acts only as agent. Underwriters perform due diligence, help prepare the prospectus, build a book of investor demand, and set the offering price, earning the difference between purchase and sale price (the underwriting spread).

Other forms

The term is also used in real estate lending, where loan underwriters verify that a borrower meets the lender's criteria, and in broadcasting, where "underwriting" refers to corporate sponsorship announcements on public radio and television.
