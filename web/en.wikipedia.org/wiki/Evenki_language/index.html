Evenki (Эвэнки; also rendered Ewenki or Ewenke) is the largest member of the northern group of the Tungusic languages, a language family spoken across a vast area of Siberia and northern Asia. It is spoken by the Evenki people of Russia (in Yakutia, Krasnoyarsk Krai, Irkutsk and Amur oblasts, and the former Evenk Autonomous Okrug), as well as in Inner Mongolia and Heilongjiang in China and in Mongolia.

Status

Evenki is severely endangered. Of the tens of thousands of ethnic Evenki, only a minority — estimates range from roughly 13,000 to 30,000 speakers — still use the language, and most fluent speakers are elderly. Russian (in Russia) and Chinese or Mongolian (in China) dominate education and administration, and intergenerational transmission has largely broken down outside a few remote communities.

Dialects and writing

The language is traditionally divided into northern, southern and eastern dialect groups, with the southern dialects around the Stony Tunguska River forming the basis of the literary standard in Russia. Evenki had no widely used writing system before the 20th century. A Latin-based alphabet was introduced in 1931, replaced by Cyrillic in 1937. In China, Evenki has historically been written in the classical Mongolian script; Latin and Cyrillic have also been used experimentally.

Linguistic features

Like other Tungusic languages, Evenki is agglutinative, forming long words from chains of suffixes, and exhibits vowel harmony. Word order is typically subject–object–verb. The language distinguishes inclusive and exclusive forms of the first-person plural pronoun ("we including you" versus "we excluding you") and has a rich system of cases — around thirteen in the standard language — and of participles used in place of subordinate clauses.
