Roche lobe

The Roche lobe is the region around a star in a binary system within which orbiting material is gravitationally bound to that star. It is an approximately teardrop-shaped region bounded by a critical gravitational equipotential, with the point of the teardrop pointing directly at the other star. The concept is named for the French astronomer Édouard Roche, who first described it in 1873.

A three-dimensional rendering of the Roche potential in a binary system, showing the two Roche lobes meeting at the inner Lagrange point.

Definition

In a binary star system, each star's Roche lobe is bounded by the equipotential surface passing through the inner Lagrange point, L1, which lies between the two stars. Material inside a star's Roche lobe is bound to that star; material that crosses L1 may be transferred to the companion or escape the system. The average radius of a Roche lobe can be approximated by formulae such as Eggleton's, depending on the mass ratio of the two stars.

Mass transfer

If a star expands — for example as it becomes a red giant — until it fills its Roche lobe, its outer material can stream through L1 onto the companion in a process called Roche-lobe overflow. This drives the evolution of close binary systems, including cataclysmic variables, X-ray binaries and some progenitors of Type Ia supernovae. Alternatively, if a star is tidally distorted beyond its Roche lobe, it may be disrupted.
