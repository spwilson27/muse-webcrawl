NSFNET

The National Science Foundation Network (NSFNET) was a program of coordinated, evolving projects sponsored by the National Science Foundation (NSF) from 1985 to 1995 to promote advanced research and education networking in the United States. The NSFNET was one of the first networks created for the purpose of connecting supercomputing centres, and it evolved into the principal backbone of the early Internet, linking regional academic networks and thousands of institutions before the backbone was privatised and decommissioned on 30 April 1995.

History

The NSF established five supercomputer centres in 1985 and needed to give researchers nationwide remote access to them. After plans to use the ARPANET stalled, the NSF built its own TCP/IP network: a 56 kbit/s backbone (fuzzball routers) connecting the centres went live in 1986, alongside NSF-funded regional "mid-level" networks. Traffic grew explosively. In 1987 a cooperative agreement for managing and upgrading the backbone was awarded to a consortium led by Merit Network with IBM and MCI. The backbone was upgraded to T1 (1.5 Mbit/s) in 1988 and to T3 (45 Mbit/s) by 1991, and the network's Acceptable Use Policy restricted it to research and education, pushing commercial users toward new commercial providers.

Privatisation

In the early 1990s the NSF planned an orderly transition to a commercial Internet architecture of competing backbone providers interconnected at Network Access Points. The NSFNET backbone was retired on 30 April 1995; a smaller very-high-speed Backbone Network Service (vBNS) continued to serve research. NSFNET is credited with carrying the Internet from a research experiment to mass use: by its final year it connected thousands of networks and carried tens of trillions of bytes per month.
