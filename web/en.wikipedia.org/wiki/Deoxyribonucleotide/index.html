Deoxyribonucleotide

A deoxyribonucleotide is the monomer, or single unit, of DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid). Each deoxyribonucleotide comprises three parts: a nitrogenous base, a deoxyribose sugar, and one phosphate group. The nitrogenous base is always bonded to the 1′ carbon of the deoxyribose, which lacks a hydroxyl group at the 2′ position (unlike the ribose sugar found in RNA).

Types

Four deoxyribonucleotides occur in DNA, distinguished by their bases: deoxyadenosine monophosphate (dAMP, base adenine), deoxyguanosine monophosphate (dGMP, guanine), deoxycytidine monophosphate (dCMP, cytosine), and thymidine monophosphate (dTMP, thymine). In the DNA polymer, nucleotides are linked by phosphodiester bonds between the 3′ hydroxyl of one sugar and the 5′ phosphate of the next.

Synthesis and use

Cells synthesize deoxyribonucleotides by reducing ribonucleotides with the enzyme ribonucleotide reductase. Their triphosphate forms (dATP, dGTP, dCTP, dTTP) are the substrates of DNA polymerase during replication. Synthetic deoxyribonucleotides are also essential reagents in molecular biology, including dideoxy forms used as chain terminators in Sanger sequencing and primers for the polymerase chain reaction.
