History of the Arabs

The history of the Arabs begins with the peoples of the Arabian Peninsula and the Semitic-speaking kingdoms of its fringes, rises with the birth of Islam and the Arab conquests of the 7th century, and continues through the caliphates, Ottoman rule and the modern Arab states.

Pre-Islamic Arabia

Arabs are first mentioned in Assyrian inscriptions of the 9th century BCE. South Arabia (modern Yemen) grew rich on the incense trade, producing the kingdoms of Saba (Sheba), Himyar and others, builders of the Marib Dam. In the north, the Nabataeans built Petra and Palmyra flourished as a caravan city; the Lakhmids and Ghassanids served as buffer clients of Persia and Byzantium. Bedouin tribes lived by herding, raiding and trade around oasis towns such as Mecca, a sanctuary city of the Kaaba.

Islam and the conquests

Muhammad (c. 570–632) united Arabia under Islam. Under the Rashidun caliphs his successors defeated Byzantium and Sasanian Persia, taking Syria, Egypt, Iraq and Persia within two decades. The Umayyad Caliphate (661–750), ruling from Damascus, extended Arab rule from Spain to Central Asia and made Arabic the administrative language.

The Abbasid age and fragmentation

The Abbasid Caliphate (750–1258), centred on Baghdad, presided over the Islamic Golden Age: translation, mathematics, medicine and philosophy flourished. Politically the empire fragmented into dynasties — Fatimids in Egypt, Umayyads in Córdoba, and successor states — while Arab identity spread through language and Islam. The Crusades and the Mongol sack of Baghdad (1258) reshaped the region; the Mamluks and then the Ottomans ruled most Arab lands from 1517.

Modern era

The 19th-century Nahda (Arab Renaissance) revived Arab letters and nationalism. After the First World War, Britain and France divided the Ottoman Arab provinces under mandates; independence followed the Second World War. The discovery of oil transformed the Gulf states, the Arab–Israeli conflict dominated regional politics, and the Arab Spring of 2011 brought uprisings across the Arab world, whose peoples today number over 450 million in 22 Arab League states.
