The radian, denoted by the symbol rad, is the unit of angle in the International System of Units (SI) and is the standard unit of angular measure used in many areas of mathematics. It was formerly an SI supplementary unit and is since 1995 an SI derived unit. The radian is defined in the SI as being a dimensionless unit, with 1 rad = 1. As such, it is generally omitted, or the unit is sometimes written simply as "rad".

One radian is defined as the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc that is equal in length to the radius. The full angle of one complete revolution is 2π radians, which equals 360 degrees, so one radian is 180/π degrees, or approximately 57.2958 degrees.

Definition

The radian is defined in the SI as a dimensionless unit: 1 rad = 1. The unit was officially adopted in the 20th century and standardized in the SI in 1960. A complete revolution is 2π radians, relating the angle directly to the circumference of a circle, 2πr.

History

The concept of a radian measure, as opposed to the degree of an angle, is normally credited to Roger Cotes in 1714; he described the radian in everything but name, recognizing its naturalness as a unit of angular measure. The term radian first appeared in print on 5 June 1873, in examination questions set by James Thomson (brother of Lord Kelvin) at Queen's College, Belfast.

Usage

The radian is the natural unit for measuring angles in mathematics and physics because it makes many formulae simpler: in calculus, trigonometric functions have elegant derivatives only when their arguments are in radians. For example, the limit of sin(x)/x as x approaches zero equals 1 only when x is measured in radians.

Subdivisions and related units

One radian is divided into 1000 milliradians (mrad). The gradian, or gon, divides a right angle into 100 parts, so a full circle is 400 gradians.
