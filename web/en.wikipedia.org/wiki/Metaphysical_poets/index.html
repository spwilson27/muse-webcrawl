Metaphysical poets

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The metaphysical poets are a group of 17th-century English poets whose work is characterised by inventive, elaborate and often startling metaphors — the conceit — witty argument, colloquial diction and a union of intellect and passion. The group is not a formal school but a retrospective label. Its central figure is John Donne, and its principal members include George Herbert, Andrew Marvell, Henry Vaughan, Richard Crashaw, Thomas Traherne, Abraham Cowley and John Cleveland.

The term

John Dryden complained that Donne "affects the metaphysics" in his love poetry, and Samuel Johnson, in his Life of Cowley (1779), fixed the label while criticising the style: in the metaphysicals' work "the most heterogeneous ideas are yoked by violence together", though Johnson conceded their wit and learning. The group's reputation collapsed in the 18th and 19th centuries and was revived by Herbert Grierson's 1921 anthology Metaphysical Lyrics and Poems of the Seventeenth Century and T. S. Eliot's famous review-essay "The Metaphysical Poets", which praised Donne's "sensibility" and lamented a later "dissociation of sensibility" in English poetry.

Style and themes

Metaphysical poems typically open abruptly in mid-argument ("For God's sake hold your tongue"), proceed by logical, lawyerly reasoning, and deploy conceits drawn from the new science, geography, law and theology — Donne compares parted lovers to the two legs of a pair of compasses, and a flea to a marriage bed and temple. Sacred and secular verse alike wrestle with love, death, faith and the soul: Herbert's The Temple shapes devotional poems (some in patterned "shaped verse"), Vaughan's Silex Scintillans glows with visionary light, and Marvell's "To His Coy Mistress" turns the carpe diem argument into metaphysical seduction.
