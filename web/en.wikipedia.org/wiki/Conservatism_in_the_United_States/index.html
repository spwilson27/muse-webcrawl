Conservatism in the United States

Ronald Reagan, president 1981–1989, whose coalition defined late-20th-century American conservatism.

Conservatism in the United States is a political and social philosophy characterised by respect for tradition, support for free markets and limited government, a strong national defence, and emphasis on individual liberty, property rights and the rule of law. Distinct from European conservatism, American conservatism largely seeks to conserve the classical liberal principles of the American founding — a paradox often noted by historians since Louis Hartz's The Liberal Tradition in America (1955).

History

Antecedents lie in the Federalists, the Whig Party, and the laissez-faire jurisprudence of the late 19th century. Modern American conservatism coalesced after the Second World War as an intellectual and political movement opposed to the New Deal state and to communism. William F. Buckley Jr.'s magazine National Review (founded 1955) and Frank Meyer's doctrine of fusionism sought to unite traditionalists, free-market libertarians and anti-communists. Barry Goldwater's 1964 presidential campaign, though defeated, energised the movement's grassroots; Ronald Reagan's victories in 1980 and 1984 brought it to power, delivering tax cuts, deregulation and a more confrontational Cold War policy.

Currents and tensions

Strands within the coalition include fiscal conservatives and supply-siders; social conservatives, including the religious right organised from the late 1970s; neoconservatives favouring an assertive foreign policy; paleoconservatives critical of globalism; and libertarians pressing for minimal government. After the Cold War, and especially with the Tea Party movement (from 2009) and the election of Donald Trump (2016), nationalist and populist themes — immigration restriction, tariffs and scepticism of foreign intervention — strained the fusionist settlement, producing open debate between "national conservatives" and defenders of the Reagan-era consensus.

Organisations and influence

The movement is expressed principally through the Republican Party and supported by think tanks such as the Heritage Foundation and the American Enterprise Institute, media outlets, and advocacy groups. Its judicial wing, organised around the Federalist Society and originalist interpretation of the Constitution, achieved lasting influence through federal court appointments.
