More than half of the world's population now lives in cities.

Urbanization (or urbanisation) is the population shift from rural to urban areas, the corresponding decrease in the proportion of people living in rural areas, and the ways in which societies adapt to this change. It includes both the growth of existing cities and the formation of new ones, and it is closely linked to industrialisation, modernisation and economic development.

History

Cities remained small minorities of world population until the Industrial Revolution, when factory employment, railways and improved food supplies triggered rapid urban growth in Britain, then across Europe and North America. In the 20th and 21st centuries the focus of urban growth shifted to developing countries: cities such as Lagos, Dhaka and Kinshasa have grown into megacities of over ten million, and the world's urban population surpassed its rural population around 2007.

Causes and effects

"Push" factors such as rural poverty and land scarcity and "pull" factors such as jobs, services and education draw migrants to cities. Urbanization brings higher productivity, innovation and access to services, but also congestion, air and water pollution, housing pressure and the growth of informal settlements (slums), in which roughly one billion people live. Cities account for the majority of energy use and carbon emissions, yet their density can also enable more sustainable transport and infrastructure per person.

Planning responses

Governments respond through urban planning, investment in public transport and sanitation, slum upgrading and new towns. Concepts such as smart cities, transit-oriented development and the "15-minute city" attempt to manage the quality of urban growth.
