Laboratory glassware

MaterialsBorosilicate glass, soda–lime glass, fused quartz
Common itemsBeakers, flasks, test tubes, burettes, condensers, pipettes
Key propertyChemical resistance, thermal shock resistance

Laboratory glassware refers to the wide variety of glass apparatus used in scientific laboratories for measuring, mixing, heating, storing, and reacting chemicals. Glass is favored for its transparency, chemical inertness toward most reagents, and ease of cleaning and shaping, although plastic ware is now used for some purposes.

Materials

Most modern laboratory glassware is made of borosilicate glass (such as Pyrex or Duran), which has a low coefficient of thermal expansion and resists thermal shock, allowing vessels to be heated directly. Soda–lime glass is cheaper and used for items such as bottles and some test tubes. Fused quartz is used where high temperatures or ultraviolet transparency are required.

Common items

Containers and reaction vessels

  • Beakers — cylindrical vessels with a spout, for mixing and rough measurement.
  • Erlenmeyer (conical) flasks — narrow-necked flasks that reduce splashing and evaporation.
  • Round-bottom flasks — used with heating mantles for distillation and reflux.
  • Test tubes — small open tubes for handling small samples.

Measuring glassware

  • Volumetric flasks and volumetric pipettes — calibrated for precise volumes (Class A or B tolerances).
  • Graduated cylinders — for approximate volume measurement.
  • Burettes — long graduated tubes with a stopcock, used in titrations.

Other apparatus

Condensers (such as the Liebig and Allihn types) cool vapors in distillation setups; separatory funnels partition immiscible liquids; desiccators store moisture-sensitive samples; watch glasses, stirring rods, and glass funnels serve auxiliary roles.

Ground glass joints

Much laboratory glassware uses standardized interchangeable ground-glass joints (taper joints), allowing flasks, condensers, and adapters to be assembled quickly into complex apparatus without corks or rubber bungs.

Care and safety

Glassware must be inspected for cracks and star fractures before use, especially under vacuum or pressure. Cleaning ranges from detergent washing to acid baths for trace analysis, and broken glass is disposed of in dedicated sharps containers.
