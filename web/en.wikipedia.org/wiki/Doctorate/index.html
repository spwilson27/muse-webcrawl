A doctorate (from Latin doctor, "teacher") or doctoral degree is the highest level of academic degree, awarded by universities and other institutions of higher education. In most countries, a doctorate qualifies the holder to teach at the university level in the degree's field or to work in a specific profession. Major types include research doctorates such as the Doctor of Philosophy (PhD), professional doctorates such as the Doctor of Medicine (MD) and Juris Doctor (JD), and honorary doctorates awarded for distinguished contributions.

History

The doctorate originated in the medieval European universities, where the licentia docendi (licence to teach) was the terminal qualification; the first doctorates were in law, medicine and theology. The modern research doctorate modeled on the German PhD was introduced at Yale in 1861 in the United States and spread worldwide during the twentieth century.

Requirements

Research doctorates typically require the candidate to complete advanced coursework, pass qualifying or comprehensive examinations, and produce and defend a dissertation (thesis) presenting original research before a committee of examiners. Professional doctorates emphasize applied practice, sometimes with a capstone project in place of a dissertation. Holders are entitled to the title "Doctor."
