Working time

Working time is the period of time that a person spends at paid labour. It excludes unpaid work such as domestic labour and childcare, and personal time for rest, sleep and leisure. Working time is regulated in most countries through labour law, collective agreements and international standards, covering maximum hours, rest breaks, paid annual leave and overtime. Average annual hours actually worked per worker vary widely between advanced economies — under 1,400 hours in Germany and around 1,750–1,800 in the United States in recent OECD data.

History

Pre-industrial work followed seasonal and task-based rhythms, but the factory system imposed clock-regulated days of 12–16 hours, six days a week, for adults and children alike. The 19th-century labour movement made hours its central demand: Robert Owen's slogan of 1817 — "Eight hours labour, eight hours recreation, eight hours rest" — became the programme of the international eight-hour-day movement, commemorated in May Day. In Britain the Factory Acts progressively limited hours for women and children (Ten Hours Act 1847); in the United States the Fair Labor Standards Act 1938 set the 40-hour standard week with overtime premiums. The first ILO convention of 1919 adopted the 8-hour day and 48-hour week; France legislated the 35-hour week in 1998–2000.

Modern regulation

The EU Working Time Directive (2003/88/EC) guarantees a maximum average 48-hour week including overtime, 11 hours' daily rest, weekly rest, breaks and at least four weeks' paid annual leave, though the UK historically allowed individual opt-outs from the 48-hour cap. Many countries set statutory minima for paid leave far above US levels, where there is no federal entitlement to paid vacation. Part-time work, flexible working, compressed weeks and four-day-week trials (notably in Iceland 2015–2019 and the UK 2022 pilot, after which most participating firms kept the policy) are reshaping norms.

Effects of long hours

WHO/ILO research published in 2021 estimated that working 55 or more hours per week is associated with a 35% higher risk of stroke and 17% higher risk of dying from ischaemic heart disease compared with 35–40 hours, attributing some 745,000 deaths in 2016 to long working hours. Phenomena such as Japan's karōshi (death from overwork) and China's "996" schedule (9 a.m.–9 p.m., 6 days) — ruled unlawful by Chinese authorities in 2021 — illustrate continuing contention over excessive hours.
