Vasicek model

In finance, the Vasicek model is a mathematical model describing the evolution of interest rates. Proposed by Oldřich Vašíček in 1977, it was the first model to capture mean reversion in interest rates and remains a foundational example of a one-factor short-rate model. It is a special case of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process applied to the instantaneous short rate.

The model

The short rate rt follows the stochastic differential equation:

drt = a(b − rt)dt + σ dWt

where b is the long-run mean rate, a is the speed of reversion toward that mean, σ is the instantaneous volatility, and Wt is a Wiener process under the risk-neutral measure. When the rate is above b the drift is negative, and vice versa, so rates are pulled toward the long-run level while being continuously disturbed by random shocks. Because the process is Gaussian, the short rate at any future time is normally distributed with a mean that decays exponentially toward b.

Bond pricing

The model admits closed-form prices for zero-coupon bonds, which are exponential-affine functions of the short rate, and hence an analytic term structure of interest rates. This tractability made the model extremely influential in the development of interest-rate derivatives pricing.

Limitations and extensions

The model's best-known defect is that, being Gaussian, it allows interest rates to become negative with positive probability — a feature once thought unrealistic, though less troubling in the era of negative policy rates in Europe and Japan. The model also cannot be calibrated to fit an arbitrary initial yield curve exactly; the Cox–Ingersoll–Ross model, which keeps rates non-negative, and the Hull–White model, which adds a time-dependent drift to fit the initial term structure, were developed partly in response.
