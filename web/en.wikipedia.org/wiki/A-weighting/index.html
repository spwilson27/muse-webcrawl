A-weighting

A-weighting
Frequency weighting for sound measurements
Unit commonly reported: dB(A) or dBA.

A-weighting is a frequency-dependent adjustment applied to measurements of sound pressure level so that the result better reflects the sensitivity of human hearing. Instruments using it attenuate low and very high frequencies, where the ear is less sensitive at moderate levels, and slightly emphasise part of the midrange. Results are usually written dB(A), dBA or LAeq when averaged over time.

Origins

The curve derives from the equal-loudness contours measured by Fletcher and Munson in the 1930s, particularly the contour for relatively quiet sounds around 40 phons. Standardising bodies incorporated A-weighting into sound level meters because it was simple, repeatable and roughly matched perceived loudness for many everyday noises.

Use

A-weighting dominates environmental noise, occupational hearing-conservation and product-noise measurements. Rules for road traffic, aircraft, workplaces and appliances often specify A-weighted levels because regulators need a single comparable number. It is also used in audio engineering for noise specifications, although other curves may be preferred for particular signals.

Limitations

Because human sensitivity changes with level, a fixed curve based on quiet sounds cannot capture loudness perfectly. A-weighting can understate strong low-frequency noise from sources such as wind turbines, large engines or music with heavy bass. For low-frequency assessment, C-weighting, Z-weighting (unweighted within the instrument band) or spectral analysis may be more informative. Despite these limits, A-weighting remains entrenched through decades of standards and comparable data.
