Ancient Egyptian funeral practices

The ancient Egyptians developed elaborate funeral practices designed to ensure the survival of the soul in the afterlife. These practices included mummification of the body, the performance of burial rituals such as the Opening of the Mouth ceremony, and interment of the dead with grave goods ranging from everyday objects to precious treasures, depending on the wealth of the deceased.

Beliefs about the afterlife

Egyptian religion held that the human soul consisted of several parts, including the ka (life force), the ba (personality), and the akh (the transfigured spirit). Preservation of the body was essential so that the ka would have a dwelling. After death the soul was judged in the Hall of Maat, where the heart was weighed against the feather of truth before Osiris; the worthy passed into the Field of Reeds, an idealised version of earthly life.

Mummification

Embalmers removed the internal organs, storing the lungs, liver, stomach, and intestines in canopic jars, while the heart was usually left in place. The body was dried with natron salt for about forty days, then wrapped in linen bandages with amulets placed between the layers. The whole process traditionally took seventy days. The Book of the Dead, a collection of spells, was often buried with the deceased to guide them through the underworld (Duat).

Burial customs

Funerals included a procession to the tomb, ritual mourning, and the Opening of the Mouth ceremony, in which a priest touched the mouth of the mummy or statue with ritual implements to restore the senses for the afterlife. Tombs ranged from simple pit graves to the pyramids of the Old and Middle Kingdoms and the rock-cut tombs of the Valley of the Kings in the New Kingdom.

Grave goods

Objects buried with the dead included food, furniture, clothing, jewellery, and shabti figures — small figurines intended to act as servants for the deceased in the afterlife. The tomb of Tutankhamun, discovered nearly intact in 1922, is the most famous example of the wealth that could accompany a royal burial.
