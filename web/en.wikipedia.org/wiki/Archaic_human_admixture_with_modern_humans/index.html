Archaic human admixture with modern humans

Archaic admixture
Archaic groupsNeanderthals, Denisovans, others
Neanderthal ancestry~1–4% in non-African genomes
Denisovan ancestryUp to ~5% in some Oceanian populations
Key evidenceAncient DNA sequencing (2010– )

Genetic studies on ancient DNA have shown that anatomically modern humans interbred with several groups of archaic humans, including Neanderthals and Denisovans. This archaic human admixture occurred after modern humans dispersed out of Africa, principally between about 50,000 and 60,000 years ago, though additional episodes of gene flow have been identified both earlier and later.

Neanderthal admixture

The sequencing of the Neanderthal genome in 2010 by Svante Pääbo's team demonstrated that present-day non-African populations derive roughly 1–4% of their ancestry from Neanderthals. Because the proportion is broadly similar in Europeans, East Asians, and other non-African groups, the main admixture event is thought to have occurred shortly after the out-of-Africa expansion, probably in western Asia. East Asian populations carry somewhat more Neanderthal ancestry, reflecting additional pulses of gene flow or dilution in Europeans by later, less-admixed groups. Some introgressed segments were advantageous — influencing immunity, skin and hair traits, and adaptation to cold — while others were selected against, leaving "deserts" of Neanderthal ancestry around conserved genes, notably on the X chromosome.

Denisovan admixture

Denisovans, identified from a finger bone found in Denisova Cave in Siberia, contributed ancestry mainly to populations in Asia and Oceania. Indigenous peoples of New Guinea and Australia carry the highest proportions, up to about 5%, and Melanesians, Aboriginal Australians, and some Southeast Asian island groups preserve distinct episodes of Denisovan gene flow from at least two genetically divergent Denisovan populations. A famous adaptive legacy is the EPAS1 haplotype in Tibetans, which aids life at high altitude and derives from Denisovan-like ancestors.

Evidence within Africa

Statistical models of present-day African genomes indicate gene flow from one or more "ghost" archaic populations that diverged before the Neanderthal–modern human split, contributing a few percent of ancestry to some West African groups. Direct fossil-genome confirmation is lacking because of poor DNA preservation in tropical climates.

Significance

Archaic admixture reshaped understanding of human evolution: rather than fully replacing archaic populations, expanding modern humans absorbed them in small measure. Introgressed DNA continues to affect present-day traits and disease risks, including immune responses, metabolism, and susceptibility to certain viruses, and it provides a molecular record of contacts between human lineages separated for hundreds of thousands of years.
