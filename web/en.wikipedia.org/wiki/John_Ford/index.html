John Ford

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: John Ford directing in Monument Valley: the director in hat and eyepatch-era glasses beside his camera, buttes behind]

John Ford (born John Martin Feeney; 1 February 1894 – 31 August 1973) was an American film director and producer, among the most influential and honoured filmmakers in history. He won a record four Academy Awards for Best Director — for The Informer (1935), The Grapes of Wrath (1940), How Green Was My Valley (1941) and The Quiet Man (1952) — and defined the Western as America's myth with Stagecoach (1939), My Darling Clementine (1946), the Cavalry Trilogy, The Searchers (1956) and The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance (1962).

Ford's stock company (John Wayne above all, but also Henry Fonda, Maureen O'Hara, Ward Bond and dozens of bit players) and his Monument Valley locations created the visual grammar of the West; his cutting in the camera and terror of producers kept his style pure composition and ritual.

Career shape

Born in Maine to Irish immigrants, Ford followed his brother Francis to Hollywood and directed some 140 films across fifty years, from silent Westerns with Harry Carey to the John Wayne era. The Grapes of Wrath and How Green Was My Valley (which beat Citizen Kane to Best Picture) made the forties his decade of prestige; his wartime service (he filmed at Midway and D-Day, wounded at the former) produced great documentaries.

The Searchers and reassessment

The Searchers — Wayne's Ethan Edwards, obsessed and racist, framed in the doorway that opens and closes the film — was coolly received in 1956 and has risen to the summit of the canon (regular top-ten in critics' polls; a formative influence on Scorsese, Spielberg, Lucas). Ford's own politics and his films' treatment of Native Americans have been re-argued ever since; the films out-argue everyone.
