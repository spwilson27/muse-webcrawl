Hysteroscopy

Hysteroscopy is the inspection of the uterine cavity by endoscopy with access through the cervix. It allows for the diagnosis of intrauterine pathology and serves as a method for surgical intervention (operative hysteroscopy). The hysteroscope carries channels for a light source, a camera and instruments, while fluid or gas distends the uterine cavity for visualisation.

Direct visualisation of the endometrial cavity guides diagnosis and treatment.

Indications

Diagnostic hysteroscopy is used to investigate abnormal uterine bleeding, postmenopausal bleeding, infertility, recurrent miscarriage and suspected endometrial polyps, submucosal fibroids, intrauterine adhesions (Asherman syndrome) and congenital anomalies such as a uterine septum. Operative hysteroscopy allows polypectomy, myomectomy of submucosal fibroids, division of adhesions or a septum, removal of retained products of conception or foreign bodies, and endometrial ablation.

Procedure

Modern practice frequently uses an outpatient "see and treat" approach with miniaturised scopes and a vaginoscopic (no-touch) technique, often without anaesthesia. Distension media include saline for mechanical and bipolar work and non-conductive solutions for monopolar electrosurgery; fluid absorption must be monitored to avoid overload. Complications include uterine perforation, bleeding, infection and fluid overload.

History

The first successful hysteroscopy is credited to Pantaleoni in 1869, who used a modified cystoscope to examine the uterus and cauterise a polyp; the technique only became routine with the improvements in optics, light sources and distension media of the later twentieth century.
