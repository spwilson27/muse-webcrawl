User interface design (UI design) is the design of user interfaces for machines and software — such as computers, home appliances, mobile devices and other electronic devices — with the goal of making the user's interaction as simple and efficient as possible, in terms of accomplishing user goals. It encompasses the look, layout and interactivity of a product's interface: the arrangement of buttons, text, images and controls, the choice of typography and colour, and the responses of the interface to user input.

UI design is one part of the broader discipline of user experience (UX) design, which also covers research into users' needs, the structure of information, and the overall feel of the product. Whereas UX design concerns the whole experience, UI design focuses specifically on the interface itself — the point of contact between the human and the machine.

History

Early computer interfaces were batch-processing systems and later text-based command lines, which demanded that users memorise commands. The graphical user interface (GUI), developed at Xerox PARC in the 1970s with the Alto computer and popularised by the Apple Macintosh (1984) and Microsoft Windows, replaced commands with direct manipulation of visible objects — windows, icons, menus and a pointer (the "WIMP" paradigm). The spread of the web in the 1990s added web design as a branch of the field; the launch of the iPhone in 2007 shifted the centre of gravity to touch interfaces on mobile devices, with flat, gesture-based design languages such as Apple's Human Interface Guidelines and Google's Material Design.

Principles

Widely taught principles of good interface design include:

  • Clarity and simplicity — the interface should communicate what elements do and avoid unnecessary decoration.
  • Consistency — similar elements should look and behave similarly throughout a product and follow platform conventions.
  • Feedback — the system should keep users informed about what is happening, within a reasonable time.
  • Visibility of system status and affordances — controls should make their possible actions perceptible (Donald Norman's concept of affordance and signifiers).
  • Error prevention and forgiveness — designs should prevent mistakes where possible and make actions reversible (undo) where not.
  • Recognition rather than recall — minimise the user's memory load by making options visible; one of Jakob Nielsen's ten usability heuristics.
  • Accessibility — interfaces should be usable by people with diverse abilities, following standards such as the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG).

Process

Interface design typically proceeds iteratively: research into users and tasks; sketching and wireframing low-fidelity layouts; building interactive prototypes; visual design applying the product's design system (a library of reusable components, styles and patterns); and usability testing with representative users, whose results feed back into redesign. Requirements analysis balances the goals of the users, the constraints of the technology, and the objectives of the organisation — sometimes summarised as keeping the design at the intersection of what is useful, usable and desirable.

Types of interface

Graphical and touch interfaces

The dominant forms today, relying on visual metaphors and direct manipulation with a pointer, finger or stylus.

Voice user interfaces

Interfaces operated by speech, as in smart speakers and virtual assistants, requiring design of dialogue flows rather than screens.

Conversational and gestural interfaces

Chat-based interfaces, and gesture recognition in gaming, virtual and augmented reality, extend design beyond the screen; tangible user interfaces embed computation in physical objects.
