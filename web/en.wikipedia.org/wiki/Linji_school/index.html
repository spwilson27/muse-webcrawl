The Linji school (Chinese: 臨濟宗; pinyin: Línjì zōng) is one of the five houses of Chan Buddhism in China, founded by the Tang-dynasty master Linji Yixuan (Japanese: Rinzai Gigen, died 866). It became the dominant form of Chan in the Song dynasty and was transmitted to Japan, where it survives as the Rinzai school of Zen, and to Korea and Vietnam.

Teaching and practice

Linji is famous for startling, confrontational teaching methods intended to jolt students out of conceptual thinking: shouts (the "Katsu!"), blows with a stick, paradoxical dialogues and the maxim "If you meet the Buddha on the road, kill him". His recorded sayings, the Record of Linji, expound the doctrine of the "true person of no rank" — the awakened nature already present in each student, which no external authority can supply. Linji classified his exchanges with students in schemes such as the Three Mysterious Gates and the Fourfold Shouts.

Later development

In the Song dynasty the Linji lineage, particularly through Dahui Zonggao (1089–1163), systematised the intensive contemplation of a huatou — the critical phrase of a gong'an (kōan) — as the school's central practice, in polemic against the "silent illumination" of the rival Caodong school. In Japan, Rinzai Zen, introduced formally by Eisai in the Kamakura period and reinvigorated by Hakuin Ekaku in the eighteenth century, retained this emphasis on kōan study and a rigorous, often austere training style, and became closely associated with the samurai class and the arts.
