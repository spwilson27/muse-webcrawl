The tree of life is a metaphor and a scientific model describing the relationships of all living organisms on Earth through evolution: all life descends, with modification, from a last universal common ancestor (LUCA) that lived roughly four billion years ago, and the branching pattern of descent is drawn as a tree — more formally, a phylogenetic tree.

History

Charles Darwin made the tree the central image of evolution: the only figure in On the Origin of Species (1859) is a branching diagram, and he wrote that "the affinities of all the beings of the same class have sometimes been represented by a great tree", which he believed expressed the truth of common descent. Ernst Haeckel later drew literal, oak-like trees of life. In the 20th century the reconstruction of the tree was transformed by molecular data: Carl Woese's comparison of ribosomal RNA sequences led to the three-domain system of Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya (1990), redrawing the deepest branches and showing that most of life's diversity is microbial.

Complications

The tree metaphor is complicated by horizontal gene transfer, especially among prokaryotes, and by hybridisation and endosymbiosis (the origins of mitochondria and chloroplasts), which cause lineages to merge as well as split. Some biologists therefore speak of a "web" or "network" of life, at least for the microbial world, though a tree-like signal remains strong for the core genes of cellular organisms. Large collaborative projects continue to assemble a comprehensive open tree of life from published phylogenies.
