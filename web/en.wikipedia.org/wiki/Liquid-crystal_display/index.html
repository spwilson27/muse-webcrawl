Liquid-crystal display

Liquid-crystal display (LCD)
TypeFlat-panel display
Operating principleLight modulation by liquid crystals between polarisers
Key developmentsLiquid crystals discovered 1888 (Reinitzer); twisted nematic effect 1970; TFT active matrix 1980s–90s
ApplicationsTelevisions, monitors, phones, calculators, instrument panels

A liquid-crystal display (LCD) is a flat-panel display or other electronically modulated optical device that uses the light-modulating properties of liquid crystals combined with polarisers to produce images in colour or monochrome. Liquid crystals do not emit light directly; most LCDs rely on a backlight or reflector to produce images.

Working principle

An LCD pixel consists of a layer of liquid crystal molecules aligned between two transparent electrodes and two polarising filters whose axes are arranged, in a typical twisted-nematic (TN) cell, at right angles to each other. With no electric field, the crystal twists the polarisation of light so it passes the second filter; applying a voltage untwists the molecules, blocking light and darkening the pixel. Colour displays add red, green and blue sub-pixels with colour filters. Simple displays are driven in passive-matrix fashion, while most modern panels use an active matrix of thin-film transistors (TFTs) that hold each pixel's state, improving contrast and response time. Panel variants include TN, in-plane switching (IPS) and vertical alignment (VA), trading viewing angle, contrast and speed. Modern backlights use white LEDs, sometimes with local dimming.

History

Liquid crystals were discovered in 1888 by the Austrian botanist Friedrich Reinitzer, who observed that cholesteryl benzoate had two melting points. Practical display research began at RCA in the 1960s under George Heilmeier, whose team demonstrated the dynamic-scattering display in 1968. The twisted-nematic field effect was patented by Martin Schadt and Wolfgang Helfrich at Hoffmann-La Roche in 1970 and underlies most LCDs. LCD watches and calculators appeared in the 1970s, portable computer screens in the 1980s, and — after improvements in TFT manufacturing — LCD televisions and monitors overtook cathode-ray tubes in sales in the mid-2000s. LCDs remain the most widely used display technology, although OLED displays compete in premium phones and televisions.

Advantages and limitations

LCDs are thin, light, energy-efficient and free of geometric distortion and flicker, and can be made in very small and very large sizes. Their drawbacks relative to emissive displays include limited contrast (backlight leakage), viewing-angle dependence in cheaper panels, and finite pixel response times that can blur fast motion.
