Income tax in the United States

Income tax in the United States is imposed by the federal government, and by many states and localities. Income taxes are imposed on individuals, corporations, estates and trusts. The federal income tax, administered by the Internal Revenue Service (IRS), is the federal government's largest source of revenue.

History

The first federal income tax was enacted during the Civil War in 1861–1862 to fund the war effort and was later repealed. After the Supreme Court struck down a peacetime income tax in Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co. (1895), the Sixteenth Amendment, ratified in 1913, authorised Congress to tax incomes without apportionment among the states. The modern system dates from the Revenue Act of 1913 and grew greatly during World War II, when withholding from wages was introduced.

Structure

Taxpayers compute taxable income as gross income minus deductions, then apply graduated marginal rates. Individuals file Form 1040 annually, generally by April 15. The Internal Revenue Code is Title 26 of the United States Code. Most states impose their own income taxes, with rates and bases varying widely; a few states have no income tax on wages.

Taxable income and rates

Gross income includes wages, salaries, business income, capital gains, dividends, interest, rents and most other income. Capital gains and qualified dividends are taxed at preferential rates for individuals. Payroll taxes for Social Security and Medicare are separate from income tax.
