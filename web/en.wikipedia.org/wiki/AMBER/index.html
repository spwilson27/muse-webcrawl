AMBER was a feasibility study into advanced, distance-based road-user charging conducted in Singapore in the late 2000s and early 2010s, examining whether satellite (GNSS) positioning could replace the gantry-based Electronic Road Pricing (ERP) system that Singapore pioneered in 1998. Rather than charging vehicles only when they pass fixed gantries, a distance-based system can charge according to how far, where, and when a vehicle travels on congested roads.

Background

Singapore's ERP system — the world's first urban congestion charging scheme of its kind — used overhead gantries and in-vehicle units with stored-value cards. By the mid-2000s the Land Transport Authority (LTA) was studying a next-generation system (often called ERP2) based on the Global Navigation Satellite System, which would remove the need for gantries and allow more flexible, fine-grained pricing. Trials under the AMBER programme tested the accuracy and reliability of GNSS positioning in Singapore's dense urban environment, where tall buildings can obstruct satellite signals, and evaluated on-board units and back-office charging.

Outcome

The studies informed Singapore's long-term move toward a satellite-based ERP system. The LTA subsequently contracted for a next-generation, GNSS-based road pricing system, originally planned to replace the gantry system in the early 2020s; implementation was later deferred and phased, with new on-board units to be installed progressively. Distance-based charging has remained an influential reference case for cities worldwide considering alternatives to cordon and gantry schemes.
