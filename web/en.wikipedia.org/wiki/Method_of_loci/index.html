Method of loci

The method of loci (Latin: loci, plural of locus, "places") is a mnemonic device in which items to be remembered are associated with specific physical locations in a familiar environment. Also known as the memory palace or memory journey technique, it is among the oldest known mnemonic systems and remains the technique of choice for competitive memory athletes.

Legend and history

Classical tradition attributes the method to the Greek poet Simonides of Ceos, who, after a banquet hall collapsed, was able to identify the crushed bodies of the guests by remembering where each had been sitting. The technique was described in Roman rhetorical handbooks — the Rhetorica ad Herennium, Cicero's De oratore, and Quintilian's Institutio oratoria — as part of the art of memory for orators who needed to deliver long speeches without notes. It flourished again in the medieval and Renaissance memory arts.

Method

The practitioner commits to memory a familiar route or building (a "palace"), with a sequence of distinct locations (loci). Each item to be remembered is converted into a vivid, often bizarre image and mentally placed at a locus. Recall proceeds by mentally walking the route and "seeing" the images in order. Research in cognitive psychology confirms the technique's effectiveness, and neuroimaging studies show memory champions using it engage brain regions for spatial navigation, such as the hippocampus.
