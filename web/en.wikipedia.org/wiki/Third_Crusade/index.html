Third Crusade

[Image: Richard I and Saladin in single combat, from a medieval manuscript — the two leaders never actually met]

The Third Crusade (1189–1192), also known as the Kings' Crusade, was an attempt by three European monarchs of Western Christianity to reconquer the Holy Land following the capture of Jerusalem by Saladin in 1187 after the Battle of Hattin. It was partially successful, recapturing the important cities of Acre and Jaffa and reversing most of Saladin's conquests along the coast, but it failed to win back Jerusalem.

The expeditions

The aged Holy Roman Emperor Frederick Barbarossa led the largest army overland through Anatolia, defeating the Seljuks at Iconium, but drowned crossing the Saleph River in June 1190; most of his army dispersed. Philip II of France and Richard I of England travelled by sea, quarrelling en route. Richard conquered Cyprus from its Byzantine ruler Isaac Komnenos — a vital supply base for the crusader states thereafter — and arrived at Acre in June 1191, where the crusaders, with the kings' help, forced the surrender of the besieged city in July.

Richard and Saladin

After Philip's departure, Richard led the army south, defeating Saladin at Arsuf (September 1191) and recapturing Jaffa. Twice he advanced within sight of Jerusalem but judged it indefensible if taken. Negotiations with Saladin — whose brother al-Adil Richard dealt with cordially, even proposing a marriage alliance — produced the Treaty of Jaffa (September 1192): the coast from Tyre to Jaffa remained Christian, and unarmed Christian pilgrims were granted access to Jerusalem. Richard sailed for home in October 1192; on the journey he was captured by Leopold of Austria and held to ransom by Emperor Henry VI.

Legacy

The crusade preserved a reduced Kingdom of Jerusalem, now centred on Acre, for another century. The mutual respect of Richard and Saladin became legend in both Christian and Muslim tradition.
