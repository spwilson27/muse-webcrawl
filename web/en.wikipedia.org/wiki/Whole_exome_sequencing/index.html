Whole exome sequencing

Placeholder image: schematic of an exome-sequencing workflow.

Whole exome sequencing (WES) is a genomic technique for sequencing all of the protein-coding regions of genes in a genome, collectively known as the exome. It consists of two steps: selecting DNA corresponding to exonic regions, usually by hybridisation capture with biotinylated probes, and sequencing the enriched material with high-throughput DNA sequencing. Although the exome makes up only about 1–2% of the human genome, it contains the great majority of known disease-causing variants, making WES far cheaper and faster than whole genome sequencing for many purposes.

Applications

WES became established around 2009–2010, when studies demonstrated its power to identify the genes responsible for rare Mendelian disorders, such as Miller syndrome and Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease, from small numbers of patients. It is now widely used in clinical diagnostics for rare diseases and developmental disorders, in cancer genomics (often comparing tumour and normal exomes), and in population and medical genetics research, including large biobank-scale projects.

Limitations

Because capture is imperfect, coverage of some exons is uneven; WES also cannot detect most non-coding regulatory variants, large structural variants, or repeat expansions, and it yields incidental findings that raise ethical questions. Falling costs of whole genome sequencing have narrowed its price advantage, though WES remains common in clinical practice.
