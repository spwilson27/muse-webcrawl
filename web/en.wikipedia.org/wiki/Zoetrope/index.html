Zoetrope

A zoetrope is one of several pre-film animation devices that produce the illusion of motion by displaying a sequence of drawings or photographs showing progressive phases of that motion. The name derives from the Greek words zoe ("life") and tropos ("turning") — a "wheel of life".

Description

The zoetrope consists of a cylinder with cuts vertically in the sides. On the inner surface of the cylinder is a band with images. As the cylinder spins, the user looks through the slits at the images on the opposite side of the cylinder's interior. The scanning of the slits keeps the pictures from simply blurring together, and the user sees a rapid succession of images, producing the illusion of motion. In the generic form, the slits serve as a shutter equivalent to those in modern animated GIFs or praxinoscopes.

History

The mathematician Simon von Stampfer independently invented a similar device, the stroboscope, in 1833, and William George Horner's 1834 "daedalum" or "wheel of the devil" was an early version of the same principle. The device was popularised in the 1860s: in the United States, Milton Bradley patented his version in 1867 under the name "zoetrope", and it became the most successful of the Victorian optical toys, alongside the phenakistoscope and the flip book. In France, Émile Reynaud's praxinoscope (1877) replaced the slits with mirrors, giving a brighter, clearer image.

Modern zoetropes

Three-dimensional zoetropes use a sequence of models rather than drawings, illuminated by a strobe light synchronised with rotation; large examples have been built as public art and museum exhibits, notably the Pixar zoetrope created for exhibitions of animation art, and Gregory Barsamian's kinetic sculptures. Zoetrope-like sequences are also used in London Underground station installations that animate as trains pass.
