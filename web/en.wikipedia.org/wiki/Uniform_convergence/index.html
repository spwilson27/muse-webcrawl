Uniform convergence

In the mathematical field of analysis, uniform convergence is a mode of convergence of functions stronger than pointwise convergence. A sequence of functions (f_n) converges uniformly to a limiting function f on a set E if, given any arbitrarily small positive number ε, a number N can be found such that each of the functions f_N, fN+1, ... differs from f by no more than ε at every point of E. Crucially, N depends only on ε and not on the point chosen.

Comparison with pointwise convergence

In pointwise convergence the index required may depend on both ε and the point x, so convergence can occur at wildly different rates across the domain. Classic examples show that the pointwise limit of continuous functions need not be continuous (e.g. x^n on [0,1] converges to a discontinuous function), whereas a uniform limit of continuous functions is always continuous. Uniform convergence also licenses exchanging limits with integration on a bounded interval and, under additional hypotheses, with differentiation.

Tests and generalizations

For series, the Weierstrass M-test provides a practical sufficient condition for uniform convergence by comparison with a convergent series of constants; other tools include Dirichlet's and Abel's uniform convergence tests. The concept extends to sequences of functions between metric and topological spaces using uniform spaces, where convergence in the uniform (supremum) metric captures the same idea. Cauchy's criterion characterizes uniform convergence internally, and Dini's theorem shows that a monotone sequence of continuous functions converging pointwise to a continuous limit on a compact set converges uniformly.
