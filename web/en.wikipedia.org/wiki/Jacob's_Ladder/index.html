Jacob's Ladder

Jacob's Ladder (Hebrew: סֻלָּם יַעֲקֹב, Sulam Yaakov) is a ladder or staircase reaching to heaven that the patriarch Jacob dreams of in the Book of Genesis. The account, in Genesis 28:10–19, is one of the best-known episodes of the Hebrew Bible and has been richly interpreted in Jewish, Christian, and Islamic tradition.

The biblical account

Fleeing his brother Esau, Jacob travels from Beersheba toward Haran and stops for the night at a place he later names Bethel ("house of God"), resting his head on a stone. In a dream he sees a ladder set on the earth with its top reaching the sky, and angels of God ascending and descending on it. God stands above it and renews the covenant made with Abraham and Isaac, promising Jacob the land, innumerable descendants, and divine protection:

"I am the Lord, the God of Abraham your father and the God of Isaac; the land on which you lie I will give to you and to your offspring... Know that I am with you and will keep you wherever you go."

Awaking, Jacob declares the place "the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven," sets up the stone as a pillar, and anoints it with oil.

Interpretation

The Hebrew word sullam occurs only here in the Bible and has been rendered as ladder, staircase, or ramp, possibly evoking a Mesopotamian ziggurat. Jewish commentators read the ladder as symbolizing prayer, the Torah given at Sinai (by gematria, sullam and Sinai share the numerical value 130), or the exile and rise and fall of empires. In Christianity, Jesus' words in John 1:51 — "you will see heaven opened and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of Man" — led theologians to see the ladder as a figure of Christ, and it became a common image of spiritual ascent, as in John Climacus's Ladder of Divine Ascent. Islam also recounts the dream in tradition connected with the Prophet's ascension narratives.

In culture

The image has given its name to plants, a high-voltage electrical arc device, toys, and numerous works of music and art, and "Jacob's ladder" remains a common metaphor for the connection between earth and heaven and for spiritual progress.
