Coefficient of inbreeding

The coefficient of inbreeding of an individual is the probability that two alleles at any locus in that individual are identical by descent from a common ancestor of the individual's parents. It is a standard measure in population genetics and animal breeding, usually denoted F.

Definition and calculation

Sewall Wright developed the path-coefficient method for computing the coefficient of inbreeding from a pedigree. For each common ancestor A of the individual's two parents, one traces a path through the pedigree and sums contributions of the form

F = Σ (1/2)n + m + 1 (1 + FA),

where n and m are the numbers of generations from each parent up to the common ancestor, and FA is the ancestor's own inbreeding coefficient. Offspring of a parent–offspring or full-sibling mating have F = 0.25; offspring of first cousins have F = 0.0625; and offspring of unrelated parents have F = 0 by definition relative to the base population.

Relationship to relatedness

The coefficient of inbreeding of an individual is closely related to the coefficient of relationship (coancestry) between its parents: the inbreeding coefficient of the offspring equals the coancestry coefficient of the parents. Related measures such as Wright's F-statistics (FST, FIS, FIT) describe inbreeding and population structure at the level of populations rather than pedigrees.

Applications

In animal and plant breeding, the coefficient of inbreeding is used to predict inbreeding depression and to manage mating programmes that maintain genetic diversity. In human genetics, high coefficients are associated with consanguineous marriage and with elevated risk of recessive genetic disorders, and the concept underlies pedigree analyses of historical populations such as European royal families.
