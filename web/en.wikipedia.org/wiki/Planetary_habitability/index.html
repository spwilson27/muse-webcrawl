Planetary habitability

Planetary habitability is the measure of a planet's or a natural satellite's potential to develop and sustain an environment hospitable to life. Life may be generated directly on a planet or satellite endogenously, or be transferred to it from another body, through a hypothetical process known as panspermia. Environments do not need to contain life to be considered habitable, nor are accepted habitable zones the only areas in which life might arise.

The habitable zone

As it is understood, the surface of a planet must lie within its star's circumstellar habitable zone (CHZ) — the range of orbits where liquid water can persist on the surface under sufficient atmospheric pressure — for the planet to have the potential to support complex, surface-dwelling life as we know it. The concept was articulated in the 1950s–60s (notably by Su-Shu Huang and Stephen Dole) and formalised in modern form by James Kasting and colleagues in 1993. For the Sun, the conservative habitable zone extends roughly from 0.95 to 1.67 astronomical units. Earth lies within it; Venus is interior to it and Mars near its outer edge.

The zone's boundaries depend on climate feedbacks: the inner edge is set by runaway greenhouse effects, the outer edge by the maximum warming attainable from a CO2 greenhouse before carbon dioxide itself condenses. Around smaller, cooler red-dwarf stars the zone lies much closer in, raising issues of tidal locking and stellar flares; around hot stars it lies farther out, but such stars live shorter lives.

Other requirements

  • Stable star: low variability and a lifetime long enough (billions of years) for life to evolve.
  • Right planet size: enough gravity to retain an atmosphere and sustain a molten interior, but not so much that the planet becomes a mini-Neptune.
  • Atmosphere and magnetosphere: to regulate temperature, shield radiation and protect surface water from stellar wind erosion.
  • Essential elements: carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulfur (CHNOPS), plus trace metals.
  • Energy sources: starlight for photosynthesis, or chemical energy (as at hydrothermal vents) for subsurface life.
  • Climate stabilisation: on Earth, the carbonate–silicate cycle acts as a long-term thermostat.

Beyond the classic zone

Habitability need not be confined to planetary surfaces. In the outer Solar System, icy moons such as Europa and Enceladus harbour global subsurface oceans kept liquid by tidal heating, far outside the Sun's classical habitable zone, and are leading targets in the search for life. This has prompted the broader notion of a "subsurface" or "deep" habitability, and metrics such as the Earth Similarity Index and the Planetary Habitability Index attempt to rank exoplanets by their resemblance to Earth or by a broader capacity to sustain any biology.

Exoplanets

Since the first confirmed exoplanets of the 1990s, thousands have been discovered, including dozens of roughly Earth-sized worlds in their stars' habitable zones — e.g. Proxima Centauri b, the TRAPPIST-1 planets, Kepler-186f and Kepler-452b. Missions and instruments including Kepler, TESS and the James Webb Space Telescope characterise such planets' atmospheres, searching for biosignature gases such as oxygen, methane and water vapour. The Drake equation frames the probability that habitable worlds actually host communicating civilisations.

Planetary protection and ethics

Assessing habitability also informs planetary protection — protocols that limit biological contamination of other worlds by Earth probes, and of Earth by returned samples — so that future life-detection measurements remain trustworthy.
