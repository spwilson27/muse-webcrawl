Hebrew (עִבְרִית, ʿIvrit) is a Northwest Semitic language of the Afro-Asiatic family, historically the language of the Israelites and the Hebrew Bible. It is the only Canaanite language still spoken and a rare successful example of a revived dead language.

History

Biblical Hebrew flourished from about the 10th century BCE. After the Babylonian exile Aramaic increasingly replaced it in speech, but Hebrew remained for liturgy, law (Mishnaic Hebrew), and literature. By the 19th century it was revived by figures such as Eliezer Ben-Yehuda as Modern Hebrew, now the official language of Israel.

Alphabet and phonology

Hebrew is written right-to-left in a 22-letter abjad, with vowel points (niqqud) developed by Masoretes. Modern pronunciation has five vowels and a consonant inventory reduced from Biblical Hebrew.

Grammar

Like other Semitic languages, Hebrew builds words from triliteral roots fitted into patterns (binyanim for verbs). Word order is typically subject–verb–object in Modern Hebrew.
