Neutrino oscillation

Neutrino oscillation
TypeQuantum mechanical phenomenon in particle physics
First proposed1957 (Bruno Pontecorvo)
Confirmed1998 (Super-Kamiokande, atmospheric neutrinos); 2001–2002 (SNO, solar neutrinos)
ImplicationNeutrinos have non-zero mass; physics beyond the Standard Model

Neutrino oscillation is a quantum mechanical phenomenon in which a neutrino created with a specific lepton flavour (electron, muon or tau) can later be measured to have a different flavour. The probability of measuring a particular flavour varies periodically as the neutrino propagates through space. Oscillation requires that the neutrino flavour states are mixtures of states with different masses — described mathematically by the PMNS (Pontecorvo–Maki–Nakagawa–Sakata) mixing matrix — and therefore that neutrinos have non-zero mass, contrary to the original assumption of the Standard Model of particle physics.

History

The idea of neutrino oscillation was first proposed by Bruno Pontecorvo in 1957, and developed in the 1960s. The phenomenon became pressing with the solar neutrino problem: beginning with Raymond Davis Jr.'s Homestake experiment in the late 1960s, detectors consistently measured only about one-third to one-half of the electron neutrinos predicted to be produced by nuclear fusion in the Sun. For decades it was unclear whether the solar models or the particle physics were wrong.

The answer came in two steps. In 1998 the Super-Kamiokande detector in Japan announced strong evidence that muon neutrinos produced in the atmosphere disappear as they travel through the Earth, consistent with oscillation into tau neutrinos. In 2001–2002 the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory (SNO) in Canada measured all three neutrino flavours from the Sun separately and showed that the total flux matched solar models while the electron-neutrino portion was depleted — direct proof that solar neutrinos change flavour in transit. Davis and Masatoshi Koshiba received the 2002 Nobel Prize in Physics for the detection of cosmic neutrinos, and in 2015 Takaaki Kajita (Super-Kamiokande) and Arthur B. McDonald (SNO) shared the Nobel Prize "for the discovery of neutrino oscillations, which shows that neutrinos have mass".

Theory

Because the mass eigenstates into which a flavour state decomposes propagate at slightly different speeds, their quantum phases drift apart, and the probability of detecting each flavour oscillates with distance divided by energy (L/E). Oscillations depend on two measured mass-squared differences (about 7.5×10−5 eV² for the "solar" splitting and about 2.5×10−3 eV² for the "atmospheric" splitting) and three mixing angles, all of which have now been measured — the last, θ13, by the Daya Bay, RENO and Double Chooz reactor experiments in 2012. Within matter, interactions with electrons modify the oscillation pattern (the Mikheyev–Smirnov–Wolfenstein or MSW effect), which is important for neutrinos emerging from the Sun.

Open questions

Neutrino oscillation establishes that at least two neutrinos have mass but not the absolute mass scale or the ordering of the masses ("normal" versus "inverted" hierarchy), questions targeted by experiments such as JUNO and DUNE. Oscillations also depend on a possible CP-violating phase, which — if large — could help explain the matter–antimatter asymmetry of the universe. Searches continue for additional "sterile" neutrinos that would oscillate without interacting weakly.
