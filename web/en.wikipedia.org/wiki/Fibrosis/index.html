Fibrosis, also known as fibrotic scarring, is a pathological wound healing process in which connective tissue replaces normal parenchymal tissue to the extent that it goes unchecked, leading to considerable tissue remodelling and the formation of permanent scar tissue. Repeated injuries, chronic inflammation and infection can cause an excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix components, principally collagen, produced by activated fibroblasts known as myofibroblasts.

Pathophysiology

Fibrosis is driven by inflammatory and profibrotic mediators, above all transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), which stimulates fibroblast proliferation and differentiation into contractile myofibroblasts expressing α-smooth muscle actin. The resulting excess deposition of collagen stiffens tissue and progressively impairs organ function.

Types and sites

  • Pulmonary fibrosis — scarring of the lungs, including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.
  • Cirrhosis — end-stage liver fibrosis from chronic hepatitis, alcohol or fatty liver disease.
  • Cardiac fibrosis — stiffening of the heart muscle, contributing to heart failure.
  • Cystic fibrosis — a genetic disease in which thickened secretions damage the lungs and pancreas.
  • Retroperitoneal fibrosis, mediastinal fibrosis and arthrofibrosis of joints.
  • Keloid and hypertrophic scars of the skin.

Treatment

Established fibrosis is difficult to reverse; treatment generally aims to remove the underlying cause and halt progression. Antifibrotic drugs such as pirfenidone and nintedanib slow the decline in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Research focuses on TGF-β signalling inhibitors, senolytics and matrix-degrading therapies.
