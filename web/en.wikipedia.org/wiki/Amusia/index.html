Amusia

Amusia
SpecialtyNeurology, neuropsychology
SymptomsInability to perceive or produce musical pitch and melody despite normal hearing
TypesCongenital amusia; acquired amusia
PrevalenceCongenital form: roughly 1.5–4% of the population

Amusia is a musical disorder that appears mainly as a defect in processing pitch but also encompasses musical memory and recognition. People with amusia — sometimes described in everyday language as "tone deaf" — have normal hearing and intelligence but cannot detect out-of-tune notes, recognise familiar melodies without lyrics, or sing in tune. The term was introduced in the late 19th century and popularised in its modern sense by neurologist Heinrich Knoblauch and later researchers.

Types

Congenital amusia is a lifelong neurodevelopmental disorder, present from birth, that runs in families and affects an estimated 1.5–4% of the population. Acquired amusia results from brain damage, typically stroke or trauma affecting the temporal lobes, especially in the right hemisphere, and may affect perception, production or both.

Neuroscience

Research led by Isabelle Peretz and colleagues, using the Montreal Battery of Evaluation of Amusia (MBEA), has shown that congenital amusia primarily involves impaired fine-grained pitch discrimination and is associated with abnormal connectivity between the right auditory cortex and the inferior frontal gyrus. Rhythm perception is often relatively preserved in the congenital form, although a subset of amusic individuals also shows rhythm and beat deficits. Speech intonation is usually understood despite the pitch deficit, suggesting partly separate processing for music and language.
