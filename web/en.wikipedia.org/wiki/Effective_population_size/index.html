Effective population size

The effective population size (Ne) is the size of an idealised population that would experience the same amount of genetic drift (or inbreeding) as the population under consideration. The concept was introduced by Sewall Wright in the 1930s and is a central quantity in population genetics and conservation biology.

Idealised population

The idealised (Wright–Fisher) population has constant size, discrete generations, random mating, equal sex ratio, and Poisson-distributed family sizes, with no selection, migration or mutation. Real populations depart from these assumptions, so the effective size is usually much smaller than the census size N; ratios Ne/N of around 0.1 are commonly reported for wild populations.

Definitions

Depending on the quantity matched, several effective sizes are distinguished:

  • Variance effective size — matches the variance in allele-frequency change between generations.
  • Inbreeding effective size — matches the rate at which heterozygosity is lost through identity by descent.
  • Eigenvalue effective size — governs the long-term approach to drift–mutation equilibrium.

For a population with unequal numbers of breeding males (Nm) and females (Nf), the effective size is Ne = 4NmNf / (Nm + Nf), so a skewed sex ratio depresses Ne. Fluctuations in size matter disproportionately: over generations Ne approximates the harmonic mean of the census sizes, so a single severe bottleneck can dominate.

Applications

Effective size determines the rate at which populations lose genetic variation and accumulate inbreeding, and it sets the threshold below which selection becomes ineffective relative to drift: alleles with selection coefficients smaller than roughly 1/(2Ne) behave almost neutrally (see nearly neutral theory). Conservation biologists use the "50/500 rule" as a rough guideline — an Ne of about 50 to avoid short-term inbreeding depression and 500 or more to retain long-term evolutionary potential, with later authors suggesting 100/1000.
