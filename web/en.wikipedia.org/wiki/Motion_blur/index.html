Motion blur

Motion blur
CauseRelative motion between subject and camera during a finite exposure time
AppearanceStreaking or smearing along the direction of motion
Related toShutter speed, exposure time, frame rate
Artistic useConveying speed and movement; long-exposure photography
RemovalDeblurring algorithms (deconvolution)

Motion blur is the apparent streaking of moving objects in a photograph, film, or video frame that occurs when the image being recorded changes during a single exposure — either because the subject moves rapidly or because the camera itself moves (camera shake). Because film and digital sensors integrate light over the exposure time, any object that shifts position while the shutter is open is recorded spread along its path of motion.

In photography

The amount of blur depends on the shutter speed, the speed of the subject relative to the camera, and the focal length of the lens. Slow shutter speeds at night or in low light produce pronounced blur of moving subjects; deliberately long exposures turn car headlights into ribbons of light and make waterfalls appear silky — a prized creative effect. Conversely, freezing motion requires fast shutter speeds, and panning — swinging the camera to follow a subject — can keep the subject sharp while blurring the background, conveying speed. Photographers distinguish subject motion blur from camera shake, which blurs the whole frame and can be reduced with stabilization or a tripod.

In film and video

At the standard cinema frame rate of 24 frames per second with a 180-degree shutter, each frame contains the motion blur corresponding to an exposure of about 1/48 second. This blur is essential to the perception of smooth motion: footage shot with a very narrow shutter angle looks staccato and jittery, an effect exploited in battle scenes in films such as Saving Private Ryan. Animation historically lacked natural motion blur, so animators drew artificial equivalents — smear frames, speed lines, and multiples — a practice carried into modern animation and anime.

In computer graphics and games

In 3D computer graphics, motion blur must be simulated: rendering engines integrate samples over time or apply post-processing blurs along velocity vectors. Temporal accumulation in film renderers gives realistic results; in real-time video games, cheaper per-object or camera-motion blur effects are common, though many players disable them for clarity. Motion blur is also used in user-interface animation and in scientific visualization. Unwanted blur can be attacked with deblurring algorithms, which attempt to invert the smearing filter (deconvolution), with limits set by noise and information loss.

Biological analogue

Human vision has its own integration time, so rapidly moving objects blur in perception as well, though the visual system partially compensates; the apparent blur of a fast fan or propeller is a familiar example. Some animals, such as birds of prey, have faster temporal resolution and perceive less blur.
