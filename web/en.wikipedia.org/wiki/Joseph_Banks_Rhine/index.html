Joseph Banks Rhine (September 29, 1895 – February 20, 1980), usually known as J. B. Rhine, was an American botanist who founded the science of parapsychology as a branch of psychology, the parapsychology laboratory at Duke University, the Journal of Parapsychology and the Foundation for Research on the Nature of Man.

Career

Rhine trained as a botanist at the University of Chicago, earning his PhD in 1925. After attending a lecture by Arthur Conan Doyle on spiritualism and reading William McDougall's work, he turned toward psychical research and joined McDougall at Duke University in 1928.

At Duke's parapsychology laboratory, Rhine and his wife Louisa E. Rhine conducted card-guessing experiments using the specially designed Zener cards — decks of five symbols (circle, cross, waves, square, star) — to test for extrasensory perception (ESP), a term Rhine popularised. His books Extra-Sensory Perception (1934) and New Frontiers of the Mind (1937) brought the experiments wide public attention and made "ESP" a household term. He later studied psychokinesis with dice-rolling experiments.

Criticism and legacy

Rhine's results were controversial: critics cited methodological flaws, selective reporting and failures to replicate, and parapsychology remained outside mainstream science. Nevertheless, his insistence on laboratory methods and statistical evaluation reshaped psychical research, and he coined much of its vocabulary. In 1965 he left Duke to found the independent Foundation for Research on the Nature of Man (later the Rhine Research Center) in Durham, North Carolina.
