A céilí (Irish: céilidhe; Scottish Gaelic: cèilidh) is a traditional Irish or Scottish social gathering, which in its most basic form simply means a social visit. In contemporary usage it usually involves playing Gaelic folk music and dancing, either at a house party or a larger concert at a social hall or other community gathering place.

History

The term derives from the Old Irish céle, meaning "companion". Historically, a céilí was an informal social visit to a neighbour's house, where stories were told, songs sung, and news exchanged during the long winter evenings. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, céilithe became more formalised events centred on group dancing, and the Gaelic League helped codify céilí dancing as part of the Irish cultural revival.

Dancing

Céilí dances are group dances performed in formations such as lines, circles, squares and progressive sets. Well-known dances include the Walls of Limerick, the Siege of Ennis, the Haymakers' Jig and the Fairy Reel. Unlike competitive Irish stepdancing, céilí dancing is social in character, with the emphasis on participation and enjoyment rather than technique. A caller may announce the figures for less experienced dancers.

Music

Music at a céilí is typically provided by a céilí band, which may include fiddle, button accordion, flute, concertina, banjo, piano and drums. The repertoire consists mainly of jigs, reels, hornpipes, polkas and waltzes played at a steady tempo suitable for dancing.

Modern practice

Céilithe remain popular in Ireland, Scotland and throughout the Irish and Scottish diaspora, at weddings, festivals, fundraisers and St Patrick's Day celebrations. Organisations such as Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann promote céilí music and dance worldwide.
