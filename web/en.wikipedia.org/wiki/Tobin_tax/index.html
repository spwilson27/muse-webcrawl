Tobin tax
Proposed byJames Tobin (1972)
TypeTax on spot currency conversions
Proposed rate0.1%–0.25% of transaction value

A Tobin tax is a tax on spot conversions of one currency into another, proposed by American economist James Tobin in 1972 (in his Janeway Lectures, published in 1978) in the wake of the collapse of the Bretton Woods system of fixed exchange rates. Tobin's purpose was to "throw some sand in the wheels" of international currency markets — to discourage short-term speculative trading and "hot money" flows while barely affecting long-term trade and investment, and thereby give governments greater autonomy in monetary policy.

The proposal

Tobin proposed a small, uniform tax — initially around 0.1 to 0.25 per cent — on all spot currency transactions, to be adopted internationally. Because the tax would fall proportionately more heavily on short-term round-trip trades than on long-term positions, it would penalize speculation more than commerce. Tobin also suggested that revenues could fund international development purposes, an idea later taken up by others, including variants of the tax on broader financial transactions (financial transaction taxes, or FTTs).

Debate and implementations

Supporters argue the tax would reduce exchange-rate volatility, curb purely speculative volume, and raise substantial revenue for public or global goods. Critics contend it would be difficult to implement universally, easy to evade by relocating trading, and might harm market liquidity. Sweden's financial transaction tax of the 1980s is often cited as a cautionary example, having driven trading abroad and been repealed. In 2011 the European Commission proposed an EU-wide FTT; eleven member states pursued it under enhanced cooperation, though it remained unimplemented for years. Several countries levy narrow transaction taxes of their own (such as stamp duty on share purchases in the UK).
