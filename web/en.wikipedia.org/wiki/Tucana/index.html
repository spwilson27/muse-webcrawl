Tucana (The Toucan) is a constellation of the southern sky, named after the toucan, a South American bird. It is one of twelve constellations created by Petrus Plancius from the observations of Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman and first appeared in 1598; it was included in Johann Bayer's star atlas Uranometria of 1603. Tucana is best known for containing most of the Small Magellanic Cloud and the globular cluster 47 Tucanae.

Stars

Tucana's stars are relatively faint. Its brightest is Alpha Tucanae, an orange giant of magnitude 2.87 about 200 light-years away, actually a spectroscopic binary. Beta Tucanae is a complex multiple system of six stars. The star HD 219482 and others in the constellation have been found to host exoplanets, and the sun-like star HD 221287 has a super-Jupiter companion. The dwarf galaxy host of fast radio burst research and the deep-field observations of the Hubble and James Webb telescopes include fields in Tucana, near the south ecliptic pole region.

Deep-sky objects

  • 47 Tucanae (NGC 104) — the second-brightest globular cluster in the sky after Omega Centauri, visible to the naked eye as a fuzzy star of magnitude 4. It lies about 13,000–15,000 light-years away and contains millions of stars, including many millisecond pulsars; it may harbour an intermediate-mass black hole at its centre.
  • Small Magellanic Cloud (NGC 292) — an irregular dwarf galaxy about 200,000 light-years away, a satellite of the Milky Way, appearing as a detached patch of the Milky Way to southern observers. It is a site of active star formation and includes the young cluster NGC 346.
  • NGC 121 — an old globular cluster in the Small Magellanic Cloud.
  • Tucana Dwarf Galaxy — a remote, isolated dwarf spheroidal galaxy in the Local Group, notable for containing only old stars.

History

The southern navigators Keyser and de Houtman charted the stars during the first Dutch expedition to the East Indies (1595–97). Because the toucan was an exotic New World bird then becoming known in Europe, the constellation joined a menagerie of southern constellations named for animals, including Pavo, Apus and Grus, with which Tucana shares borders.
