Urology

Urology (from Greek οὖρον, "urine", and -λογία, "study of") is the branch of medicine and surgery concerned with the urinary tract of men and women and with the male reproductive organs. Physicians specialising in the field are urologists. Because urology deals with both medical and surgical problems, it is classified as a surgical specialty, though urologists also manage many conditions non-operatively.

Scope

Organs within the urologist's remit include the kidneys, adrenal glands, ureters, urinary bladder, urethra, prostate, seminal vesicles, penis, scrotum, and testes. Common conditions treated include:

  • Urinary tract infections and urinary incontinence
  • Kidney stones and urinary tract obstruction
  • Benign prostatic hyperplasia
  • Cancers of the prostate, bladder, kidney, testis, and penis
  • Erectile dysfunction and male infertility
  • Congenital abnormalities of the urinary tract in children

Subspecialties and techniques

Subspecialty areas include urologic oncology, endourology and stone disease, female pelvic medicine, paediatric urology, reconstructive urology, neurourology, and andrology. Urologists pioneered many minimally invasive approaches, including transurethral resection of the prostate, ureteroscopy, shock-wave lithotripsy, laparoscopic and robot-assisted prostatectomy and nephrectomy, and cystoscopic diagnosis and treatment of bladder disease.

History

The treatment of urinary stones is among the oldest surgical practices — perineal lithotomy was described in ancient Indian and Greco-Roman texts. Urology emerged as a distinct specialty in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries with the development of the cystoscope and organised professional societies, such as the American Urological Association (founded 1902).
