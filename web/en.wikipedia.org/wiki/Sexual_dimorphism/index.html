Sexual dimorphism

Sexual dimorphism is the condition in which the sexes of the same species exhibit different morphological characteristics, including characteristics not directly involved in reproduction. The differences may include size, weight, colour, markings, and secondary sex characteristics such as horns, antlers, plumage, manes or specialised structures used in courtship and competition. Sexual dimorphism occurs in a great many animals and some plants, and is usually the product of sexual selection acting together with natural selection.

Causes

Charles Darwin proposed in The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex (1871) that exaggerated male ornaments and weapons evolve through sexual selection: competition between males for mates, and female choice of mates. Where one sex invests more in offspring — typically females, through gestation and lactation — the other sex tends to compete more intensely, favouring larger size, weapons or showy displays. Ecological factors can also contribute: the sexes may diverge to exploit different food resources, reducing competition between them. Conversely, in species where males provide most parental care, "reverse" dimorphism may evolve, with females larger or more brightly coloured, as in some shorebirds and raptors.

Examples

  • Peafowl: males bear an enormous iridescent train; females are mottled brown.
  • Lions: males carry a mane and are substantially larger than females.
  • Deer: males of most species grow antlers, shed and regrown annually.
  • Elephant seals: males may weigh three to four times as much as females and develop an inflatable proboscis.
  • Anglerfish: in some deep-sea species the dwarf male attaches permanently to the much larger female.
  • Spiders and insects: females are often far larger than males; in some mantises and spiders, males risk being eaten during mating.
  • Birds of paradise and mandrills: extreme colour dimorphism driven by female choice.

Size dimorphism

Body-size dimorphism is commonly measured by the ratio of male to female mass. It is pronounced in polygynous mammals such as gorillas and elephant seals, modest in monogamous species, and reversed in many birds of prey, where females are the larger sex. Rensch's rule describes the pattern that, across related species, size dimorphism tends to increase with overall body size when males are larger, and decrease when females are larger.

In humans

Humans are moderately sexually dimorphic. On average, men are taller and heavier, with greater upper-body muscle mass, higher bone density and more body hair, while women have a higher proportion of body fat, wider pelvises, and developed mammary glands. Many differences emerge or intensify at puberty under the influence of sex hormones — testosterone in males and oestrogens in females. There is substantial overlap in most traits between the sexes, and the degree of dimorphism varies among populations.

In plants

Dimorphism is rarer in plants but occurs in some dioecious species (with separate male and female individuals), where the sexes may differ in flower size, growth rate or defensive chemistry; examples include some willows, hollies and the maidenhair tree (Ginkgo biloba).
