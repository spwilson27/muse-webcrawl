CAP theorem

[Hero image placeholder: CAP theorem triangle diagram]

In theoretical computer science, the CAP theorem, also named Brewer's theorem after computer scientist Eric Brewer, states that any distributed data store can provide only two of the following three guarantees:

  • Consistency — every read receives the most recent write or an error.
  • Availability — every request receives a (non-error) response, without the guarantee that it contains the most recent write.
  • Partition tolerance — the system continues to operate despite an arbitrary number of messages being dropped (or delayed) by the network between nodes.

Because network failures are unavoidable in a distributed system, partition tolerance cannot be given up in practice. As a result, designers of distributed databases must choose between consistency and availability whenever a network partition occurs.

History

Eric Brewer first presented the idea as a conjecture at the ACM Symposium on Principles of Distributed Computing (PODC) in 2000. In 2002, Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch of MIT published a formal proof of the conjecture, establishing it as a theorem. Brewer later revisited the theorem in a 2012 article, "CAP Twelve Years Later: How the 'Rules' Have Changed," arguing that the choice is not a permanent binary one, and that systems can and should manage the trade-off dynamically as partitions heal.

Explanation

When a network partition separates nodes into groups that cannot communicate, the system faces two options. It may cancel operations to preserve consistency, reducing availability, or it may proceed with operations on both sides, risking inconsistent states. If no partition exists, both consistency and availability can be guaranteed simultaneously.

Alternatives and extensions

The PACELC theorem, proposed by Daniel J. Abadi, extends CAP by noting that even in the absence of partitions (else), a system must trade off latency against consistency. Many real-world systems, such as Google's Spanner, aim to minimise the practical impact of the trade-off using synchronised clocks and careful engineering.
