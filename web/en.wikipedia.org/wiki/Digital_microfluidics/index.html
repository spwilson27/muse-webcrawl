Digital microfluidics

Digital microfluidics (DMF) is a technology for manipulating discrete droplets of fluid on an array of electrodes, most commonly by the phenomenon of electrowetting. In contrast to continuous-flow microfluidics, in which liquid is pumped through fixed channels, digital microfluidics treats each droplet as an individually addressable unit that can be dispensed, transported, merged, split and mixed under electronic control. The approach is a form of lab-on-a-chip and is sometimes described as a fluidic analogue of a digital electronic circuit.

Principle of operation

Most digital microfluidic devices are based on electrowetting-on-dielectric. A droplet sits on a hydrophobic surface covering an electrode that is insulated by a thin dielectric layer. When a voltage is applied to the electrode, the contact angle of the droplet on that electrode decreases and the droplet becomes more wetting on that side; the resulting imbalance of surface forces pulls the droplet onto the energised electrode. By switching voltages across a two-dimensional array of electrodes in sequence, a droplet can be stepped from electrode to electrode along an arbitrary path.

Two configurations are common. In the open configuration the droplet rests on a single patterned substrate. In the closed (two-plate) configuration the droplet is sandwiched between a bottom plate carrying the electrode array and a top plate, usually indium tin oxide on glass, which serves as a ground electrode. The closed format supports all of the basic operations, including dispensing from reservoirs and splitting a droplet into two by pulling it in opposite directions.

Operations

  • Dispensing — a droplet is drawn out from a reservoir and pinched off.
  • Transport — sequential electrode actuation moves a droplet across the array.
  • Merging and mixing — two droplets are brought together and coalesce; mixing is accelerated by shuttling the merged droplet.
  • Splitting — a droplet is elongated over several electrodes and then pulled apart into two daughter droplets.

Applications

Digital microfluidics has been applied to clinical diagnostics, including immunoassays and DNA analysis, to cell-based assays, to proteomics sample preparation, and to chemical synthesis. Commercial systems have been developed for newborn screening and for next-generation sequencing library preparation. The technology has also been explored for cooling hot spots on microelectronic chips, using droplets to carry heat away from localised areas.

Advantages and limitations

Because fluid movement is controlled electronically rather than by pumps, valves or fixed channels, devices are reconfigurable: the same chip can run different protocols simply by changing the actuation sequence. Volumes are small and precisely metered by electrode geometry. Limitations include biofouling of the hydrophobic surface by proteins, the need for relatively high actuation voltages, evaporation in open systems, and difficulty handling some organic solvents and whole blood.
