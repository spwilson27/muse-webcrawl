The Guiana Shield is one of the three cratons of the South American Plate, a 1.7-billion-year-old Precambrian geological formation in northeast South America that underlies Guyana, Suriname, French Guiana, and parts of Venezuela, Colombia and northern Brazil. Together with the Amazon rainforest it covers, it forms one of the largest tracts of undisturbed tropical forest in the world and one of the regions of highest biodiversity.

Geology and geography

The shield is composed of ancient metamorphic and igneous rocks, among the oldest on the Earth's surface. Its most striking landforms are the tepuis — sheer-sided, flat-topped sandstone mountains such as Mount Roraima and Auyán-tepui, from which Angel Falls, the world's tallest waterfall, plunges 979 metres. The shield's highest point is Pico da Neblina on the Brazil–Venezuela border at 2,995 metres. Major rivers draining the shield include the Orinoco headwaters, the Essequibo, and tributaries of the Amazon such as the Rio Negro and Trombetas.

Ecology

The isolated summits of the tepuis host highly endemic flora and fauna — many species, particularly of carnivorous plants, frogs and orchids, occur on single mountains. The surrounding lowland forests are home to jaguars, giant otters, harpy eagles and a huge diversity of plants. Because large areas remain roadless and sparsely populated, the Guiana Shield forests are a major global carbon store.

Resources and conservation

The region holds deposits of gold, diamonds, bauxite and iron ore, and mining — much of it informal and illegal — is a leading cause of localised deforestation and mercury pollution of rivers. Protected areas include Canaima National Park in Venezuela (a UNESCO World Heritage Site), Iwokrama Forest in Guyana, and the Central Suriname Nature Reserve.
