SN1 reaction

The SN1 reaction (substitution nucleophilic unimolecular) is a substitution reaction in organic chemistry in which the rate-determining step involves a single reactant species. The mechanism was proposed in the 1930s by Christopher Ingold and Edward Hughes as part of their classification of nucleophilic substitution reactions.

Mechanism

The reaction proceeds in two (sometimes three) steps:

  • Ionisation: the bond between carbon and the leaving group breaks heterolytically, generating a planar carbocation intermediate. This slow step determines the overall rate, so the rate law is first order: rate = k[substrate].
  • Nucleophilic attack: the nucleophile attacks the flat carbocation from either face, giving a mixture of retention and inversion of configuration at a stereogenic centre — typically racemisation.
  • Deprotonation (when the nucleophile is neutral, e.g. water or alcohol) restores neutrality.

Factors favouring SN1

SN1 reactivity increases with carbocation stability: tertiary substrates react fastest, then secondary; primary and methyl substrates rarely react by SN1. Polar protic solvents, which stabilise ions, and good leaving groups such as halides and tosylates accelerate the reaction. Because a carbocation intermediate is formed, rearrangements (hydride or alkyl shifts) may occur, distinguishing SN1 from the concerted SN2 reaction.
