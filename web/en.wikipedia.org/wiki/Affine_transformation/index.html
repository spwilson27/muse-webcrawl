Affine transformation

In Euclidean geometry, an affine transformation or affinity is a geometric transformation that preserves lines and parallelism, but not necessarily Euclidean distances and angles. More generally, an affine transformation is an automorphism of an affine space: it maps a point to a point, a line to a line, and a plane to a plane.

An affine transformation can be written as the composition of a linear map and a translation: in coordinates, x ↦ Ax + b, where A is an invertible matrix and b is a vector. Equivalently, it can be represented by a single matrix multiplication using homogeneous coordinates.

Properties

Affine transformations preserve collinearity (points on a line remain on the corresponding line), parallelism, midpoints, centroids, and ratios of areas and volumes. They do not in general preserve lengths, angles, or circles, although similarities and Euclidean motions are special cases that do. Examples of affine transformations include scaling, shearing, rotation, reflection, and translation. A transformation that reverses orientation, such as a reflection, has a matrix with negative determinant.

Applications

Affine transformations are fundamental in computer graphics, where they position, scale, and orient objects and textures; in image processing, where affine warps correct geometric distortion; and in robotics and computer vision for modelling camera geometry and coordinate changes.
