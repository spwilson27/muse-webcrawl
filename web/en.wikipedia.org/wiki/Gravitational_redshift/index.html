Gravitational redshift

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of light climbing out of a gravitational well, its wavelength stretching to the red]
Light loses energy climbing out of a gravitational field and redshifts.

Gravitational redshift is the phenomenon that light emitted from a source within a gravitational field is shifted toward longer wavelengths (the red end of the spectrum) when observed from a region of weaker gravity — equivalently, clocks deeper in a gravitational well run slower (Time dilation), so atoms there emit light whose frequency is lower as measured far away. It is one of the three classical tests of General relativity proposed by Albert Einstein, alongside Mercury's perihelion precession and the deflection of light by the Sun.

The effect was measured in the laboratory by Robert Pound and Glen Rebka in 1959–60 using the Mössbauer effect over a 22.5-metre tower at Harvard, and to high precision by Gravity Probe A (1976, a hydrogen Maser on a rocket). In astronomy it is detected in the spectra of white dwarfs (Sirius B) and, strikingly, in the orbit of the star S2 around the Milky Way's black hole (2018, by the GRAVITY collaboration and Andrea Ghez's group) — general relativity's redshift confirmed in an extreme field. The cosmological redshift of distant galaxies is a related but distinct phenomenon of expanding Spacetime.

Equivalence-principle view

Einstein derived the effect in 1907–11, before the full theory, from the equivalence principle alone: an accelerating observer sees light Doppler-shifted, so a gravitational field must do likewise. This universality — all clocks and all frequencies shift by the same factor — is a uniquely sharp prediction, tested to parts in 10⁵ by comparisons of different atomic clocks.
