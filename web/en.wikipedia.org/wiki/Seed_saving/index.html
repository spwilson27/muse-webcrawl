Seed saving

In agriculture and gardening, seed saving (sometimes known as brown bagging) is the practice of saving seeds or other reproductive material (e.g. tubers) from flowers, vegetables, grain, herbs, and other plants for use from year to year. It was the traditional way farms and gardens were maintained for the last 12,000 years, and remains central to preserving heirloom varieties and locally adapted landraces.

Practice

Seed savers select plants showing desirable traits — flavour, yield, disease resistance, earliness, or adaptation to local conditions — and allow them to mature fully. Seeds are harvested, cleaned, dried, and stored in cool, dry, dark conditions to maintain viability. Successful saving requires attention to pollination biology: self-pollinating crops such as beans, peas, lettuce, and tomatoes are straightforward, while cross-pollinating crops such as squash and maize require isolation distances or hand-pollination to keep varieties true to type. Hybrid (F1) varieties do not breed true, so seed saving focuses on open-pollinated varieties.

Movement and seed banks

The twentieth-century consolidation of the commercial seed industry, and the spread of uniform hybrid varieties, prompted organised seed-saving movements. Seed Savers Exchange, founded in the United States in 1975, and Garden Organic's Heritage Seed Library in the United Kingdom, maintain collections and facilitate exchanges among gardeners. Community seed banks and seed libraries have proliferated since the 2000s. Large institutional collections, such as the Svalbard Global Seed Vault, serve as back-ups for crop diversity.

Legal issues

Plant variety protection, patents on seed, and contractual restrictions can limit farmers' ability to save seed of protected varieties, a controversy that has generated litigation and policy debate in many countries. Traditional farm-saved seed remains exempt or privileged in many jurisdictions under plant breeders' rights frameworks.
