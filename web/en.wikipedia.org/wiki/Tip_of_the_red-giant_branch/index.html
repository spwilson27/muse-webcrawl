Tip of the red-giant branch

The tip of the red-giant branch (TRGB) is a standard candle used by astronomers to measure distances to galaxies. It marks the maximum luminosity a low-mass star reaches while ascending the red-giant branch of the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram, just before its degenerate helium core ignites in the helium flash and the star abruptly drops to the horizontal branch.

Physical basis

Stars of roughly 0.8 to 2 solar masses climb the red-giant branch as a hydrogen-burning shell feeds an inert, contracting helium core. The core's mass at ignition is almost independent of the star's total mass, age, and composition, so all such stars flash at nearly the same luminosity — about 2,500–3,000 times that of the Sun, corresponding to an absolute magnitude in the near-infrared I band of roughly MI ≈ −4.05. In a galaxy's colour–magnitude diagram the red-giant branch therefore ends in a sharp cutoff; detecting that edge gives the distance modulus directly.

Use as a distance indicator

The method was put on a firm footing by Lee, Freedman, and Madore in 1993, using I-band photometry in which the tip's brightness is nearly insensitive to metallicity and age for old stellar populations. With the Hubble Space Telescope, the TRGB has been measured in galaxies out to roughly 10–20 megaparsecs, and it underpins one of the principal local determinations of the Hubble constant, notably by the Carnegie–Chicago Hubble Program, which found values near 70 km/s/Mpc — between the higher supernova-based and lower cosmic-microwave-background-based estimates. Because it relies on entirely different physics from Cepheid variables, the TRGB is a key cross-check in the "distance ladder" debate. The James Webb Space Telescope is extending tip measurements to still larger distances.
