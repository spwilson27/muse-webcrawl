James Larkin (21 January 1876 – 30 January 1947), often called Big Jim Larkin, was an Irish trade union leader and socialist activist, founder of the Irish Transport and General Workers' Union (ITGWU) and leader of the workers in the Dublin Lockout of 1913, the most severe industrial dispute in Irish history. A charismatic orator of towering presence, Larkin pioneered the tactic of the sympathetic strike and transformed Irish labour organisation by unionising unskilled workers whom craft unions had ignored.

Early life and union organising

Larkin was born in Liverpool to Irish immigrant parents from County Armagh and worked as a docker from his teens. Radicalised by the dock strikes of the 1890s, he became an organiser for the National Union of Dock Labourers and led successful strikes in Liverpool and, in 1907, in Belfast — where he briefly united Protestant and Catholic dockers — and Dublin. Expelled from the union after disputes over his militant methods, he founded the ITGWU in 1909 and its newspaper, The Irish Worker. With James Connolly he founded the Irish Labour Party in 1912, and together they built the Irish Citizen Army during the Lockout.

The Dublin Lockout, 1913

In 1913 the employers' federation led by William Martin Murphy resolved to break the ITGWU, locking out more than 20,000 workers who refused to renounce the union. The dispute lasted from August 1913 into early 1914, marked by police baton charges — on "Bloody Sunday", 31 August 1913, two workers were killed — and by food ships sent by British trade unionists. Though the workers were eventually starved back to work without a formal victory, the Lockout established union organisation in Ireland and entered national mythology; W. B. Yeats's poem "September 1913" is associated with the period.

America, prison and return

Larkin travelled to the United States in 1914 to raise funds. There he joined the Socialist Party of America and then the early American communist movement; in 1920 he was convicted of "criminal anarchism" in New York and sentenced to five to ten years in Sing Sing. Pardoned and deported in 1923, he returned to Ireland to a labour movement now led by others, quarrelled with the ITGWU leadership under William O'Brien, and was expelled in 1924. He founded the rival Workers' Union of Ireland and the Irish Worker League, visited the Soviet Union, and sat in the Dáil for periods in the 1920s–40s. He died in Dublin in 1947; his funeral drew enormous crowds. A reconciliation between the rival unions he had split began shortly before his death and eventually led to the formation of SIPTU in 1990.
