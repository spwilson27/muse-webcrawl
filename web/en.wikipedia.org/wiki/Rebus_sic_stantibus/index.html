Rebus sic stantibus (Latin: "things thus standing") is a doctrine in the law of treaties and in contract law which allows a party to withdraw from, or terminate, a treaty when there has been a fundamental change of circumstances that was not foreseen by the parties when the treaty was concluded. It is one of the few exceptions to the foundational principle of pacta sunt servanda ("agreements must be kept").

Codification

The doctrine is codified in Article 62 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties of 1969. Under that article, a fundamental change of circumstances may not be invoked to terminate a treaty unless the existence of those circumstances constituted an essential basis of the consent of the parties to be bound, and the effect of the change is radically to transform the extent of obligations still to be performed. The doctrine cannot be invoked for treaties establishing a boundary, nor where the change resulted from a breach by the invoking party of an obligation under the treaty.

Historical development

The idea that obligations presuppose the persistence of the circumstances under which they were assumed can be traced to medieval and early modern writers on the law of nations, including Hugo Grotius, who treated promises as implicitly conditioned on the state of affairs remaining substantially the same. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries states invoked changed circumstances with some frequency, sometimes abusively, which made later codifiers cautious: Article 62 is deliberately drafted in negative and restrictive terms.

Case law

The International Court of Justice has considered the plea in several cases, including the Fisheries Jurisdiction cases (1973) and the Gabčíkovo–Nagymaros Project case (1997). In each instance the Court held that the stringent conditions of Article 62 were not satisfied: political changes, new environmental knowledge, or shifts in economic conditions did not radically transform the obligations still to be performed. The plea has therefore rarely, if ever, succeeded before the Court, though the Court accepts that Article 62 reflects customary international law.

In contract law

Analogous doctrines exist in domestic contract law, such as the doctrine of frustration in common law systems and the German concept of Störung der Geschäftsgrundlage (frustration of the foundation of the transaction), now codified in § 313 of the German Civil Code.
