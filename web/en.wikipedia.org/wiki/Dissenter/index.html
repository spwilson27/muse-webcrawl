Dissenter

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A dissenter is a person who dissents from an established system, organisation, or belief. In English history, the term Dissenters (capitalised) specifically refers to Protestants who refused to conform to the Church of England after the Restoration of 1660, and who formed separate congregations outside the established church. They are also known as Nonconformists.

English Dissenters

English Dissent emerged from the religious settlement imposed after 1660. The Act of Uniformity of 1662 required clergy to use the Book of Common Prayer and accept episcopal ordination; about two thousand ministers who refused were ejected from their livings on St Bartholomew's Day 1662, an event remembered as the Great Ejection. The Clarendon Code – including the Conventicle Act and the Five Mile Act – penalised separatist worship.

The principal Dissenter groups included Presbyterians, Congregationalists (Independents), Baptists, and Quakers, later joined by Methodists, who began inside the Church of England but separated in the 1790s. Dissenters were excluded from Oxford and Cambridge universities by religious tests, from municipal office by the Test and Corporation Acts, and from some professions; many therefore became prominent in commerce and industry. In response they founded dissenting academies, which offered a modern education including science and modern languages.

Civil rights

The Toleration Act 1689 granted freedom of worship to most Protestant Dissenters (though not to Roman Catholics or Unitarians at first). The Test and Corporation Acts were repealed in 1828, Catholic emancipation followed in 1829, and university tests were abolished in 1871. Dissenters were central to campaigns for parliamentary reform, the abolition of slavery, and repeal of the Corn Laws, and the "Nonconformist conscience" was a significant force in Liberal politics in the later nineteenth century.

General usage

More generally, "dissenter" is used for anyone who publicly disagrees with an official policy or orthodoxy – for example, political dissidents under authoritarian regimes, or a judge who writes a dissenting opinion.
