Knowledge argument

Mary the colour scientist in her black-and-white room

The knowledge argument (also known as Mary's room or Mary the super-scientist) is a thought experiment in the philosophy of mind proposed by Frank Jackson in his 1982 article "Epiphenomenal Qualia". It is intended to challenge physicalism — the view that all facts, including facts about consciousness, are physical facts.

The thought experiment

Mary is a brilliant scientist who knows everything there is to know about the physics, chemistry and neurobiology of colour vision, but who has lived her entire life in a black-and-white room, learning from black-and-white books and television. When she is released and sees a red tomato for the first time, she learns something new: what it is like to see red. Jackson argued that since Mary already had all the physical information, the new knowledge must be knowledge of a non-physical fact — the phenomenal quality, or quale, of the experience — so physicalism is false.

Responses

Physicalists have replied in several ways. The ability hypothesis (associated with Laurence Nemirow and David Lewis) holds that Mary gains no new factual knowledge but only new abilities — to recognise, imagine and remember red. The acquaintance hypothesis says she gains knowledge by acquaintance rather than new propositional facts. Others argue she learns an old fact under a new, phenomenal concept. Daniel Dennett has contended that, properly understood, Mary with truly complete physical knowledge would not be surprised. Jackson himself later changed his mind and endorsed physicalism. Together with the "hard problem of consciousness", Mary's room remains one of the most discussed arguments about qualia and dualism.
