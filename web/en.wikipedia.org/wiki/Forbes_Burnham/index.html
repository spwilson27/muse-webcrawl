Linden Forbes Sampson Burnham (20 February 1923 – 6 August 1985) was a Guyanese politician who led Guyana from 1964 until his death in 1985, first as premier and prime minister and then, after the 1980 constitution, as executive president. He was the founder of the People's National Congress (PNC) and a dominant, controversial figure in Guyana's post-independence history.

Early life and career

Burnham was born in Georgetown, British Guiana, won the colony's scholarship to study law in London, and was called to the bar. In 1950 he co-founded the People's Progressive Party (PPP) with Cheddi Jagan; after a split in 1955 he formed the PNC, which drew its support mainly from urban Afro-Guyanese voters. With British and American backing, his coalition won the 1964 election, and he led the colony to independence as Guyana on 26 May 1966.

Rule

Burnham declared Guyana a "Co-operative Republic" in 1970 and pursued "co-operative socialism", nationalising the bauxite and sugar industries and much of the economy. His governments were widely accused of electoral fraud, notably in 1968, 1973 and 1980, and of authoritarianism and economic mismanagement; shortages and emigration grew during the late 1970s. The period saw the 1978 Jonestown tragedy in Guyana's northwest and the assassination in 1980 of the historian and opposition activist Walter Rodney, widely attributed to state agents.

Abroad, Burnham positioned Guyana in the Non-Aligned Movement, maintained relations with Cuba and other socialist states, and defended Guyana's claim in its territorial dispute with Venezuela over the Essequibo.

Death and legacy

Burnham died on 6 August 1985 in Georgetown during throat surgery. He was succeeded by Desmond Hoyte. His legacy remains sharply contested: credited by supporters with asserting sovereignty and building national institutions, he is criticised by opponents for rigged elections, economic decline and the entrenchment of ethnic-political division between Afro-Guyanese and Indo-Guyanese communities.
