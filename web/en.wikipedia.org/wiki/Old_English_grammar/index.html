Old English grammar

Old English was a highly inflected language, as shown in its surviving manuscripts.

The grammar of Old English (Ænglisc) differs greatly from that of Modern English. Old English was a synthetic, fusional language with a system of grammatical gender, five cases for nouns and adjectives, dual number for pronouns, and strong and weak verb conjugations — features largely inherited from Proto-Germanic and shared with its closest relatives, Old Frisian and Old Saxon.

Nouns

Old English nouns belonged to one of three genders — masculine, feminine, or neuter — and were declined in four or five cases: nominative, accusative, genitive, dative, and (in remnants) instrumental. Nouns were divided into strong and weak declensions, with the strong a-stems the most numerous. For example, the masculine a-stem stān ("stone"):

The Modern English plural in -s and possessive in 's descend from the masculine a-stem nominative/accusative plural -as and genitive singular -es.

Pronouns

Personal pronouns preserved a dual number alongside singular and plural: wit "we two" and git "you two". The third-person pronouns hē (he), hēo (she), and hit (it) underlie their modern counterparts.

Verbs

Verbs were divided into strong verbs, which formed the past tense by ablaut (vowel change) across seven classes — as in singan "sing", sang, sungen — and weak verbs, which used a dental suffix (the ancestor of the modern -ed). Verbs were inflected for person, number, tense (present and past only), and mood (indicative, subjunctive, imperative). A small group of preterite-present verbs survives as the modern modal auxiliaries.

Word order

Word order was freer than in Modern English owing to inflection. The unmarked order in main clauses was often subject–verb–object or verb-second, while subordinate clauses frequently placed the verb at the end, reflecting the language's West Germanic heritage.
