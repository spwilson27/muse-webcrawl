Stephen LaBerge (born 1947) is an American psychophysiologist specialising in the scientific study of lucid dreaming — dreams in which the sleeper is aware that they are dreaming. At Stanford University in the late 1970s he provided the first laboratory proof that lucid dreams occur during REM sleep, by having trained dreamers signal their lucidity with pre-arranged eye movements recorded on a polygraph. He founded The Lucidity Institute in 1987 to continue research and teach lucid dreaming techniques.

Research

LaBerge earned a bachelor's degree in mathematics from the University of Arizona and a doctorate in psychophysiology from Stanford in 1980; his dissertation reported the eye-signal experiments, developed with Lynn Nagel and William Dement's sleep laboratory. The technique exploited the fact that the eye muscles escape the paralysis (atonia) of REM sleep: subjects who became lucid in a dream moved their eyes in an agreed pattern, timestamping the lucid episode against the physiological record. His subsequent studies measured time perception in dreams, the physiological correlates of dream actions, and methods of inducing lucidity.

Techniques and popularisation

LaBerge devised the MILD technique (Mnemonic Induction of Lucid Dreams), based on prospective memory, and helped develop induction devices such as the NovaDreamer mask, which detects REM sleep and flashes light cues into the dream. His books Lucid Dreaming (1985) and Exploring the World of Lucid Dreaming (1990, with Howard Rheingold) brought the subject to a wide audience and remain standard introductions.
