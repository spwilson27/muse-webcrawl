Engine-indicating and crew-alerting system

An engine-indicating and crew-alerting system (EICAS) is an integrated aircraft system that displays engine parameters and provides visual and aural alerting of system malfunctions to the flight crew. Introduced with the Boeing 757 and 767 in the early 1980s, EICAS replaced the traditional array of individual engine gauges and the flight engineer's panel, enabling two-pilot operation of large airliners and forming a core part of the "glass cockpit". Boeing aircraft from the 747-400 onward, and Embraer, Bombardier and other manufacturers' jets, use EICAS or closely related systems; Airbus uses the equivalent ECAM (electronic centralised aircraft monitor).

EICAS presents primary engine data on one display and alert messages and synoptics on another.

Operation

The primary display continuously shows the main engine parameters — typically N1 (fan speed) or EPR (engine pressure ratio), exhaust gas temperature (EGT), fuel flow, and N2/N3 speeds — together with total fuel quantity and, on many installations, gear and flap positions. The secondary display normally presents synoptic diagrams or additional parameters (oil pressure and temperature, vibration, hydraulic, electrical, pneumatic and APU data) on crew selection, and automatically displays alert messages when faults occur.

Alerting philosophy

EICAS messages are prioritised by urgency and colour-coded. Warnings (red) require immediate crew action; cautions (amber) require timely action; advisories (also amber, but indented and without aural alert) require awareness; and memos (white or green) record normal system states such as "SEATBELTS ON". During critical phases such as take-off, lower-priority alerts are inhibited to reduce distraction. By presenting only relevant information — the "dark cockpit" principle of no annunciations when all is normal — EICAS reduces crew workload and scan patterns compared with older electromechanical panels.

Development

EICAS was developed by Boeing together with the electronic flight instrument system (EFIS) for the 757/767 programme, the first airliners designed around CRT displays and digital avionics. Its success in allowing the elimination of the flight engineer established the pattern for all subsequent Boeing flight decks, including the 777 and 787, where the concept evolved into the larger-format integrated display and alerting systems of modern fly-by-wire aircraft.
