Elwyn Ralph Berlekamp (September 6, 1940 – April 9, 2019) was an American mathematician and professor of mathematics and electrical engineering and computer sciences at the University of California, Berkeley. He was a pioneer of coding theory, inventing fundamental decoding algorithms for error-correcting codes, and a major contributor — with John Horton Conway and Richard Guy — to combinatorial game theory through the book Winning Ways for Your Mathematical Plays.

Early life and education

Berlekamp was born in Dover, Ohio. As an undergraduate at MIT he was a Putnam Fellow in 1961. He completed his PhD in electrical engineering at MIT in 1964 under Robert G. Gallager, with Claude Shannon among his influences. He worked at Bell Labs from 1964 to 1966 before joining the Berkeley faculty in 1966, where he remained for most of his career.

Coding theory

At Bell Labs and Berkeley, Berlekamp developed efficient algorithms for decoding Bose–Chaudhuri–Hocquenghem (BCH) and Reed–Solomon codes — the Berlekamp–Massey algorithm (independently found by James Massey) finds the shortest linear-feedback shift register generating a sequence and remains the standard method for decoding these codes, which are used in CDs, DVDs, QR codes, deep-space communication, and data storage. The Berlekamp–Welch algorithm later gave a polynomial-time method for decoding Reed–Solomon codes as an interpolation problem. His 1968 book Algebraic Coding Theory became a classic of the field. He also invented the Berlekamp switching game and worked on polynomial factorisation over finite fields (the Berlekamp algorithm).

Games, finance, and later life

With Conway and Guy, Berlekamp developed the mathematical theory of partizan games, producing Winning Ways (1982) and analysing games from Dots and Boxes to Go endgames with David Wolfe in Mathematical Go (1994). He also co-founded Cyclotomics, a research company, and worked with Axcom Trading Advisors on applying information-theoretic ideas to commodity trading. He was a member of the National Academy of Sciences and the National Academy of Engineering, and received the IEEE Richard W. Hamming Medal in 1991. He died in 2019 of complications from pulmonary fibrosis.
