Kőnig's theorem (graph theory)

In the mathematical area of graph theory, Kőnig's theorem, proved by Dénes Kőnig in 1931, describes an equivalence between the maximum matching problem and the minimum vertex cover problem in bipartite graphs. It was discovered independently, also in 1931, by Jenő Egerváry in the more general case of weighted graphs.

Statement

In any bipartite graph, the number of edges in a maximum matching equals the number of vertices in a minimum vertex cover. Equivalently, the size of a maximum matching equals the size of a minimum vertex cover: |Mmax| = |Cmin|.

Background

A matching in a graph is a set of edges no two of which share an endpoint; a vertex cover is a set of vertices such that every edge has at least one endpoint in the set. In any graph, a minimum vertex cover has at least as many vertices as a maximum matching has edges, because the edges of a matching require distinct covering vertices. Kőnig's theorem says that for bipartite graphs this bound is always tight — a fact that fails for general graphs, as odd cycles show.

Consequences

The theorem yields a proof of Hall's marriage theorem, implies that every regular bipartite graph has a perfect matching, and underlies polynomial-time algorithms for assignment problems. Its weighted generalization (the Kőnig–Egerváry theorem) connects to linear programming duality, and it is closely related to Dilworth's theorem and the max-flow min-cut theorem.
