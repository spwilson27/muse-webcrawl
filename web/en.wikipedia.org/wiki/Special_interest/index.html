Special interest (politics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Related conceptsLobbying, interest group, advocacy group, rent-seeking, regulatory capture
FieldPolitical science, public policy

A special interest group (or simply special interest) is an organization, association, or coalition that seeks to influence public policy, legislation, or government spending in favour of a particular cause, industry, profession, or segment of society. The term is often used pejoratively in political debate to suggest that the interest being advanced is narrow, self-serving, or contrary to the broader public interest.

Special interest groups pursue their goals through lobbying, campaign contributions, public relations campaigns, litigation, and grassroots mobilization. They range from trade associations and professional bodies to labour unions, single-issue campaigns, and ideological organizations.

Types

Economic interest groups

Economic groups represent the material interests of their members. These include business associations (such as chambers of commerce), trade associations for particular industries, professional associations (of doctors, lawyers, or engineers, for example), and labour unions. Because their members have concentrated financial stakes in policy outcomes, economic groups are often the best funded and most persistent participants in the policy process.

Ideological and single-issue groups

Some groups organize around a belief or a single policy question — for example gun rights or gun control, abortion, taxation, or environmental protection. Although commonly contrasted with "public interest" groups, the distinction is contested: a group advocating clean air and a group advocating lower fuel standards may both claim to serve the public.

Public interest groups

Public interest groups claim to seek benefits that are broadly shared and from which their members gain no direct material advantage. Consumer organizations, good-government watchdogs, and many environmental organizations describe themselves in these terms, though critics sometimes dispute the label.

Role in democracy

Political scientists have long debated the role of organized interests in democratic systems. Pluralist theorists, following writers such as Robert Dahl, argue that competition among groups disperses power and ensures that many voices are heard. Critics, notably E. E. Schattschneider and Mancur Olson, point out that the "pressure system" has an upper-class bias and that small groups with concentrated interests organize more easily than large, diffuse publics — a dilemma captured in Olson's The Logic of Collective Action (1965).

Criticism

The phrase "special interest" is frequently used by politicians to attack opponents' supporters while describing their own allies as representing "the people" or "Main Street." Criticisms of special interest influence include regulatory capture, in which a regulatory agency comes to serve the industry it oversees; rent-seeking, in which groups lobby for transfers rather than producing new wealth; and unequal access, in which wealthy interests can hire professional advocates beyond the reach of ordinary citizens.

Defenders respond that interest representation is protected by freedoms of speech, assembly, and petition, and that organized groups provide legislators with information and expertise.
