Hanna-Barbera was an American animation studio founded in 1957 by William Hanna and Joseph Barbera, the creators of Tom and Jerry at MGM. For more than four decades it was the dominant producer of television animation in the United States, creating a huge library of characters including Yogi Bear, Huckleberry Hound, the Flintstones, the Jetsons, Jonny Quest, and Scooby-Doo.

History

When MGM closed its cartoon studio in 1957, Hanna and Barbera, with director George Sidney, formed H-B Enterprises, soon renamed Hanna-Barbera Productions. To fit television budgets they pioneered "limited animation" — fewer drawings, held poses, and cycles of repeated movement — a cost-saving style that became the visual signature of TV cartoons. Their early syndicated hits, The Huckleberry Hound Show and The Yogi Bear Show, proved cartoons could thrive on TV.

Prime time and Saturday morning

The Flintstones (1960–1966) was the first animated series made for prime time and remained the most successful until The Simpsons decades later; The Jetsons (1962) offered its futuristic counterpart. Through the 1960s and 1970s the studio supplied much of American Saturday-morning television: Scooby-Doo, Where Are You!, The Smurfs (a later hit of 1981), superhero and adventure series, and countless spin-offs and crossovers.

Later years

The studio was sold to Taft Broadcasting in 1966 and acquired by Turner Broadcasting in 1991, which used its library to launch Cartoon Network. William Hanna died in 2001, the same year the studio was folded into Warner Bros. Animation; Joseph Barbera died in 2006. The Hanna-Barbera name and library remain foundational to Warner Bros.' animation business.

Legacy

Often criticised for cheap production values yet hugely popular worldwide, Hanna-Barbera shaped the childhoods of generations and set the economic model for made-for-TV animation. Many of its techniques were developed by veterans of the golden age of theatrical shorts, and its alumni seeded much of the later American animation industry.
