Ferenc Szálasi (6 January 1897 – 12 March 1946) was a Hungarian fascist politician, founder and leader of the Arrow Cross Party, and head of the German-backed "Government of National Unity" that ruled Hungary in the final months of the Second World War, from October 1944 to the spring of 1945. His brief regime was responsible for the murder of thousands of Hungarian Jews and for continuing Hungary's war effort on Nazi Germany's side until the country's complete occupation by the Red Army. He was tried and executed for war crimes in 1946.

Early life and ideology

Szálasi was born in Kassa (now Košice) into a family of mixed Armenian, Slovak, German, and Hungarian ancestry. He served as an officer in the Austro-Hungarian Army in the First World War, then in the general staff of the Hungarian Army, retiring as a major in 1935 to pursue politics. He developed an ideology he called "Hungarism" — a blend of extreme nationalism, anti-Semitism, irredentism aimed at restoring Hungary's pre-1920 borders, and a corporatist, anti-capitalist and anti-communist social programme. His successive movements were repeatedly banned by the government of Regent Miklós Horthy, and Szálasi was imprisoned from 1938 to 1940; the Arrow Cross nevertheless won substantial support in the 1939 elections, especially among Budapest workers and the lower middle class.

Takeover and regime

When Horthy attempted to take Hungary out of the war in October 1944, Germany executed Operation Panzerfaust, kidnapped Horthy's son, and forced the regent to appoint Szálasi as prime minister on 16 October 1944. Szálasi assumed the title "Leader of the Nation" (Nemzetvezető) and head of state. His government resumed the deportation of Jews — suspended by Horthy after most of provincial Jewry had already been sent to Auschwitz — driving tens of thousands of Budapest Jews on death marches toward the Austrian border for forced labour. Arrow Cross militiamen meanwhile murdered thousands of Jews in Budapest itself, shooting many into the Danube; Raoul Wallenberg and other diplomats struggled to protect those in "protected houses." Szálasi's government also conscripted boys and old men, evacuated state assets westward, and fought on as the Red Army besieged and destroyed Budapest.

Fall, trial, and execution

Szálasi fled westward as Hungary fell, and was captured by American troops in Austria in May 1945. Extradited to Hungary, he was tried by a People's Court in Budapest in February 1946, convicted of war crimes and crimes against the people, and hanged on 12 March 1946 together with several of his ministers. Estimates of Jewish deaths during his six months in power range from 10,000 to 15,000 murdered in Budapest alone, in addition to those who perished on the death marches and in labour service.
