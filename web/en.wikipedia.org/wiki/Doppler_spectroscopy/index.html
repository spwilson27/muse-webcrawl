Doppler spectroscopy

Doppler spectroscopy (also known as the radial-velocity method, or colloquially, the wobble method) is an indirect method for finding extrasolar planets and brown dwarfs from radial-velocity measurements via observation of Doppler shifts in the spectrum of the planet's parent star. It was proposed by Otto Struve in 1952, and the first successful detection, 51 Pegasi b, was announced in 1995 by Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz, who won the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics in part for this discovery.


Artist's impression of a star's radial-velocity wobble caused by an orbiting exoplanet.

Principle

As a planet orbits a star, its gravitational pull causes the star to move in a small orbit around the system's common centre of mass. These motions produce shifts in the star's spectral lines: when the star moves toward Earth the lines are blueshifted, and when it moves away they are redshifted. By measuring the periodic variation in radial velocity, astronomers can infer the planet's orbital period and a minimum mass (m sin i).

History

Precise measurements became possible in the 1980s and 1990s with iodine absorption cells and high-resolution spectrographs such as ELODIE, HARPS, and HIRES. The method has discovered hundreds of exoplanets, particularly massive "hot Jupiters" in short-period orbits, though it is biased toward large planets close to their stars.

Limitations

The method yields only a lower limit on a planet's mass unless the orbital inclination is known. Stellar activity such as starspots can mimic planetary signals, and long-period or low-mass planets produce signals near or below the noise floor of current instruments, though extreme-precision spectrographs aim for 10 cm/s precision, enough to detect an Earth analogue.
