Chain rule

In calculus, the chain rule is a formula that expresses the derivative of the composition of two differentiable functions f and g in terms of the derivatives of f and g. In Leibniz's notation it reads:

dy/dx = (dy/du) · (du/dx),

where u = g(x) and y = f(u). More precisely, if h(x) = f(g(x)), then h′(x) = f′(g(x)) · g′(x). The rule is one of the fundamental tools of differential calculus and underlies techniques from implicit differentiation to the backpropagation algorithm used to train neural networks.

The chain rule multiplies the rate of change of the outer function by that of the inner function.

Intuition

If a variable y depends on u, which itself depends on x, then the rate at which y changes with x is the product of the two rates: a change in x is first amplified or shrunk by the inner function and then by the outer function. This is why the derivatives multiply rather than add.

Examples

To differentiate sin(x2), set the outer function to sin and the inner to x2; the result is cos(x2) · 2x. A standard mnemonic imagines the differentials in Leibniz notation "cancelling", as in dy/dx = (dy/du)(du/dx) — formally unjustified for ordinary derivatives, yet a reliable guide to the correct formula.

Generalizations

The chain rule generalises to functions of several variables, where derivatives become Jacobian matrices and multiplication becomes matrix multiplication: the derivative of a composite map is the composite (product) of the derivatives. In differential geometry this becomes the functorial behaviour of the differential (pushforward) of a smooth map. A probabilistic analogue exists for densities of transformed random variables, and Faà di Bruno's formula gives the higher derivatives of a composition.

History

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz used the rule in a 1676 memorandum, though without a modern proof; Guillaume de l'Hôpital published it in his 1696 textbook Analyse des Infiniment Petits. Rigorous proofs arrived with the nineteenth-century formalisation of limits, and the subtle cases (where the inner function's increments vanish) required care that Joseph-Louis Lagrange and later analysts supplied.
