British African-Caribbean people

RegionsLondon, Birmingham, Manchester, Leeds, Bristol and other cities
LanguagesBritish English, Caribbean creoles (e.g. Jamaican Patois)
OriginsPrimarily Jamaica, Trinidad and Tobago, Barbados, Grenada, Guyana and other Caribbean countries

British African-Caribbean people (or Afro-Caribbean Britons) are a British ethnic group descended from Caribbean migrants, most of whose ancestors were brought from Africa to the Caribbean as enslaved people by European colonists. In the 2021 census of England and Wales, about 623,000 people identified as "Black, Black British, Black Welsh, Caribbean or African: Caribbean," with larger numbers of mixed heritage.

History

A small Black presence existed in Britain from the Roman period onward, and Black communities grew in port cities such as London, Liverpool and Cardiff in the 18th and 19th centuries. Large-scale migration came after the Second World War: the arrival of the ship Empire Windrush at Tilbury in June 1948, carrying 492 migrants from Jamaica, became the symbolic start of modern Caribbean migration. The "Windrush generation" came as British subjects to help rebuild post-war Britain, filling jobs in the NHS, public transport and industry.

Migration was curtailed by the Commonwealth Immigrants Acts of 1962 and 1968. Communities faced discrimination in housing and employment, and tensions erupted in the Notting Hill riots of 1958 and later disturbances in the 1980s. In 2018 the "Windrush scandal" revealed that many long-settled Caribbean Britons had been wrongly detained, denied services or deported because they lacked documents proving their status, leading to an official apology and a compensation scheme.

Culture

Caribbean Britons have profoundly influenced British culture. The Notting Hill Carnival, founded in the 1960s and now one of the world's largest street festivals, celebrates Caribbean music and costume. Caribbean musical styles — calypso, ska, reggae, lovers rock, jungle, garage and grime — have shaped British popular music, while Caribbean food shops, churches, sound systems and carnival arts are fixtures of many British cities. Notable figures of Caribbean heritage include politicians, athletes such as sprinter Linford Christie, and writers and broadcasters across the arts.
