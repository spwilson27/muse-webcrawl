Sales taxes in the United States

Sales taxes in the United States are taxes placed on the sale or lease of goods and services in the United States. Sales tax is governed at the state level, and no national general sales tax exists. As of 2017, 45 states, the District of Columbia, the territories of Puerto Rico and Guam impose general sales taxes that apply to the sale or lease of most goods and some services, and Washington applies sales taxes to a majority of services. States may also delegate sales and use tax authority to counties, cities, or other local jurisdictions.


Combined state and local sales tax rates by state

Overview

Sales tax is calculated by multiplying the purchase price by the applicable tax rate. The seller collects it at the time of sale and remits it to the state. Rates vary widely: California has the highest state-level rate at 7.25%, while five states — Alaska, Delaware, Montana, New Hampshire, and Oregon — levy no statewide sales tax, although Alaska permits local sales taxes. Combined state and average local rates are highest in states such as Tennessee, Louisiana, and Arkansas, where totals approach or exceed 9.5%.

Exemptions

Many states exempt certain necessities, most commonly groceries, prescription drugs, and medical devices, or tax them at a reduced rate. Sales for resale are generally exempt, as are sales to charitable and governmental organisations. Goods purchased for use in a state where the seller did not collect tax are generally subject to a compensating use tax.

Online sales and nexus

Historically, states could require tax collection only by sellers with a physical presence ("nexus") in the state. In South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc. (2018), the Supreme Court overruled the physical-presence requirement, allowing states to require remote sellers with substantial economic activity in the state to collect sales tax. All states with a general sales tax subsequently adopted economic-nexus and marketplace-facilitator rules.

History

Although selective excise taxes existed earlier, the first broad retail sales taxes were adopted during the Great Depression, beginning with states such as West Virginia (1921, in an early form), Georgia (1929) and Mississippi (1930), as collapsing property-tax revenues forced states to find new sources of income.
