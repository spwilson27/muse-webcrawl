History of Egypt under the Muhammad Ali dynasty

The history of Egypt under the Muhammad Ali dynasty covers the period from 1805, when Muhammad Ali Pasha seized power in the aftermath of the French occupation, until the Egyptian revolution of 1952 abolished the monarchy founded by his line. During this century and a half Egypt was transformed from an Ottoman province into a modernising, effectively independent state, before falling under British occupation in 1882 while the dynasty remained on the throne.

Muhammad Ali and the founding of modern Egypt

Muhammad Ali, an Albanian Ottoman commander, became wali (governor) of Egypt in 1805 and eliminated the Mamluk elite in the Citadel massacre of 1811. He built a conscript army trained by European officers, founded factories, schools and a navy, reformed land tenure and taxation, and expanded Egypt's power into Arabia, the Sudan, Greece and Syria. His campaigns against the Ottoman sultan brought European intervention; the Convention of London (1840) forced him to relinquish Syria but confirmed hereditary rule of Egypt in his family.

Abbas, Sa'id and Isma'il

Abbas I (r. 1848–1854) retrenched some reforms, while Sa'id (r. 1854–1863) granted the concession for the Suez Canal. Under Isma'il the Magnificent (r. 1863–1879) Egypt borrowed heavily from European banks to fund railways, ports, irrigation and the new quarters of Cairo; the Suez Canal opened in 1869. Isma'il obtained the title of Khedive in 1867. Mounting debt led to the sale of Egypt's canal shares to Britain in 1875, Anglo-French financial control, and Isma'il's deposition in 1879.

Occupation and kingdom

After the 'Urabi revolt and the British occupation of 1882, Khedives Tawfiq and Abbas II reigned under British supervision. In 1914 Britain declared a protectorate and installed Hussein Kamel as Sultan. Fuad I became sultan and then, with the declaration of independence in 1922, King of Egypt. His son Farouk I succeeded in 1936. The monarchy's prestige collapsed after the 1942 Abdeen Palace incident and Egypt's defeat in the 1948 Arab–Israeli War; the Free Officers' coup of July 1952 forced Farouk to abdicate, and the republic was proclaimed in 1953, ending the dynasty.
