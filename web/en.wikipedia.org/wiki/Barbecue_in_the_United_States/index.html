Barbecue in the United States refers to the regional traditions of cooking meat slowly over indirect heat and wood smoke — "low and slow" — that developed especially in the American South and Midwest. The word derives from barbacoa, the term Spanish explorers took from the Taíno people of the Caribbean for a wooden framework used to cook over fire. American barbecue traditions were shaped by Indigenous techniques, West African cooking brought by enslaved people, and European preservation methods; African-American pitmasters were central to developing and transmitting the craft.

Regional styles

Carolinas: The oldest tradition centres on whole hog or pork shoulder. In Eastern North Carolina the meat is dressed with a thin vinegar-and-pepper sauce; the Lexington (Piedmont) style uses shoulder only and a tomato-tinged "dip". South Carolina is known for its golden mustard-based sauce. Memphis specialises in pork ribs — served "dry" with a spice rub or "wet" with sauce — and pulled-pork sandwiches topped with coleslaw. Kansas City uses a wide variety of meats cooked in hickory smoke and glazed with a thick, sweet tomato-and-molasses sauce; it is the home of burnt ends, the caramelised points of brisket. Central Texas barbecue, influenced by German and Czech meat markets, features beef brisket seasoned simply with salt and coarse black pepper, smoked over post oak, and served by weight on butcher paper with pickles, onions and white bread; Texas also claims beef ribs, jalapeño-cheddar sausage ("hot guts"), and mesquite-fired styles in the south and west of the state.

Other traditions

Other notable styles include Alabama white sauce (a mayonnaise-based sauce for chicken, from Big Bob Gibson's in Decatur), Kentucky mutton with black "dip", St. Louis spare ribs, Chicago aquarium-smoker rib tips, and Santa Maria–style tri-tip grilled over red oak in California. Sides commonly include coleslaw, baked beans, cornbread, macaroni and cheese, collard greens and hushpuppies.

Culture

Barbecue is bound up with communal gatherings — church picnics, political rallies, Juneteenth celebrations and Fourth of July cookouts — and with a competitive circuit of contests (such as Memphis in May and the American Royal in Kansas City) sanctioned by bodies like the Kansas City Barbeque Society. In recent decades celebrated pitmasters and restaurants have brought regional barbecue national and international attention, while debates over authenticity, sauce and wood choice remain a staple of American food culture.
