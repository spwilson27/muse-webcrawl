The Victorian gold rush was a period in the history of Victoria, Australia, roughly between 1851 and the late 1860s, when the colony became the centre of one of the world's greatest gold rushes. Following payable gold discoveries near Clunes, Buninyong and Ballarat in 1851, hundreds of thousands of prospectors arrived from around the world, transforming Victoria's population, economy and society, and producing in some years a substantial share of the world's gold output.

Discoveries and boom

Though gold had been found in the region earlier, discoveries were initially suppressed for fear of unsettling the convict-era social order. In 1851, following rushes in neighbouring New South Wales, finds at Clunes, Buninyong, Ballarat and Bendigo were publicised. Victoria's fields proved extraordinarily rich in alluvial gold. Within a decade Victoria's population multiplied roughly sevenfold, Melbourne became one of the great boom cities of the British Empire, and enormous nuggets were unearthed, including the Welcome Nugget and, later in 1869, the Welcome Stranger, the largest alluvial gold nugget ever found.

The diggers and the licence system

Miners — "diggers" — were required to hold an expensive monthly gold licence whether or not they found gold, and licence hunts by police were widely resented. Resentment culminated in the Eureka Rebellion of December 1854, when miners at Ballarat raised the Eureka flag and built a stockade that was stormed by troops and police, killing at least 22 diggers. Though defeated militarily, the miners won most of their demands: the licence was replaced by a cheaper Miner's Right, and miners gained the vote, making Eureka a celebrated landmark in Australian democratic tradition.

Legacy

Chinese miners, who arrived in large numbers, faced discriminatory poll taxes and violence, foreshadowing the White Australia policy. As surface gold was exhausted, mining shifted to deep-lead and quartz reef mining by companies. The wealth of the rush era built grand public buildings in Melbourne and the gold towns, funded railways and institutions, and left a lasting mark on Australian identity, folklore and politics.
