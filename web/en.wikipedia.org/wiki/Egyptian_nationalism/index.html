Egyptian nationalism

Egyptian nationalism is the nationalism of Egyptians and Egyptian culture, asserting Egypt as a distinct, continuous nation with roots in Pharaonic antiquity. Unlike pan-Arab nationalism, which emphasises Egypt's Arab and Islamic identity and unity with other Arab states, Egyptian nationalism traditionally stresses a specifically Egyptian identity shaped by the Nile Valley's long history.

Mass demonstrations during the Egyptian revolution of 1919.

Origins

The movement emerged in the late nineteenth century under British occupation (from 1882). The ʻUrabi revolt of 1879–82 raised the slogan "Egypt for the Egyptians". Thinkers such as Mustafa Kamil, founder of the National Party, agitated for British evacuation, while the rediscovery and excavation of Pharaonic antiquities encouraged "Pharaonism" — pride in ancient Egyptian civilisation — in the 1920s.

1919 revolution and the Wafd

After the First World War, Saad Zaghloul and the Wafd Party demanded independence; Zaghloul's arrest in 1919 triggered a nationwide revolution that led to Britain's unilateral declaration of Egyptian independence in 1922, though with reserved points limiting sovereignty. Liberal-era nationalism celebrated figures such as the sculptor Mahmoud Mokhtar, whose statue Egypt's Awakening (1928) became an emblem of the movement.

Nasser and after

The 1952 revolution brought Gamal Abdel Nasser to power; although Nasser championed pan-Arabism — including the short-lived United Arab Republic with Syria (1958–61) — his defiance of Britain and France in the Suez Crisis of 1956 also made him an Egyptian national hero. After the 1967 defeat and Nasser's death, Anwar Sadat re-emphasised Egyptian state interests, a trend continued under his successors, while debate between Egyptian, Arab and Islamic identities persists.
