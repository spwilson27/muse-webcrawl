Parable

TypeDidactic short story / literary genre
PurposeTo illustrate a moral or spiritual lesson
Notable sourcesSynoptic Gospels, Aesop's Fables, rabbinic literature, Buddhist Jātaka tales

A parable is a succinct, didactic story, in prose or verse, that illustrates one or more instructive lessons or principles. It differs from a fable in that fables employ animals, plants, inanimate objects, or forces of nature as characters, whereas parables generally feature human characters.

A teacher narrating a parable to an audience.

A parable is a type of metaphorical analogy. Like allegory, it presents a comparison, but it is typically a brief narrative in which the details are realistic and drawn from everyday life, while the point of the story is something distinct from those surface details.

Etymology

The word parable comes from the Greek παραβολή (parabolē), meaning "comparison, illustration, analogy." It was the name given by Greek rhetoricians to an illustration in the form of a brief fictional narrative. The term passed through Latin parabola into English.

History

Parables are among the oldest forms of teaching narrative. They appear in the Hebrew Bible, where the prophet Nathan confronts King David with a parable about a rich man who stole a poor man's lamb (2 Samuel 12). Rabbinic literature of the Second Temple period and later made extensive use of the mashal, a parable form typically introduced with the phrase "to what may this be compared?" Jewish teachers such as Hillel and, later, the rabbis of the Talmud used parables to interpret scripture and teach ethics.

The most famous parables in Western culture are the parables of Jesus, recorded in the Synoptic Gospels. These include the Parable of the Good Samaritan, the Parable of the Prodigal Son, the Parable of the Sower, and the Parable of the Mustard Seed. Jesus is portrayed as teaching in parables frequently, and the Gospel of Mark states that "he did not speak to them without a parable" (Mark 4:34).

Parables also appear in Buddhist literature, notably in the Jātaka tales recounting the Buddha's previous lives and in Mahāyāna texts such as the Lotus Sutra, which contains the famous parable of the burning house. Islamic tradition includes parables in the Qur'an and in Sufi teaching stories, such as those of Nasreddin.

Characteristics

Scholars such as Adolf Jülicher argued that the parables of Jesus each make a single main point, resisting elaborate allegorical decoding of every detail. Later scholars, including C. H. Dodd and Joachim Jeremias, situated the parables in their first-century Palestinian context and in the proclamation of the Kingdom of God. Dodd's classic definition describes a parable as "a metaphor or simile drawn from nature or common life, arresting the hearer by its vividness or strangeness, and leaving the mind in sufficient doubt about its precise application to tease it into active thought."

Examples

  • The Good Samaritan (Luke 10)
  • The Prodigal Son (Luke 15)
  • The Sower (Matthew 13; Mark 4; Luke 8)
  • The Mustard Seed (Matthew 13; Mark 4; Luke 13)
  • The Parable of the Talents (Matthew 25)
  • The Workers in the Vineyard (Matthew 20)
