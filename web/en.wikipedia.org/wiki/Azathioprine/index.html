Azathioprine

Azathioprine, sold under the brand name Imuran among others, is an immunosuppressive medication. It is used in rheumatoid arthritis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, and in kidney transplants to prevent rejection. It is listed on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Medical uses

Azathioprine is used to prevent organ rejection in transplant recipients, usually combined with other agents, and to treat a range of autoimmune diseases including inflammatory bowel disease, autoimmune hepatitis, lupus, and multiple sclerosis in some settings. It is a prodrug of mercaptopurine and acts slowly, often taking several months to reach full effect.

Mechanism of action

Azathioprine is converted in the body to 6-mercaptopurine, which is further metabolised to thioguanine nucleotides. These fraudulent nucleotides are incorporated into DNA and RNA and inhibit purine synthesis, suppressing the proliferation of lymphocytes and thereby dampening the immune response.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include bone marrow suppression, nausea, and increased susceptibility to infection. Toxicity is strongly influenced by the activity of the enzyme thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT); patients with low TPMT activity are at risk of life-threatening myelosuppression, and genetic or phenotypic testing is recommended before treatment. Long-term use carries a small increased risk of certain cancers, including skin cancers and lymphoma. Azathioprine was synthesised by George Herbert Hitchings and Gertrude Elion, whose work on purine analogues won them the Nobel Prize in 1988.
