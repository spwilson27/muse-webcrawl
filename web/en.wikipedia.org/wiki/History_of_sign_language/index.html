History of sign language

FieldLinguistics / Deaf history
Key figuresPedro Ponce de León, Charles-Michel de l'Épée, Thomas Gallaudet

The history of sign language documents the natural visual languages of deaf communities and their long struggle for recognition against oralist education policies.

Early history

References to signing appear in ancient texts; Plato's Cratylus notes communication by hands. In the 16th century Pedro Ponce de León taught deaf children of Spanish nobility, and Juan Pablo Bonet published the first manual alphabet (1620). Village sign languages arose wherever hereditary deafness was common, as on Martha's Vineyard.

Deaf education

Charles-Michel de l'Épée founded the first public deaf school in Paris (1760), basing teaching on the sign language of Parisian deaf people — the ancestor of French Sign Language and, through Laurent Clerc and Thomas Gallaudet's Hartford school (1817), of American Sign Language. The 1880 Milan Congress pushed oralism, suppressing signing in schools for decades.

Recognition

William Stokoe's linguistic analysis (1960) proved ASL a full language. Deaf Pride and the 1988 Deaf President Now protest at Gallaudet University advanced deaf rights; many countries now legally recognise their national sign languages.
