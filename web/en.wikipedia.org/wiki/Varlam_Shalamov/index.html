Varlam Tikhonovich Shalamov (Russian: Варлам Тихонович Шаламов; 18 June 1907 – 17 January 1982) was a Russian writer, journalist, and poet, best known for Kolyma Tales, a cycle of short stories about life in the Gulag labour camps of the Soviet Union. A survivor of seventeen years in the camps of the Kolyma region in the Soviet Far East, Shalamov is regarded as one of the most unflinching chroniclers of the camp system.

Early life

Shalamov was born in Vologda, in northwestern Russia, the son of an Orthodox priest. He studied law at Moscow State University in the 1920s and became involved with a Trotskyist student group, for which he was arrested in 1929 and sentenced to three years in the Vishera camp in the northern Urals.

Arrests and the Kolyma

After his release, Shalamov worked as a journalist in Moscow. In 1937, during the Great Purge, he was arrested again and sentenced to five years in Kolyma, a sentence later extended. In 1943 he received a further ten-year sentence for "anti-Soviet agitation" after remarking that the émigré writer Ivan Bunin was a Russian classic. He survived starvation, cold, and brutal labour in gold mines, working at various points as a medical assistant (feldsher), which likely saved his life. He was released in 1951 but was not permitted to leave the Magadan region until 1953.

Kolyma Tales

Between 1954 and 1973 Shalamov composed Kolyma Tales (Kolymskie rasskazy), six cycles comprising more than 140 short stories. Written in a spare, documentary style, the stories depict the dehumanising conditions of the camps: extreme cold, hunger, violence, and the erosion of human solidarity. Shalamov insisted that the camp was an entirely negative experience — a "negative school" from which nothing good could be taken — a position that set him apart from Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, with whom he had a complicated relationship. The tales circulated in samizdat and were published abroad from the 1960s; a complete Russian edition appeared only after his death.

Later life and death

Shalamov lived in poverty in Moscow, gradually losing his hearing and health. He died on 17 January 1982 in a home for the aged and disabled, reportedly after a forced transfer he resisted. His work was officially published in the USSR only during perestroika, in the late 1980s.

Legacy

Kolyma Tales is now considered a masterpiece of twentieth-century Russian prose and a central document of Gulag literature. English translations, notably by John Glad and later Donald Rayfield, brought Shalamov a wide international readership.
