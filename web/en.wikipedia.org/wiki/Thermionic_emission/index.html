Thermionic emission

Thermionic emission is the liberation of charged particles from a material's surface in response to heat. In metals, heating gives electrons enough energy to overcome the work function and escape into a vacuum.

History

The phenomenon was observed by Edmond Becquerel (1853) and named the Edison effect after Thomas Edison's 1883 observation of current flow in an evacuated bulb. Owen Willans Richardson derived the governing law, winning the 1928 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Richardson's law

The current density J = A T² exp(−W/kT), where A is the Richardson constant, T the absolute temperature, W the work function, and k Boltzmann's constant. Materials with low work functions—thoriated tungsten, oxide-coated cathodes—emit efficiently at lower temperatures.

Applications

Thermionic cathodes underpinned vacuum tubes, cathode-ray tubes, X-ray tubes, electron microscopes, and thermionic converters that turn heat directly into electricity. The effect also underlies the Schottky effect and space-charge behaviour in electron guns.
