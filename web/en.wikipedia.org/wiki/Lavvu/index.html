Lavvu

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A lavvu (also spelled lávvu) is a temporary dwelling used by the Sámi people of northern Norway, Sweden, Finland, and Russia's Kola Peninsula. It has a design similar to a Native American tipi, but is less vertical and more stable in high winds. It enables the indigenous cultures of the treeless plains of northern Scandinavia to follow the reindeer herds.

The lavvu is still used today as a temporary shelter by the Sámi, and increasingly by other people for camping. It is also used at cultural events to symbolise Sámi identity.

Structure

The structure of a lavvu consists of three or more evenly spaced forked or notched poles that form a tripod, and about ten or more straight poles that lean against the tripod to form a cone. A cover, traditionally made of reindeer hides and later of canvas or other fabric, is wrapped around the poles. Lavvus are mainly distinguished from the goahti (a more permanent turf or timber hut) and from the tipi by their lower, wider profile and their internal pole arrangement.

A fire is lit in the centre of the lavvu, with smoke escaping through an opening at the top. Seating and sleeping areas are arranged around the fire, traditionally on a floor of birch twigs and reindeer skins.

Historical use

During the Second World War, lavvus were used by resistance movements and by people displaced from their homes in Finnmark. In modern times, lavvus have become a symbol of Sámi protests, most famously during the Alta controversy of the late 1970s and early 1980s, when demonstrators camped in lavvus outside the Norwegian parliament in Oslo.
