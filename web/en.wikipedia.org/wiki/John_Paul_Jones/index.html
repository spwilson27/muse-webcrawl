John Paul Jones (born John Paul; July 6, 1747 – July 18, 1792) was a Scottish-born naval commander who served in the Continental Navy during the American Revolutionary War. Often called the "Father of the American Navy" — a title sometimes shared with John Barry and John Adams — he is celebrated for his victory over HMS Serapis at the Battle of Flamborough Head (1779), during which he is said to have replied to a British demand to surrender, "I have not yet begun to fight!"

Early life

Jones was born at Arbigland in Kirkcudbrightshire, Scotland, the son of a gardener, and went to sea at 13, apprenticed in the merchant service out of Whitehaven. He rose to command a brig in the West Indies trade but abandoned his ship — and added "Jones" to his name — after killing a mutinous crewman in Tobago in 1773 and fleeing to Virginia to avoid trial.

Continental Navy

When the Revolution began, Jones received a lieutenant's commission in the new Continental Navy (1775). He commanded the sloop Providence on commerce-raiding cruises and, in 1777, sailed the sloop-of-war Ranger to France. In April 1778 Ranger raided Whitehaven — the last attack on an English port by foreign forces for centuries — and captured HMS Drake, the first British warship taken by an American vessel in British waters.

In 1779 Jones took command of a small squadron centred on the converted French East Indiaman Bonhomme Richard. On 23 September 1779, off Flamborough Head, he engaged the frigate HMS Serapis and the Countess of Scarborough. In one of the bloodiest single-ship actions of the age, the sinking Bonhomme Richard grappled Serapis, and the British captain struck his colours; Jones transferred his crew to the captured frigate and watched his own ship sink two days later.

Russian service and death

After the war Congress voted him a gold medal (1787), but with the Continental Navy disbanded Jones accepted a rear admiral's commission in the Imperial Russian Navy of Catherine the Great, serving against the Ottoman Turks on the Black Sea in 1788. Court intrigues ended his Russian career, and he retired to Paris, where he died in obscurity in 1792. His remains, rediscovered in 1905, were reinterred with honours in the crypt of the United States Naval Academy Chapel at Annapolis in 1913.
