Chandos portrait

The Chandos portrait is the most famous portrait of William Shakespeare (c. 1564–1616). It was painted between 1600 and 1610 by an unknown artist, possibly John Taylor, and is named after James Brydges, 1st Duke of Chandos, who owned it in the eighteenth century. In 1856 it became the very first portrait acquired by the newly founded National Portrait Gallery in London, where it remains as accession number NPG 1. Whether it truly depicts Shakespeare from life remains debated.

Description

The portrait shows a bearded man in his thirties or forties, turned slightly to his right, against a dark background. He has brown hair, a receding hairline, and wears a loose white collar or ruff and a dark doublet; in his left ear is a single gold earring. The picture has darkened considerably with age and has been cut down, retouched and overpainted at various times; technical examination has revealed alterations including the lengthening of the beard and hair.

Provenance and authenticity

The painting's provenance can be traced with reasonable confidence back through the actor Thomas Betterton and the playwright Sir William Davenant, who claimed to be Shakespeare's godson (and, by rumour, his son). It passed to the Duke of Chandos and then through several collections before the Earl of Ellesmere donated it to the nation in 1856. When the National Portrait Gallery investigated the portrait for its 2006 exhibition Searching for Shakespeare, it concluded that the evidence for the identification, while not conclusive, was stronger for the Chandos than for other candidate portraits, and curators judged it "probably" a genuine likeness painted from life. Together with the Droeshout engraving in the First Folio and the Stratford funerary monument, it is one of the three images with the strongest claim to represent Shakespeare.
