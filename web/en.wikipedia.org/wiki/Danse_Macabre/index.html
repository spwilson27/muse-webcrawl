Danse Macabre

The Danse Macabre (French pronunciation: [dɑ̃s ma.kabʁ]; from the French phrase), also called the Dance of Death, is an artistic genre of allegory from the Late Middle Ages on the universality of death. The Danse Macabre consists of the dead, or a personification of death, summoning representatives from all walks of life to dance along to the grave — typically a pope, emperor, king, child and labourer appear — in a kind of dance of death. The effect is both frivolous and terrifying, and emphasises that death does not discriminate.

The genre was produced under the impact of the Black Death and the Hundred Years' War, and is a form of memento mori intended for spiritual instruction.

Origins

The earliest recorded visual example is the mural series painted in 1424–25 on the cloister walls of the Cimetière des Saints-Innocents in Paris (destroyed in the 17th century but known through Guyot Marchant's printed edition of 1485). The motif likely grew from sermon literature, the legend of the Three Living and the Three Dead, and morality plays, sharpened by the demographic catastrophe of the Black Death of 1346–1353, which created an obsession with mortality in European culture.

Form and content

In typical sequences, Death — as a skeleton, corpse or shrouded figure — addresses thirty or more living partners in strict social hierarchy, from pope and emperor down to peasant, child and fool. Each figure is accompanied by verses in which Death summons them and the living lament. The dialogue often carries social satire: corrupt judges, greedy misers and vain prelates are reproached, giving the genre a reformist edge.

Famous examples

Notable cycles survive or are recorded at Basel, Lübeck (Bernt Notke, 1463, destroyed 1942), Tallinn (Notke, fragment), La Chaise-Dieu, Kermaria-an-Isquit, and in the cloister of St Paul's Cathedral, London. Hans Holbein the Younger's woodcut series The Dance of Death (designed c. 1526, published 1538 in Lyon) miniaturised the genre into a book of 41 scenes and remains its most influential version.

Influence

The Danse Macabre left a lasting mark on Western art and music, inspiring works from Saint-Saëns' tone poem Danse macabre (1874) to Liszt's Totentanz and modern film, notably the closing sequence of Ingmar Bergman's The Seventh Seal (1957).
