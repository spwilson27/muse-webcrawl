Powel Crosley Jr. (September 18, 1886 – March 28, 1961) was an American inventor, industrialist, and entrepreneur from Cincinnati, Ohio. Sometimes called "the Henry Ford of radio", he built the Crosley Corporation into a major manufacturer of low-cost radios, refrigerators, and small cars, founded the powerful clear-channel radio station WLW, and owned the Cincinnati Reds baseball team.

Radio and manufacturing

Crosley entered business making automobile accessories, then, in 1921, seeking an affordable radio for his son, began selling the "Harko" receiver for about $20 — far below competitors' prices. The Crosley Radio Corporation soon became one of the world's largest radio manufacturers. His broadcasting station WLW grew into a 500,000-watt experimental "nation's station" in the 1930s, briefly the most powerful radio transmitter in the United States, heard across much of North America. Crosley products expanded to include the Shelvador refrigerator, whose shelves in the door became an industry-standard feature, and home appliances of every kind.

Automobiles and baseball

Crosley pursued cheap small cars for decades, launching the compact Crosley automobile in 1939 and reviving it after the Second World War; the line included the Hotshot sports car, the first American post-war sports car, before production ended in 1952. In 1934 he bought the Cincinnati Reds, whose ballpark was renamed Crosley Field in his honour; the Reds won the World Series in 1940 under his ownership, and the field bore his name until 1970. Crosley also built grand homes in Cincinnati and Sarasota, Florida, the latter — Seagate — now preserved as an event venue.
