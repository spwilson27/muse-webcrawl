Plasmodium is a genus of unicellular eukaryotes that are obligate parasites of vertebrates and insects. The life cycles of Plasmodium species involve development in a blood-feeding insect host — usually the mosquito — which then transmits the parasite to a vertebrate host during a blood meal. Five species infect humans and cause malaria: P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale, P. malariae, and the zoonotic P. knowlesi. More than 200 species have been described, infecting mammals, birds, and reptiles.

Life cycle

The life cycle has three broad stages. In the mosquito, gametocytes taken up in a blood meal fuse to form zygotes that develop into oocysts on the gut wall, releasing sporozoites that migrate to the salivary glands. When the mosquito bites a vertebrate, sporozoites enter the bloodstream and invade liver cells (the exoerythrocytic stage), where each produces tens of thousands of merozoites; in P. vivax and P. ovale, dormant hypnozoites may persist in the liver and cause relapses months or years later. Merozoites then invade red blood cells and multiply asexually in synchronised cycles that produce the periodic fevers of malaria; some merozoites develop into gametocytes, completing the cycle when taken up by another mosquito.

Human disease

Plasmodium falciparum causes the most severe disease and the great majority of malaria deaths, which number in the hundreds of thousands per year, mostly among young children in sub-Saharan Africa. Diagnosis is traditionally by microscopic examination of blood films, now supplemented by rapid antigen tests. Treatment depends on species and drug resistance; artemisinin-based combination therapies are the standard for falciparum malaria.

History

The parasites were first seen in the blood of malaria patients by Charles Louis Alphonse Laveran in 1880, and Ronald Ross demonstrated mosquito transmission in 1897–1898. The complete life cycle, including the liver stage, was worked out in the 1940s by Henry Shortt, P. C. C. Garnham, and colleagues.
