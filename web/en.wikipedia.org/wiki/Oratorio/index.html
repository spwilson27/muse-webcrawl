Oratorio

FormLarge-scale vocal concert work
OriginEarly 17th century, Italy
ForcesSoloists, choir, orchestra
SubjectUsually sacred, unstaged

An oratorio is a large musical composition for orchestra, choir and soloists. Unlike an opera, an oratorio is generally performed as a concert work without scenery, costumes or stage action, and its subject is usually sacred, drawn from the Bible or religious texts.

History

The oratorio emerged in early 17th-century Rome, taking its name from the Oratory of Saint Philip Neri, where musical prayer meetings were held. Early examples include Cavalieri's Rappresentatione di Anima, et di Corpo (1600). Giacomo Carissimi developed the Latin oratorio, and the form spread through Italy and Germany.

Baroque oratorio

The form reached its height with George Frideric Handel, whose English oratorios — above all Messiah (1741), with its famous "Hallelujah" chorus — remain concert staples. J. S. Bach's Passions are closely related works. Oratorios typically combine recitatives advancing a narrative, arias and large choruses.

Later oratorios

Haydn's The Creation (1798) and Mendelssohn's Elijah (1846) extended the tradition into the Classical and Romantic eras. Later works include Elgar's The Dream of Gerontius (1900) and secular oratorios on non-religious subjects.
