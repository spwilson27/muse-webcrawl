Fereydun

Fereydun (Persian: فریدون, Fereydūn), also known in Middle Persian as Frēdōn and in Avestan as Θraētaona (Thraetaona), is a mythical Iranian king and hero of the Shahnameh, the Persian national epic by Ferdowsi. He is a symbol of victory, justice, and generosity in Persian literature, and is celebrated for overthrowing the tyrannical Zahhak, the serpent-shouldered king who had ruled Iran for a thousand years.

Legend

According to the Shahnameh, Fereydun was the son of Abtin and Faranak. Zahhak, warned by a dream that a child named Fereydun would overthrow him, sought the infant's life; Fereydun was hidden in the Alborz mountains and nourished by the miraculous cow Barmayeh. When he reached manhood, Fereydun joined the revolt of Kaveh the Blacksmith, who raised his leather apron as a banner — the Derafsh Kaviani — and marched against Zahhak. Fereydun defeated Zahhak but, instructed by the angel Sorush not to kill him, chained him in a cave on Mount Damavand, where he remains bound until the end of time.

Reign and division of the world

Fereydun is said to have reigned for 500 years. In his old age he divided his empire among his three sons: Salm received the west (Rum and the lands of the Romans), Tur received Turan and the east, and Iraj, the youngest and favourite, received Iran. Jealous of Iraj, Salm and Tur murdered him, sending his head to their father. Fereydun prayed for vengeance, which was accomplished by Iraj's grandson Manuchehr. The division of the world among Fereydun's sons underlies the epic's central conflict between Iran and Turan.

Origins

Fereydun's prototype is the Avestan hero Θraētaona, son of Āθβiya (Abtin), who slew the three-headed, six-eyed dragon Aži Dahāka (Zahhak). The figure has been compared with dragon-slaying heroes in other Indo-European traditions.
