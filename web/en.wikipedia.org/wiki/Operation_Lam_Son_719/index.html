Date8 February – 25 March 1971

ResultNorth Vietnamese tactical victory; Ho Chi Minh trail temporarily disrupted

Operation Lam Son 719 (Vietnamese: Chiến dịch Lam Sơn 719) was a limited-objective offensive campaign conducted in southeastern Laos by the armed forces of the Republic of Vietnam (South Vietnam) between 8 February and 25 March 1971, during the Vietnam War. The United States provided logistical, aerial and artillery support, but American ground troops were barred by law from crossing into Laos.

Named after the village of Lam Sơn, birthplace of the Vietnamese hero Lê Lợi, the operation was intended to cut the Ho Chi Minh trail, North Vietnam's vital supply network, destroy its stockpiles around Tchepone, and demonstrate the success of "Vietnamization" — the policy of transferring combat responsibility to South Vietnamese forces.

The offensive

Some 20,000–30,000 ARVN troops advanced along Route 9 into Laos, supported by American helicopter lifts and B-52 strikes. North Vietnam responded with a force of up to 40,000 troops, tanks and heavy anti-aircraft artillery. ARVN units became bogged down in a series of besieged firebases; when President Nguyễn Văn Thiệu ordered a halt short of Tchepone and a piecemeal advance, the operation lost momentum. Photographs of panicked soldiers clinging to helicopter skids during the withdrawal in March became emblematic images of the war.

Losses and significance

South Vietnamese casualties were heavy — on the order of 1,500–2,200 killed and many more wounded — while the United States lost over 100 helicopters destroyed and hundreds damaged, with 215 American dead. North Vietnamese losses were also severe, estimated by US sources at over 13,000. Although supplies on the trail were destroyed and Hanoi postponed its planned offensive, Lam Son 719 exposed serious weaknesses in ARVN leadership, planning and coordination, undermining confidence in Vietnamization barely a year before the 1972 Easter Offensive.
