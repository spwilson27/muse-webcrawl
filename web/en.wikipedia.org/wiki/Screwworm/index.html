Screwworm is the common name for the parasitic larvae of certain blow flies, most importantly the New World screwworm (Cochliomyia hominivorax) of the Americas and the Old World screwworm (Chrysomya bezziana) of Africa and Asia. Unlike most maggots, which feed on dead tissue, screwworm larvae burrow into and feed on the living flesh of warm-blooded animals, their ridged bodies resembling wood screws. Infestation (myiasis) can quickly enlarge wounds and kill untreated hosts, making screwworms among the most damaging livestock pests.

Life cycle

Female flies lay hundreds of eggs at the edge of a wound or body opening. Larvae hatch within about a day, burrow head-down into living tissue in a characteristic spiral, feed for several days, then drop to the ground to pupate. Adults emerge in one to two weeks depending on temperature; females mate only once in their lives, a fact exploited in control programs.

Eradication

The screwworm is the classic success story of the sterile insect technique, developed by USDA entomologists Edward Knipling and Raymond Bushland. Vast numbers of laboratory-reared flies, sterilised by irradiation, were released so that wild females mating with sterile males produced no offspring. The method eradicated the pest from the island of Curaçao in trials (1954), then from the United States (declared eradicated in 1966/1982), Mexico, and all of Central America down to a permanent sterile-fly barrier maintained in Panama. An accidental introduction into Libya in 1988 was likewise eradicated by 1991. Outbreaks in the Florida Keys (2016, threatening the endangered Key deer) and a northward resurgence in Central America reported in the 2020s have required renewed sterile-fly releases.

Impact

Before eradication, screwworms cost the U.S. livestock industry hundreds of millions of dollars annually and caused horrific suffering in infested animals; human cases, though uncommon, occurred, typically in wounds of the nose and ears.
