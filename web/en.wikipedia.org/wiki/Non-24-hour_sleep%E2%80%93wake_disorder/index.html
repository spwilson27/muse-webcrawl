Non-24-hour sleep–wake disorder

Non-24-hour sleep–wake disorder
Other namesNon-24, free-running disorder, hypernychthemeral syndrome
SpecialtySleep medicine, psychiatry
SymptomsProgressively shifting sleep times, insomnia, daytime sleepiness
CausesCircadian rhythm not entrained to the 24-hour day; often blindness
TreatmentMelatonin, tasimelteon, light therapy, structured schedules

Non-24-hour sleep–wake disorder (often shortened to Non-24) is a chronic circadian rhythm sleep disorder in which a person's biological clock fails to synchronize, or entrain, to the 24-hour light–dark cycle. Instead, the sleep–wake pattern drifts later (or, rarely, earlier) each day, so that over days and weeks the person's sleep period cycles all the way around the clock.

The condition is most common in people who are totally blind and lack light perception, because light is the principal cue that resets the human circadian clock. It also occurs, much more rarely, in sighted people, sometimes following brain injury or in association with psychiatric conditions.

Symptoms and course

Because the sleep period continually migrates, sufferers pass through phases in which they sleep at conventional times and feel well, alternating with phases in which they are awake at night and sleepy during the day, producing symptoms resembling both insomnia and hypersomnia. Over a full cycle of the disorder, the pattern repeats. The length of the free-running cycle is typically slightly longer than 24 hours, commonly around 24.2 to 24.9 hours, though longer cycles occur.

Mechanism

In sighted people, light striking intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells signals the suprachiasmatic nucleus to keep the endogenous circadian period aligned with the solar day. When light perception is absent, or the clock's intrinsic period is too far from 24 hours, entrainment fails and the rhythm "free-runs" at its natural period.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis relies on sleep diaries or actigraphy kept for several weeks, showing the progressive daily delay. Treatment aims to entrain the clock: timed melatonin in the evening is widely used, and the melatonin receptor agonist tasimelteon was approved specifically for Non-24 in totally blind adults. In sighted patients, morning bright-light therapy and strict sleep scheduling may help.
