Battle of Washita River

The Battle of Washita River (also called the Washita Massacre) took place on 27 November 1868 in present-day Roger Mills County, Oklahoma. At dawn, the U.S. 7th Cavalry under Lt. Col. George Armstrong Custer attacked the winter village of the Southern Cheyenne chief Black Kettle on the Washita River, as part of General Philip Sheridan's winter campaign against the Plains tribes.

The attack

Black Kettle, a noted peace advocate who had survived the Sand Creek massacre of 1864, was camped under assurances of safety. Custer's troopers, advancing in four columns through snow to the music of the regimental band, overwhelmed the village quickly. Black Kettle and his wife were killed, and Cheyenne casualties are estimated from Custer's official figure of 103 warriors to Cheyenne accounts of far fewer fighting men and many women and children. The soldiers burned the village, shot some 675 captured ponies, and took 53 women and children prisoner. A detached party under Major Joel Elliott was surrounded and killed by warriors from neighbouring villages, an episode that drew criticism of Custer for abandoning them.

Legacy

Whether the event was a battle or a massacre remains debated: the Army presented it as a victory over a hostile camp linked to raids in Kansas, while the Cheyenne regard it as an attack on a peaceful village. The site is now preserved as the Washita Battlefield National Historic Site, established in 1996.
