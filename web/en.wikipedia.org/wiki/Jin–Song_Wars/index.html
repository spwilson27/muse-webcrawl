The Jin–Song Wars were a series of conflicts between the Jurchen-led Jin dynasty and the Han Chinese Song dynasty in the 12th and 13th centuries. The wars began in 1125 when the Jin, having destroyed the Liao dynasty, turned on their former Song allies. Within two years Jin forces had captured the Song capital of Kaifeng in the Jingkang incident, taking the emperors Huizong and Qinzong prisoner and conquering all of northern China.

Course of the wars

A Song prince escaped south and re-established the dynasty as the Southern Song, eventually settling at Hangzhou. In the 1130s Song generals such as Yue Fei recovered territory and checked the Jin advance, but the Song court, dominated by the peace faction around Qin Hui, negotiated the Treaty of Shaoxing (1141), which fixed the border along the Huai River and obliged the Song to pay annual tribute. Yue Fei was executed in 1142.

Hostilities flared repeatedly. The Jin prince Hailing's invasion of 1161 collapsed after defeats at Tangdao and Caishi. A Song attempt to recover the north in 1206–1208 also failed. In 1217–1224 the Jin, themselves under Mongol attack, tried unsuccessfully to compensate by invading the Song. A century of uneasy coexistence ended when the Song allied with the Mongols to destroy the Jin in 1234 — a victory that soon left the Song alone facing Mongol conquest, completed in 1279.

Significance

The wars reshaped China: political and economic gravity shifted permanently southward, gunpowder weapons developed rapidly, and the fall of the north became a lasting theme in Chinese literature and patriotic memory, with Yue Fei enshrined as a national hero.
