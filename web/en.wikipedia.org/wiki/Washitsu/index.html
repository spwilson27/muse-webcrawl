A washitsu (和室, literally "Japanese-style room") is a room in a Japanese house designed in the traditional style, in contrast to a yōshitsu or Western-style room. The defining feature of a washitsu is its floor of tatami mats; most also include sliding doors (fusuma), translucent paper screens (shoji), an alcove (tokonoma) for displaying art or flowers, and built-in cupboards (oshiire) for storing futon bedding.

Layout and size

Room sizes are traditionally measured by the number of tatami mats they contain — common sizes are four-and-a-half mats (yojōhan), six mats (rokujō), and eight mats (hachijō). Because a tatami mat has a standard 2:1 proportion, the mats are arranged in interlocking patterns that avoid four corners meeting at a single point. The tokonoma alcove, typically holding a hanging scroll (kakemono) and an ikebana flower arrangement, occupies one wall and is considered the room's focal point; the seat of honour is placed with one's back to it.

Furnishings and use

Furniture in a washitsu is low or absent. Guests sit on flat cushions (zabuton) around a low table (chabudai), sometimes with legless chairs (zaisu). In colder months a heated table (kotatsu) may be used. At night, futon bedding is laid out directly on the tatami and stored away in the morning, so the same room serves as living room, dining room, and bedroom. Shoes and slippers are never worn on tatami.

Modern context

Post-war Japanese housing increasingly favoured Western-style rooms, but most modern apartments and houses still include at least one washitsu, which is used for guests, tea ceremony practice, or elderly family members. Traditional inns (ryokan) are built almost entirely around washitsu.
