Steiner surface

A Steiner surface (also called a Roman surface in its best-known example) is a self-intersecting immersion of the real projective plane into three-dimensional space. The surfaces are named after Jakob Steiner, who investigated them during a stay in Rome in 1844; the models were catalogued by his student Ernst Kummer and others.

Definition

Steiner surfaces arise from quadratic parametrisations: maps from the projective plane to ℝ³ given by degree-two polynomials, equivalently the images of the Veronese-type embeddings after projection. They are quartic surfaces and generically possess double lines where the surface crosses itself, triple points where three sheets meet, and pinch points.

Examples

The most famous example is the Roman surface, which has three double lines, six pinch points and one triple point. Other cases include the cross-cap and Steiner's quartic surfaces of various types classified by their singularities.
