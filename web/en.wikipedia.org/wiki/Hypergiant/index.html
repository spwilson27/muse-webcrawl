A hypergiant (luminosity class 0) is among the most massive and luminous stars known. Hypergiants occupy the top of the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram, with luminosities hundreds of thousands to millions of times that of the Sun, and they lose mass at extraordinary rates through powerful stellar winds and episodic eruptions. They are rare and short-lived, representing a brief phase in the evolution of the most massive stars.

Properties

Hypergiants are subdivided by temperature into blue, yellow and red hypergiants. Blue hypergiants include the luminous blue variables (LBVs) such as Eta Carinae and P Cygni, which undergo violent outbursts. Yellow hypergiants, such as Rho Cassiopeiae, are rare transitional objects that can shed large fractions of their mass. Red hypergiants — including VY Canis Majoris, VX Sagittarii and UY Scuti — are among the largest stars by radius, with photospheres extending beyond 1,000 solar radii, though their ill-defined surfaces make exact sizes uncertain.

Stability and fate

Their luminosities approach the Eddington limit, at which radiation pressure balances gravity; exceeding it drives mass loss. Because hypergiants evolve rapidly, they are key probes of massive-star evolution. Depending on how much mass they lose, they may end as stripped-envelope supernovae, or collapse directly, and the most massive may encounter pair-instability. The Humphreys–Davidson limit describes the observed upper luminosity boundary for cool hypergiants.
