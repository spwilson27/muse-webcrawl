Five points of Calvinism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The five points of Calvinism, sometimes known by the acrostic TULIP, summarize the soteriological doctrines set out by the Synod of Dort (1618–1619) in its Canons, issued in response to the five articles of the Arminian Remonstrance of 1610. Although named for John Calvin, the five points were formulated by his followers a generation after his death, and they do not exhaust Calvinist theology; they specifically concern the doctrine of salvation.

The points

Total depravity — as a result of the Fall, every part of human nature is corrupted by sin, so that people are unable by their own will to choose God or merit salvation. Unconditional election — God chose those to whom he would grant salvation from eternity, not on the basis of foreseen faith or merit, but solely out of his mercy. Limited atonement (also called particular redemption) — Christ's death was sufficient for all but intended effectually to save the elect alone. Irresistible grace — the Holy Spirit's inward call effectually brings the elect to faith and cannot be finally resisted. Perseverance of the saints — those truly regenerated and united to Christ will persevere in faith to the end and cannot totally or finally fall away.

History

The Dutch theologian Jacobus Arminius had questioned aspects of strict predestinarian teaching; after his death in 1609 his followers, the Remonstrants, presented five articles challenging the Belgic Confession. The States General convened the Synod of Dort, which rejected the Remonstrant positions and answered each of the five articles in turn, producing the Canons of Dort. The familiar English acrostic TULIP, arranging the points in a slightly different order from the Canons, became popular in English-language Reformed writing in the twentieth century.
