Lolita

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The 1955 Olympia Press first edition of Lolita, plain green paper wrappers — the unassuming dress of a scandalous book]

Lolita is a 1955 novel by Vladimir Nabokov, written in English and first published in Paris by the Olympia Press. Framed as the prison confession of 'Humbert Humbert', a middle-aged literature scholar, it recounts his obsession with, abduction and sexual abuse of his twelve-year-old stepdaughter Dolores Haze — 'Lolita' — across a post-war America of motels and highways, in prose whose beauty is inseparable from the narrator's monstrousness.

Refused by four American publishers, published in Paris, praised by Graham Greene (one of 1955's three best books) and attacked in Britain, it reached the United States in 1958, topped bestseller lists, and has never left print or controversy. It is regularly ranked among the greatest novels of the twentieth century; Stanley Kubrick filmed it in 1962, Adrian Lyne again in 1997. The name 'Lolita' entered the language — often, teachers of the novel protest, in a sense the book itself condemns.

Structure and style

A mock foreword by 'John Ray, Jr., Ph.D.' warns and moralises; Humbert's confession follows, patterned with doubles (his nemesis-double Quilty), games, anagrams and literary echoes from Poe to Mérimée. The novel's central formal wager — that an unreliable monster can narrate irresistibly — forces the reader's complicity and revulsion into a single experience; Nabokov insisted the book had 'no moral in tow' yet also that its heart was Humbert's late recognition of the 'deadly pain' he had inflicted.

Reception

Early notices ranged from 'dull, pretentious' disgust to recognition of a masterpiece; scholarly consensus now treats it as a supreme stylistic achievement and a profound study of predation and self-deception — and it remains a test case in debates about art and its subjects. The two translations Nabokov made and supervised (into Russian, 1967) are themselves literary events.
