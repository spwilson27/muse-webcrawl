Wolfenden report

The Wolfenden report, formally the Report of the Committee on Homosexual Offences and Prostitution, was published in the United Kingdom on 4 September 1957. Chaired by Sir John Wolfenden, the committee had been appointed in 1954 amid public concern following a series of high-profile prosecutions of homosexual men — including Lord Montagu of Beaulieu, Peter Wildeblood and Michael Pitt-Rivers — and the celebrated conviction and chemical castration of the codebreaker Alan Turing, who died in 1954.

Recommendations

The report's most famous recommendation was that "homosexual behaviour between consenting adults in private should no longer be a criminal offence". It grounded this in a classic liberal distinction: the function of the criminal law, the committee argued, was to preserve public order and decency and protect the vulnerable, "not to impose a particular pattern of moral behaviour" — there must remain "a realm of private morality and immorality which is, in brief and crude terms, not the law's business". It proposed 21 as the age threshold. On prostitution, by contrast, the report recommended tougher enforcement against street solicitation, leading to the Street Offences Act 1959.

Debate and implementation

The report was debated in Parliament in 1957–58 but the government of Harold Macmillan declined to act on the homosexuality recommendation. The Homosexual Law Reform Society was founded in 1958 to campaign for implementation. A decade later, Leo Abse's private member's bill, supported by Home Secretary Roy Jenkins, became the Sexual Offences Act 1967, which decriminalised homosexual acts in private between two men over 21 in England and Wales — though with stricter conditions (a narrow definition of "in private") than Wolfenden had proposed. Scotland followed in 1980 and Northern Ireland in 1982, after the European Court of Human Rights ruling in Dudgeon v United Kingdom.

Legacy

The report is a landmark in post-war British social liberalisation and in the philosophy of law: its public/private distinction framed the celebrated Hart–Devlin debate between the jurist H. L. A. Hart, who defended Wolfenden's position, and Lord Devlin, who argued that society may enforce its shared morality. (Wolfenden's own son, Jeremy Wolfenden, was gay — a fact that lent the report a private poignancy noted by later biographers.)
