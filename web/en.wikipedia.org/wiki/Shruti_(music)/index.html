Shruti (music)

In Indian music, a shruti (Sanskrit: श्रुति, "what is heard") is the smallest interval of pitch that the human ear can detect and a singer or musical instrument can accurately produce. The concept has been discussed since ancient times, notably in Bharata's Natya Shastra (c. 200 BCE – 200 CE), which describes an octave divided into 22 shrutis.

The 22 shrutis

Classical Indian theory holds that the seven basic notes (svara) of the octave — Sa, Ri, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni — are not equally spaced but are anchored to particular shruti positions. Successive treatises, including Sarngadeva's 13th-century Sangita Ratnakara, assign the shrutis among the notes in groups (traditionally 4-3-2-4-4-3-2). The exact pitch ratios corresponding to the shrutis have been debated for centuries; modern scholars relate them to just intonation ratios arising from the harmonic series, making many shrutis close to intervals of 5-limit just intonation, though the unison Sa and octave ratios are exact (1:1 and 2:1).

Use in practice

Shruti is both a theoretical framework and a practical tuning reference. In performance, a drone instrument — tanpura, harmonium, or an electronic shruti box — provides the tonal centre, and the precise intonation of a note can vary by raga, phrase, and gamaka (ornament), which skilled listeners perceive within the broad categories of the shruti system. The word also names the electronic drone boxes and apps used by students and performers.

Comparison with Western theory

A shruti is finer than a Western semitone (the octave in 12-tone equal temperament having 12 semitones of 100 cents), so comparisons liken shrutis loosely to quarter tones or microtones, though Indian theory developed independently around melodic rather than harmonic practice.
