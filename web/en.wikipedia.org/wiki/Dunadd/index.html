Dunadd (Scottish Gaelic Dùn Add, "fort on the [River] Add") is a hillfort in Argyll and Bute, Scotland, considered one of the strongholds of the Gaelic kingdom of Dál Riata between roughly the 6th and 9th centuries AD. It stands on a rocky outcrop above the Moine Mhòr, the "great moss", near Kilmartin.

The site

Dunadd occupies a series of terraces rising to a summit citadel. At the top, a rock bears remarkable carvings: a boar in Pictish style, an ogham inscription, and a carved footprint together with a basin cut into the rock. The footprint is traditionally interpreted as part of royal inauguration ceremonies — a king placing his foot in the print to bind himself to the land — analogous to the Stone of Destiny traditions of later Scottish kingship.

Archaeology

Excavations, notably in 1980–81, found evidence of high-status metalworking, including moulds for brooches and fine metalware, imported pottery from the Mediterranean world and continental Europe, and glass — evidence that Dunadd was a major royal and craft centre connected to wide trade networks. Written sources suggest the fort was besieged in 683 and 736, when it was taken by the Pictish king Óengus I.
