Lightning rod

A lightning rod (in British English, a lightning conductor) is a metal rod mounted on top of a structure, connected by a heavy conductor to the ground, intended to protect the structure from lightning strikes. If lightning hits the building, it is intercepted by the rod and its current is conducted safely into the earth rather than passing through the structure, where it could cause fire or structural damage.

Invention

The lightning rod was invented by Benjamin Franklin in the early 1750s, following his experiments on electricity — including the famous (and possibly embellished) kite experiment of 1752 — and described in his Experiments and Observations on Electricity. Franklin proposed an iron rod, sharpened to a point, extending above the highest part of a building and connected to ground. Independently, the Czech theologian Prokop Diviš erected a grounded "weather machine" in 1754.

How it works

A lightning protection system consists of air terminals (rods), down conductors, and a grounding electrode system. The popular image of the rod "attracting" lightning over a wide area is misleading: a rod protects a limited zone, commonly modelled by the "rolling sphere" method in standards such as NFPA 780 and IEC 62305. The point's intense local electric field helps launch an upward connecting leader when a storm's stepped leader approaches, so the strike terminates on the rod rather than on the building fabric.

History and debate

Franklin favoured pointed rods while his English rival Benjamin Wilson advocated blunt ones — a scientific dispute that acquired political colouring during the American Revolution. Lightning rods spread rapidly across Europe and America, dramatically reducing fires in churches, whose tall steeples had been frequent strike targets, and in gunpowder stores.
