Pellagra epidemics were linked to diets dominated by untreated maize.

Pellagra is a disease caused by a lack of the vitamin niacin (vitamin B3) or its precursor, the amino acid tryptophan. Its classic symptoms are summarised as "the three Ds": dermatitis, diarrhoea, and dementia; untreated, it leads to a fourth D, death. The photosensitive skin rash often forms a distinctive ring around the neck known as Casal's necklace, after Gaspar Casal, who described the disease in Spain in 1735.

History

Pellagra became epidemic in the American South in the early 20th century, where poor tenant farmers subsisted on a diet of cornmeal, molasses and fatty pork; between 1906 and 1940 it affected an estimated three million Americans and caused around 100,000 deaths. US Public Health Service physician Joseph Goldberger demonstrated through dietary studies and controversial self-experiments that pellagra was nutritional, not infectious. In 1937 Conrad Elvehjem identified nicotinic acid as the curative factor. Enrichment of flour with niacin largely eliminated the disease in developed countries.

Causes and occurrence today

Maize contains niacin in a bound, unavailable form unless treated with alkali (nixtamalization), a traditional Mesoamerican practice that protected populations there. Today pellagra is rare but occurs with alcoholism, malabsorption, certain drugs, and in refugee and famine settings.
