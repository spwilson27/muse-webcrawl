Chametz

Chametz (Hebrew: חָמֵץ, "leaven") are foods with leavening agents that are forbidden on the Jewish holiday of Passover. According to halakha, Jews may not own, eat or benefit from chametz during Passover. This law appears several times in the Torah; the punishment for eating chametz on Passover is the exceptionally severe divine punishment of kareth (excision). Chametz is traditionally removed from the home in a thorough spring cleaning before the festival, and any that remains is ritually annulled, burned, or sold to a non-Jew (mechirat chametz).

Definition

Chametz is any food product made from wheat, barley, rye, oats or spelt — the "five grains" — that has come into contact with water and been allowed to ferment and rise. In practice this includes bread, pasta, most cereals, cookies, beer and most whisky, among many other products. By contrast, matzah, the unleavened bread eaten on Passover, is made from the same grains but is baked within eighteen minutes of the flour meeting water, before fermentation can begin. Ashkenazi custom additionally avoids kitniyot — rice, corn, legumes and similar foods — during Passover, though these are not chametz proper.

Removal before Passover

In the days before Passover, observant households clean thoroughly to remove all traces of chametz. On the night before the festival, a formal search by candlelight (bedikat chametz) is conducted, traditionally with a feather and wooden spoon, for ten pieces of bread deliberately hidden around the home. The following morning any remaining chametz is burned (biur chametz) and a declaration (kol chamira) annuls any chametz that may have been overlooked. Valuable chametz — such as liquor stocks or shop inventories — is often formally sold to a non-Jew for the duration of the holiday and bought back afterwards, a practice known as mechirat chametz; in Israel, the Chief Rabbinate sells the chametz of the state as a whole in a widely publicised ceremony.

After Passover

Chametz that was owned by a Jew during Passover in violation of the prohibition (chametz she'avar alav haPesach) is forbidden for consumption even after the holiday ends, by rabbinic penalty. For this reason many consumers are careful to buy post-Passover chametz only from stores whose stock was properly sold.
