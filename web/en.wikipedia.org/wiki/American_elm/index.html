The American elm (Ulmus americana) is a species of elm native to eastern North America, occurring from Nova Scotia west to Alberta and Montana, and south to Florida and central Texas. The American elm is an extremely hardy tree that can withstand winter temperatures as low as −42 °C (−44 °F). It was once one of the most widely planted shade trees in North America, famed for its graceful, arching, vase-shaped canopy that lined streets in towns and cities.

Description

The American elm is a deciduous hermaphroditic tree which, before the arrival of Dutch elm disease, commonly grew to more than 30 m (100 ft) tall with a trunk up to 1.5 m (5 ft) in diameter. The leaves are alternate, 7–20 cm (3–8 in) long, with double-serrate margins and an oblique base. The small, inconspicuous flowers appear in early spring before the leaves, and the fruit is a flat samara about 2 cm long.

Dutch elm disease

The species has been devastated by Dutch elm disease, a fungal disease spread by bark beetles, which arrived in North America in the 1930s. Tens of millions of trees died, transforming streetscapes across the continent. The tree survives in the wild largely because it reaches reproductive age before succumbing, and isolated trees beyond the disease's reach still flourish.

Cultivars and recovery

Disease-tolerant cultivars such as 'Princeton', 'Valley Forge', 'New Harmony' and the Minnesota selection 'St. Croix' have been developed and are widely replanted. The cultivar 'Valley Forge' showed particularly high levels of tolerance in trials by the U.S. National Arboretum.

Notable trees

The Liberty Tree in Boston, under which the Sons of Liberty gathered, was an American elm. One of the largest surviving specimens is the "Herbie" elm formerly of Yarmouth, Maine, which stood for over 200 years.
