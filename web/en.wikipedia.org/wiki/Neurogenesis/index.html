Neurogenesis

Neural stem cell proliferation, migration, differentiation and maturation.

Neurogenesis is the process by which neurons are generated from neural stem cells and progenitor cells. It occurs most actively during embryonic development, when it builds essentially the entire nervous system, but it continues in restricted regions of the adult mammalian brain — a finding that overturned the long-standing dogma that no new neurons are born in adulthood.

Developmental neurogenesis

During embryogenesis, neuroepithelial cells of the neural tube divide symmetrically to expand the progenitor pool, then asymmetrically to produce neurons in an inside-out sequence in the neocortex. Radial glial cells act both as progenitors and as scaffolds for neuronal migration. Neurogenesis is governed by morphogens and transcription-factor codes that pattern regional identity, and is followed by gliogenesis.

Adult neurogenesis

In adult mammals, robust neurogenesis is largely confined to two niches: the subventricular zone of the lateral ventricles, whose new neurons migrate to the olfactory bulb, and the subgranular zone of the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus. Adult hippocampal neurogenesis is implicated in learning, pattern separation and mood regulation; it is enhanced by exercise and enriched environments and reduced by stress and ageing. The extent of adult neurogenesis in the human hippocampus remains debated, with studies reporting both persistence into old age and sharp decline after childhood; carbon-14 birth-dating studies support substantial neuronal turnover in the adult human hippocampus.

Clinical relevance

Altered neurogenesis has been linked to depression, epilepsy and neurodegenerative disease, and harnessing endogenous stem cells is a goal of regenerative neurology.
