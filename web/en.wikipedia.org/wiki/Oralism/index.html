Oralism

Oralism is the education of deaf students through oral language by using lip reading, speech, and mimicking the mouth shapes and breathing patterns of speech. Oralism came into popular use in the United States around the late 1860s. In 1867, the Clarke School for the Deaf in Northampton, Massachusetts, was the first school to start teaching in this manner. Oralism and its contrast, manualism, have been debated heavily within deaf education for much of the twentieth century.

History

Origins

Beginning in the eighteenth century, educators such as Thomas Braidwood in Britain and Samuel Heinicke in Germany promoted speech-based instruction for deaf pupils. In the United States, Horace Mann and Samuel Gridley Howe advocated oral methods after visiting European schools in the 1840s. The Clarke School, founded in 1867, became the leading American institution of oral education.

Congress of Milan

In 1880, the Second International Congress on Education of the Deaf, held in Milan, Italy, passed resolutions declaring the "incontestable superiority of speech over signs" and effectively banned sign language from deaf classrooms in much of Europe and North America. Of the delegates, almost none were deaf. The resolutions led to the dismissal of many deaf teachers and a decline in deaf literacy and academic achievement that critics documented for decades afterward.

Twentieth century and decline

Alexander Graham Bell was a prominent advocate of oralism, promoting day schools and speech instruction and opposing the formation of a separate deaf culture. Research in the 1960s, notably William Stokoe's linguistic analysis of American Sign Language, established sign languages as complete natural languages. This, together with evidence that oral-only outcomes were often poor, drove a shift toward Total Communication in the 1970s and later toward bilingual-bicultural approaches combining sign language with written and spoken language.

Criticism

Critics within the Deaf community argue that oralism suppressed a natural language, isolated deaf children from deaf role models, and consumed instructional time with speech drills at the expense of academic content. Supporters counter that spoken-language proficiency improves access to employment and the hearing world. The debate continues in modern form in controversies over cochlear implants and mainstreaming.
