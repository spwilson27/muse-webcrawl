A femme fatale (French: [fam fatal], literally "fatal woman") is a stock character of a mysterious, beautiful, and seductive woman whose charms ensnare her lovers, often leading them into compromising, deadly traps. She is usually portrayed as having an ability to entrance and hypnotise her victim, who feels unable to resist her. Her archetype appears across mythology, literature, opera, and cinema, where she became a defining figure of film noir in the 1940s.

History

The figure has ancient roots. In mythology, Lilith, the Sirens, Circe, and Helen of Troy all lure men to ruin; the biblical Salome and Delilah betray their lovers to death. In the nineteenth century, the Romantics and Decadents revived the type: John Keats's "La Belle Dame sans Merci" (1819) depicts a knight seduced and abandoned by a fairy lady, while Richard Wagner's Kundry and Oscar Wilde's Salome embodied the dangerous woman for fin-de-siècle audiences.

Film noir

The femme fatale reached her canonical form in American film noir of the 1940s: Phyllis Dietrichson in Double Indemnity (1944), Gilda in Gilda (1946), and Kathie Moffat in Out of the Past (1947). These women were beautiful, intelligent, and duplicitous, manipulating insurance investigators and private detectives into murder and fraud. Critics have read the character as both a symptom of postwar anxiety about women's independence and a figure of genuine fascination and agency on screen.

Modern interpretations

The archetype persisted in neo-noir (e.g., Basic Instinct, 1992) and has been reclaimed by feminist criticism, which notes that the femme fatale is often the only character in her story who seeks freedom and acts on her own desires. Contemporary works frequently subvert the trope by centring her perspective.
