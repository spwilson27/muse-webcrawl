Fibre-optic gyroscope

A fibre-optic gyroscope (FOG) senses changes in orientation using the Sagnac effect, thus performing the function of a mechanical gyroscope. However its principle of operation is instead based on the interference of light which has passed through a coil of optical fibre, which can be as long as 5 kilometres (3 miles).

Principle of operation

Two beams from a laser are injected into the same fibre but in opposite directions. Due to the Sagnac effect, the beam travelling against the rotation experiences a slightly shorter path delay than the other beam. The resulting differential phase shift is measured through interferometry, thus translating one component of the angular velocity into a shift of the interference pattern. Splitting a beam into two counter-propagating parts and recombining them after traversal of a multi-turn coil greatly multiplies the accumulated phase difference.

Advantages

A FOG provides extremely precise rotational rate information; because it has no moving parts, it avoids friction, wear and sensitivity to acceleration that limit mechanical gyroscopes. It offers fast start-up, high reliability and long life. FOGs are used in inertial navigation systems for aircraft, spacecraft, ships, submarines and guided weapons, and in surveying and stabilization platforms.

History

The Sagnac effect was demonstrated by Georges Sagnac in 1913. Fibre-optic implementations became practical after the development of low-loss single-mode fibre; early demonstrations appeared in 1976, and commercial devices followed in the 1980s and 1990s, notably developed further in France and the United States. The related ring laser gyroscope works on the same interferometric principle.
