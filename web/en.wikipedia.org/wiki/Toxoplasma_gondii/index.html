Toxoplasma gondii is an obligate intracellular parasitic protozoan (an apicomplexan) that causes the disease toxoplasmosis. It is one of the most common parasites in the world: it can infect virtually all warm-blooded animals, and a large fraction of the human population — estimates range from about a third to over half in some countries — carries a lifelong latent infection. The parasite was first described in 1908 from the gundi, a North African rodent, from which it takes its species name.

Life cycle

The definitive hosts of T. gondii are felids (cats), in whose intestines the parasite reproduces sexually, producing oocysts that are shed in faeces and contaminate soil, water and vegetation. Intermediate hosts — including rodents, birds, livestock and humans — ingest oocysts or eat tissue cysts in undercooked meat. In the intermediate host the parasite spreads through the body as fast-dividing tachyzoites, then converts under immune pressure into slow-growing bradyzoites enclosed in tissue cysts, especially in muscle and brain, where it can persist for life. When a cat eats an infected rodent, the cycle completes.

Disease

In healthy people, toxoplasmosis is usually asymptomatic or causes mild flu-like symptoms, and the infection then becomes latent. It can be severe in immunocompromised patients, such as those with AIDS, in whom reactivation may cause life-threatening encephalitis. Infection during pregnancy can be transmitted to the fetus, causing congenital toxoplasmosis with possible miscarriage, hydrocephalus, eye damage and intellectual disability.

Behavioural effects

T. gondii is famous for its "manipulation hypothesis": infected rodents lose their innate aversion to cat urine and are even attracted to it, making them likelier to be eaten and completing the parasite's cycle. In humans, latent infection has been statistically associated in some studies with altered behaviour and increased risk of certain psychiatric conditions, though the significance of these correlations remains debated.

Prevention and treatment

Prevention includes cooking meat thoroughly, washing vegetables, and care in handling cat litter, especially during pregnancy. Treatment of active disease commonly uses combinations such as pyrimethamine and sulfadiazine.
