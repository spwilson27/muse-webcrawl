Khorasan

RegionNortheastern Iran, northern Afghanistan, southern Central Asia
Historical capitalsNishapur, Merv, Herat, Balkh, Tus
EtymologyMiddle Persian Xwarāsān, "where the sun arrives from" (the east)

Khorasan (Persian: خراسان), also called Greater Khorasan, is a historical region in the Iranian plateau of western and central Asia, comprising a vast area that today includes northeastern Iran, much of Afghanistan, and parts of Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, and Uzbekistan. The name comes from Middle Persian and means "(land) where the sun rises" — the east.

Geography

In its broadest medieval usage, Khorasan stretched from the Caspian Sea and the edges of the central Iranian desert in the west to the Hindu Kush and the Oxus (Amu Darya) river in the north and east. Its great oasis cities — Nishapur, Merv, Herat, Balkh, and Tus — sat astride the Silk Road routes linking Iran with Central Asia and China, and grew wealthy on trade, agriculture, and scholarship.

History

The region was a core territory of the Parthian Empire and a province of the Sasanian Empire, whose rulers administered it as one of the empire's four quarters. After the Muslim conquest of Persia in the 7th century, Khorasan became a frontier province of the caliphate and a gateway for the spread of Islam into Central Asia. It was from Khorasan that the Abbasid Revolution of 747–750, led by Abu Muslim, marched west to overthrow the Umayyads.

Under dynasties such as the Tahirids, Saffarids, and Samanids, Khorasan enjoyed considerable autonomy and a golden age of Persian Islamic culture: Nishapur, Merv, and Balkh were famed centers of learning, and the region produced figures such as the poet Ferdowsi, the polymath Omar Khayyam, the theologian al-Ghazali, and the physician-philosopher Avicenna (associated with Bukhara). The Mongol invasions of 1219–1221 devastated its cities, Herat and Nishapur among them, though the region later revived under the Timurids, when Herat became a brilliant court capital.

Modern usage

In modern Iran, the old province of Khorasan was for long the country's largest; in 2004 it was divided into three provinces: Razavi Khorasan (centered on Mashhad), North Khorasan, and South Khorasan. The name also survives in militant branding (e.g., "Islamic State – Khorasan Province") that deliberately invokes the historical region.
