A mulch is a layer of material applied to the surface of soil. Mulching is used in gardens, landscaping and agriculture to conserve soil moisture, suppress weeds, moderate soil temperature, reduce erosion and, as organic mulches decompose, improve soil fertility and structure.

Functions

By shading the soil surface, mulch slows evaporation and keeps moisture available to plant roots; it also blocks light from weed seeds, insulates roots against temperature extremes, and prevents rain from splashing soil (and soil-borne fungal spores) onto foliage. Organic mulches feed soil organisms as they break down. Studies in agriculture also show mulch can reduce erosion on slopes and, in plasticulture, warm the soil to advance early-season crops.

Materials

Organic mulches include shredded bark and wood chips (long-lasting, common in ornamental beds), straw and hay (vegetable gardens), leaf mould and compost, grass clippings, and living mulches such as low cover crops. Fresh wood materials can temporarily draw nitrogen from the surface soil as they decay, which matters mainly where they are dug in rather than laid on top. Inorganic mulches include black and coloured plastic films used commercially for vegetables, woven landscape fabric, gravel and stone, and recycled rubber. Plastic mulches warm soil and block weeds effectively but must be removed and disposed of after use.

Application

Mulch is typically spread 5–10 cm deep and kept a few centimetres away from trunks and stems, since piling it against bark ("mulch volcanoes" around trees) traps moisture and invites rot and pests. In colder climates mulch is applied after the ground cools in autumn to stabilise temperatures over winter, and in spring to conserve moisture through the growing season.
