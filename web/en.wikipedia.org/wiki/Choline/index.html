Choline

Choline
IUPAC: 2-hydroxy-N,N,N-trimethylethanaminium
Formula: C5H14NO+
Role: Essential nutrient; precursor to acetylcholine and phosphatidylcholine

Choline is an essential nutrient for humans and many animals. It is a water-soluble compound, usually grouped with the B vitamins though it is not formally one. The body can synthesise small amounts in the liver, but dietary intake is required to meet needs.

Functions

Choline is a precursor of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine, of membrane phospholipids such as phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin, and, after oxidation to betaine, a methyl donor in one-carbon metabolism alongside folate. Deficiency can cause fatty liver and muscle damage; adequate intake matters especially in pregnancy for fetal brain development.

Sources

Rich sources include eggs, liver, meat, fish, soybeans and cruciferous vegetables. Recommended adequate intakes are about 425 mg/day for adult women and 550 mg/day for adult men in the United States.

History

Choline was isolated from bile (Greek cholē) by Adolph Strecker in 1862 and chemically characterised soon after. Its essentiality for humans was formally recognised by the Institute of Medicine in 1998.
