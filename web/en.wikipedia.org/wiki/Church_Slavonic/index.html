Church Slavonic

Church Slavonic, also known as Church Slavic, is the conservative Slavic liturgical language used by the Orthodox Church in several Slavic countries, including Bulgaria, Russia, Serbia, Ukraine, and others, as well as by some Eastern Catholic churches. It is the direct descendant of Old Church Slavonic, the first literary Slavic language, created in the ninth century by the missionaries Saints Cyril and Methodius.

History

Church Slavonic developed from Old Church Slavonic, the language into which Cyril and Methodius translated the Bible and other liturgical texts for their mission to Great Moravia in 863 AD. As the language spread among the Slavs, it absorbed features of local vernaculars, giving rise to regional recensions (redactions), such as the Russian, Bulgarian, and Serbian recensions. The Russian recension, standardized in the Synodal edition of the Bible (the Elizabeth Bible of 1751), became dominant in the Russian Orthodox Church.

Characteristics

Church Slavonic preserves archaic features of Slavic grammar, including dual number, a rich system of participles, and the aorist and imperfect past tenses that have disappeared from most modern Slavic languages. It is written in a special form of Cyrillic with additional letters and diacritics (titlas) used for sacred abbreviations (nomina sacra). Although no longer anyone's native language, it remains the principal liturgical language of Slavic Orthodoxy, much as Latin long served the Roman Catholic Church.

Recensions

The Russian recension is used in Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, and in the Orthodox Church in America. The Serbian recension survives mainly in older printed books. The Bulgarian (Middle Bulgarian) recension is preserved in some manuscripts. In Croatia, a Glagolitic tradition of Church Slavonic was used in Roman Catholic liturgy along the Dalmatian coast for centuries.
