Plasmodium (life cycle)

The life cycle of Plasmodium alternates between a vertebrate host and a female Anopheles mosquito.

The life cycle of the malaria parasite Plasmodium is complex, involving two hosts — a vertebrate (such as a human) and a female Anopheles mosquito — and alternating sexual and asexual reproduction. Five species commonly infect humans: P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale, P. malariae and P. knowlesi. Understanding the cycle underlies malaria diagnosis, treatment and prevention.

In the human host

Infection begins when an infected mosquito takes a blood meal and injects sporozoites with its saliva. Sporozoites travel through the bloodstream to the liver, where they invade hepatocytes and multiply asexually (exo-erythrocytic schizogony), producing thousands of merozoites. In P. vivax and P. ovale, some parasites form dormant hypnozoites that can reactivate months or years later, causing relapses.

Released merozoites invade red blood cells and undergo repeated asexual cycles (erythrocytic schizogony): ring stage, trophozoite, then schizont, which ruptures to release more merozoites. The synchronised rupture of red cells produces the characteristic periodic fevers of malaria. Some parasites differentiate into sexual stages — male and female gametocytes — which circulate in the blood awaiting uptake by another mosquito.

In the mosquito

When a mosquito ingests gametocytes, they emerge as gametes in the midgut and fuse to form a zygote, which develops into a motile ookinete. The ookinete crosses the gut wall and forms an oocyst, within which sporozoites develop (sporogony). The oocyst ruptures, releasing sporozoites that migrate to the salivary glands, ready to infect the next vertebrate host. This extrinsic incubation typically takes 10–18 days depending on species and temperature.
