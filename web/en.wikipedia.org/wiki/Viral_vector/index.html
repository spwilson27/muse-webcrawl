Viral vector

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

UsesGene therapy, vaccines, molecular biology research, oncolytic therapy
Common vectorsAdenovirus, adeno-associated virus (AAV), lentivirus, retrovirus, herpesvirus, VSV

Viral vectors are tools commonly used by molecular biologists to deliver genetic material into cells. This process can be performed inside a living organism (in vivo) or in cell culture (in vitro). Viruses have evolved specialized molecular mechanisms to efficiently transport their genomes inside the cells they infect. Delivery of genes by a virus is termed transduction, and the infected cells are described as transduced. Molecular biologists first harnessed this machinery in the 1970s, beginning with work by Paul Berg and others using SV40 and lambda phage.

Key properties

An effective viral vector is designed to be safe (with virulence genes removed and replication disabled or controlled), specific to its target cells (tropism, sometimes engineered by pseudotyping), stable in expressing the delivered gene at useful levels, and identifiable, often via a selectable marker. Vectors differ in cargo capacity, whether they integrate into the host genome, duration of expression, and immunogenicity.

Types

Retroviral vectors integrate into the host genome and give long-term expression, but mainly transduce dividing cells and carry some insertional mutagenesis risk. Lentiviral vectors, derived from HIV, can transduce non-dividing cells and are widely used in research and in CAR-T cell manufacture. Adenoviral vectors have large cargo capacity and high expression but are strongly immunogenic and transient; they are used in several vaccines. Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors are minimally pathogenic, largely non-integrating, and provide long-lasting expression, and are the basis of approved gene therapies such as those for spinal muscular atrophy and inherited retinal dystrophy. Herpes simplex virus vectors offer very large capacity and neuronal tropism, including oncolytic applications.

Applications

Viral vectors are central to gene therapy—delivering functional genes to treat genetic disease—and to modern vaccinology: replication-deficient adenovirus and other vectored vaccines deliver antigen genes, an approach used in Ebola vaccines and in several COVID-19 vaccines (for example, the Oxford–AstraZeneca and Janssen vaccines, which use adenovirus vectors). In basic research, viral vectors express or knock down genes in cells and animals, deliver CRISPR components, and trace neural circuits. Oncolytic viruses are engineered vectors that selectively replicate in and destroy tumor cells while stimulating anti-tumor immunity.

Safety

Safety concerns include insertional mutagenesis (implicated in leukemia cases in early retroviral gene therapy trials for X-SCID), immune responses to vector capsids that can limit re-dosing, shedding, and the generation of replication-competent virus during manufacturing. Modern vector design, screening, and regulation address these risks, and ex vivo approaches—modifying cells outside the body before returning them—avoid many in vivo hazards.
