Molten salt reactor

A molten salt reactor (MSR) is a class of nuclear fission reactor in which the primary coolant, and often the fuel itself, is a mixture of hot molten fluoride or chloride salts. MSRs operate at high temperature (typically 600–700 °C) but near atmospheric pressure, in contrast to pressurized water reactors.

History

The concept was pioneered at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in the 1950s under Alvin Weinberg, beginning with the Aircraft Reactor Experiment (1954) and culminating in the Molten-Salt Reactor Experiment (MSRE), which operated from 1965 to 1969 and demonstrated uranium-233 bred from thorium. The technology was shelved in the 1970s in favor of other reactor programs.

Advantages and challenges

Proposed advantages include strong negative temperature feedback, passive drain-down safety via a freeze plug, efficient use of thorium fuel, and high thermodynamic efficiency. Challenges include corrosion of structural alloys by hot salts, handling of radioactive salt and fission products, tritium management, and the chemistry needed to process fuel online.
