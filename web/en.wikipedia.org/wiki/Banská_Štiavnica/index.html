Banská Štiavnica (German: Schemnitz; Hungarian: Selmecbánya) is a historic town in central Slovakia, set in the caldera of an extinct volcano in the Štiavnica Mountains. It was for centuries one of the most important mining towns in Europe, famed for its gold and silver, and its remarkably preserved medieval and Renaissance centre was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1993.

Mining history

Mining in the district is documented from the Middle Ages, and the town received royal free-town privileges in the thirteenth century. German-speaking miners and merchants made Schemnitz a leading producer of silver — in the eighteenth century it was among the largest sources of precious metals in the Habsburg Monarchy. Mining technology pioneered here included water-powered machinery fed by a celebrated system of reservoirs (tajchy) and the first use of gunpowder in mining (1627). In 1762 Maria Theresa founded the Mining Academy at Schemnitz, often cited as the world's first technical university-level institution, which drew students from across Europe.

The town today

The historic core preserves Gothic and Renaissance burgher houses around Trinity Square, the Old Castle (converted from a church), the New Castle, and the Kammerhof, seat of the mining administration. The Slovak Mining Museum and an open-air mining museum in the former shafts keep the industry's memory, while the Academy's forestry and mining successor institutions maintain academic ties in the town. Banská Štiavnica is also known for its Calvary, an eighteenth-century pilgrimage complex on Scharffenberg hill.
