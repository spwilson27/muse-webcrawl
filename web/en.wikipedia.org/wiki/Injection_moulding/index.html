Injection moulding

Injection moulding (U.S. spelling: injection molding) is a manufacturing process for producing parts by injecting molten material into a mould (or mold). Injection moulding can be performed with a host of materials mainly including metals (for which the process is called die-casting), glasses, elastomers, confections, and most commonly thermoplastic and thermosetting polymers.

Process

Material for the part is fed into a heated barrel, mixed (using a helical screw), and injected into a mould cavity, where it cools and hardens to the configuration of the cavity. After a product is designed, usually by an industrial designer or an engineer, moulds are made by a mould-maker (or toolmaker) from metal, usually either steel or aluminium, and precision-machined to form the features of the desired part.

The process cycle consists of four main stages: clamping, in which the two halves of the mould are closed and clamped; injection, in which molten plastic is forced into the cavity; cooling, during which the part solidifies; and ejection, in which the finished part is pushed from the mould by ejector pins.

Applications

Injection moulding is widely used for manufacturing a variety of parts, from the smallest components to entire body panels of cars. Typical products include bottle caps, plastic housings for electronics, automotive dashboards, syringes, toys such as LEGO bricks, and containers. The process is economical at high production volumes because the cost of the mould is spread across many parts, and cycle times can be as short as a few seconds.

History

American inventor John Wesley Hyatt patented the first injection moulding machine in 1872, using it to mould celluloid products such as billiard balls. In 1946 James Watson Hendry built the first screw injection moulding machine, which allowed much more precise control of injection speed and quality. The great majority of modern machines still use a reciprocating screw design.

Defects

Common moulding defects include sink marks, weld lines, short shots, flash, warping and burn marks. Most defects can be mitigated by adjusting mould design, melt temperature, injection pressure and cooling rates.
