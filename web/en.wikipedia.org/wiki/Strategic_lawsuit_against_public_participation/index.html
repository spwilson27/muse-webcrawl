A strategic lawsuit against public participation (SLAPP) is a lawsuit intended to censor, intimidate, and silence critics by burdening them with the cost of a legal defence until they abandon their criticism or opposition. The term was coined by professors George W. Pring and Penelope Canan of the University of Denver in the 1980s.

Characteristics

A typical SLAPP is a civil claim — often for defamation, but also for business torts such as interference with contract — brought by a corporation, developer, or public figure against individuals or community groups who have spoken out on a matter of public concern. The plaintiff's goal is typically not to win on the merits but to impose litigation costs and a chilling effect on speech. SLAPPs commonly arise in disputes over environmental development, consumer criticism, and investigative journalism.

Anti-SLAPP legislation

Many US states have enacted anti-SLAPP statutes providing an expedited motion to strike or dismiss, a stay of discovery, and recovery of attorney's fees for successful defendants. California's statute (Code of Civil Procedure §425.16), enacted in 1992, is among the most widely used. The European Union adopted an anti-SLAPP directive in 2024 to protect journalists and civil society in cross-border cases, and the United Kingdom introduced provisions targeting SLAPPs in economic-crime legislation following high-profile "libel tourism" cases in London.
