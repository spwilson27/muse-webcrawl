Muhammad Ahmad ibn Abd Allah (1844–1885) was a Sudanese religious leader of the Samaniyya Sufi order who in 1881 proclaimed himself the Mahdi — the messianic redeemer expected in Islamic eschatology — and launched a revolt against the Turco-Egyptian administration of Sudan. His movement, followed by devotees known as the Ansar, united the peoples of Sudan in a jihad that within four years destroyed Egyptian rule in the country.

The Mahdist Revolt

Born into a family of boat-builders on the Nile near Dongola, Muhammad Ahmad studied religion at Khartoum and Berber and became known for ascetic piety. On 29 June 1881 he announced his mission as Mahdi. When Egyptian forces moved against him on Aba Island that August, his followers routed them, and the revolt spread. Mahdist armies annihilated an Egyptian expedition under Hicks Pasha at Shaykan in November 1883 and captured El Obeid; in January 1885 they stormed Khartoum, where General Charles Gordon was killed — an event that shook the British government of Gladstone.

The Mahdist State and death

Muhammad Ahmad established a theocratic state ruled from Omdurman, opposite Khartoum, organised on his reading of early Islamic precedent: he appointed caliphs after the model of the Rashidun, abolished the Sufi orders in favour of loyalty to himself, and enforced strict Islamic law. He died suddenly — probably of typhus — on 22 June 1885, barely six months after the fall of Khartoum, and was succeeded by his chief caliph, Abdallahi ibn Muhammad, who ruled until the Anglo-Egyptian reconquest destroyed the Mahdist state at the Battle of Omdurman in 1898.

In modern Sudan the Mahdi is remembered as a national hero and founder of Sudanese statehood; his descendants, and the Ansar movement associated with the Umma Party, remain influential in Sudanese religious and political life.
