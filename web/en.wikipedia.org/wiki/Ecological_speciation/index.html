Divergent natural selection between environments drives ecological speciation.

Ecological speciation

Ecological speciation is the process by which new species arise as a by-product of divergent natural selection between contrasting environments. When populations adapt to different habitats, resources, predators or mating environments, the traits under divergent selection — or traits genetically linked to them — reduce gene flow between the populations, producing reproductive isolation.

Mechanisms

Reproductive isolation can evolve through several routes: habitat or temporal isolation, when organisms prefer the environments to which they are adapted; immigrant inviability, when migrants between environments have low fitness; sexual isolation, when divergent traits such as body size, colour pattern or host preference influence mate choice; and extrinsic postzygotic isolation, when hybrids are poorly suited to either parental environment. A "magic trait" under divergent selection that also affects mating — such as body size in fish feeding on different prey — makes ecological speciation especially likely.

Examples

Well-studied cases include threespine sticklebacks that repeatedly formed benthic and limnetic species pairs in postglacial lakes; the apple maggot fly Rhagoletis pomonella, which shifted from hawthorn to introduced apple trees in North America; Timema stick insects on different host plants; and crater-lake cichlid fishes. Ecological speciation can occur with gene flow (in sympatry or parapatry) as well as in allopatry, provided selection, rather than drift alone, drives divergence.
