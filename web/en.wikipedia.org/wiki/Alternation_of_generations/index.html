Alternation of generations

Key stages
SporophyteDiploid (2n), produces spores by meiosis
GametophyteHaploid (n), produces gametes by mitosis
Found inAll land plants; some algae and fungi

Alternation of generations (also known as metagenesis or heterogenesis) is the predominant type of life cycle in plants and algae. In plants both phases are multicellular: the haploid sexual phase – the gametophyte – alternates with a diploid asexual phase – the sporophyte.

A mature sporophyte produces haploid spores by meiosis, a process which reduces the number of chromosomes to half, from two sets to one. The resulting haploid spores germinate and grow into a mature gametophyte, which produces gametes (sperm and eggs) by mitosis. Fertilization, the union of two gametes, restores the diploid number and gives rise to a new sporophyte, completing the cycle.

In plants

In bryophytes (mosses, liverworts and hornworts) the gametophyte is the dominant, free-living generation, and the sporophyte grows upon it and is nutritionally dependent on it. In ferns and other vascular plants the sporophyte is dominant and independent, while the gametophyte is reduced — in seed plants to the microscopic pollen grain and the embryo sac within the ovule.

In algae

Many algae show alternation of generations. In some, such as the brown alga Laminaria, the two generations differ greatly in size (heteromorphic); in others, such as Ulva, they are identical in appearance (isomorphic).

Evolutionary significance

The alternation of generations is a defining feature of the land plants (embryophytes). Botanists debate whether the sporophyte generation arose by delaying meiosis in a zygote that first underwent mitotic divisions (the antithetic theory) or by transformation of an originally similar generation (the homologous theory).
