Supersonic speed

RegimesSubsonic, transonic, supersonic, hypersonic

Supersonic speed is the speed of an object that exceeds the speed of sound (Mach 1). For objects travelling in dry air at a temperature of 20 °C at sea level, this speed is approximately 343 m/s, 1,235 km/h or 767 mph. Speeds greater than five times the speed of sound (Mach 5) are usually termed hypersonic. An object travelling at supersonic speed in air leaves a sonic boom in its wake and experiences sharply increased drag near the sound barrier.

Mach number and flow regimes

The Mach number — named for physicist Ernst Mach — is the ratio of an object's speed to the local speed of sound, which varies with temperature and altitude: at high altitude, where air is cold, Mach 1 corresponds to a lower true airspeed than at sea level. Aerodynamicists divide flight into regimes: subsonic (below ~Mach 0.8), transonic (Mach 0.8–1.2, where mixed subsonic and supersonic flow occurs over the aircraft), supersonic (Mach 1–5) and hypersonic (above Mach 5).

Physical effects

Above Mach 1, air cannot "get out of the way" of an approaching object by pressure signals, so shock waves form — thin regions of abrupt pressure, temperature and density change, visible in the conical wake behind a supersonic body. The continuous shock trailing a supersonic aircraft produces the sonic boom heard on the ground. Other effects include wave drag, aerodynamic heating of surfaces, condensation ("vapour cones") in humid transonic flow, and the cracking of a whip — whose tip is an everyday object that briefly exceeds the speed of sound. Rifle bullets and many artillery shells are also supersonic.

Breaking the sound barrier

The "sound barrier" was once feared to be a physical limit to flight. Chuck Yeager first exceeded Mach 1 in controlled level flight in the rocket-powered Bell X-1 on 14 October 1947. Supersonic flight is now routine for military aircraft, achieved commercially by Concorde and the Tu-144, and on land by the Thrust SSC car, which set a supersonic land speed record in 1997.
