Pickering Treaty

The Treaty of Canandaigua was negotiated in 1794 between the United States and the Six Nations of the Iroquois.

The Pickering Treaty, formally the Treaty of Canandaigua, is a treaty signed after the American Revolutionary War between the Grand Council of the Six Nations of the Iroquois (Haudenosaunee) Confederacy and President George Washington's administration, represented by the treaty commissioner Timothy Pickering, then Postmaster General. It was signed at Canandaigua, New York on 11 November 1794.

Terms

The treaty established peace and friendship between the United States and the Six Nations (the Seneca, Cayuga, Onondaga, Oneida, Mohawk and Tuscarora), acknowledged the lands reserved to the Oneida, Onondaga and Cayuga Nations, and defined the western boundary of Seneca territory. It promised that the United States would not claim or disturb the Seneca in the free use and enjoyment of their lands, and provided for annual payments of goods — the "treaty cloth" annuity — to the Six Nations.

Legacy

The treaty is still considered in force by the Haudenosaunee. The United States continues to deliver the annual payment of treaty cloth, and the anniversary of the signing is commemorated each year in Canandaigua. The treaty document is held by the New York State Archives. A wampum belt, the George Washington Covenant Belt, was created to record the agreement from the Indigenous side. The treaty's guarantees have been invoked in later land-claim litigation by Iroquois nations.
