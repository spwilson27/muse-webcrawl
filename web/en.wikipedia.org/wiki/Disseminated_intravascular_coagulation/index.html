Disseminated intravascular coagulation

Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is a serious, potentially life-threatening condition in which abnormal blood clotting occurs throughout the body's blood vessels. Widespread activation of the coagulation system produces numerous small clots (microthrombi) that can damage organs, while simultaneous consumption of platelets and clotting factors leads to severe bleeding. DIC is not a disease in itself but a complication of an underlying disorder.

Causes

DIC can be triggered by a wide range of conditions, including:

  • Sepsis and severe infection (the most common cause)
  • Major trauma and severe tissue injury
  • Cancer, particularly acute promyelocytic leukaemia and metastatic tumours
  • Obstetric emergencies such as placental abruption, amniotic fluid embolism, and pre-eclampsia
  • Severe transfusion reactions, snake envenomation, and extensive burns

Pathophysiology

An underlying disorder causes massive release of tissue factor and other procoagulant substances into the circulation. Thrombin is generated systemically, depositing fibrin in the microvasculature while platelets and coagulation factors are consumed faster than they can be replaced. Fibrinolysis may be simultaneously impaired, worsening organ ischaemia. The result is a paradoxical combination of thrombosis and haemorrhage.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on laboratory findings in the appropriate clinical setting: low platelet count, prolonged prothrombin time, elevated fibrin degradation products and D-dimer, and reduced fibrinogen. Scoring systems such as that of the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis help standardise diagnosis.

Treatment

The cornerstone of treatment is management of the underlying cause. Supportive care may include transfusion of platelets, fresh frozen plasma, and cryoprecipitate when bleeding is present, and anticoagulation with heparin in selected thrombotic presentations. Prognosis depends largely on the underlying condition.
