Geometric invariant theory

In mathematics, geometric invariant theory (GIT) is a method for constructing quotients by group actions in algebraic geometry, used to construct moduli spaces. It was developed by David Mumford in 1965, using ideas from the paper of Hilbert on classical invariant theory.

Background

Classical invariant theory studies polynomial functions invariant under the action of a linear algebraic group on a vector space. Geometric invariant theory extends this by selecting, via the notion of stability, which orbits should be retained in a quotient so that the resulting quotient is a well-behaved geometric object such as a projective variety.

Stability

Given an action of a reductive group on a projective variety with a chosen linearization, points are classified as stable, semistable or unstable. The GIT quotient parametrizes the closed orbits of semistable points. The Hilbert–Mumford criterion reduces the test for stability to checking one-parameter subgroups of the group.

Applications

GIT has been central to the construction of moduli spaces, including the moduli space of curves and moduli of vector bundles on curves. It also connects to symplectic geometry through the Kempf–Ness theorem, which relates GIT stability to the existence of zeros of a moment map.
