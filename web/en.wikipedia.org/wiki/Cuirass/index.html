Cuirass

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Placeholder image

A cuirass (French: cuirasse, from cuir, "leather") is a piece of armour consisting of breastplate and backplate fastened together, protecting the torso. Originally made of leather, cuirasses were later made of bronze, iron and steel, and were worn from antiquity through the early modern period.

History

Bronze cuirasses were used by Greek hoplites — the early "bell cuirass" flaring at the waist, later the "muscle cuirass" (thorax) sculpted to imitate an idealized torso, a form also adopted by Roman officers. Medieval plate armour gradually superseded the standalone cuirass, but the steel cuirass returned to prominence with the heavy cavalry of the 16th–19th centuries: cuirassiers, including Napoleon's famous regiments, wore polished breast- and backplates proofed against pistol balls and sword cuts. Cuirasses remained in ceremonial and battlefield use into the First World War — the French army entered the war in 1914 with cuirassier regiments still wearing them — and they persist today in the ceremonial dress of units such as the British Household Cavalry and the French Republican Guard.
