A saint is a person recognized as having an exceptional degree of holiness, likeness to God, or closeness to the divine. In Christianity the term (from Latin sanctus) is used in several ways: in the New Testament it refers to all believers, while in the Catholic, Orthodox and other traditions it designates in particular those deceased Christians acknowledged by the Church as being in heaven and worthy of veneration. Saints are honoured with feast days, churches and patronages, and the faithful ask for their intercession in prayer.

Historical development

Veneration began with the martyrs of the early church, whose tombs and anniversaries of death were honoured locally. From the fourth century, confessors, bishops, monks and virgins joined the ranks, and recognition rested on popular acclamation and episcopal approval. The first recorded papal canonization was that of St Ulrich of Augsburg by John XV in 993; by the twelfth century the papacy reserved canonization to itself. The process today — cause, investigation of virtues, beatification, and canonization upon verified miracles — was formalized over centuries and streamlined by John Paul II in 1983.

In different traditions

Eastern Orthodoxy speaks of "glorification" and recognizes well over a thousand saints, many of them "new martyrs" of the Soviet era. Anglican and Lutheran churches retain calendars of saints as exemplars of faith, without the doctrine of invocation. Most other Protestant traditions, following the Reformation, use "saint" only in the biblical sense of all Christians. Analogous holy persons exist in other religions, including Hindu sants, Buddhist arhats and bodhisattvas, and Sufi awliya.

Cult and culture

Saints function as patrons of places, professions and causes — St George of England and soldiers, St Nicholas of children and sailors, St Anthony of lost things. Their relics, images and feast days structured the pre-modern calendar, and thousands of towns, churches and institutions bear their names.
