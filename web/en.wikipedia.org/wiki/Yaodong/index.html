A yaodong (Chinese: 窰洞; pinyin: yáodòng; Wade–Giles: yao-tung) is a particular form of earth-sheltered dwelling common on the Loess Plateau in northern China. They are generally carved out of a hillside or excavated horizontally from a central courtyard; the earth that surrounds the indoor space provides insulation throughout the year.

Types

Three main types are distinguished. Cliffside yaodong (靠崖窯) are dug into the face of a loess cliff or gully wall, often in tiers, with arched entrances faced in brick or stone. Sunken yaodong (地坑窯) are built around a rectangular pit excavated in flat ground — a sunken courtyard — with rooms tunnelled into its four walls; seen from above, the village is nearly invisible. Freestanding yaodong (獨立窯) are vaulted structures built above ground of rammed earth, adobe, or brick, imitating the cave form with an arched roof covered in soil.

Construction and climate

Loess is soft enough to excavate yet, when compacted, strong enough to stand in vertical faces, making it an ideal material. The typical room is about 3–4 metres wide and 5–10 metres deep, with a barrel-vaulted ceiling. Thick earthen walls keep interiors cool in summer and, heated by a kang stove-bed, warm in winter. A chimney through the roof vents smoke from cooking and heating. Windows and doors are usually placed in a wooden or brick façade filling the arch opening.

History and present use

Cave dwelling on the plateau is ancient, and yaodong housed communities for centuries; in the mid-20th century an estimated 40 million people in northern China lived in such homes. Yan'an in northern Shaanxi, where yaodong sheltered the leadership of the Chinese Communist Party in the 1930s and 1940s, is especially associated with them. Earthquakes are the chief hazard: the 1556 Shaanxi earthquake killed huge numbers, many when loess cave dwellings collapsed.

Today many yaodong have been abandoned for modern housing, but others remain inhabited and valued for their comfort and low energy needs, and some have been renovated as hotels and tourist attractions. Architects have cited the yaodong as a model of sustainable, vernacular design.
