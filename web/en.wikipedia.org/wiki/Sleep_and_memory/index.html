Sleep and memory

Sleep and memory
FieldNeuroscience, psychology
Key processMemory consolidation during sleep
Sleep stages involvedSlow-wave sleep (NREM) and REM sleep

The relationship between sleep and memory is a major topic in neuroscience. Research indicates that sleep — especially slow-wave (deep NREM) sleep — plays a crucial role in memory consolidation, the process by which newly encoded memories are stabilised and integrated into long-term storage. People who sleep after learning generally retain more than those who stay awake for an equivalent period, an effect demonstrated as early as Jenkins and Dallenbach's 1924 experiments.

Mechanisms

During slow-wave sleep, hippocampal place-cell sequences from waking experience are "replayed" in coordination with thalamocortical sleep spindles and cortical slow oscillations, a dialogue believed to transfer memories from the hippocampus to neocortical long-term stores (the "active systems consolidation" model). REM sleep is implicated in consolidating procedural and emotional memories and in creative integration of information.

Types of memory

Declarative memory (facts and events) is most strongly associated with slow-wave sleep, whereas procedural skills such as motor sequences benefit from both late-night REM-rich sleep and stage-2 spindles. Sleep deprivation impairs encoding the following day as well as consolidation of what was previously learned.

Applications

Findings support the value of full nights of sleep before and after study, and of naps for learning. Targeted memory reactivation — replaying odours or sounds associated with learning during sleep — can selectively strengthen specific memories in laboratory settings.
