The Battle of Crécy took place on 26 August 1346 in northern France between a French army commanded by King Philip VI and an English army led by King Edward III. The battle, part of the Hundred Years' War, was a landmark in the history of warfare: the combined use of the longbow and dismounted men-at-arms destroyed the flower of French chivalry and signalled a shift in the balance between cavalry and infantry on the medieval battlefield.

Background

Edward III landed in Normandy in July 1346 with an army of roughly 15,000, marched on Paris, then retreated north toward Flanders, pursued by Philip VI's much larger host. Near the village of Crécy-en-Ponthieu, Edward chose a strong defensive position on a ridge, dismounted his men-at-arms, and posted thousands of longbowmen on the flanks, with the young Edward, Prince of Wales — the "Black Prince" — commanding the vanguard division.

The battle

The French arrived piecemeal late in the afternoon and attacked at once, against advice to wait until morning. Genoese crossbowmen advanced first but were outranged and driven back by the longbowmen — English archers could loose perhaps ten arrows a minute against the crossbow's one or two — and were then ridden down by their own impatient knights. Wave after wave of French cavalry charged uphill into storms of arrows and the prepared English line; fighting continued into the night. King John of Bohemia, fighting blind for France, was killed with his retinue; his crest of three ostrich feathers and motto Ich dien were adopted, by tradition, by the Prince of Wales. Philip VI was wounded and fled. French losses numbered in the thousands, including an extraordinary toll of counts and great lords; English losses were likely only a few hundred.

Aftermath

Edward marched on to besiege Calais, which fell in 1347 and remained an English bridgehead in France for two centuries. Crécy, followed a decade later by the similar victory at Poitiers, established the tactical system — dismounted defence spearheaded by massed archery — that underpinned English arms for the rest of the war.
