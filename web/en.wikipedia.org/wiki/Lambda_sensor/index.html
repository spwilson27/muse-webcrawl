Lambda sensor

An oxygen sensor (or lambda sensor, where lambda denotes the air–fuel equivalence ratio) is an electronic device that measures the proportion of oxygen (O2) in the gas or liquid being analysed. In automotive applications it is fitted in the exhaust system of an internal combustion engine, where it allows the engine management system to keep the air–fuel mixture at the stoichiometric ratio needed for the catalytic converter to work efficiently.

Principle

The most common type uses a zirconia (zirconium dioxide) ceramic element that generates a voltage dependent on the difference in oxygen concentration between the exhaust gas and outside air, acting as a Nernst cell. At the stoichiometric point (λ = 1, an air–fuel ratio of about 14.7:1 for petrol) the output switches sharply between roughly 0.1 V (lean) and 0.9 V (rich). Because the sensor only works when hot, modern sensors include an electric heater and reach operating temperature within tens of seconds of starting.

Use in engine management

The engine control unit uses the upstream sensor's signal in a closed loop, rapidly trimming fuel injection so the mixture oscillates slightly around stoichiometric, which allows the three-way catalytic converter to simultaneously oxidise hydrocarbons and carbon monoxide and reduce nitrogen oxides. A second, downstream sensor monitors converter efficiency for on-board diagnostics. Wideband sensors, using an additional pumped cell, can measure λ accurately over a broad range and are used in lean-burn and diesel engines and in motorsport data logging.

Other applications

Similar zirconia and electrochemical oxygen sensors are used in diving rebreathers to monitor the partial pressure of oxygen, in industrial furnaces and boilers, and in medical and laboratory analysers.
