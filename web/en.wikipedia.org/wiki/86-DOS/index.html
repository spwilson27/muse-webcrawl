86-DOS (known internally as QDOS, for "Quick and Dirty Operating System") is a discontinued operating system developed and marketed by Seattle Computer Products (SCP) for its Intel 8086-based computer kit. Written principally by Tim Paterson beginning in April 1980, 86-DOS was designed to resemble Digital Research's CP/M operating system in its application programming interface, so that existing CP/M software could be ported easily, while taking advantage of the 16-bit 8086 processor.

History

Seattle Computer Products sold a gas-plasma terminal and then an 8086 CPU board for the S-100 bus, but no operating system was available for the chip because Digital Research's CP/M-86 was delayed. Paterson therefore wrote his own system in about two months of work spread over 1980, consulting the CP/M documentation to make the API compatible at the system-call level, though the internal structure and file system were his own — notably, 86-DOS used the FAT12 file system rather than CP/M's directory scheme.

In late 1980, Microsoft, which was supplying IBM with languages for its forthcoming personal computer, needed an 8086 operating system. Microsoft first licensed 86-DOS in December 1980 and then, in July 1981, purchased all rights to it for a reported total of $75,000. Microsoft hired Paterson, adapted the system as MS-DOS, and licensed it to IBM as PC DOS for the IBM PC launched in August 1981. SCP retained rights to sell the system with its own hardware, and later litigation between SCP and Microsoft over the value of those rights was settled out of court.

Versions

Early releases were numbered in the 0.x range under the QDOS name; the system was renamed 86-DOS around version 0.2 in late 1980. Version 1.0 followed in early 1981, adding support for floppy-disk formats used by SCP's hardware. The lineage continued in Microsoft's hands as MS-DOS 1.x and beyond, making 86-DOS the ancestor of the DOS family that dominated personal computing through the 1980s and early 1990s.
