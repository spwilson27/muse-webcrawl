Greco-Buddhist art

Greco-Buddhist art is the artistic expression of Greco-Buddhism, the cultural syncretism between Hellenistic Greek culture and Buddhism that developed in Central and South Asia after the conquests of Alexander the Great. Its most famous product is the art of Gandhara, in what is now northwestern Pakistan and eastern Afghanistan, which flourished roughly from the 1st century BCE to the 5th century CE.

Characteristics

Greco-Buddhist sculpture fused Indian religious subjects with Greek artistic techniques: naturalistic anatomy, wavy hair, heavy toga-like drapery folds, and contrapposto poses. Gandharan workshops produced some of the earliest anthropomorphic images of the Buddha, who in earlier Indian art had been represented only by symbols such as the wheel, footprints, or an empty throne. Greek mythological figures were absorbed into Buddhist narrative art — Heracles, for example, was recast as the Buddha's guardian Vajrapani, and Tyche became associated with the goddess Hariti.

Spread

Through the Silk Road, Gandharan conventions for depicting the Buddha were transmitted to Central Asia and China, and from there to Korea and Japan, profoundly shaping East Asian Buddhist iconography. Stucco and schist sculptures from sites such as Taxila, Hadda, and Bamiyan — including the colossal Bamiyan Buddhas destroyed in 2001 — testify to the style's reach.
