William Whiston


Born 9 December 1667, Norton-juxta-Twycross, Leicestershire, England
Died 22 August 1752 (aged 84), London
Known for Translation of Josephus; A New Theory of the Earth

William Whiston (9 December 1667 – 22 August 1752) was an English theologian, historian, natural philosopher, and mathematician, a leading figure in popularising Newton's ideas.

Career at Cambridge

Whiston studied at Clare College, Cambridge, became Isaac Newton's protégé, and succeeded him as Lucasian Professor of Mathematics in 1702. His lectures and writings, including A New Theory of the Earth (1696), sought to reconcile the biblical account of Creation and the Flood with Newtonian physics, proposing that a comet had caused the Deluge.

Expulsion for heresy

Whiston adopted Arian views denying the co-eternity of Christ with the Father and published sermons and essays arguing that Arianism was the faith of the primitive Church. In 1710 he was expelled from Cambridge and prosecuted for heresy. Thereafter he supported himself by public lecturing in London on science and religion.

Later work

Whiston published a translation of the works of Josephus (1737) that remained standard for generations, worked on the problem of longitude, including a reward claim with Humphry Ditton based on signal rockets, and in old age joined the Baptists. His Memoirs (1749) are a valuable, if eccentric, record of his times.
