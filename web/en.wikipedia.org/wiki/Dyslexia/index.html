Dyslexia, also known as reading disorder, is a learning disorder characterised by difficulty with reading and spelling despite normal intelligence and adequate educational opportunity. It is the most common learning disability and occurs in all populations. People with dyslexia commonly have trouble with phonological awareness — the ability to recognise and manipulate the sounds of spoken language — which makes it hard to map letters to sounds when decoding written words.

Dyslexia is distinct from reading difficulties caused by hearing or vision problems or by insufficient teaching. It is generally believed to result from a combination of genetic and environmental factors, and brain imaging studies show differences in the activation of regions involved in language processing, particularly in the left hemisphere.

Signs and symptoms

Early signs include delayed speech, difficulty learning nursery rhymes, trouble remembering letter names and sounds, and confusing letters that look similar, such as b and d. In school-age children, reading is often slow and effortful, spelling is inconsistent, and handwriting may be poor. Adults with dyslexia may read accurately but slowly, and often report a lifelong history of avoiding reading for pleasure. Intelligence is unaffected, and many people with dyslexia show strengths in areas such as reasoning, problem-solving and creative thinking.

Causes

Dyslexia runs in families and is highly heritable; twin studies suggest a substantial genetic component. Researchers have proposed several candidate genes affecting neuronal migration and brain development. The leading cognitive explanation is the phonological deficit hypothesis: an impairment in representing and manipulating speech sounds that interferes with learning grapheme–phoneme correspondences. Other theories emphasise magnocellular visual processing or rapid auditory processing, but these remain debated.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is made through educational and psychological assessment of reading accuracy, fluency and comprehension, together with tests of phonological skills, working memory and processing speed. Modern diagnostic frameworks describe dyslexia as a dimensional condition rather than a single category, and emphasise early identification so that intervention can begin before children fall far behind their peers.

Management

There is no cure, but outcomes improve markedly with early, structured intervention. Evidence-based approaches include systematic phonics, multisensory structured literacy programmes (such as Orton–Gillingham-derived methods), and guided oral reading to build fluency. Accommodations such as extra time in examinations, text-to-speech technology, audiobooks and speech-to-text software help people with dyslexia demonstrate their knowledge. Many jurisdictions legally require schools to screen for and support dyslexia.

History

The condition was described in the late nineteenth century: German physician Oswald Berkhan identified cases in 1881, and Scottish ophthalmologist James Hinshelwood and English physician W. Pringle Morgan published influential descriptions in the 1890s, the latter coining discussion of "congenital word blindness". The term dyslexia was proposed by Rudolf Berlin in 1887. Twentieth-century work by Samuel Orton and others laid the foundations of modern assessment and remediation.
