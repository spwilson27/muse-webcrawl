Anarchy (international relations)

In international relations theory, anarchy refers to the absence of any authority above the sovereign state capable of making and enforcing rules. Because there is no world government, the international system is said to be "anarchic", in contrast to domestic political systems, which are hierarchically ordered under a government. Anarchy in this technical sense does not mean chaos or disorder; it describes an ordering principle of the system, not its degree of violence.

The concept

Kenneth Waltz, in Theory of International Politics (1979), made anarchy the starting point of neorealism: systems are defined by their ordering principle (anarchy abroad, hierarchy at home), by the functions of their units, and by the distribution of capabilities among them. Under anarchy, states exist in a "self-help" system: each must provide for its own security because no higher authority can be relied upon to rescue it. From this condition realists derive the security dilemma, the imperative of the balance of power, and the recurring possibility of war. Classical realists such as Hans Morgenthau grounded the same conclusion in human nature rather than system structure.

School perspectives

  • Realism treats anarchy as a permanent constraint that forces states to seek power and self-preservation.
  • Liberalism accepts anarchy but argues that institutions, interdependence and democratic norms can mitigate its effects.
  • Constructivism, in Alexander Wendt's famous formulation, holds that "anarchy is what states make of it": the meaning of anarchy depends on whether states view one another as enemies, rivals or friends, and cultures of anarchy can evolve.
  • The English School speaks of an "anarchical society", arguing that states under anarchy nevertheless form a society governed by shared rules and institutions.

Criticism

Critics note that hierarchy exists within the supposedly anarchic system — in empires, spheres of influence and great-power dominance — and that some regions and issue-areas approach governance without government. Others question whether anarchy can bear the explanatory weight structural theory places on it, since the same anarchy has accommodated both war-torn and deeply peaceful eras.
