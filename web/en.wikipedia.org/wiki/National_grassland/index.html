A national grassland is an area of protected land in the United States, administered by the U.S. Forest Service, consisting primarily of grassland or prairie. There are 20 national grasslands, covering about 3.8 million acres, almost all west of the Mississippi River in the Great Plains and western states.

National grasslands preserve remnants of the North American prairie.

Origins

Most national grasslands were assembled from farmland that failed during the Dust Bowl of the 1930s, when drought and poor farming practices devastated the plains. Under the Bankhead–Jones Farm Tenant Act of 1937, the federal government bought back marginal and eroded submarginal land ("land utilization projects"), reseeded it with native grasses, and eventually transferred it to the Forest Service; the areas were formally designated national grasslands in 1960.

Management and use

Unlike national parks, national grasslands are managed for multiple uses: livestock grazing under permit, recreation such as hiking, hunting and birdwatching, wildlife habitat, and in some cases energy development. They include notable areas such as the Pawnee and Comanche National Grasslands in Colorado, the Buffalo Gap and Fort Pierre grasslands in South Dakota, and the Little Missouri National Grassland in North Dakota, the largest of them.
