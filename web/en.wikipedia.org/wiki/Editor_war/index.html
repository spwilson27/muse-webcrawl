The editor war is the rivalry between users of the Emacs and vi (now usually Vim) text editors, two of the longest-running and most customisable programs in computing. It is a classic example of a "holy war" in hacker culture: a debate over tools and design philosophy conducted with great passion and considerable humour, in which participants on both sides are in on the joke as much as they are serious.

The two camps

Vi, created by Bill Joy in 1976, is a modal editor: the same keys type text in insert mode and issue commands in normal mode. Its defenders prize its ubiquity (a vi clone is installed on virtually every Unix-like system), its speed and its composable command language. Emacs, developed from 1976 by Richard Stallman and others, is a modeless, self-documenting, almost infinitely extensible environment — critics call it "a great operating system, lacking only a decent editor", while devotees run their mail, calendars and shells inside it.

Culture

The war flourished on Usenet newsgroups such as alt.religion.emacs, where Stallman was jokingly canonised as "Saint IGNUcius" of the Church of Emacs; vi partisans replied in kind. Signature jokes include the claim that Emacs stands for "Escape Meta Alt Control Shift" (for its modifier-key chords) and that vi users can be identified by their inability to exit the program. Surveys and usage have shifted over decades, with Vim repeatedly topping developer polls, but both editors remain in heavy daily use — and the war, by tradition, will never be settled.
