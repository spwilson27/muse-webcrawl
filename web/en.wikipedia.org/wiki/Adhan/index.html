The adhan (Arabic: أذان, adhān) is the Islamic call to public prayer, recited by a muezzin from the mosque (traditionally from the minaret) at prescribed times of day to summon Muslims to the five obligatory daily prayers (salah). Rooted in the practice of the first community at Medina after the Hijrah, the adhan consists of a fixed set of Arabic phrases glorifying God and attesting to the prophethood of Muhammad. A second, quieter call, the iqama, is delivered inside the mosque immediately before the prayer begins.

Shia Muslims add the phrase Ashhadu anna 'Aliyyan waliyy Allāh ("I bear witness that Ali is the vicegerent of God") and repeat Hayya 'ala khayr al-'amal ("Hasten to the best of deeds"), among other variations. Listeners traditionally repeat the phrases quietly after the muezzin and add a supplication after the call ends asking God to grant Muhammad intercession and a place in Paradise.

History

Hadith literature relates that in Medina the community debated how to announce prayer times — considering a bell like the Christians', a horn like the Jews', or a fire — until a companion, Abdullah ibn Zayd, reported a dream in which a man taught him the words of the adhan; Umar confirmed a similar dream, and Muhammad ordered Bilal ibn Rabah to deliver the call because of his fine voice. Bilal, who performed the first adhan from a rooftop in Medina and later from atop the Kaaba at the conquest of Mecca, is honoured as the first muezzin. The dawn addition "prayer is better than sleep" is attributed to Bilal's own initiative, approved by Muhammad.

Performance and law

The adhan is delivered in a loud, melodious voice, often in a melodic mode (maqam) chosen by the muezzin and the hour; in Turkey, specific maqams are traditionally associated with each prayer time. It is a communal obligation (fard kifaya) in the view of many jurists that the call be made in each neighbourhood, and it is also recited into the right ear of a newborn infant. Between 1932 and 1950, Turkey famously required the adhan to be recited in Turkish rather than Arabic; the restriction was lifted in 1950 and the Arabic call restored amid great public emotion.

Modern usage

Today the call is broadcast by loudspeaker, radio, television, satellite channels, and smartphone apps that compute prayer times and play recorded adhans by celebrated muezzins of Mecca and Medina. In several Western cities, mosques have received permission to broadcast a weekly or daily amplified call, sometimes prompting public debate about religious sound in shared space comparable to church bells. During Ramadan, the adhan for Maghrib signals the breaking of the fast (iftar).
