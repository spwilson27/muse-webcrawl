The Nibelungenlied (Middle High German: Der Nibelunge Liet, "The Song of the Nibelungs") is an epic poem written around 1200 in Middle High German. It is Germany's national epic, and its author, an anonymous poet probably working in the Danube region (possibly Passau), drew on oral traditions reaching back to the Migration Period — the destruction of the Burgundian kingdom by the Huns in 437 — blended with Norse legendary material.

Plot

The hero Siegfried of Xanten, who has slain a dragon, bathed in its blood to become invulnerable (save for one spot covered by a linden leaf), and won the hoard of the Nibelungs, comes to Worms and helps King Gunther win the superhumanly strong Icelandic queen Brunhild by deception. In return he marries Gunther's sister Kriemhild. When the two queens quarrel over precedence, Kriemhild reveals the deception; the Burgundian vassal Hagen treacherously murders Siegfried at a spring and sinks the Nibelung hoard in the Rhine. Thirteen years later the widowed Kriemhild marries Etzel (Attila), king of the Huns, invites her brothers to Hungary, and engineers their destruction: in a night of slaughter in Etzel's hall the Burgundians — "Nibelungs" — perish almost to a man, Hagen last of all, and Kriemhild, having killed Hagen herself with Siegfried's sword, is cut down by Hildebrand.

Reception

Forgotten after the Middle Ages, the poem was rediscovered in 1755 and celebrated by German Romantics as a national epic, to the lasting regret of scholars who saw its "Nibelung loyalty" (Nibelungentreue) sloganized in both World Wars. Wagner's Ring cycle draws on the same legends through their Norse versions. Together with the Song of Roland and the Shahnameh, the Nibelungenlied is a central example of the heroic epic tradition examined by oral-formulaic scholarship after Milman Parry.
