Interferometric synthetic aperture radar, abbreviated InSAR (or deprecated IfSAR), is a radar technique used in geodesy and remote sensing. This geodetic method uses two or more synthetic aperture radar (SAR) images to generate maps of surface deformation or digital elevation, using differences in the phase of the waves returning to the satellite or aircraft.

Principle

A SAR instrument emits microwave pulses and records the phase of the echoes. Comparing two images of the same area taken at slightly different positions or times yields an interferogram: phase differences encode changes in the path length to the ground. With a spatial baseline, phase encodes topography; with a temporal baseline, phase changes reveal ground motion between acquisitions down to millimetre scale.

Applications

InSAR measures deformation from earthquakes, volcanic inflation and deflation, glacier flow, landslides, groundwater extraction, and subsidence above mines and reservoirs. The Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (2000) used single-pass interferometry to map Earth's topography. Satellite missions such as ERS, Envisat, ALOS, TerraSAR-X and the Copernicus Sentinel-1 constellation supply data for operational monitoring.

Limitations

Atmospheric water vapour delays radar signals and adds noise; vegetation and snow decorrelate the signal between passes; steep terrain causes layover and shadowing. Persistent-scatterer and small-baseline time-series methods mitigate these effects.
