Heliacal rising

The heliacal rising (from Greek ἡλιακός, "of the sun") of a star or planet occurs annually, when it first becomes visible above the eastern horizon just before sunrise, after a period when it was hidden by the Sun's glare. Historically, the most important such rising was that of Sirius, which in ancient Egypt heralded the annual flooding of the Nile and marked the New Year.

A star's heliacal rising is its first dawn appearance after conjunction with the Sun.

Explanation

Each day a star rises and sets about four minutes earlier than the day before, owing to the Earth's orbital motion. A star that is too near the Sun in the sky is invisible in daylight or twilight. As the Earth moves along its orbit, the Sun appears to shift eastward against the background stars, so a given star emerges into the dawn sky (heliacal rising) and, months later, makes its last evening appearance after sunset (heliacal setting). The date of heliacal rising depends on the star's magnitude, the observer's latitude, atmospheric conditions, and the angle of the ecliptic to the horizon.

Sirius and the Egyptian calendar

The heliacal rising of Sirius (Egyptian Sopdet) coincided with the onset of the Nile flood and the summer solstice in the third millennium BCE, anchoring the Egyptian civil calendar of 365 days. Because the civil year lacked a leap day, the rising drifted through the calendar in the 1,460-year Sothic cycle — a fact used by Egyptologists to date ancient reigns. Precession has since shifted the event: at Cairo, Sirius now rises heliacally in early August (Gregorian).

Other cultures

The heliacal rising of the Pleiades marked the New Year and the start of the planting or seafaring seasons for many cultures, from Hesiod's Greeks to the Māori (Matariki) and several Native American and African peoples. Babylonian astronomers systematically recorded heliacal risings in texts such as the MUL.APIN tablets, and Greek parapegmata (star calendars) were keyed to them.
