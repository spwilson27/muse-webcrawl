Lorenzo de' Medici (1 January 1449 – 8 April 1492), known as Lorenzo the Magnificent (Italian: Lorenzo il Magnifico), was an Italian statesman, banker, and patron of the arts who ruled the Republic of Florence from 1469 until his death. The most powerful and enthusiastic patron of Renaissance culture in Italy, he supported artists including Michelangelo, Botticelli, Leonardo da Vinci, and Verrocchio, and his court made Florence the cultural capital of Europe.

Early life and rule

Lorenzo was the grandson of Cosimo de' Medici, founder of the Medici political dynasty, and son of Piero "the Gouty". Educated by humanist tutors and sent on diplomatic missions as a teenager, he assumed leadership of Florence at twenty on his father's death in 1469, ruling jointly with his younger brother Giuliano. Although Florence was formally a republic, Lorenzo governed through control of councils, patronage, and the Medici bank's wealth.

The Pazzi conspiracy

On 26 April 1478, during Mass in Florence Cathedral, conspirators of the rival Pazzi family and their allies attacked the brothers: Giuliano was murdered, but Lorenzo escaped wounded into the sacristy. The plot's failure unleashed savage reprisals against the Pazzi and brought Florence into war with Pope Sixtus IV and Naples. Lorenzo ended the war in 1480 by a bold personal diplomatic mission to King Ferrante of Naples, detaching him from the papal alliance — the feat that confirmed his reputation as Italy's indispensable statesman, the "needle of the Italian balance".

Patronage

Lorenzo's Florence sheltered the Platonic Academy around Marsilio Ficino, the poets Poliziano and Luigi Pulci, and the young Michelangelo, whom Lorenzo took into his household after spotting his talent in the Medici sculpture garden. Lorenzo was himself a notable vernacular poet. He commissioned works from Botticelli, Ghirlandaio, Filippino Lippi, and others, and expanded the Medici library and collections.

Decline and death

The Medici bank declined badly under Lorenzo, with branches closing amid bad loans, and his regime grew more authoritarian. Plagued by gout, he died at the villa of Careggi on 8 April 1492, weeks after hearing the fiery Lenten sermons of Girolamo Savonarola. His death is often treated as a symbolic close of the Italian Renaissance's golden age; within two years his son Piero was expelled and the French invasions began.
