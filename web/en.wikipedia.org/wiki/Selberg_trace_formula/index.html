Selberg trace formula

The Selberg trace formula, introduced by Atle Selberg in 1956, is a fundamental identity in harmonic analysis and number theory. In its original form it computes the trace of certain operators acting on functions on a compact quotient of the upper half-plane, expressing that trace in two ways: as a sum over the eigenvalues of the Laplacian (the spectral side), and as a sum over the lengths of closed geodesics on the corresponding hyperbolic surface (the geometric side).

Statement and idea

For a compact Riemann surface obtained as the quotient of the hyperbolic plane by a discrete group, the formula equates a weighted count of Laplacian eigenvalues with contributions from conjugacy classes of the group: the identity element contributes a term involving the area of the surface, while hyperbolic elements contribute terms involving the lengths of the closed geodesics they represent, weighted by a test function and its Fourier transform. The formula is an analogue for non-compact, continuous spectra settings of the Poisson summation formula, and Selberg developed it partly to study the distribution of eigenvalues and the Selberg zeta function.

Applications

The trace formula gives results on the equidistribution of closed geodesics, Weyl's law for eigenvalue counts, and connections between spectra and geometry ("hearing the shape" of a hyperbolic surface). Through the Selberg zeta function it encodes information about prime geodesics, mirroring the role of the Riemann zeta function for prime numbers.

Generalizations

James Arthur and others generalized Selberg's formula to reductive groups over adeles, producing the Arthur–Selberg trace formula, a central tool of the Langlands program. It underlies proofs of functoriality results, the classification of automorphic representations of classical groups, and much of modern analytic number theory, including work on the Ramanujan conjecture and subconvexity of L-functions.
