Glass-ceramic

Glass-ceramics are polycrystalline materials produced through controlled crystallization of base glass, producing a fine uniform dispersion of crystals throughout the bulk material. Crystallization is accomplished by subjecting suitable glasses to a carefully regulated heat treatment schedule, resulting in the nucleation and growth of crystal phases within the glass. In many cases, the crystallization process can proceed to near completion, but often leaves a small proportion of residual glassy phase.

Glass-ceramic materials share many properties with both glasses and ceramics. They have an amorphous phase and one or more crystalline phases and are produced by a so-called "controlled crystallization" in contrast to spontaneous crystallization, which is usually not wanted in glass manufacturing.

History

The discovery of glass-ceramics is credited to S. Donald Stookey of Corning Glass Works, who in the 1950s accidentally overheated a sample of photosensitive glass and found it had transformed into an opaque, mechanically strong material. This led to products such as Pyroceram, used for cookware and stove tops.

Properties and uses

Glass-ceramics can have very low or even zero coefficients of thermal expansion, high mechanical strength, and transparency or opacity depending on composition. Common applications include glass-ceramic cooktops (such as Schott Ceran), cookware, telescope mirror blanks (such as Zerodur), dental restorations, and biocompatible implants (bioglass-ceramics).
