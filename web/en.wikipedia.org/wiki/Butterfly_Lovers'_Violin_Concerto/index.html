Butterfly Lovers' Violin Concerto

ComposersHe Zhanhao, Chen Gang
Composed1959
PremiereMay 1959, Shanghai
Durationabout 25–28 minutes
MovementsOne (three sections)

The Butterfly Lovers' Violin Concerto (Chinese: 梁祝小提琴协奏曲; pinyin: Liáng Zhù Xiǎotíqín Xiézòuqǔ) is a violin concerto composed in 1959 by He Zhanhao and Chen Gang, then students at the Shanghai Conservatory of Music. Adapted from the Chinese legend of the Butterfly Lovers — the tragic romance of Liang Shanbo and Zhu Yingtai — it is one of the most famous works of Chinese orchestral music, blending Western symphonic form and violin technique with melodies and colour drawn from Chinese Yue opera.

Background

The legend of Liang Shanbo and Zhu Yingtai, sometimes called the Chinese Romeo and Juliet, tells of Zhu Yingtai, a young woman who disguises herself as a man to attend school, where she falls in love with her classmate Liang Shanbo. When her family forces her into an arranged marriage, Liang dies of grief; at his tomb, Zhu throws herself in, and the lovers are transformed into butterflies. The story had already been adapted into a celebrated Yue opera and a 1954 film.

Music

The concerto is written in a single movement of three broad sections corresponding to sonata form, and depicts episodes of the story: the lovers' meeting and schooling together (exposition, in a lyrical pentatonic theme), their resistance to the arranged marriage (a turbulent development section in which the solo violin struggles against the orchestra), and the transformation into butterflies (a recapitulation in which the love theme returns and dissolves into a serene coda). The solo violin imitates techniques of the Chinese erhu and pipa — slides, glissandi, and ornamented portamenti — while the pentatonic themes derive from the Yue opera versions of the tale.

History and reception

The concerto was premiered in Shanghai in May 1959 with the violinist Yu Lina as soloist, and quickly became a signature work of modern Chinese classical music. Suppressed during the Cultural Revolution — its composers were criticised and the work banned as feudal and bourgeois — it returned triumphantly afterwards and has since been performed and recorded worldwide by soloists including Midori, Sarah Chang, and Lü Siqing. It remains the most internationally performed Chinese violin concerto and a staple of Chinese New Year concerts abroad.
