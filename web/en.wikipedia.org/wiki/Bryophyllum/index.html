Bryophyllum

Bryophyllum (from the Greek βρῦον/βρύειν bryon/bryein = sprout, φύλλον phyllon = leaf) is a genus of plants in the family Crassulaceae, native to Madagascar and naturalized in many tropical and subtropical regions. It is a section or subgenus within the genus Kalanchoe in some classifications, comprising roughly 40 species. The group is notable for vegetative reproduction: small plantlets develop along the margins of the leaves, which drop off and root to form new plants.

Description

Bryophyllums are succulent perennial plants with fleshy, often scalloped leaves. Many species bear notches along the leaf margins in which bulbils (plantlets) form; these contain tiny leaves, stems and roots, and grow into mature plants when they come into contact with soil. This mode of asexual reproduction is a striking example of vegetative propagation and has made species such as Bryophyllum pinnatum and Bryophyllum daigremontianum ("mother of thousands") popular houseplants — and, in some regions, invasive weeds.

Uses and toxicity

Bryophyllum pinnatum (syn. Kalanchoe pinnata) is used in traditional medicine in several cultures for treating wounds, inflammation and other complaints. However, bryophyllums contain bufadienolide cardiac glycosides, which are toxic to livestock; poisoning of cattle and other grazing animals has been reported in Australia and South Africa where the plants have become invasive.

Taxonomy

The genus was established by Richard Anthony Salisbury in 1805. Molecular phylogenetic studies have shown Bryophyllum to be nested within Kalanchoe, and it is now commonly treated as Kalanchoe section Bryophyllum, though the name Bryophyllum remains widely used in horticulture.
