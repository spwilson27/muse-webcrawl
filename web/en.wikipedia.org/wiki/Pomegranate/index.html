Pomegranate

The pomegranate (Punica granatum) is a fruit-bearing deciduous shrub in the family Lythraceae, subfamily Punicoideae, that grows between 5 and 10 metres tall. The pomegranate is rich in symbolic and mythological associations in many cultures, and was cultivated throughout the Mediterranean region and northern India for several millennia. The fruit is typically in season in the Southern Hemisphere from March to May and in the Northern Hemisphere from September to February.

Description

The shrub bears glossy, narrow oblong leaves and bright red, orange, or pink trumpet-shaped flowers. The fruit is a berry between 5 and 12 cm in diameter with a rounded hexagonal shape and a thick, reddish skin. Inside, a spongy white mesocarp divides the fruit into compartments filled with hundreds of seeds, each surrounded by a juicy, red, translucent aril. The number of seeds varies widely; the fruit's many seeds made it a widespread symbol of fertility, abundance, and prosperity.

Cultivation and uses

The pomegranate originated in the region extending from modern-day Iran to northern India and has been cultivated since ancient times. It is drought-tolerant and grown today across the Mediterranean, the Middle East, California and Arizona, South Asia, and parts of Africa. The arils are eaten fresh and used in salads, desserts, and garnishes; the juice is drunk fresh, made into grenadine syrup, and concentrated into pomegranate molasses, a staple of Middle Eastern cooking. In Indian cuisine the dried seeds (anardana) provide a sour flavouring. Pomegranate extracts are rich in polyphenols such as punicalagins, and the fruit has a long history in traditional medicine, though health claims for commercial juices have drawn regulatory scrutiny.

Cultural significance

In Greek mythology, Persephone's eating of pomegranate seeds in the underworld bound her to spend part of each year with Hades, explaining the seasons. The fruit appears in the Bible — carved on the pillars of Solomon's Temple and embroidered on priestly robes — and in Jewish tradition, where its seeds are said to number 613, matching the commandments. It is a national symbol of Armenia and Azerbaijan, features in Persian, Turkish, and Arab art and cuisine, and gives its name to the Spanish city of Granada.
