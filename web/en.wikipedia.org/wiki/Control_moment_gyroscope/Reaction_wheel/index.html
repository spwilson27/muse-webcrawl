Reaction wheel

A reaction wheel (RW) is a type of flywheel used primarily by spacecraft to change their angular momentum without using fuel. Reaction wheels work by changing their rotation speed, which causes the spacecraft to counter-rotate proportionately through conservation of angular momentum. They are used for three-axis attitude control and are particularly useful when the spacecraft must be rotated through very small angles, such as when keeping a telescope pointed at a star.

Reaction wheels are contrasted with control moment gyroscopes, which tilt the axis of a spinning rotor to apply torque. A momentum wheel is a reaction wheel operated at a nonzero bias speed, providing gyroscopic stiffness as well as controllable torque.

Principle of operation

A reaction wheel consists of an electric motor driving a flywheel. To rotate the spacecraft in one direction, the motor accelerates the wheel in the opposite direction; the spacecraft experiences an equal and opposite reaction torque. Because only electrical power is required, reaction wheels are favored for long missions where expending propellant for attitude control would be costly.

Over time, external disturbance torques — such as gravity-gradient torque, solar radiation pressure, and aerodynamic drag in low orbit — can cause the wheel to accumulate angular momentum until it approaches its maximum speed, a condition called saturation. Saturated wheels must be "desaturated" using thrusters or magnetorquers, which apply external torque so the wheel can be spun down.

Applications

Reaction wheels are used on many spacecraft, including the Hubble Space Telescope, the Kepler space telescope, and numerous Earth-observation satellites. Typically three wheels are mounted orthogonally to provide control about all axes, and a fourth is often added in a skewed configuration for redundancy in case of failure.

Failures

Reaction wheel failures have ended or degraded several missions. The Kepler mission's prime phase ended in 2013 after the failure of a second of its four reaction wheels, although the spacecraft was later repurposed for the K2 mission using solar radiation pressure for stabilization. Friction and bearing wear are common failure modes, and wheels are often operated at speeds chosen to minimize wear.
