Control moment gyroscope

A control moment gyroscope (CMG) is an attitude control device used on spacecraft. It consists of a spinning rotor of constant speed mounted in a powered gimbal; by tilting the rotor's axis, a gyroscopic torque is applied to the spacecraft, changing its orientation without expending propellant.

Operation

Because the rotor spins at high constant speed, a modest gimbal torque can produce a large output torque — a form of torque amplification. CMGs are therefore favored on large spacecraft such as the International Space Station, whose four CMGs each contain a rapidly spinning steel flywheel and can slew the station without firing thrusters.

Singularities and steering

Arrays of CMGs can encounter singular configurations in which no combination of gimbal motions can produce torque about a particular axis. CMG steering laws — mathematical algorithms that coordinate the gimbals — are designed to avoid or pass through these singularities. CMGs contrast with reaction wheels, which change rotor speed rather than axis direction; reaction wheels provide less torque but simpler control.

History

Gyroscopic attitude control was proposed in the early twentieth century and developed for satellites from the 1960s onward; CMGs flew on the Skylab space station in the 1970s and on several large Soviet and Russian stations before their adoption on the ISS.
