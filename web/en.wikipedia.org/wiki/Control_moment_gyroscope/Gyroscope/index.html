Gyroscope

A gyroscope (from Ancient Greek γῦρος gŷros, "round" and σκοπέω skopéō, "to look") is a device used for measuring or maintaining orientation and angular velocity. It is a spinning wheel or disc in which the axis of rotation (spin axis) is free to assume any orientation by itself. When rotating, the orientation of this axis is unaffected by tilting or rotation of the mounting, according to the conservation of angular momentum.

History

Devices exploiting the same principle existed earlier, but the gyroscope was named by Léon Foucault in 1852, who used it in experiments demonstrating the rotation of the Earth. Gyroscopic compasses, which find true north rather than magnetic north, were developed for ships in the early 20th century. Gyroscopes became essential instruments in aviation, navigation and weapon guidance.

Applications

Gyroscopes are used in compasses, autopilots, inertial navigation systems and the stabilisation of spacecraft and satellites. Large control moment gyroscopes — spinning rotors on gimbals that exchange momentum with the spacecraft — provide attitude control for the International Space Station. Modern consumer electronics use tiny MEMS vibrating-structure gyroscopes to sense rotation in smartphones, game controllers, drones and virtual-reality headsets. Other technologies, such as ring laser and fibre-optic gyroscopes, measure rotation through the Sagnac effect without moving parts.

Physics

A spinning rotor resists changes to its orientation because of its angular momentum; an applied torque produces precession, motion of the spin axis at right angles to the torque. These properties are described by the Euler equations of rigid-body dynamics.
