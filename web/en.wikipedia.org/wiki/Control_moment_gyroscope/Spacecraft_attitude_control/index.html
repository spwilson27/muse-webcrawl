Spacecraft attitude control

Attitude control is the process of controlling the orientation of an aircraft, spacecraft, or other vehicle with respect to an inertial frame of reference or another entity such as the celestial sphere, certain fields, or nearby objects. For spacecraft, attitude control is performed by the attitude determination and control system (ADCS), which combines sensors that measure the vehicle's orientation with actuators that apply the torques needed to reorient it.

Attitude determination

Attitude determination uses sensors such as sun sensors, Earth horizon sensors, star trackers, magnetometers, and gyroscopes. Star trackers provide the most accurate absolute attitude measurements by matching observed star patterns against a catalog. Rate gyroscopes measure angular velocity, and their outputs are integrated to propagate attitude between absolute measurements, commonly using estimation algorithms such as the Kalman filter.

Actuators

Common actuators include:

  • Reaction wheels and control moment gyroscopes, which exchange angular momentum with the spacecraft body;
  • Magnetorquers (magnetic torque rods), which interact with a planetary magnetic field and also serve to desaturate momentum devices;
  • Thrusters, which provide large torques at the cost of propellant;
  • Solar sails and drag devices, which exploit environmental forces on some small satellites.

Stabilization methods

Spacecraft may be spin-stabilized, rotating about a principal axis so that gyroscopic stiffness resists disturbances, or three-axis stabilized, actively holding a fixed orientation. Gravity-gradient stabilization uses the tidal variation of a planet's gravitational field across an extended structure to passively align the vehicle with the local vertical. Many spacecraft combine methods, using momentum bias from a spinning wheel for stiffness together with active control.

Disturbance torques

Attitude control systems must counteract environmental disturbances including gravity-gradient torque, solar radiation pressure, residual magnetic dipole interactions, and — in low Earth orbit — aerodynamic drag. These torques tend to build up momentum in reaction wheels over time, requiring periodic momentum dumping.
