International Space Station

The International Space Station (ISS) is a large space station assembled and maintained in low Earth orbit by a collaboration of five space agencies and their contractors: NASA (United States), Roscosmos (Russia), ESA (Europe), JAXA (Japan), and CSA (Canada). The station serves as a microgravity and space environment research laboratory in which scientific research is conducted in astrobiology, astronomy, meteorology, physics, and other fields.

History and assembly

The first module, Zarya, was launched in 1998, and the station has been continuously inhabited since November 2000 — the longest continuous human presence in space. Modules were added over more than a decade by Space Shuttle and Russian Proton and Soyuz flights; the station's solar arrays and trusses generate power and radiate heat, and its structure spans roughly the size of a football field. Visiting vehicles include Soyuz and Crew Dragon crew spacecraft and Progress, Cygnus and other cargo craft.

Attitude control

The ISS maintains its orientation primarily with four large control moment gyroscopes (CMGs), each containing a rotor spun at a constant speed and gimballed so that exchanging momentum with the station produces torque without expending propellant. Russian-segment thrusters and visiting-vehicle burns supplement the CMGs, particularly for large manoeuvres and momentum desaturation.

Research and future

Crews of typically seven people conduct experiments that cannot be performed on Earth, study the effects of long-duration spaceflight on the human body, and test technologies for exploration beyond low Earth orbit. The partner agencies plan to operate the station until around 2030, after which it is to be deorbited into the South Pacific, with commercial stations expected to succeed it.
