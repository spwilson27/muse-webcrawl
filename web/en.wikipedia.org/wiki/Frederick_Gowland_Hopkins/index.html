Frederick Gowland Hopkins

BornJune 20, 1861, Eastbourne, England
DiedMay 16, 1947 (aged 85), Cambridge, England
OccupationBiochemist
AwardsNobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (1929)

Sir Frederick Gowland Hopkins (20 June 1861 – 16 May 1947) was an English biochemist who was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1929, shared with Christiaan Eijkman, for the discovery of vitamins. He is often called the father of British biochemistry.

Career

Hopkins came to science by an unconventional route, working first as an insurance clerk and analyst before studying medicine and chemistry in London. In 1898 he moved to Cambridge, where he built up the teaching and research of biochemistry, becoming the first Professor of Biochemistry there in 1914 and heading the Dunn Institute of Biochemistry.

Research

Among Hopkins's many contributions were the isolation of the amino acid tryptophan (1901, with Sydney Cole), the discovery of the tripeptide glutathione (1921), and work on muscle lactic acid and biological oxidation. In celebrated feeding experiments published in 1906–1912 he showed that animals fed purified diets of protein, fat, carbohydrate, and salts failed to grow unless given minute amounts of milk — demonstrating the existence of "accessory food factors," later identified as vitamins.

Honors

Hopkins was knighted in 1925, served as president of the Royal Society from 1930 to 1935, and received the Nobel Prize in 1929. Many leading biochemists of the next generation trained in his Cambridge laboratory.
