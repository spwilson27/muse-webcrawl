Luge

A luge is a small one- or two-person sled on which one sleds supine (face up) and feet-first. A luger steers by using the calf muscles to flex the sled's runners or by exerting opposite shoulder pressure to the seat. Luge is also the name of the sport organised by the Fédération Internationale de Luge (FIL), one of the fastest of the Winter Olympic sliding sports, with athletes reaching speeds above 140 km/h (87 mph).

History

Sledging on ice tracks developed in the resorts of the Swiss Alps in the late nineteenth century, alongside bobsleigh and skeleton, notably at St. Moritz and Davos. The first organised luge races were held in the 1880s, and the sport's international federation was founded in 1957. Luge made its Olympic debut at the 1964 Winter Games in Innsbruck and has been contested ever since.

Competition

Olympic luge events comprise men's singles, women's singles, doubles and, since 2014, a mixed team relay. Singles races are decided over four timed runs, doubles over two. Artificial tracks are refrigerated ice courses about 1,000–1,500 metres long; natural-track luge, raced on existing mountain roads, has its own world championships. Germany has historically dominated the sport, producing champions such as Georg Hackl, Silke Kraushaar and Felix Loch.

Equipment and technique

The sled rides on two steel runners mounted on flexible bridges (Kufen). Lugers wear aerodynamic suits, gloves with small spikes for the start paddle, and a helmet with a visor. Steering is done by subtle pressure changes rather than by pulling ropes, and the start — rocking the sled back and forth on "start handles" before paddling away — is crucial to a fast run.

Safety

The fatal accident of Georgian luger Nodar Kumaritashvili during training for the 2010 Vancouver Olympics led to tightened track-design and speed limits at subsequent Games.
