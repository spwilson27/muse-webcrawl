Ole Christensen Rømer (25 September 1644 – 19 September 1710) was a Danish astronomer who, in 1676, made the first quantitative measurements of the speed of light, demonstrating that light travels at a finite speed. He also invented a modern thermometer scale, the Rømer scale, and served as royal astronomer and chief of police in Copenhagen.

Speed of light

Working at the Paris Observatory with Giovanni Domenico Cassini, Rømer observed the eclipses of Jupiter's moon Io. He noticed that the observed times of the eclipses depended on the Earth's position in its orbit: when the Earth was moving away from Jupiter, the eclipses occurred later than predicted, and when approaching, earlier. Rømer correctly explained this by the finite speed of light — the light from more distant Jupiter took longer to arrive.

From the accumulated delay, roughly 22 minutes across the diameter of the Earth's orbit (the modern value is about 16.7 minutes), Christiaan Huygens derived a speed of light of about 212,000 km/s, well within the right order of magnitude of the modern value of about 299,792 km/s. Rømer announced his result to the French Academy of Sciences in 1676; it was initially controversial, but was widely accepted after further observations, including those of James Bradley in 1729.

Other work

Rømer developed one of the first practical temperature scales (1701), using brine, freezing water and boiling water as fixed points; Daniel Fahrenheit visited him in 1708 and adapted the scale into the Fahrenheit scale. In Copenhagen he carried out a major overhaul of Danish weights and measures, reformed the calendar (introducing the Gregorian calendar in Denmark in 1700), installed street lighting and sewers, and designed astronomical instruments, including altazimuth and transit instruments. Most of his papers were destroyed in the Copenhagen fire of 1728.
