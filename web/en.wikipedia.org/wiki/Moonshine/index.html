Moonshine

Moonshine is a high-proof distilled spirit, generally made from corn mash, produced in an unlicensed still. The word derives from the practice of producing and transporting the spirit at night "by the light of the moon" to avoid detection. Once a staple of illicit distillation in the United States — especially Appalachia — moonshine is now also produced legally by licensed craft distillers, usually as unaged corn whiskey.

History

Illicit distilling in America dates to the whiskey excise tax of 1791, which provoked the Whiskey Rebellion. After the Civil War, revenue enforcement in the southern mountains hardened into a cat-and-mouse culture of moonshiners and "revenuers". Prohibition (1920–1933) made moonshining a major industry and fed organised crime; modified cars used to out-run federal agents ("bootlegging") helped give rise to stock car racing and NASCAR. Figures such as Junior Johnson moved from running moonshine to professional racing.

Production and safety

Traditional moonshine is made by fermenting a mash of corn, sugar, and water and distilling it in a copper pot still. Poorly made illicit spirit can be dangerous: methanol contamination and lead leached from improvised condensers (such as car radiators) have caused poisoning and blindness. Modern legal "moonshine" is simply taxed, regulated unaged whiskey, often sold in mason jars at proofs from 40% to over 60% alcohol by volume.

In popular culture

Moonshining features in American folklore, country music, and television series such as The Dukes of Hazzard and Discovery Channel's Moonshiners.
