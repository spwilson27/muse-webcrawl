Liburna

A liburna or liburnian was a type of small galley used for raiding and patrols. Originally used by the Liburnians, an ancient Illyrian tribe from Dalmatia, it was later adopted by the Roman navy.

TypeGalley / warship
OriginLiburnia, Dalmatia
UsersLiburnians, Roman navy
PropulsionOars and sail

History

The liburna gave its name to the Liburnians, a seafaring people of the eastern Adriatic coast who were noted pirates in the early first millennium BC. Their fast, light vessels were ideally suited to the island-strewn waters of the Dalmatian coast.

The Romans adopted the design after their wars with the Illyrian tribes. Liburnae played a famous role at the Battle of Actium in 31 BC, where Octavian's lighter liburnian vessels outmanoeuvred the heavier quinqueremes of Mark Antony and Cleopatra. The historian and admiral Agrippa's fleet relied heavily on the type.

Design

The liburna was a light, fast bireme-type galley, typically rowed with one or two banks of oars and fitted with a single mast and square sail. It carried a ram at the waterline and was crewed by oarsmen, sailors and a contingent of marines. Its shallow draft allowed it to operate close inshore and among islands where larger warships could not follow.

Later use

By the late Roman period the term liburna had become a generic word for a warship in Latin literature. In the Byzantine navy the name survived in the form of the dromon, the standard Byzantine war galley descended in part from the liburna tradition.
