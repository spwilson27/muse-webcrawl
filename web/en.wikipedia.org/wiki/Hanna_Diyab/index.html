Hanna Diyab (Arabic: Ḥannā Diyāb; born c. 1688) was a Syrian Maronite Christian cloth merchant and storyteller from Aleppo. He is famous as the man who told the tales of "Aladdin" and "Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves" — among the best-known stories in the world — to the French orientalist Antoine Galland, who included them in his translation of the One Thousand and One Nights. Diyab's own Arabic autobiography, rediscovered and published only in the twenty-first century, is a remarkable account of his travels from Aleppo to Paris and back.

Life

Diyab was born in Aleppo into a family of cloth merchants and trained in the trade. As a young man he entered the service of the French traveller Paul Lucas, accompanying him through Egypt, Tripoli, Cyprus, Jerusalem and Lebanon before sailing to France. Arriving in Paris in 1708, he was presented at the court of Louis XIV and shown the royal collections; Galland recorded in his diary in 1709 that Diyab recounted a series of tales over several sessions — fourteen in all, of which Galland used several in the later volumes of Les mille et une nuits, including "Aladdin and the Magic Lamp" and "Ali Baba".

Autobiography

After returning to Aleppo, Diyab worked as a cloth merchant and in the 1760s dictated or wrote a memoir of his journeys, sometimes called The Book of Travels. The manuscript, held by the Vatican Library, attracted little attention until its Arabic edition (2015) and English translation (2021, as The Book of Travels in the Library of Arabic Literature series) revealed a wry, observant account of the early modern Mediterranean and of Paris under the Sun King — including his own sidelong view of the orientalist enterprise that made his tales famous.

Legacy

The "orphan tales" of the Nights — those, like Aladdin and Ali Baba, with no earlier Arabic written source — are now widely attributed to Diyab's oral storytelling, reshaping scholarly understanding of the collection as a European–Middle Eastern collaboration rather than a pure translation.
