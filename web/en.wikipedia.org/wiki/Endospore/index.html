Endospore

An endospore is a dormant, tough, and non-reproductive structure produced by some bacteria in the phylum Bacillota. The name "endospore" is suggestive of a spore or seed-like form (endo meaning "within"), but it is not a true spore in the reproductive sense: a single vegetative cell produces a single endospore, which later germinates into a single vegetative cell, so the process is one of survival rather than reproduction. Endospores enable bacteria to remain dormant for extended periods, even centuries, and to survive extremes of heat, desiccation, radiation and disinfectants that would kill the growing cell.

Endospores appear as bright, refractile bodies within or released from vegetative cells.

Formation (sporulation)

Sporulation is triggered by nutrient limitation and is best studied in Bacillus subtilis. The master regulator Spo0A initiates an asymmetric cell division that produces a small forespore compartment and a larger mother cell. The mother cell engulfs the forespore, which then develops a thick peptidoglycan cortex and proteinaceous spore coat, accumulates calcium dipicolinate and small acid-soluble spore proteins (SASPs) that protect the DNA, and dehydrates its core. Finally the mother cell lyses, releasing the mature endospore. The whole process takes roughly eight hours.

Germination

When nutrients return, germinant receptors in the spore membrane trigger rapid rehydration, loss of heat resistance, breakdown of the cortex and SASPs, and outgrowth into a vegetative cell — a transformation that can begin within minutes.

Resistance and significance

The dehydrated core, mineralised with calcium dipicolinate, together with DNA-saturating SASPs and the protective coat, makes endospores among the most durable biological structures known; viable spores have reportedly been revived from materials tens of millions of years old, though such claims are debated. Endospore-formers include important pathogens: Bacillus anthracis (anthrax), Clostridium botulinum (botulism), C. tetani (tetanus) and Clostridioides difficile. Their heat resistance dictates sterilisation standards — standard autoclaving at 121 °C is designed to kill endospores — and food-canning procedures such as the "botulinum cook".
