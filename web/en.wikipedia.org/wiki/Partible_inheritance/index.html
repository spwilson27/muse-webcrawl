Partible inheritance

Partible inheritance is a system of inheritance in which property is apportioned among heirs, in contrast to primogeniture, where the eldest child (traditionally the eldest son) inherits the whole estate. In partible systems the inheritance may be divided equally among all children, among sons only, or according to other customary rules.

History

Partible inheritance was widespread in early medieval Europe. Among the Franks, the Salic law tradition of dividing a kingdom among sons led repeatedly to the partition of the Frankish realm, notably in the Treaty of Verdun (843). In Wales, the custom of gavelkind divided land among all sons, and gavelkind also survived in Kent in England until 1925. Islamic inheritance law prescribes fixed fractional shares for a wide range of relatives, making it a strongly partible system.

Napoleon's Civil Code of 1804 imposed equal partition among children in France, abolishing primogeniture in much of continental Europe. Critics have long blamed partibility for the progressive fragmentation of farms into uneconomic parcels; defenders regard it as more equitable and note that families could and did reconsolidate holdings by purchase and marriage strategies.

Consequences

The contrast between partible and impartible inheritance shaped family structure, migration, and economic development: regions of impartible inheritance tended to send younger sons away as soldiers, clergy, servants or emigrants, while partible regions retained labour on ever-smaller holdings. Anthropologists also distinguish partible inheritance from the inheritance of indivisible items, such as titles or heirlooms, which may pass by seniority even within otherwise partible systems.
