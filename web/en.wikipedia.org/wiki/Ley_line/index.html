Ley lines are straight alignments drawn between various historic structures, prehistoric sites and prominent landmarks. The concept was developed in the early 20th century by the English antiquarian Alfred Watkins, who claimed that such alignments were deliberately constructed as ancient trackways. In 1921, Watkins argued that ancient monuments in Britain — standing stones, burial mounds, churches and hill forts — were built along straight lines that could be traced across the landscape, and that these "leys" represented prehistoric trade routes marked by sighting points.

Alfred Watkins and the ley hunters

Watkins presented his theory in Early British Trackways (1922) and his most famous book, The Old Straight Track (1925). He derived the word "ley" from Old English place-name elements such as -ley and -leigh, meaning a clearing, which he noticed at points on his alignments. The idea attracted enthusiastic amateur "ley hunters" in the 1920s and 1930s but was dismissed by professional archaeologists, and the movement largely faded by the 1940s.

Earth mysteries revival

In the 1960s the concept was revived and transformed. Writers such as John Michell, in The View Over Atlantis (1969), combined ley lines with ideas about sacred geometry, UFOs, dowsing, Chinese feng shui and telluric "earth energies", making leys a central element of the "Earth mysteries" movement and of much New Age thought. Publications such as The Ley Hunter journal sustained this interpretation, in which ley lines are treated as channels of spiritual or geomagnetic energy rather than physical trackways.

Criticism

Archaeologists and statisticians argue that alignments are expected by chance: given the density of historic sites in Britain, straight lines connecting several of them will arise readily at random, and studies comparing ley claims with random point sets have found comparable numbers of alignments. Critics also note that the sites connected are often from widely different periods — Neolithic stones, medieval churches, and modern landmarks — and could not have formed a contemporary system. Mainstream scholarship therefore classifies ley lines as pseudoscience, though the idea remains influential in popular culture, fiction and alternative spirituality.
