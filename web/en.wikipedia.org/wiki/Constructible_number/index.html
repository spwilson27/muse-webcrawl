Constructible number

In geometry, a constructible number is a real number that can be obtained from the integers using a finite sequence of additions, subtractions, multiplications, divisions, and square roots of positive numbers. Equivalently, a length is constructible if a segment of that length can be constructed from a unit segment using compass and straightedge in a finite number of steps. A complex number is constructible if its real and imaginary parts are constructible real numbers, equivalently if it corresponds to a constructible point in the plane.

Algebraic characterisation

The constructible numbers form a field, and indeed a Euclidean field: they are closed under taking square roots of positive elements. A real number is constructible if and only if it lies in a tower of quadratic extensions of the rational numbers; consequently every constructible number is an algebraic number whose degree over the rationals is a power of two, and its minimal polynomial must have degree a power of two. The converse is not automatic: a number is constructible exactly when its Galois closure has degree a power of two (equivalently, when the Galois group of its minimal polynomial is a 2-group).

Impossible constructions

The algebraic characterisation settles the three classical problems of antiquity, all shown impossible in the 19th century. Doubling the cube requires constructing the cube root of 2, an algebraic number of degree 3, which is not a power of two — Pierre Wantzel proved this in 1837. Trisecting an arbitrary angle fails for the same reason. Squaring the circle requires constructing π, which Ferdinand von Lindemann proved in 1882 to be transcendental, hence not constructible at all.

Constructible polygons

A regular polygon with n sides is constructible if and only if n is the product of a power of two and distinct Fermat primes. This result, whose sufficiency was shown by Carl Friedrich Gauss (who constructed the regular heptadecagon, or 17-gon, in 1796 at the age of 19) and necessity by Wantzel, is known as the Gauss–Wantzel theorem. The only known Fermat primes are 3, 5, 17, 257 and 65537.
