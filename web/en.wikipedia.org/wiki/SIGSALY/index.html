SIGSALY

SIGSALY
Type: Secure voice encryption system
Developed by: Bell Telephone Laboratories
In service: 1943–1946
Notable users: Roosevelt, Churchill

SIGSALY was a secure voice communication system used in World War II for the highest-level Allied communications, including the transatlantic link between Franklin D. Roosevelt and Winston Churchill. Developed by Bell Telephone Laboratories, it was the first system to use pulse-code modulation (PCM) and the information-theoretically secure one-time pad.

How it worked

SIGSALY digitised speech using a vocoder that analysed the voice into a small set of slowly varying parameters. These were encrypted by combining them (modulo addition) with truly random noise recorded on special shellac phonograph records — the one-time key, distributed in pairs to both ends and destroyed after use. Twelve terminals were installed around the world; each installation weighed tens of tonnes, filled a room and required extensive air conditioning.

Legacy

Though cumbersome, SIGSALY was never broken, and its work at Bell Labs — involving figures such as Claude Shannon and Alan Turing, who visited during the project — laid foundations for digital audio, PCM telephony and modern cryptography.
