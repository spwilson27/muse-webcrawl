Maya numerals

Maya numerals are the numerals of the vigesimal (base-20) positional number system used by the pre-Columbian Maya civilisation. The system is one of the earliest to employ a true zero as a positional placeholder, independent of Old World developments.

The digits

Numbers from 0 to 19 are written additively with three symbols: a dot (worth 1), a horizontal bar (worth 5), and a shell-like glyph for zero. Thus 6 is one bar and one dot, 13 is two bars and three dots, and 19 is three bars and four dots. In carved inscriptions, "head variant" forms replaced the bars and dots with glyphs of deity heads.

Positional notation

Larger numbers are written vertically, with each higher position worth twenty times the one below: the lowest position counts units (1s), the next 20s, then 400s, and so on. In the Long Count calendar the system was slightly modified: the third position counted 18 × 20 = 360 (the tun, approximating the year) rather than 400. With this notation the Maya could record enormous numbers — inscriptions give dates spanning millions of years — supporting the computations of Maya astronomy.

Zero

The shell glyph, representing completion, appears in Long Count inscriptions by the first century BCE (some of the earliest examples come from Chiapa de Corzo and Tres Zapotes, in the broader Isthmian tradition). The Maya zero predates the widespread use of zero in Europe by many centuries and stands as one of the few independent inventions of the concept.
