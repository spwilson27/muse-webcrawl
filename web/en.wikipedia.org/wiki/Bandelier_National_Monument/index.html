Bandelier National Monument

Bandelier National Monument is a United States National Monument near Los Alamos in Sandoval and Los Alamos counties, New Mexico. It preserves the homes and territory of the Ancestral Puebloans of a later era in the southwestern United States. Most of the pueblo structures date to two eras, from 1150 to 1600 CE. The monument covers over 33,000 acres (13,000 ha) of rugged canyon and mesa country, including the Frijoles Canyon visitor area with cliff dwellings, kivas and petroglyphs.

History

The plateau and canyons were inhabited by Ancestral Pueblo people who built dwellings in the soft volcanic tuff of the Pajarito Plateau, farming the canyon floors along Frijoles Creek. Major villages such as Tyuonyi were occupied until the late 16th century, when drought and other pressures led residents to move toward the Rio Grande valley; their descendants live at Cochiti and San Ildefonso pueblos.

The monument is named for Adolph Bandelier, a Swiss-American anthropologist who researched the cultures of the area in the 1880s. President Woodrow Wilson proclaimed it a national monument in 1916. During the Manhattan Project, monument lands and lodge facilities were used to house personnel of the Los Alamos laboratory.

Features

  • Frijoles Canyon — visitor centre, the Main Loop Trail, Tyuonyi pueblo and cliff dwellings with ladders into cavate rooms.
  • Tsankawi — a detached unit with an unexcavated village and worn trails in the tuff.
  • Bandelier CCC Historic District — Civilian Conservation Corps buildings of the 1930s.
