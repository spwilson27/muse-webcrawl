Seismic magnitude scales

Seismic magnitude scales are used to describe the overall strength or "size" of an earthquake. These are distinguished from seismic intensity scales, which categorize the intensity or severity of ground shaking (quaking) caused by an earthquake at a given location. Magnitudes are usually determined from measurements of an earthquake's seismic waves as recorded on a seismogram. Several different magnitude scales have been developed over the years, each based on different aspects of the seismic waves.

Historical development

Prior to the development of modern magnitude scales, the only way to estimate an earthquake's size was the maximum degree of observed intensity, commonly using the Rossi–Forel or Modified Mercalli intensity scales. The first true magnitude scale was devised by Charles F. Richter in 1935. Richter defined the local magnitude, ML, as the logarithm to base 10 of the maximum amplitude (in thousandths of a millimetre) measured on a standard Wood–Anderson torsion seismometer at a distance of 100 kilometres from the earthquake's epicentre.

Richter's scale had limitations: it was calibrated for southern California and for shallow, moderate earthquakes recorded on a particular instrument. Later scales extended magnitude measurement to other regions, depths, and sizes.

Moment magnitude

The moment magnitude scale, developed by Thomas C. Hanks and Hiroo Kanamori in 1979, is based on the seismic moment of the earthquake, a measure of the total energy released in terms of the area of fault rupture, the average displacement, and the rigidity of the rock. Because it is tied to the physical size of the faulting, Mw does not saturate for the largest earthquakes and has become the standard scale used by agencies such as the United States Geological Survey for significant earthquakes.

Saturation

Most amplitude-based scales "saturate" — they stop increasing — for very large earthquakes, because the amplitudes they measure no longer grow in proportion to the earthquake's size. The Richter scale saturates around magnitude 7, and the surface-wave scale around 8. Moment magnitude avoids this problem.

Relation to energy

Magnitude is logarithmic: an increase of one whole number corresponds to roughly a 32-fold increase in released energy, and an increase of 0.2 corresponds approximately to a doubling of energy.
