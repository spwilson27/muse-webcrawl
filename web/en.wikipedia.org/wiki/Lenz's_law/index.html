Lenz's law

Lenz's law, formulated by Emil Lenz in 1834, states that the direction of an induced electric current in a conductor by a changing magnetic field is such that the magnetic field created by the induced current opposes changes in the initial magnetic field. It is a qualitative law that gives the direction of the induced electromotive force (EMF) and is reflected in the negative sign in Faraday's law of induction.

Statement

Faraday's law with Lenz's sign reads ℇ = −dΦB/dt: the induced EMF is equal in magnitude but opposite in sign to the rate of change of magnetic flux. If a bar magnet's north pole approaches a coil, the induced current flows so as to make the near face of the coil a north pole, repelling the magnet; if the magnet is withdrawn, the induced current reverses and attracts it. In this way Lenz's law embodies conservation of energy: work must be done against the opposing force, and that work is converted into electrical energy.

Applications

Lenz's law underlies eddy-current braking in trains and roller coasters, induction cooktops, metal detectors, and the behaviour of generators and transformers. Le Chatelier's principle is an analogous statement for chemical equilibria, sometimes described as an extension of the same idea of opposition to change.
