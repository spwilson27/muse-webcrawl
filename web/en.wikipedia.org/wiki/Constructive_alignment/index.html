Constructive alignment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Constructive alignment is a principle in educational design, developed by the educational psychologist John B. Biggs, stating that teaching and assessment should be aligned with the intended learning outcomes that students are expected to achieve. It is a central concept in outcomes-based education and is widely used in curriculum design in higher education.

The principle

The term combines two ideas. "Constructive" refers to constructivism: learners construct knowledge through their own activities rather than receiving it passively. "Alignment" refers to the deliberate coordination of three elements: (1) intended learning outcomes, stated as verbs describing what students should be able to do (drawing on Bloom's taxonomy and Biggs's own SOLO taxonomy); (2) teaching and learning activities likely to elicit those verbs; and (3) assessment tasks that genuinely test them and provide feedback. Biggs argued that students learn what they think will be assessed — "backwash" — so if assessment rewards memorisation while stated outcomes demand analysis, students will memorise. Alignment makes the assessment embody the intended outcomes, turning backwash into a force for good teaching.

Application and criticism

Constructive alignment underpins curriculum frameworks such as the Bologna Process in Europe and outcomes-based accreditation in Australia and elsewhere. Critics note that tightly specified outcomes can be reductionist, ill-suited to open-ended or creative disciplines, and can encourage teaching to the test if outcomes are poorly written; proponents reply that the model is a heuristic for coherence, not a mechanistic checklist.
