Fluoxetine, sold under the brand name Prozac among others, is an antidepressant of the selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) class. It is used for the treatment of major depressive disorder, obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD), bulimia nervosa, panic disorder, and premenstrual dysphoric disorder. It increases the level of serotonin in the brain by blocking its reabsorption (reuptake) into presynaptic cells.

Fluoxetine capsules sold as Prozac.

History

Fluoxetine was discovered at Eli Lilly and Company by scientists including David T. Wong, Jong-Sin Horng and F. P. Bymaster, with work led by Ray Fuller; it was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 1987. Prozac became a cultural phenomenon — the bestseller Listening to Prozac (1993) by Peter D. Kramer popularised debate about "cosmetic psychopharmacology" — and it was among the most widely prescribed antidepressants in the world before its patent expired in 2001.

Medical uses and side effects

Common side effects include nausea, insomnia, headache, anxiety, decreased libido and sexual dysfunction. Fluoxetine has an unusually long half-life and an active metabolite, norfluoxetine, which means the drug persists for weeks after discontinuation — reducing withdrawal symptoms but prolonging interactions, notably with monoamine oxidase inhibitors, where a five-week washout is required to avoid serotonin syndrome. Like other antidepressants, it carries regulatory warnings about increased risk of suicidal thoughts in children, adolescents and young adults.

Pharmacology

Fluoxetine blocks the serotonin transporter (SERT), raising extracellular serotonin. It is metabolised in the liver, largely by CYP2D6, and is itself a potent inhibitor of that enzyme.
