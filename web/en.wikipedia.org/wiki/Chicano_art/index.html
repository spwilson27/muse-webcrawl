Chicano art

Chicano art is an artistic movement and body of work produced by Mexican-American artists, emerging from the Chicano Movement (El Movimiento) of the 1960s and 1970s. It expresses the cultural, political and social experience of Chicano communities in the United States, drawing on Mexican muralism, pre-Columbian imagery, Catholic iconography, and the visual culture of the barrio.

A community mural in the Chicano tradition

Origins

The movement took shape alongside the civil-rights struggles of the 1960s: the farm workers' movement led by César Chávez and Dolores Huerta, the student walkouts in Los Angeles (1968), and demands for Chicano studies in universities. Inspired by the Mexican muralists Los Tres Grandes — Diego Rivera, José Clemente Orozco and David Alfaro Siqueiros — artists painted large public murals on the walls of community buildings, housing projects and freeway pillars, most famously in San Diego's Chicano Park (established 1970) and throughout East Los Angeles.

Themes and media

Recurring themes include Aztec and Maya mythology, the Virgin of Guadalupe, revolutionary heroes such as Emiliano Zapata and Pancho Villa, migrant labour, family, and resistance to discrimination. Alongside murals, the movement produced:

  • Silkscreen posters for strikes, boycotts and cultural events, disseminated by collectives such as San Francisco's La Raza Silkscreen Center.
  • Easel painting and printmaking, organised through groups like Los Four (Los Angeles), the Royal Chicano Air Force (Sacramento), and Mujeres Muralistas (San Francisco).
  • Lowrider customisation and other forms of vernacular and folk art.
  • Performance and conceptual work, notably by the collective Asco in Los Angeles.

Institutional recognition

For decades Chicano art was largely excluded from mainstream museums. The landmark travelling exhibition CARAS VEMOS: Chicano Art, 1965–1985 (1990), organised by the Wight Art Gallery at UCLA, was the first major national survey of the field. Subsequent exhibitions and scholarly work have established Chicano art as a significant chapter of American art history, and major museums have since built permanent collections in the area.
