Deforestation of the Amazon rainforest

The Amazon rainforest, covering much of the Amazon basin of South America (about 60% in Brazil), has lost a substantial fraction of its forest cover—roughly 17–20% since 1970—mainly to cattle ranching, soy farming, logging, mining, roads and settlement. Deforestation releases stored carbon, threatens biodiversity and Indigenous peoples, and risks tipping parts of the forest toward savanna.

Amazon deforestation
Main drivers: Cattle ranching, soy, logging, mining
Institutions: INPE (satellite monitoring), IBAMA
Peak year (Brazil, modern series): 2004
Risk: Dieback / tipping point at ~20–25% loss

History and rates

Large-scale clearing accelerated under Brazil's military government (1964–85) with highways such as the Trans-Amazonian and settlement schemes. Rates measured by INPE's PRODES satellites peaked in 2004 (~27,800 km²/yr in Brazilian Amazon), fell sharply to 2012 under enforcement, soy and beef moratoria and protected areas, rose again in the late 2010s–early 2020s, then dropped markedly from 2023 under renewed enforcement under President Lula.

Causes and consequences

Cattle pasture occupies the majority of cleared land; soy (often for animal feed exports), land speculation and illegal land grabbing (grilagem), gold mining and infrastructure also drive loss. Consequences include carbon emissions, reduced rainfall recycling ("flying rivers"), fires, species extinction, and violence against Indigenous and environmental defenders. Scientists including Carlos Nobre warn of a dieback tipping point beyond roughly 20–25% deforestation combined with climate change.

Responses

Responses include Indigenous territories and conservation units (which show low deforestation), the Amazon Fund, supply-chain commitments, and international climate finance, alongside persistent enforcement challenges.
