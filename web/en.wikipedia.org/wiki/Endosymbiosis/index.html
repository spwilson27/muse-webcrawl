Endosymbiosis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Endosymbiosis
Also called: Endosymbiotic theory / serial endosymbiosis theory
Championed by: Lynn Margulis (1967 onwards)

Endosymbiosis, in its evolutionary sense, is the theory that key organelles of eukaryotic cells — mitochondria and chloroplasts — originated as free-living bacteria taken inside another cell and retained as permanent partners (endosymbionts).

Evidence

Mitochondria and chloroplasts resemble bacteria in size, have their own circular DNA and bacteria-like ribosomes, are bounded by double membranes, and reproduce by fission. Gene sequencing places mitochondria among the alphaproteobacteria and chloroplasts among the cyanobacteria. The theory was proposed in early form by Konstantin Mereschkowski (1905) and Ivan Wallin (1920s), then revived and established by Lynn Margulis from 1967, against initial ridicule.

Consequences

The acquisition of mitochondria — the site of cellular respiration and most ATP production — likely preceded and enabled the complexity of eukaryotic life; chloroplasts arrived later in the lineage leading to plants and algae. Secondary endosymbiosis, in which whole algae were engulfed, spread photosynthesis across many more groups. The theory is now a cornerstone of evolutionary biology, and most scientists regard the evidence as conclusive.
