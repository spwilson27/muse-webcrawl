Dynamo

A dynamo is an electrical generator that creates direct current using a commutator. Dynamos were the first electrical generators capable of delivering power for industry, and the foundation upon which many other electrical conversion devices were based, including the electric motor, the alternating-current alternator, and the rotary converter.

History

The first dynamo based on Faraday's principle of electromagnetic induction was built in 1832 by Hippolyte Pixii, using a permanent magnet rotated by a crank; a commutator converted the alternating induced current into pulses of direct current. Practical self-exciting dynamos followed in the 1860s with the work of Werner von Siemens and Charles Wheatstone, and Zénobe Gramme's ring armature machine of 1871 made continuous DC generation commercially viable for arc lighting and industry.

Construction

A dynamo consists of a rotating armature winding, a field structure providing a magnetic field (from permanent magnets or, in larger machines, field coils fed by part of the output), and a commutator with brushes that reverses the connections of the armature coils each half-turn so that the external current flows in one direction.

Uses

Dynamos powered early electricity networks and industrial machinery before being largely displaced by alternators with rectifiers. The term survives in everyday use for small bicycle generators (including "bottle dynamos" and hub dynamos) and hand-cranked generators, most of which actually produce alternating current.
