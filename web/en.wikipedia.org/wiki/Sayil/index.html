Sayil

Sayil
Region: Yucatán, Mexico
Culture: Maya
Period: Terminal Classic
Style: Puuc

Sayil is an ancient Maya archaeological site located in the Mexican state of Yucatán, in the Puuc region southwest of the modern city of Mérida. Together with nearby sites such as Uxmal, Kabah, and Labná, Sayil forms part of a UNESCO World Heritage area recognized for its distinctive Puuc architectural style. The site flourished during the Terminal Classic period, roughly between AD 800 and 1000.

Architecture

The most famous structure at Sayil is the Great Palace (Palacio Norte), a massive three-storey building about 85 metres long. It is one of the largest palaces known from the Maya world and is notable for its rows of columns and its elaborate stone-mosaic upper façade, decorated with masks of the rain god Chaac and with descending "diving god" figures. Unlike earlier Maya palaces, the Great Palace appears to have combined residential, administrative, and ceremonial functions, housing a large population of elites.

Other structures at the site include the Mirador temple, a ball court, numerous platforms, and extensive chultunes (underground cisterns) used to store rainwater — essential in the Puuc hills, which lack surface water sources such as cenotes.

History and settlement

At its height, Sayil may have supported a population of around 10,000 people in the city itself and several thousand more in the surrounding area. The city depended on intensive agriculture and on an artificial system of water storage, including hundreds of chultunes and large aguadas. Sayil was abandoned around AD 1000, likely as part of the broader collapse of Puuc cities in the early Postclassic period.

Exploration

The site was documented in the 19th century by explorers John Lloyd Stephens and Frederick Catherwood, and later studied by Teobert Maler and researchers of the Middle American Research Institute. Sayil is open to the public and forms part of the Puuc Route of archaeological sites in Yucatán.
