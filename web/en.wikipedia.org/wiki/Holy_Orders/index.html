Holy Orders

In certain Christian traditions, Holy Orders is the sacrament (or rite) by which men (and, in some churches, women) are ordained as bishops, priests, or deacons — the three orders of ordained ministry. The Roman Catholic Church, the Eastern Orthodox Church, the Oriental Orthodox Churches, and the Anglican Communion all recognize Holy Orders as a sacrament instituted by Christ, conferred by the laying on of hands by a bishop and the prayer of consecration, and understood to imprint an indelible sacramental character on the recipient.

The three orders

  • Episcopate (bishop) — the fullness of Holy Orders. Bishops are understood to succeed the apostles (apostolic succession) and alone can ordain others.
  • Presbyterate (priest) — priests act as co-workers of the bishop, preaching, celebrating the Eucharist and other sacraments, and shepherding congregations.
  • Diaconate (deacon) — deacons serve in liturgy, word, and charity; in the Latin Church the diaconate may be "transitional" (a step toward priesthood) or "permanent," including married men.

Practice across traditions

In Catholic and Orthodox practice, ordination is reserved to baptized men, and in the Latin Church priests (with rare exceptions) promise celibacy, while Orthodox and Eastern Catholic priests may be married if married before ordination. Many Anglican provinces and some other churches ordain women to all three orders; the Catholic Church teaches it has no authority to do so. Historically, the Latin rite also conferred "minor orders" (porter, lector, exorcist, acolyte) and the subdiaconate; after the Second Vatican Council these were reformed by Pope Paul VI (1972) into the instituted ministries of lector and acolyte, leaving the three major orders conferred by the sacrament.

Theology

Ordination is understood as configuring the ordained to Christ the head and shepherd (in persona Christi in priestly acts). The essential rite is the imposition of hands with the consecratory prayer; accompanying rites include anointing with chrism, the giving of the instruments of office (such as a chalice and paten for priests), and vesting.
