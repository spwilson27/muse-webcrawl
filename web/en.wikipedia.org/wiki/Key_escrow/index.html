Key escrow

Key escrow arrangement

Key escrow (also known as a "fair" cryptosystem) is an arrangement in which the keys needed to decrypt encrypted data are held in escrow by a trusted third party, so that they can be released under certain circumstances — for example, to a government agency acting under a court order, or to an organization recovering its own data. The concept is closely related to key recovery systems used legitimately in enterprises so that encrypted data is not lost if an employee's key is lost.

Government proposals and the Clipper chip

Key escrow became a major public controversy during the "Crypto Wars" of the 1990s. In 1993 the United States government proposed the Escrowed Encryption Standard, implemented in the Clipper chip, in which each encryption device contained a unique key, copies of which would be held by two government agencies and released to law enforcement with legal authorization. The proposal was widely opposed by the technology industry and civil-liberties groups, and was effectively abandoned after researcher Matt Blaze demonstrated a flaw in the protocol in 1994 and after strong commercial resistance.

Criticism

Critics argue that escrow databases of keys would be high-value targets for attackers and abusive insiders, that building such systems at scale is technically fraught, and that mandated escrow amounts to deliberately weakening security. Proponents, particularly in law enforcement, argue that widespread unbreakable encryption frustrates lawful investigations — the "going dark" problem. A prominent 2015 report by leading cryptographers, Keys Under Doormats, renewed the technical case against exceptional-access systems.
