Stocks

Stocks are devices used in former times as a form of corporal punishment and public humiliation. The stocks consist of placing boards around the ankles and wrists of the offender; the boards have holes in which the limbs are placed, and the two halves of the board are hinged and locked together. The offender is forced to sit or stand in a public place, exposed to whatever treatment passers-by may choose to inflict, which historically included being pelted with rotten food, mud, and stones, and being urinated or defecated upon.

History

Stocks were used throughout Europe from the medieval period into the 19th century. In England, a statute of 1351 (the Statute of Labourers) required every town and village to maintain a set of stocks, and they were commonly used to punish petty offences such as drunkenness, cheating, and disorderly conduct. The punishment typically lasted several hours. Unlike the pillory, in which the head and hands were confined and the victim stood upright, the stocks usually confined the feet (and sometimes hands) with the victim seated.

In colonial America the stocks were widely used, and in some Puritan communities they stood on the village green alongside the pillory and whipping post. Their use declined during the 19th century as public punishment fell out of favour; the last recorded use in the United Kingdom was in 1872 at Newbury, Berkshire. Statutory provision for the punishment was removed in England in 1837, though the stocks themselves remained standing in many villages as historical relics.

Legacy

Many sets of stocks survive in English villages, often on village greens or in churchyards, and replica stocks are a feature of historical festivals. The phrase "stock" characters and the modern notion of the "laughing stock" derive from the humiliation associated with the device.
