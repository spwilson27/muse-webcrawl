Wavelength-division multiplexing

In fiber-optic communications, wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) is a technology which multiplexes a number of optical carrier signals onto a single optical fiber by using different wavelengths (colours) of laser light. This technique enables bidirectional communications over a single strand of fiber as well as multiplication of capacity, and it underpins modern long-haul and submarine telecommunication networks.

Principle

A multiplexer at the transmitter combines several signals, each generated by a laser tuned to a precise wavelength, onto one fiber. At the receiver, a demultiplexer separates the wavelengths onto separate detectors. Because the channels occupy different optical frequencies, they do not interfere, and each can be modulated independently at electronic speeds. Wavelength assignments for dense systems are standardised by the ITU-T on a grid in the 1,550 nm band (C band), with channel spacings of 100, 50 or 25 GHz.

Types of WDM

Coarse WDM (CWDM) uses widely spaced channels (20 nm apart) across the 1270–1610 nm range; it needs no temperature-controlled lasers and is used in metropolitan and access networks. Dense WDM (DWDM) packs many closely spaced channels into the C band (and sometimes the L band), typically 40, 80 or more channels per fiber, each carrying 100 Gbit/s or more with coherent modulation, giving aggregate capacities of tens of terabits per second on a single fiber pair.

History

WDM concepts were explored in the 1970s, but practical systems depended on stable narrow-linewidth lasers and low-loss fiber amplifiers. The erbium-doped fiber amplifier (EDFA), developed in the late 1980s, made WDM commercially transformative in the mid-1990s because it could amplify all channels at once without electronic regeneration, enabling the rapid growth of internet backbone capacity.
