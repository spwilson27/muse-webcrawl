Collective bargaining

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Collective bargaining is a process of negotiation between employers and a group of employees (usually represented by a trade union) aimed at agreements to regulate working salaries, working conditions, benefits and other aspects of workers' compensation and rights. The employees' representatives bargain over a contract (collective agreement) governing the workplace for a fixed term, in exchange (explicitly or implicitly) for industrial peace during it.

Systems

Legal frameworks define the game: recognition and duty-to-bargain rules (the US NLRA 1935, with its representation elections and unfair-labour-practice policing; enterprise-level bargaining thereafter dominant in the US and Japan), sectoral bargaining with administrative extension (France, Spain, the Nordics' pattern bargaining), and co-determination hybrids (German works councils handling plant-level matters under sectoral agreements). Bargaining power tracks labour-market tightness, strike credibility, legal immunities and employer organisation; outcomes shape wage structure economy-wide (bargaining coverage is a prime explanation of cross-national wage inequality differences). Contemporary stress tests: gig-economy classification fights (are app workers employees who can bargain at all? — competition law's collision with labour law), public-sector bargaining politics, and the return of cost-of-living militancy in the 2022–24 inflation, which revived strike statistics not seen in decades in several countries.
