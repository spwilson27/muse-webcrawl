Paul Ehrlich (14 March 1854 – 20 August 1915) was a German physician and scientist who worked in the fields of haematology, immunology and antimicrobial chemotherapy. He is credited with finding a cure for syphilis in 1909 and with inventing the precursor technique to Gram staining bacteria. With Élie Metchnikoff he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1908 "in recognition of their work on immunity." Ehrlich popularised the concept of a "magic bullet" — a drug that targets a disease-causing organism without harming the patient — and is regarded as the founder of chemotherapy.

Early research

Ehrlich was born in Strehlen in Silesia. As a medical student he became fascinated by the selective staining of tissues by synthetic dyes, a line of work that led to his classification of white blood cells (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils), methods for staining the tubercle bacillus, and a diazo reaction used in diagnosing typhoid fever. After contracting tuberculosis he spent two years recovering in Egypt before returning to Berlin, where Robert Koch appointed him to his Institute for Infectious Diseases.

Immunology

Working on diphtheria antitoxin in the 1890s, Ehrlich developed the first quantitative standardisation of serum potency, enabling reliable antitoxin production with Emil von Behring. His "side-chain theory" (1897) proposed that cells bear chemical side-chains (receptors) that bind toxins and nutrients; when a toxin locked into a side-chain, the cell produced excess copies that were shed into the blood as antibodies. The theory — an early picture of receptor biology and antibody formation — underlies his Nobel citation and much of modern immunology.

Salvarsan and chemotherapy

From 1904 Ehrlich directed the Georg-Speyer-Haus in Frankfurt and pursued the systematic screening of chemical compounds against pathogens. Building on the observation that the arsenic compound atoxyl killed trypanosomes, his laboratory tested hundreds of arsenicals. In 1909 his assistant Sahachiro Hata found that compound 606 — arsphenamine, marketed from 1910 as Salvarsan — cured syphilis. Salvarsan and its successor Neosalvarsan were the first effective treatments for syphilis and the world's first blockbuster drugs, vindicating Ehrlich's vision of rationally designed chemotherapy. He died of a stroke in Bad Homburg in 1915.

Legacy

The Paul Ehrlich Institute, Germany's federal institute for vaccines and biomedicines, bears his name, as does the Paul Ehrlich and Ludwig Darmstaedter Prize. His life was dramatised in the 1940 film Dr. Ehrlich's Magic Bullet. The methods he pioneered — screening compound libraries, structure–activity optimisation and receptor-targeted therapy — remain the working core of pharmaceutical research.
