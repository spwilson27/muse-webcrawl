Armoured fighting vehicle

An armoured fighting vehicle (British English) or armored fighting vehicle (American English), abbreviated AFV, is an armed combat vehicle protected by armour, generally combining operational mobility with direct and indirect firepower. AFVs can be wheeled or tracked and include tanks, infantry fighting vehicles, armoured personnel carriers, self-propelled artillery, and specialised engineer and reconnaissance vehicles.

History

Armoured vehicles trace their origins to armoured cars built in the early 20th century and used in the First World War. The development of the tank by Britain and France in 1915–16, first used in battle in 1916, led to a rapid expansion of armoured warfare. During the Second World War, mechanised formations built around tanks, half-tracks, and self-propelled guns transformed land warfare, and the Cold War produced large families of tracked and wheeled AFVs designed for operations on a nuclear battlefield.

Classification

  • Tanks — main battle tanks combine heavy armour, a large-calibre gun, and cross-country mobility.
  • Infantry fighting vehicles — carry infantry into battle and support them with cannon and missile fire.
  • Armoured personnel carriers — transport infantry with protection against small arms and shell splinters.
  • Self-propelled artillery — howitzers, mortars, and rocket launchers mounted on armoured chassis.
  • Armoured cars and reconnaissance vehicles — fast, lighter wheeled vehicles for scouting and security.
  • Specialised vehicles — combat engineer vehicles, armoured recovery vehicles, bridge-layers, and air-defence vehicles.

Design

AFV design balances the "iron triangle" of firepower, protection, and mobility. Armour may be steel, aluminium, or composite, supplemented by reactive armour, slat armour, and active protection systems. Modern vehicles add thermal sights, fire-control computers, battlefield networks, and protection against mines and improvised explosive devices.
