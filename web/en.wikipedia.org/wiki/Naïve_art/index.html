Naïve art

Naïve art is usually defined as visual art that is created by a person who lacks the formal education and training of a professional artist in anatomy, art history, technique, perspective and other matters. When a trained artist attempts to create this type of "naïve" work, the result is sometimes called pseudo-naïve art or faux naïve art.

Characteristics

Naïve art is recognized by its childlike simplicity of execution and vision. Paintings may have an awkward perspective, an intuitive rather than calculated sense of scale, unrefined brushwork and patches of flat colour, yet the best naïve works are also noted for their freshness, charm and meticulous detail.

History and recognition

The French customs officer Henri Rousseau (1844–1910), nicknamed "Le Douanier", is regarded as the archetypal naïve painter; his jungle scenes were championed by avant-garde artists including Picasso. In the 20th century, artists such as Grandma Moses (Anna Mary Robertson Moses) in the United States, the Croatian school centred on Hlebine, and the Polish painter Nikifor brought naïve art to wide popularity. Museums devoted to the genre include the Musée international d'Art naïf in Nice and institutions in Zagreb and Moscow.

Distinctions

Naïve art overlaps with folk art and outsider art but is not identical to either: folk art is embedded in a community's traditional culture, while outsider art (art brut) is defined by the artist's position outside the art world altogether.
