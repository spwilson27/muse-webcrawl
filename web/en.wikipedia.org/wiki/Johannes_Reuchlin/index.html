Johannes Reuchlin (29 January 1455 – 30 June 1522) was a German Catholic humanist and scholar of Greek and Hebrew, and a central figure of the German Renaissance. A jurist by profession, he served the counts of Württemberg and acted as a judge of the Swabian League.

Reuchlin's De rudimentis Hebraicis (1506) was the first Hebrew grammar and lexicon published by a Christian scholar, and he promoted the study of Hebrew and of Jewish mystical literature, the Kabbalah, in works such as De arte cabalistica.

The Reuchlin affair

When the convert Johannes Pfefferkorn, backed by the Dominicans of Cologne, pressed for the confiscation and destruction of Jewish books, Reuchlin argued in his Augenspiegel (1511) that the books should be studied, not burned. The ensuing controversy divided European humanists — who rallied to Reuchlin in the satirical Letters of Obscure Men — from the scholastic establishment, and became a prelude to the Reformation.
