Galician (Galician: galego), also known as Galego, is a Western Ibero-Romance language. Around 2.4 million people have at least some degree of competence in the language, mainly in Galicia, an autonomous community located in northwestern Spain, where it has official status along with Spanish. The language is also spoken in some border zones of the neighbouring Spanish regions of Asturias and Castile and León.

Galician is part of the Galician-Portuguese family of languages which originated in the region covering north-western present-day Spain and northern present-day Portugal. Portuguese evolved from the variant of Galician-Portuguese spoken south of the Minho river.

History

Galician-Portuguese developed from Vulgar Latin in the former Roman province of Gallaecia. In the Middle Ages it was the prestige language of lyric poetry (the cantigas) throughout the Christian kingdoms of the Iberian Peninsula, used by poets including Alfonso X of Castile. From the 15th century, political separation drove written Galician and Portuguese apart; Galilian entered a long period of decline (the Séculos Escuros, "Dark Centuries") under Castilian dominance, followed by a 19th-century literary revival, the Rexurdimento.

Status and standardisation

Galician is co-official in Galicia under the 1978 Spanish Constitution and the 1981 Statute of Autonomy. The official standard is regulated by the Royal Galician Academy (Real Academia Galega). A minority "reintegrationist" movement advocates a standard closer to Portuguese, reflecting the languages' common origin.

Dialects and phonology

Galician is divided into three main dialect blocks (western, central and eastern) with sub-variants. Distinctive features include the maintenance of seven vowel phonemes, the absence of the Castilian voiceless velar fricative /x/, and regional phenomena such as gheada (pronouncing /g/ as a fricative [ħ] or [x]) and seseo.
