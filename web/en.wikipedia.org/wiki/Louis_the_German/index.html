Louis the German

Louis the German (German: Ludwig der Deutsche; c. 806 – 28 August 876), also known as Louis II, was the first king of East Francia, the eastern portion of the Carolingian Empire, from 843 until his death. A grandson of Charlemagne and the third son of Emperor Louis the Pious, he received the eastern territories under the Treaty of Verdun in 843 and is often regarded as the founder of the German kingdom within the Frankish world.

Early life and Bavaria

Louis was born around 806, the third son of Louis the Pious and Ermengarde of Hesbaye. In the Ordinatio Imperii of 817 he was assigned Bavaria as a sub-kingdom, and he ruled there from 817, taking up residence in Regensburg. He married Hemma, sister of his stepmother the Empress Judith, in 827. When his father attempted to revise the succession in favour of his youngest son Charles, Louis joined his brothers Lothair and Pepin in the civil wars of the 830s, at times rebelling and at times reconciling with the emperor.

Treaty of Verdun and East Francia

After Louis the Pious died in 840, Lothair claimed the whole empire. Louis allied with his half-brother Charles the Bald, defeating Lothair at the Battle of Fontenoy in 841 and sealing their alliance with the Oaths of Strasbourg in 842, sworn in early forms of French and German. The Treaty of Verdun (843) partitioned the empire: Louis received the lands east of the Rhine — Franconia, Saxony, Thuringia, Alemannia and Bavaria — the kingdom that became East Francia.

Reign

Louis spent much of his reign defending his eastern frontiers against the Slavs, particularly the Moravians under Rastislav and Svatopluk, and against Viking raids in the north. He intervened repeatedly in the affairs of West Francia and quarrelled with Charles the Bald over Lotharingia; by the Treaty of Meerssen (870) he gained the eastern half of Lothair II's former kingdom. His later years were troubled by the rebellions of his sons, notably Carloman. Louis died at Frankfurt on 28 August 876, just as Charles the Bald was invading his kingdom; his sons defeated Charles at Andernach weeks later. East Francia was divided among Louis's three sons: Carloman, Louis the Younger and Charles the Fat.

Legacy

The epithet "the German" (Germanicus) was applied by later writers to distinguish Louis as ruler of the German-speaking east. His long reign consolidated East Francia as a durable political entity and a precursor of the medieval Kingdom of Germany.
