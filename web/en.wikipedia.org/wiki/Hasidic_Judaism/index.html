Hasidic Judaism (Hebrew: חסידות, romanized: Ḥăsīdūt; Yiddish: חסידיזם) is a Jewish religious group that arose as a spiritual revival movement in the territory of present-day Western Ukraine during the 18th century, and spread rapidly throughout Eastern Europe. Israel ben Eliezer, the Baal Shem Tov, is regarded as its founding father, and his disciples developed and disseminated it. Present-day Hasidism is a sub-group within Haredi Judaism and is known for its religious and social conservatism and social seclusion.

Teachings

Drawing on Lurianic Kabbalah, Hasidism emphasises God's immanence in all things (panentheism), the ideal of devekut (cleaving to God in every act, including mundane ones), and worship through joy, song and dance rather than asceticism. Central to Hasidic life is the figure of the tzadik, or Rebbe — a charismatic hereditary leader believed to channel divine blessing to his followers, who gather at his court, seek his counsel, and join in communal meals (tish) and melodies (nigunim). Hasidic storytelling, most famously the tales of Nachman of Breslov, is a distinctive literary legacy.

History

The movement began amid the hardships of Polish-Lithuanian Jewry after the Khmelnytsky massacres, and clashed fiercely with opponents (the Mitnagdim), led by the Vilna Gaon, who imposed bans of excommunication on Hasidim in the 1770s–80s. In the 19th century Hasidism organised into dynastic courts (e.g., Belz, Ger, Satmar, Lubavitch, Breslov). Most of its East European heartland was destroyed in the Holocaust; survivors rebuilt large communities in Israel (Jerusalem, Bnei Brak) and the United States (Brooklyn), which today have among the highest birth rates in the Jewish world, numbering well over half a million people.
