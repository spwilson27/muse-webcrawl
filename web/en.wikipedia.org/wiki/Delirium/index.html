Delirium

SpecialtyPsychiatry, geriatrics, intensive care
SymptomsConfusion, inattention, altered consciousness, hallucinations, fluctuating course
OnsetAcute — hours to days
CausesInfection, medications, dehydration, electrolyte disorders, surgery, alcohol withdrawal
DifferentialDementia, psychosis, depression
[Image: Delirium in an elderly hospital patient]
Delirium is common and often missed in hospitalised older patients

Delirium is an acute disturbance of attention and cognition that develops over a short period (hours to days), fluctuates during the course of the day, and is caused by a medical condition, substance intoxication or withdrawal, or multiple causes. It is distinct from dementia, which is chronic and gradual, though delirium is frequently superimposed on dementia. Delirium may be hyperactive (agitated), hypoactive (quiet and withdrawn — the most commonly missed form), or mixed.

Causes and risk factors

Delirium occurs in roughly 10–30% of older hospital patients and up to 80% of intensive-care patients. Common precipitants include infections (especially urinary tract infection and pneumonia), dehydration and electrolyte imbalance, pain, urinary retention, sleep deprivation, and medications with anticholinergic effects; alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal cause the distinctive delirium tremens. Predisposing factors include advanced age, pre-existing dementia, sensory impairment and frailty.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is clinical, aided by screening tools such as the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM). Management centres on finding and treating the underlying cause, reorientation, hydration, mobilization, ensuring glasses and hearing aids are used, and normalizing sleep; antipsychotic drugs are reserved for severe agitation and do not shorten the episode. Delirium is associated with increased mortality, longer hospital stays and a higher subsequent risk of dementia, and is considered largely preventable through multicomponent interventions.
