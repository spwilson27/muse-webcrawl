Pashtuns (Pashto: پښتانه, Pəx̌tānə), also known historically as Afghans, are an Iranian ethnic group primarily residing in southern and eastern Afghanistan and northwestern Pakistan. They speak Pashto, an Eastern Iranian language, and traditionally follow the Pashtunwali code of conduct. Pashtuns are the largest ethnic group in Afghanistan and the second-largest in Pakistan.

History

The origins of the Pashtuns are debated, with plausible links to ancient peoples of the region such as the Bactrians and Scythians. Pashtun dynasties ruled large parts of the region over the centuries, including the Hotak dynasty and the Durrani Empire founded by Ahmad Shah Durrani in 1747, which is regarded as the foundation of the modern state of Afghanistan. During the British colonial period, the Durand Line of 1893 divided Pashtun territory between British India and Afghanistan — a division that persists between Pakistan and Afghanistan today.

Culture

Pashtun society is traditionally organised by tribe and clan, with decisions made by a jirga, an assembly of elders. The pre-Islamic ethical code of Pashtunwali emphasises hospitality (melmastia), asylum (nanawatai), and honour (nang). Pashto literature has a rich poetic tradition, including the 17th-century warrior-poet Khushal Khan Khattak and the Sufi poet Rahman Baba.

Distribution

Significant Pashtun populations live in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and northern Balochistan in Pakistan and across southern and eastern Afghanistan; Karachi hosts one of the largest urban Pashtun populations in the world. A large diaspora exists in the Gulf states, Europe, and North America.
