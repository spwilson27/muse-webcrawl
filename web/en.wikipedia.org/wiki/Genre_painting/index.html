Genre painting

Genre painting (or genre art) is the depiction of scenes of everyday life — markets, households, taverns, celebrations and work — usually with ordinary people and no identifiable historical or religious narrative. The term derives from the French genre in the hierarchy of genres established by academic art theory, where such scenes ranked below history painting.

History

Scenes of daily life appeared in ancient and medieval art, but genre painting flourished as an independent category in the Northern Renaissance and especially in the Dutch Golden Age of the 17th century, with artists such as Jan Steen, Johannes Vermeer, Adriaen van Ostade and Gerard de Hooch painting intimate domestic interiors, peasant festivities and merry companies, often with moralizing undertones.

Later developments

In the 18th century, French and English artists including Jean-Baptiste Greuze and William Hogarth used genre scenes for sentiment and social commentary. Nineteenth-century realists such as Gustave Courbet elevated everyday subjects to the scale of history painting, and genre scenes remain a staple of figurative art worldwide.

Related forms

Genre painting overlaps with the Spanish bodegón, the Bamboccianti school in Rome, and — in photography and cinema — later documentary depictions of ordinary life.
