National personification

National personification
TypeAllegorical figure
ExamplesUncle Sam (United States), Britannia (Britain), Marianne (France), Germania (Germany), Bharat Mata (India)
OriginsClassical antiquity; widespread from the 18th–19th centuries

A national personification is an anthropomorphic or allegorical figure representing a nation or its people, used in art, propaganda, political cartoons, and national symbolism. Personifications frequently take the form of a woman in classical dress — a tradition inherited from the Roman practice of personifying provinces and virtues — or of an "everyman" character embodying the supposed national character.

Examples

Famous personifications include Britannia for Britain, a helmeted woman with shield and trident derived from a Roman goddess; Marianne for the French Republic; Germania for Germany; Italia Turrita for Italy; Bharat Mata ("Mother India") for India; and Columbia and later Uncle Sam for the United States. Male stereotypes such as John Bull (England), the German Michel, and the Russian bear serve a similar function in cartoons. Many nations also use national animals or heroes — such as the Polish White Eagle or the Finnish Maiden — as personifications.

Uses

National personifications appear on coins, stamps, monuments, and war posters (for example, the "Lord Kitchener Wants You" and "I Want You" recruiting posters), and remain staples of editorial cartooning, where the figure stands in for the state in commentary on international events.
