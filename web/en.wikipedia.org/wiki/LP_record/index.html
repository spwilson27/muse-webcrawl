LP record

The LP (from "long playing" or "long play") is an analog sound storage medium, a phonograph record format characterized by its use of the "microgroove" pressing technology and a speed of 33⅓ rpm; a diameter of 12 or 10 inches (30 or 25 cm); and primarily vinyl (a copolymer of vinyl chloride acetate) composition.

A vinyl LP record

History

The LP was introduced by Columbia Records in June 1948 at a press conference in New York City, with the format developed under Peter Goldmark's team. It competed with RCA Victor's 45 rpm format in the "War of the Speeds" of the late 1940s; the two formats eventually settled into complementary roles, with LPs used for albums and 45s for singles.

A microgroove LP could hold up to about 22–26 minutes of audio per side, far more than the 3–5 minutes of the older 78 rpm shellac discs. This transformed the album into the dominant artistic format for popular music from the 1960s onward and made long-form classical recordings practical. LPs offered higher fidelity and lower surface noise than shellac records, and the 12-inch sleeve became a notable canvas for cover art.

Decline and revival

LP sales declined steeply after the introduction of the Compact Disc in the 1980s, and by the early 1990s the format had nearly vanished from mainstream retail. From the late 2000s, vinyl records — LPs foremost among them — experienced a sustained revival driven by collectors, audiophiles, and younger listeners; in the early 2020s, LP revenue surpassed CD revenue in the United States for the first time since the 1980s.

Format details

Standard LPs are pressed on vinyl weighing 120–140 grams, with audiophile reissues often at 180 grams. Playback requires a turntable with a stylus that tracks the spiral groove; RIAA equalization standardizes the recording curve for playback.
