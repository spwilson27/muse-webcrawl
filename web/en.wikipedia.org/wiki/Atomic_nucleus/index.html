Atomic nucleus

Atomic nucleus
CompositionProtons and neutrons (nucleons)
Size~1.6–15 femtometres
Discovered1911 (Rutherford)

The atomic nucleus is the small, dense central region of an atom, consisting of protons and neutrons (collectively called nucleons) bound together by the strong nuclear force. Although the nucleus contains more than 99.97% of the atom's mass, its radius — roughly 1.6 to 15 femtometres — is about ten thousand times smaller than the atom as a whole.

Discovery and models

Ernest Rutherford proposed the nuclear model in 1911 after the Geiger–Marsden gold-foil experiment showed that alpha particles were occasionally deflected at large angles, implying a tiny, massive, positively charged centre. James Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932. Subsequent models include the liquid-drop model, which explains nuclear binding energies, and the nuclear shell model, which accounts for "magic numbers" of nucleons analogously to electron shells.

Properties

The number of protons (the atomic number, Z) determines the chemical element; nuclei with the same Z but different neutron numbers are isotopes. The binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron-56 and nickel-62, which is why fusion powers stars up to iron and fission of heavy nuclei releases energy. Unstable nuclei decay by radioactivity — alpha, beta or gamma emission — toward more stable configurations.

Applications

Nuclear physics underlies nuclear power, nuclear weapons, nuclear medicine (imaging and radiotherapy), radiometric dating and magnetic resonance imaging.
