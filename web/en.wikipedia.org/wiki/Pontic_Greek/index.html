Pontic Greek

Pontic Greek (Pontic: Ρωμαίικα, Romaiika; Greek: Ποντιακή διάλεκτος, Pontiaki dialektos) is a variety of modern Greek originally spoken along the eastern Black Sea coast in the region of Pontus in northeastern Anatolia, as well as in the Caucasus. Its speakers, the Pontic Greeks, were largely displaced to Greece after the 1923 population exchange between Greece and Turkey, though communities of Muslim Pontic Greek speakers remain in Turkey, particularly in the Trabzon region.

History

Pontic Greek descends from the Greek spoken in colonies founded on the Black Sea coast in antiquity, including Sinope, Trapezus (Trabzon), and Kerasous (Giresun). Isolated from the Greek mainland, it preserved a number of archaic features lost elsewhere, alongside extensive influence from Turkish and, in the Caucasus varieties, from Russian, Georgian, and Armenian.

Dialects and speakers

Dialects include the varieties of Trabzon, Of, Chaldia, and others. A distinct offshoot, Mariupolitan Greek, is spoken around Mariupol in Ukraine. Pontic is considered endangered: UNESCO lists it as definitely endangered, though revival efforts, publications, and the Pontic lyra music tradition sustain interest among younger generations in Greece.

Features

Notable features include retention of ancient infinitival constructions in some dialects, distinctive phonology, and a large stock of Turkish loanwords. Literature in Pontic includes epic poetry, folk songs, and modern writing in both the Greek alphabet and, in Turkey, the Latin alphabet.
