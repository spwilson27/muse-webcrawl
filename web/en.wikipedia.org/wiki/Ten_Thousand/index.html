Ten Thousand

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Ten Thousand were Greek mercenary hoplites hired by Cyrus the Younger.

The Ten Thousand were a force of mainly Greek mercenary units, mainly hoplites, employed by Cyrus the Younger to help him seize the throne of the Persian Empire from his brother, Artaxerxes II. Their march to the Battle of Cunaxa (401 BC) and back to Greece (399–401 BC) was recorded by Xenophon, one of their leaders, in his work Anabasis.

Composition

According to Xenophon, the Ten Thousand were composed of roughly 10,400 hoplites and 2,500 light infantry and peltasts. The force was recruited from across the Greek world, including many veterans of the Peloponnesian War, and was commanded initially by the Spartan general Clearchus.

The march upcountry

Cyrus concealed the true purpose of the expedition, claiming at first to be suppressing a revolt in Pisidia. The army marched from Sardis across Asia Minor and into Mesopotamia before the Greeks learned they were to fight the King himself. At Cunaxa, near Babylon, the Greeks routed the Persian troops opposite them, but Cyrus was killed in the battle, leaving the expedition without a paymaster or purpose deep inside hostile territory.

The retreat

After the Persian satrap Tissaphernes treacherously seized and executed Clearchus and the other generals at a parley, the Greek soldiers elected new leaders, including Xenophon. The Ten Thousand then fought their way north through the mountains of the Carduchii and Armenia, enduring ambushes, snow, and starvation, until they reached the Black Sea coast at Trapezus. Xenophon famously records the moment the vanguard sighted the sea, crying Thalatta! Thalatta! ("The Sea! The Sea!").

Legacy

The successful retreat of the Ten Thousand demonstrated the vulnerability of the Persian Empire to a disciplined Greek army and is often cited as an inspiration for the later conquests of Alexander the Great. Xenophon's Anabasis became a foundational text of Greek literature and a standard work for students of ancient Greek.
