Dual inheritance theory

Dual inheritance theory (DIT), also known as gene–culture coevolution or biocultural evolution, is an explanatory framework developed from the late 1970s onward to explain how human behaviour is the product of two different and interacting evolutionary processes: genetic evolution and cultural evolution. Culture is defined in this theory as information acquired from other individuals through social learning processes such as teaching, imitation, and observation.

Overview

The central claim is that humans inherit two systems of information: genes, transmitted vertically from parents to offspring, and culture, which can be transmitted vertically, horizontally (between peers), and obliquely (from non-parent elders). Cultural variants are subject to selection-like processes — including content biases (preferring easily learned or useful ideas), frequency-dependent biases such as conformity, and model-based biases such as prestige bias — as well as drift and guided variation. Because culture changes much faster than genes and feeds back on the selective environment, the two inheritances coevolve.

Key works and researchers

The theory was developed principally by Robert Boyd and Peter J. Richerson (Culture and the Evolutionary Process, 1985; Not By Genes Alone, 2005), Luigi Luca Cavalli-Sforza and Marcus Feldman (Cultural Transmission and Evolution, 1981), and William H. Durham (Coevolution, 1991). Joseph Henrich and Kevin Laland are among its later proponents.

Examples of gene–culture coevolution

  • Lactase persistence — the cultural practice of dairying created selection pressure favouring alleles that permit adult milk digestion, which evolved independently in several pastoralist populations.
  • Sickle cell and agriculture — slash-and-burn yam cultivation in West Africa increased standing water and malaria, favouring the sickle-cell trait.
  • Starch digestion — copy number of the amylase gene AMY1 is higher in populations with historically starch-rich diets.

Relation to other fields

DIT is related to but distinct from sociobiology, evolutionary psychology, and memetics; it differs from memetics in insisting on mathematical models of transmission and on culture as phenotype-shaping information rather than on selfish replicators alone. Its methods — formal modelling and cross-cultural databases such as D-PLACE — have influenced cultural evolution as a discipline.
