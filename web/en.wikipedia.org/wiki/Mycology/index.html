Mycology

Branch ofBiology
StudiesFungi
PractitionerMycologist

Mycology is the branch of biology concerned with the study of fungi, including their taxonomy, genetics, biochemical properties, and their uses to humans. Fungi include yeasts, moulds, mushrooms, lichens, and rusts and smuts. A biologist specialising in mycology is called a mycologist.

Mycology branches into the fields of taxonomy, systematics, morphology, ecology, biochemistry, and molecular biology. Because fungi are so diverse — with estimates of 2.2 to 3.8 million species, of which only around 150,000 have been formally described — the field remains one of the least completely catalogued areas of biology.

History

Humans have collected mushrooms for food since prehistoric times. The formal study of fungi began in the 18th and 19th centuries with botanists such as Christiaan Hendrik Persoon and Elias Magnus Fries, whose systems of classification laid the foundations of fungal taxonomy. Pier Antonio Micheli's 1729 work Nova plantarum genera is often considered a founding document of mycology, as it demonstrated that fungi reproduce by spores.

In the 20th century, the discovery of penicillin from the mould Penicillium by Alexander Fleming transformed medicine and drew enormous attention to fungal biochemistry. Molecular phylogenetics later showed that fungi are more closely related to animals than to plants.

Scope

Mycologists study fungal diversity and evolution, fungal genetics and genomics, plant and animal diseases caused by fungi (phytopathology and medical mycology), the role of fungi in ecosystems as decomposers and symbionts, and industrial applications including fermentation, food production, and biotechnology.
