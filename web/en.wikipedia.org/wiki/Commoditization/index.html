Commoditization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Commoditization is the process by which goods or services that were once distinguishable by brand, features, or quality become perceived by buyers as essentially interchangeable, so that competition shifts almost entirely to price. The term is related to, but distinct from, commodification, the broader social process of transforming things into objects of trade. A market that has undergone commoditization is said to sell a commodity product.

Process

Commoditization typically follows a pattern: a novel product commands premium prices; competitors imitate its features; standards emerge that make rival offerings compatible and comparable; and buyers, seeing little differentiation, choose on price alone. Margins compress, and suppliers respond either by cutting costs, by seeking new differentiation (service, design, ecosystem), or by exiting the market.

Examples

  • Personal computers — once strongly branded, mainstream PCs became assemblies of standard components competing on price.
  • Air travel — deregulation and online fare comparison turned seats on many routes into price-shopped commodities.
  • Memory chips and displays — standardised specifications made DRAM and flat panels classic commodity markets with boom-and-bust pricing cycles.
  • Cloud computing — basic compute and storage capacity has partly commoditized, pushing providers toward higher-level services.

Counter-strategies

Firms resist commoditization through branding, proprietary ecosystems, bundled services, switching costs, and continuous innovation that re-differentiates the offer faster than rivals can imitate it. In business-to-business markets, suppliers may move "up the value chain" from products to solutions and outcomes.
