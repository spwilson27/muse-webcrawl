National debt of the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The national debt of the United States is the total debt, or unpaid borrowed funds, carried by the federal government of the United States. It is measured as the value of the outstanding Treasury securities that have been issued by the Treasury and other federal government agencies. The terms "national deficit" and "national surplus" usually refer to the federal government budget balance from year to year, not the cumulative amount of debt.

The total public debt (gross federal debt) consists of two components: debt held by the public — Treasury securities held by investors, the Federal Reserve, state and local governments, mutual funds, pension funds, insurance companies, banks and foreign governments — and debt held by government accounts (intragovernmental debt), principally the Social Security Trust Fund and federal employee retirement funds. Gross federal debt surpassed $30 trillion in 2022 and continued rising thereafter, driven by annual budget deficits, tax cuts, pandemic relief spending and interest costs.

History

The United States has carried debt since its founding, when it assumed the Revolutionary War debts of the states under Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton. Debt spiked during the Civil War, the Great Depression and the Second World War, when debt held by the public reached about 106% of GDP in 1946. The debt-to-GDP ratio fell for decades after the war, then rose again from the 1980s onward, with sharp increases after the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic.

Debt ceiling

The United States debt ceiling is a statutory limit on the total amount of debt the Treasury may issue. Since 1960 Congress has repeatedly raised, extended or suspended the limit; periodic standoffs — notably in 2011, which led to the first-ever downgrade of U.S. sovereign debt by Standard & Poor's, and in 2023 — have raised concerns about default and fiscal brinkmanship.

Foreign holders

Foreign governments and investors hold a substantial share of Treasury securities. Japan and China have historically been the two largest foreign holders, each holding on the order of a trillion dollars, with other major holders including the United Kingdom, Luxembourg and the Cayman Islands.

Measurement and debate

Economists assess the sustainability of the debt largely through the debt-to-GDP ratio and net interest costs as a share of federal revenue. The Congressional Budget Office projects rising ratios over the long term under current law, attributing growth mainly to an ageing population, rising health-care costs and interest payments, a trajectory it describes as unsustainable. Others note that debt issued in a sovereign's own currency, and the dollar's role as the global reserve currency, give the United States unusual fiscal capacity.
