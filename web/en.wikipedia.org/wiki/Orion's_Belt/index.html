Orion's Belt is an asterism in the constellation of Orion. It consists of three bright stars — Alnitak, Alnilam and Mintaka — aligned in a short, nearly straight row near the celestial equator. Their names derive from Arabic: Alnitak and Alnilam both relate to words for a belt or string of pearls, and Mintaka means "the belt". The asterism is one of the most recognisable patterns in the night sky and has been identified in the star lore of cultures worldwide, where the three stars have been seen as a belt, a plough, a scale-beam, three hunters, or three kings.

The stars

All three are massive blue supergiants of spectral types O and B, tens of thousands of times more luminous than the Sun, though at quite different distances. Alnilam (Epsilon Orionis), the middle star, is the most distant at roughly 2,000 light-years and one of the most luminous stars known. Alnitak (Zeta Orionis), the easternmost, lies about 800–1,260 light-years away and is a triple system; the Flame Nebula and the Horsehead Nebula lie nearby. Mintaka (Delta Orionis), the westernmost, is a multiple system about 900–1,200 light-years away and sits almost exactly on the celestial equator, so it rises due east and sets due west everywhere on Earth.

Observing and navigation

Because the Belt is close to the celestial equator, it is visible from virtually all inhabited latitudes; it is prominent in the evening sky of the northern winter (southern summer). The line of the Belt extended southwest points to Sirius, the brightest star in the sky, and northeast to Aldebaran. Orion's Sword, containing the Orion Nebula, hangs from the Belt. A popular fringe theory, the "Orion correlation theory", claims the three stars mirror the layout of the Giza pyramids; this is not accepted by mainstream Egyptology or astronomy.
