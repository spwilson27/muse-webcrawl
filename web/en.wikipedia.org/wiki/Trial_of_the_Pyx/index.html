Trial of the Pyx

The Trial of the Pyx is a judicial ceremony in the United Kingdom to ensure that newly minted coins from the Royal Mint conform to the required standards of dimension, weight, and metallic composition. The name derives from the pyx, the chest in which sample coins are kept; the trial is one of the longest-established judicial procedures in England, with origins in the 12th century.

Procedure

Throughout the year, the Royal Mint sets aside one coin from every prescribed quantity of each denomination produced, placing them in the pyx box. At the annual trial, held at Goldsmiths' Hall in London, an independent jury of members of the Worshipful Company of Goldsmiths counts, weighs, and assays a random selection of the coins. The jury is presided over by the King's Remembrancer, one of the oldest judicial offices in England, and its verdict is delivered to the Chancellor of the Exchequer.

History

Mentions of the procedure date to 1248 and earlier, and the trial was placed on a statutory footing by the Coinage Act 1870. The ceremony is now held annually, typically early in the year, and applies to both circulating coins and commemorative issues produced at the Royal Mint's site in Llantrisant, Wales.
