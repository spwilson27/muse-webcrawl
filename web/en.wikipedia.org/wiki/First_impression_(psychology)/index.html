First impression (psychology)

First impression Field: Social psychology
Key findings: judgements formed within fractions of a second of exposure; primacy effect; halo effect
Related concepts: thin-slicing, impression formation, confirmation bias

In psychology, a first impression is the event when one person first encounters another person and forms a mental image of that person. Impression accuracy and persistence vary with the observer and the target being judged. First impressions are based on a wide range of characteristics: age, race, culture, language, gender, physical appearance, accent, posture, voice, and social setting.

Speed and thin-slicing

Experimental work shows that observers form judgements of traits such as competence, trustworthiness, and aggressiveness from faces after exposures as brief as a tenth of a second, with longer exposures mainly increasing confidence rather than changing the judgement. Such rapid assessments are an example of "thin-slicing"—drawing conclusions from narrow samples of behaviour.

Primacy and persistence

Early information disproportionately influences overall impressions (the primacy effect), and first impressions tend to persist because later information is interpreted in light of them, a form of confirmation bias. Solomon Asch's classic experiments showed that changing a single trait word in a description could markedly alter the impression formed of a hypothetical person.

Applications

First impressions influence job interviews, first dates, electoral politics, and courtroom credibility. Research suggests that accurate impressions of some traits (such as extraversion) form more readily than others, and that impressions improve with longer, richer interaction, though initial judgements often remain influential.
