Pleaching

A pleached avenue of limes

Pleaching is a technique of weaving the branches of trees together to form a living fence, hedge, or raised screen. Branches are interlaced, partly cut, and bent so that they continue growing and eventually graft together (inosculation), producing a strong, self-supporting structure.

History

Pleaching was widely practised in late medieval and early modern Europe for enclosing orchards and livestock – the "quickset" hedges of hawthorn described in English agricultural writing – and became a feature of formal gardens in the 17th and 18th centuries, particularly in France and England. Pleached limes and hornbeams were trained into shady raised walks (sometimes called "stilt hedges") in great gardens, a style revived by Arts and Crafts gardeners such as Gertrude Jekyll.

Technique

Young, flexible trees – commonly lime, hornbeam, beech, or hawthorn – are planted in a row and their lateral branches tied in along horizontal lines or wires, then woven into the branches of their neighbours. Crossed branches are sometimes pared and bound so that they fuse permanently. The resulting hedge is clipped annually to maintain the flat plane of foliage; a pleached avenue is held on clear trunks, giving shade and screening above head height while leaving the view beneath open.
