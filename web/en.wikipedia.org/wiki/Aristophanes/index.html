Aristophanes

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A double herm of Aristophanes and Menander: the bearded master of Old Comedy beside his New Comedy successor]

Aristophanes (c. 446 – c. 386 BC) was the great comic playwright of classical Athens, the sole surviving master of Old Comedy. Eleven of his forty-odd plays survive complete, crammed with obscenity, fantasy, personal mockery of named citizens and some of the most brilliant lyric poetry in Greek — a combination no other age has quite reproduced.

His plays are dispatches from the Peloponnesian War and its aftermath: The Acharnians (425 BC) argues for peace; The Clouds (423 BC) caricatures Socrates in his 'Thinkery'; Lysistrata (411 BC) imagines a women's sex-strike ending the war; The Birds (414 BC) builds a cloud-city of birds; The Frogs (405 BC) descends to Hades to judge a contest between Aeschylus and Euripides — and to beg the gods for a poet to save the city.

Comedy and the city

Performed at the Lenaia and Dionysia before the citizen body, Old Comedy enjoyed a licensed freedom to attack generals, demagogues (Cleon was Aristophanes' particular target) and intellectuals by name. Its structure — prologue, parodos of a fantastic chorus (wasps, frogs, clouds), agon or formal debate, parabasis where the chorus addresses the audience for the poet, and a final revel — frames flights of surreal invention.

Transmission and influence

Aristophanes survived because Byzantine schoolmasters, while deploring his morals, needed his Attic Greek. Through the Renaissance the plays seeded European comedy; modern productions worldwide still find his anti-war and anti-demagogue jokes landing squarely.
