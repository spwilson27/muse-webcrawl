The Madrid Codex, also called the Tro-Cortesianus Codex, is one of only four surviving pre-Columbian Maya codices. At 112 pages it is the longest of the four, and it functions as a collection of divinatory almanacs used by Maya priests in Yucatán, probably in the Postclassic period (roughly the 13th–15th centuries, though its dating is debated). It is held by the Museo de América in Madrid, Spain.

History

The codex surfaced in Spain in two separate fragments. The larger portion, the Troano Codex, was published by the abbé Brasseur de Bourbourg in 1869–70 after being obtained from Juan de Tro y Ortolano; the smaller Cortesianus Codex appeared in Madrid in 1875 and was said — without firm evidence — to have been sent by Hernán Cortés to Charles V. In 1880 the French Mayanist Léon de Rosny recognised that the two fragments joined along matching page edges to form a single book, screen-folded in the accordion style.

Contents

The almanacs in the codex concern the practical and ritual concerns of daily life: beekeeping, hunting and trapping deer, weaving, pottery, planting and rain ceremonies, and the prognostications attached to the 260-day tzolk'in calendar. Its pages are crowded with deities including Itzamna, Chaac the rain god, and the merchant god Ek Chuah, alongside scenes — some quite crude in draughtsmanship compared with the Dresden Codex — of sacrifice, food preparation and craft work. Scholars regard the many hands and uneven quality of the Madrid Codex as evidence that it was a working compilation copied and augmented over time by several scribes in a provincial setting.
