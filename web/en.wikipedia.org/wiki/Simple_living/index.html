Simple living

Simple living
Also known as: Voluntary simplicity
Related movements: Downshifting, minimalism, degrowth
Key text: Voluntary Simplicity (Duane Elgin, 1981)

Simple living, or voluntary simplicity, is a lifestyle characterised by reducing one's possessions, consumption, and pursuit of wealth. Practitioners adopt simplicity for reasons including spirituality, health, environmental sustainability, increased time with family, work–life balance, and opposition to consumerism.

History

Voluntary poverty and simplicity have ancient roots in religious traditions: Buddhist and Christian monasticism, Hindu renunciation, and the teachings of figures such as Diogenes and Epicurus. Henry David Thoreau's Walden (1854), an account of his experiment living in a cabin at Walden Pond, became a foundational text. In the 1930s, Ralph Borsodi and Helen and Scott Nearing promoted homesteading and self-sufficiency, influencing the back-to-the-land movement of the 1960s–70s.

Practices

Common practices include decluttering and owning fewer possessions, growing food, reducing working hours ("downshifting"), avoiding debt, choosing smaller housing — including tiny houses — and favouring local or second-hand goods. Some adherents connect simplicity with the FIRE movement (financial independence), while others emphasise ecological footprint reduction.

Criticism

Critics note that voluntary simplicity is easiest for those with the wealth and security to choose it, and that it can romanticise the involuntary poverty of others. Others argue that individual lifestyle change is no substitute for systemic economic and environmental reform.
