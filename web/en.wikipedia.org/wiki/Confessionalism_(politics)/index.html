Confessionalism (politics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Confessionalism is a system of government that apportions political and institutional power among religious communities (confessions) according to an agreed formula, usually proportional to their share of the population. It is a form of consociationalism in which sectarian identity is a recognised basis for representation in the legislature, the executive, public employment, and sometimes separate personal-status law administered by each community.

Lebanon

The best-known example is Lebanon, where the unwritten National Pact of 1943 allocated the presidency to a Maronite Christian, the premiership to a Sunni Muslim, and the speakership of parliament to a Shia Muslim, with parliamentary seats distributed between Christians and Muslims in a 6:5 ratio. The Taif Agreement of 1989, which ended the Lebanese Civil War, changed the ratio to equal Christian–Muslim representation and strengthened the Council of Ministers, while retaining the confessional allocation of the top offices. Critics argue that confessionalism entrenches sectarian division and patronage; defenders contend it guarantees communal representation and prevented domination by any single group. Reform toward a secular state is formally envisaged in the Lebanese constitution but has not been implemented.

Other examples

Confessional or quasi-confessional arrangements appear in varying degrees elsewhere. In Iraq after 2003, an informal apportionment (muhasasa) divided senior offices among Shia, Sunni, and Kurdish figures. The Dayton constitution of Bosnia and Herzegovina allocates the collective presidency among Bosniak, Croat, and Serb members. Historically, the Ottoman millet system granted religious communities autonomy in personal and communal affairs, and several European states have reserved parliamentary seats or recognised church establishments connected to political representation.
