Djenné-Jeno, also Jenne-Jeno, is an ancient urban site in the inland Niger Delta of present-day Mali, near the modern town of Djenné. First settled around 250 BC, it grew into one of sub-Saharan Africa's earliest cities and a major centre of trade and rice cultivation, linked to the trans-Saharan exchange of gold, salt, and iron.

History

Excavations led by Roderick and Susan McIntosh in the 1970s and 1980s revealed continuous occupation for some 1,600 years. The city developed a distinctive clustered settlement pattern: instead of a single nucleated centre, occupation spread across dozens of specialized satellite mounds occupied by craft specialists and occupational groups — blacksmiths, potters, fishermen, and farmers — forming an urban cluster without evidence of a centralized palace or temple dominating it.

By around AD 800–1000 the settlement covered some 33 hectares within a city wall and supported perhaps 10,000–27,000 inhabitants in its cluster. It declined in the 13th–14th centuries as populations shifted to modern Djenné, a few kilometres away, possibly in connection with the spread of Islam.

Significance

Djenné-Jeno is a key piece of evidence that complex urbanism in West Africa developed indigenously, driven by regional trade and specialized production rather than imported from North Africa or the Near East. It forms part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing of the Old Towns of Djenné (inscribed 1988).
