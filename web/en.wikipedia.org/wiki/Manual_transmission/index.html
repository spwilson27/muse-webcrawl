A manual transmission (MT), also known as a manual gearbox, standard transmission (in Canada, the United Kingdom, and the United States), or stick shift (in the United States), is a multi-speed motor vehicle transmission system where gear changes require the driver to manually select the gears by operating a gear stick and clutch (usually a foot pedal for cars or a hand lever for motorcycles).

Operation

The driver depresses the clutch pedal to disconnect the engine from the gearbox, moves the gear lever through a gate — most commonly an H-pattern — to select the desired ratio, and releases the clutch while modulating the accelerator to re-engage drive smoothly. Because the driver controls shift timing and engine speed, techniques such as rev-matching, heel-and-toe downshifting, and double-clutching can be used to smooth changes, particularly in performance and commercial driving.

Design

Most modern manual gearboxes are constant-mesh designs: all gear pairs are permanently meshed, and the selected pair is locked to the output shaft by a dog clutch collar. Baulk rings (synchromesh) friction-match the speeds of the collar and gear before engagement, allowing smooth shifts without grinding. Gearboxes typically provide four to seven forward ratios; six-speed units became common in the 2000s.

Comparison and prevalence

Manual transmissions are mechanically simpler than automatics, historically cheaper, and long offered better fuel economy and performance than early automatic designs. Modern automatic and dual-clutch gearboxes have largely erased those advantages, and the manual share of new-car sales has fallen steeply in North America (to only a few percent), though manuals long remained the majority choice in much of Europe and the developing world — a share also now declining in favour of automatics and electric vehicles, which generally need no multi-ratio gearbox at all.
