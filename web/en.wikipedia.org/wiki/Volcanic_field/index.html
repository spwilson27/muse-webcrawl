Volcanic field

A volcanic field dotted with small monogenetic cones

A volcanic field is an area of the Earth's crust that contains a collection of volcanoes, typically dozens to hundreds of small vents spread over hundreds to thousands of square kilometres, rather than a single large edifice. Most volcanic fields are monogenetic: each vent erupts only once, or over a short period, and new eruptions break out at a new location within the field rather than reusing an old cone.

Formation

Volcanic fields form where magma supply is low or distributed — for example above diffuse mantle upwelling, at the margins of tectonic plates, in extensional rifts, or far within plates (intraplate volcanism). Magma reaching the surface through separate dikes builds cinder cones, maars, small shield volcanoes and lava flows of mainly basaltic composition. Because activity migrates across the field over hundreds of thousands to millions of years, hazard assessment must treat the whole field as potentially active even though each individual vent is extinct.

Examples

Well-known volcanic fields include the San Francisco volcanic field in Arizona (with more than 600 vents, including Sunset Crater), the Auckland volcanic field in New Zealand, which underlies much of the city of Auckland and produced the Rangitoto shield about 600 years ago, the Michoacán–Guanajuato volcanic field in Mexico, where Parícutin (1943) and Jorullo (1759) famously erupted in farmland, the Chaîne des Puys in France, the Eifel fields in Germany, and the vast fields of Saudi Arabia called harrats. Monogenetic fields are also recognised on the Moon, Mars and Venus.
