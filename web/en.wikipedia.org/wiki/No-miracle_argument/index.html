No-miracles argument

The no-miracles argument (also known as the miracle argument or the ultimate argument) is the principal argument for scientific realism, the view that successful scientific theories are (approximately) true descriptions of the world, including their claims about unobservable entities such as electrons and genes. It holds that the predictive and technological success of mature science would be a miracle — an inexplicable coincidence — if its theories were not at least approximately true.

Formulation

The argument is associated above all with Hilary Putnam, who wrote in 1975 that realism "is the only philosophy that doesn't make the success of science a miracle." Richard Boyd developed the related "abductive" defence: the reliability of scientific methodology in producing successful predictions is best explained by the approximate truth of the background theories guiding it. The argument is an inference to the best explanation, mirroring the reasoning scientists themselves use.

Criticisms

The most famous reply is Larry Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction (1981): the history of science is a graveyard of once-successful theories — phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether — whose central terms failed to refer, so by induction today's theories are likely false too. Anti-realists such as Bas van Fraassen add that the argument begs the question by using abduction to defend abduction, and propose instead (in van Fraassen's constructive empiricism) that success only requires empirical adequacy, explained Darwinistically: theories that fail are discarded, so surviving theories are bound to be successful. Realists have responded with "divide and conquer" strategies (Psillos, Kitcher) distinguishing the working posits of past theories, which were retained, from idle ones, which were not.
