No-miracles argument

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The predictive success of mature science: the realist's datum

The no-miracles argument (also called the miracle argument or ultimate argument) is the most influential argument for scientific realism — the view that our best scientific theories are at least approximately true and that the entities they posit, such as electrons and genes, really exist. The argument holds that the predictive and instrumental success of science would be an inexplicable miracle if successful theories were not at least approximately true.

Formulation

The argument was given its classic statement by Hilary Putnam in 1975: realism "is the only philosophy that doesn't make the success of science a miracle". Mature scientific theories routinely make novel, precise predictions — unanticipated phenomena that are later observed — and support technologies that work. The realist argues, by inference to the best explanation, that this success is explained by the theories having latched onto the real structure of the world: their central terms refer and their claims are approximately true. If the theories were wholly false about the unobservable world, their empirical success would be a cosmic coincidence.

Criticisms

Anti-realists reply in several ways. Larry Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction (1981) notes that many once-successful theories — phlogiston chemistry, caloric thermodynamics, ether theories of light — are now regarded as false and their central terms as non-referring; success, therefore, does not guarantee truth. Constructive empiricists such as Bas van Fraassen argue that success can be explained naturalistically, by a kind of Darwinian selection of theories, without supposing truth. Larry Laudan and others have also questioned whether "approximate truth" can be defined rigorously enough to bear the argument's weight. Realists have responded with selective or structural realism: the parts of past theories responsible for their success (often mathematical structure, or "working posits" like the electron rather than idle ones) are retained in successor theories. The debate over the no-miracles argument remains central to the philosophy of science.
