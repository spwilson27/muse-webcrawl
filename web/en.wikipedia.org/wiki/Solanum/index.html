Solanum is a large and diverse genus of flowering plants in the nightshade family Solanaceae. It includes three food crops of major economic importance — the potato (Solanum tuberosum), the tomato (S. lycopersicum), and the eggplant/aubergine (S. melongena) — as well as many ornamentals, weeds, and poisonous species such as black nightshade (S. nigrum) and the woody nightshade (S. dulcamara).

Description

Species range from annual herbs to shrubs, vines, and small trees, distributed on all continents except Antarctica, with the greatest diversity in South America. Flowers are typically five-petalled, often star-shaped (rotate) or bell-shaped, with prominent cone-like clusters of yellow anthers that release pollen through apical pores — many species rely on "buzz pollination" by bees. The fruit is usually a berry.

Chemistry and toxicity

Many Solanum species produce steroidal glycoalkaloids such as solanine and chaconine, which deter herbivores and can poison livestock and humans; these compounds concentrate in green potato tubers and unripe fruits. Other species have been used medicinally and as sources of steroid precursors.

The genus name, published by Carl Linnaeus in 1753, may derive from the Latin solamen ("comfort"), referring to the sedative properties of some species, or from sol (sun).
