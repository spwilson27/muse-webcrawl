Isma'ilism (Arabic: الإسماعيلية, romanized: al-Ismāʿīliyya) is a branch or substream of Shia Islam that emphasises the doctrine of the Imamate and originated in the eighth century CE. The Isma'ilis get their name from their acceptance of Isma'il ibn Ja'far as the appointed spiritual successor (Imam) to his father, Ja'far al-Sadiq, the sixth Shia Imam, whereas the Twelver Shia in the majority accept Musa al-Kazim, Isma'il's younger brother, as the true Imam.

Isma'ili beliefs incorporate elements from a wide range of intellectual traditions. The early Isma'ili movement developed an elaborate missionary organisation, the da'wa, which spread its teachings from its bases in the Islamic world. At its political height, the Isma'ili Fatimid Caliphate (909–1171) ruled a vast empire stretching across North Africa, Egypt, the Levant, and parts of Arabia, rivalling the Sunni Abbasid Caliphate in Baghdad. After the Fatimid period, Isma'ili communities survived—often as religious minorities—across Syria, Persia, Central Asia, Yemen, and South Asia.

Today the largest Isma'ili group is the Nizaris, who recognise the Aga Khan as their living Imam; the current Imam is Aga Khan V (Rahim al-Hussaini), who succeeded his father, Aga Khan IV, in 2025. Other surviving branches include the Musta'li Isma'ilis of Yemen and India, among them the Dawoodi Bohras, Sulaymani Bohras, and Alavi Bohras, who are led by a chief missionary (da'i al-mutlaq) in the absence of a manifest Imam. The Druze faith, which emerged from Isma'ilism in the eleventh century, is now generally regarded as a distinct religion.

History

Origins and the succession dispute

The roots of Isma'ilism lie in the succession crisis following the death of the sixth Imam, Ja'far al-Sadiq, in 765 CE. Ja'far had designated his eldest surviving son, Isma'il, as his successor, but Isma'il predeceased his father. After Ja'far's death, the Shia community split over the succession. One group held that the Imamate had passed to Isma'il's son, Muhammad ibn Isma'il, and that Isma'il's designation could not be revoked; others held that Isma'il himself had not truly died, or that the Imamate should pass to Ja'far's younger son Musa al-Kazim, the choice accepted by the Twelvers. The followers of the Isma'ili line traced the Imamate through Muhammad ibn Isma'il, after whom the early Isma'ili Imams entered a period of concealment (dawr al-satr).

The early da'wa and the Qarmatians

In the late ninth century, Isma'ili missionaries (da'is) spread out from centres in Iraq and Salamiyah in Syria, preaching the imminent coming of the Mahdi, identified with Muhammad ibn Isma'il. The movement achieved striking successes: Hamdan Qarmat won converts in Iraq, Abu Sa'id al-Jannabi established a state in Bahrain (the Qarmatians), and missions reached Yemen, Sindh, Persia, and North Africa. In 899, a schism divided the movement when Abdallah, the leader at Salamiyah, claimed the Imamate for himself and his ancestors rather than acting as a deputy of the awaited Mahdi. The Qarmatians of Bahrain rejected his claim, while his loyal followers became the nucleus of the Fatimid movement.

The Fatimid Caliphate

In 909, Abdallah—now ruling as al-Mahdi bi'llah—was proclaimed caliph in Ifriqiya (modern Tunisia), founding the Fatimid Caliphate. The Fatimids conquered Egypt in 969 and founded Cairo as their capital, building the al-Azhar mosque and university. Under caliphs such as al-Mu'izz and al-Aziz, the empire extended from the Atlantic coast of North Africa to Syria and the Hejaz. The Fatimids patronised learning, trade, and the arts, and their da'wa operated an organised hierarchy of missionaries across the Islamic world, even beyond Fatimid political control. The period also produced major Isma'ili thinkers, including Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani, Hamid al-Din al-Kirmani, and the poet-philosopher Nasir Khusraw, whose works developed an elaborate cosmology blending Islamic theology with Neoplatonic philosophy.

The Nizari–Musta'li schism

The death of the Fatimid caliph al-Mustansir in 1094 produced the most consequential split in Isma'ili history. The vizier al-Afdal installed the younger son, al-Musta'li, as caliph in Cairo, while the communities of Persia and Syria, led by Hasan-i Sabbah, recognised the elder son Nizar as the rightful Imam. Hasan-i Sabbah, who had seized the fortress of Alamut in northern Persia in 1090, built a network of mountain strongholds across Persia and Syria that resisted both the Seljuk Turks and, later, the Crusaders. The Nizari state at Alamut survived until 1256, when it was destroyed by the Mongols under Hulagu. In the West, the Nizaris became known in Crusader-era sources as the "Assassins", a name associated with the Arabic hashishiyya, though its precise origin is debated.

The Musta'li line in Egypt ended with the abolition of the Fatimid Caliphate by Saladin in 1171. The Musta'li community had itself split in 1130 into the Hafizi branch, which recognised the later Fatimid caliphs, and the Tayyibi branch, which held that the Imam al-Tayyib had entered concealment. The Tayyibi da'wa was carried on from Yemen and later from India, where it survives among the Bohra communities.

Later history

After the fall of Alamut, the Nizari Imams lived in concealment for roughly two centuries before re-emerging in fifteenth-century Persia at Anjudan. In the nineteenth century, Hasan Ali Shah, the 46th Nizari Imam, received the title Aga Khan from the Qajar shah of Persia and later settled in British India, where his community prospered. His descendants—above all Aga Khan III (1877–1957), a founding figure of the All-India Muslim League and president of the League of Nations assembly, and Aga Khan IV (1936–2025)—built extensive development, education, and health networks serving Isma'ili communities and the wider societies around them. The Tayyibi Bohra communities of Gujarat and Mumbai likewise became prominent in trade and industry under the leadership of their da'is.

Beliefs and practices

Isma'ilis share the central tenets of Islam: the oneness of God (tawhid), the prophethood of Muhammad, and the authority of the Quran. Their distinctive doctrines centre on the Imamate: the belief that after the Prophet, spiritual and religious guidance continues through a hereditary line of Imams descended from Ali ibn Abi Talib and Fatima, the Prophet's daughter. The Imam of the time is regarded as the authoritative interpreter of the faith.

A hallmark of Isma'ili thought is the distinction between the exoteric (zahir) and esoteric (batin) dimensions of revelation, and the practice of ta'wil—spiritual, allegorical interpretation of scripture and ritual. Classical Isma'ili cosmology described the universe as emanating from God through a hierarchy of intellects and souls, drawing on Neoplatonic ideas transmitted into Arabic philosophy. Isma'ili law was codified in the Fatimid period by al-Qadi al-Nu'man in works such as the Da'a'im al-Islam ("The Pillars of Islam"), which remains the principal legal text of the Musta'li branches; the Nizaris follow the guidance of the living Imam in matters of practice.

Practices vary between branches. Nizari Isma'ilis gather in jamatkhanas for prayer and community life and recite a holy du'a three times daily. Tayyibi Bohras follow a ritual calendar closer to other Shia traditions, observing prayers five times daily and maintaining distinctive communal institutions under the da'i al-mutlaq.

Branches

  • Nizari Isma'ilis – the largest branch, recognising the Aga Khan as the living Imam; concentrated historically in Persia, Syria, Central Asia, and South Asia, with a worldwide diaspora today.
  • Musta'li Isma'ilis – divided into the extinct-in-Egypt Hafizi line and the surviving Tayyibi branch, which includes the Dawoodi, Sulaymani, and Alavi Bohras of Yemen and western India.
  • Druze – emerged from the Isma'ili milieu of Fatimid Cairo in the early eleventh century around the caliph al-Hakim; now considered a separate ethnoreligious group.
  • Qarmatians – an early dissident Isma'ili movement that ruled Bahrain and eastern Arabia until the eleventh century.

Demographics

Estimates of the worldwide Isma'ili population vary widely, commonly around 15 million, spread across more than 25 countries. Significant historical communities exist in Syria, Yemen, Saudi Arabia (Najran), Afghanistan (Badakhshan), Tajikistan (the Pamirs), Pakistan (Gilgit-Baltistan and Karachi), India (Gujarat and Maharashtra), and East Africa, with substantial diasporas in Canada, the United States, the United Kingdom, and other Western countries.
