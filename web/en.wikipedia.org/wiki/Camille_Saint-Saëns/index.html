Camille Saint-Saëns

Charles-Camille Saint-Saëns (1835–1921).

Charles-Camille Saint-Saëns (9 October 1835 – 16 December 1921) was a French composer, organist, conductor and pianist of the Romantic era. A child prodigy with perfect pitch who gave his first concert at the age of five, he enjoyed one of the longest careers in music history, composing more than 300 works in nearly every genre. His best-known pieces include The Carnival of the Animals (1886), the Symphony No. 3 "Organ" (1886), the opera Samson and Delilah (1877), the tone poem Danse macabre (1874) and the Introduction and Rondo Capriccioso for violin.

Life

Saint-Saëns studied at the Paris Conservatoire and became organist of La Madeleine in Paris, a post he held for twenty years. He championed the music of Liszt and Wagner in France while also reviving interest in earlier French composers, and co-founded the Société Nationale de Musique in 1871 to promote new French music. A tireless traveller and polymath with interests in astronomy, archaeology and literature, he was admired for his phenomenal memory and keyboard virtuosity.

Style and reputation

Saint-Saëns valued clarity, formal balance and craftsmanship, which made him appear conservative beside the innovations of Debussy and Stravinsky in his later years; he famously walked out of the premiere of The Rite of Spring. Nevertheless his music, admired by Ravel and others, combines classical poise with brilliant orchestration, and works such as the Second Piano Concerto and the "Organ" Symphony remain staples of the repertory.
