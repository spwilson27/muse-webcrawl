Epimetheus

In Greek mythology, Epimetheus (/ɛpɪˈmiːθiəs/; Greek: Ἐπιμηθεύς, "afterthought", literally "hindsight") is the brother of Prometheus ("foresight"). The two Titans act as representatives of mankind; Prometheus is the clever creator and benefactor of humanity, while Epimetheus is his foolish counterpart. Epimetheus received Pandora, the first woman, as a gift from the gods, and through her the evils of the world were released from a jar.

Mythology

In Hesiod's Theogony and Works and Days, Epimetheus is a son of the Titan Iapetus and the Oceanid Clymene. When Prometheus shaped mankind and the animals, Epimetheus was charged with distributing gifts among the creatures; he gave claws, wings, speed and strength to the animals, leaving nothing for humans until Prometheus compensated by giving them fire and the civilising arts.

After Prometheus angered Zeus by stealing fire for mankind, Zeus ordered the creation of Pandora, the first woman, endowed by all the gods with gifts. Despite Prometheus's warning never to accept a gift from Zeus, Epimetheus welcomed Pandora, who brought with her a jar (pithos, later mistranslated as a "box") containing toil, sickness and all the evils that afflict humanity. She opened it, releasing them into the world; only Hope (Elpis) remained inside.

By Pandora, Epimetheus was the father of Pyrrha, who with her husband Deucalion — Prometheus's son — survived the great flood and repopulated the earth by casting stones behind them.

Namesakes

Epimetheus is the name of a small inner moon of Saturn, discovered in 1966 and confirmed by Voyager 1 in 1980; it co-orbits with Janus, the two moons swapping orbits every four years. Carl Spitteler's 1880s epic poem Prometheus and Epimetheus influenced Carl Jung's discussion of introversion and extraversion.
