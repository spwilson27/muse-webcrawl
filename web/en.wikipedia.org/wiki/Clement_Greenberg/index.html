Clement Greenberg (January 16, 1909 – May 7, 1994) was an American essayist and art critic, the most influential champion of Abstract expressionism and of formalist aesthetics in the postwar period.

Career

Writing for Partisan Review and The Nation, his 1939 essay "Avant-Garde and Kitsch" contrasted authentic avant-garde art with mass-cultural kitsch. Through the 1940s–60s he championed Jackson Pollock, Arshile Gorky, Willem de Kooning, and later the color-field painters Morris Louis and Kenneth Noland.

Formalism

In "Modernist Painting" (1961) Greenberg argued that modernism is the self-critical tendency of each art to test its own medium — for painting, flatness and the delimitation of the picture plane. His teleological history of art and his immense market and museum influence made him the dominant, and most contested, figure in American art.

Reception

From the 1960s, Michael Fried, Rosalind Krauss, and others both extended and attacked his framework; postmodernist criticism defined itself largely against him. His Collected Essays and Criticism (4 vols., 1986–93) remain canonical.
