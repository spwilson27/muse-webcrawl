Brewing is the production of beer through steeping a starch source (commonly malted cereal grains, most often barley) in water and fermenting the resulting sweet liquid with yeast. It may be done in a brewery by a commercial brewer, at home by a homebrewer, or communally. Brewing has taken place since around the 6th millennium BCE and was one of the earliest industrial processes of ancient civilisations in Mesopotamia and Egypt.

The process

Brewing involves several distinct stages. In malting, grain is germinated and dried to develop enzymes. In mashing, the crushed malt is mixed with hot water so the enzymes convert starches into fermentable sugars, producing a sweet liquid called wort. The wort is separated from the grain husks (lautering), then boiled, typically for about an hour, during which hops are added for bitterness, flavour, and aroma. After cooling, yeast is pitched and fermentation proceeds for days to weeks: ale yeasts ferment warm at the top, lager yeasts cold at the bottom. The finished beer is conditioned, carbonated, and packaged in kegs, bottles, or cans.

Ingredients

Water makes up the bulk of beer, and its mineral profile historically shaped regional styles — the soft water of Pilsen for pale lager, the hard water of Burton-on-Trent for pale ale. Malt provides sugars and colour; hops contribute bitterness and preservation; yeast converts sugars to ethanol and carbon dioxide and generates much of a beer's flavour character. The Bavarian Reinheitsgebot of 1516 famously restricted beer to water, barley, and hops (yeast's role not yet being understood).

Industry

Modern brewing ranges from global firms producing hundreds of millions of hectolitres to microbreweries and brewpubs. A craft-brewing revival beginning in the late 20th century in the United States and Britain revived traditional styles and created many new ones. Beer remains the world's most widely consumed alcoholic drink and the third most popular drink overall after water and tea.
