Hanukkah (Hebrew: חֲנֻכָּה, "dedication"), also known as the Festival of Lights, is a Jewish festival commemorating the recovery of Jerusalem and the rededication of the Second Temple at the beginning of the Maccabean Revolt against the Seleucid Empire in the 2nd century BC. It begins on 25 Kislev and lasts eight nights and days.

History

After the Maccabees defeated the Seleucid forces and cleansed the Temple in Jerusalem (c. 164 BC), tradition in the Talmud holds that they found only one cruse of ritually pure oil, enough for one day, which miraculously burned for eight days — the time needed to prepare new oil. The festival was instituted by Judah Maccabee and his brothers to celebrate the rededication with eight days of joy and praise.

Observances

The central ritual is the nightly kindling of the hanukkiah, a nine-branched candelabrum: one candle is lit on the first night and an additional candle each night, using the ninth branch (the shamash) as a helper light. Other customs include eating foods fried in oil such as latkes (potato pancakes) and sufganiyot (jelly doughnuts), playing with the dreidel (a four-sided spinning top), and giving Hanukkah gelt (money or chocolate coins) to children.
