Candomble

Candomble (Portuguese: Candomblé) is an African diasporic religion that developed in Brazil during the 19th century among enslaved West Africans, principally of Yoruba, Fon, and Bantu origin. Centred on the worship of deities known as orixás (orishas), it is practised most widely in the state of Bahia and throughout Brazil, with an estimated two million adherents.

History

Candomblé took shape in Salvador, Bahia, where enslaved Africans preserved and recombined the religious traditions of their homelands. The temple Ilê Axé Iyá Nassô Oká, founded in the early 19th century by African priestesses, is regarded as one of the oldest continuously operating houses. For much of its history the religion faced police persecution; temples in Bahia were required to obtain police permits until 1976. Candomblé has since been recognized as part of Brazil's cultural heritage, and its historic temples have received official heritage protection.

Beliefs and practice

Devotees worship the orixás, deities associated with forces of nature and human qualities, beneath a remote creator, Olodumare. Each person is believed to be linked to a particular orixá. Worship takes place in temples called terreiros, led by a priestess (ialorixá) or priest (babalorixá). Ceremonies feature drumming, song in Yoruba-derived liturgical language, dance, and offerings; central rituals involve the orixá "mounting" or possessing an initiated devotee, who then dances and blesses the congregation.

Traditions are grouped into "nations" (nações), principally Ketu (Yoruba), Jeje (Fon), and Angola (Bantu), each with distinct languages, music, and pantheons.

Relationship with other religions

Under colonial Catholicism, practitioners often identified orixás with Catholic saints, producing a syncretic overlay that allowed worship to continue in public. In the 20th century a re-Africanization movement sought to remove Catholic elements. Candomblé influenced the related Afro-Brazilian religion Umbanda and remains culturally prominent in Bahian music, cuisine, and festivals such as the Festa de Iemanjá on February 2.
