Catechetical School of Alexandria

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Hero image placeholder: ancient Alexandria and its Christian school]

The Catechetical School of Alexandria was one of the most influential centres of Christian learning in the ancient world, based in Alexandria, Egypt. Tradition held that it was founded by Saint Mark the Evangelist. The school trained catechumens in the Christian faith but developed into a major centre of theology and biblical scholarship, famous for the allegorical method of interpreting Scripture.

History

The earliest historically attested head of the school is Pantaenus (died c. 200), a former Stoic philosopher. He was succeeded by Clement of Alexandria, whose writings sought to reconcile Christian teaching with Greek philosophy, and then by Origen, the school's most celebrated figure, whose lectures attracted students from across the Roman world and whose biblical scholarship produced the Hexapla.

Later heads included Heraclas, Dionysius of Alexandria, and Didymus the Blind (c. 313–398), the last great representative of the school's tradition. The school's influence waned in the fourth and fifth centuries amid the doctrinal controversies of the period and the destruction of pagan and scholarly institutions in Alexandria.

Method

The Alexandrian school favoured allegorical exegesis, reading biblical texts as bearing spiritual meanings beyond the literal sense, in contrast to the more literal-historical approach associated with the school of Antioch. Its teachers engaged extensively with Platonism and Greek learning, holding that philosophy was a preparation for the gospel.
