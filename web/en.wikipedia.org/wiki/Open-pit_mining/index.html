Open-pit mining

Bench terraces of a large open-pit mine.

Open-pit mining, also known as open-cast or open-cut mining, is a surface mining technique that extracts rock or minerals from the earth by their removal from an open-air pit, sometimes known as a borrow. Open-pit mines are used when deposits of commercially useful ore or rock are found near the surface, and are among the largest human-made excavations in the world.

Method

The pit is dug in a series of horizontal layers called benches, typically 10–15 metres high, connected by haul roads on which enormous trucks carry blasted ore and waste rock. As mining deepens, each bench is stepped back, giving the pit its terraced profile; overall wall angles are engineered for slope stability. The ratio of waste to ore (stripping ratio) rises with depth and ultimately decides whether a deposit is better mined from underground.

Scale and examples

  • Bingham Canyon Mine, Utah — among the deepest open pits, about 1.2 km deep and 4 km wide
  • Chuquicamata and Escondida, Chile — giant copper pits
  • Super Pit, Kalgoorlie, Australia — gold
  • Grasberg, Indonesia — copper and gold
  • Mir mine, Russia — diamond kimberlite pipe

Environmental issues

Open-pit mining produces vast waste-rock dumps and tailings; the final pit commonly fills with groundwater to form a pit lake that may be acidic and metal-laden. Rehabilitation obligations increasingly require progressive backfilling, recontouring and revegetation, although restoration of a deep pit to its original topography is generally impossible.
