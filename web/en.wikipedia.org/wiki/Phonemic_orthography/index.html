Phonemic orthography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A phonemic orthography is an orthography in which the graphemes (written symbols) correspond to the phonemes (significant spoken sounds) of the language. Such a system has a near one-to-one relationship between letters and sounds: each phoneme is represented by one grapheme and each grapheme represents one phoneme. Languages with highly phonemic writing systems are said to have shallow orthographies, in contrast to deep orthographies like that of English, where spelling often preserves morphemes and etymology at the expense of sound.

Examples

Serbian Cyrillic is famously phonemic, following Vuk Karadžić's principle "write as you speak and read as it is written". Finnish, Turkish (after the 1928 alphabet reform), Croatian Latin, Spanish, Italian, Korean hangul (largely), and Esperanto are all relatively shallow. The International Phonetic Alphabet is a fully phonemic (indeed phonetic) notation, though it is a transcription system rather than a practical orthography.

Deviations

No natural orthography is perfectly phonemic: assimilation, dialect variation and historical change blur the correspondence, and purely phonemic spelling can obscure relationships between words — for example if electric and electricity were spelled strictly by sound, their shared root would be hard to see. Orthographies therefore trade off phonemic transparency against morphological constancy. Reforms that increase phonemicity include the Turkish script reform, the Dutch and German spelling reforms, and periodic proposals for English spelling reform.
