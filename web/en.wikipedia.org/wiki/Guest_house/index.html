Guest house

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A guest house (also guesthouse) is a kind of lodging. In some regions of the world, notably South Asia and Southern Africa, the term describes an inexpensive, often family-run establishment resembling a small hotel or boarding house; elsewhere it may denote a private home with rooms let to paying guests, or a separate dwelling on a property provided for visitors. The term is used with somewhat different meanings in different countries.

Types and usage

In the United Kingdom, a guest house is typically a small, owner-occupied establishment offering bed and breakfast, smaller and less formal than a hotel, and usually without a licence to serve alcohol. In India, government guest houses (also called circuit houses or dak bungalows in the colonial period) provide accommodation for officials, while commercial guest houses serve budget travellers. In southern Africa, guest houses are a major form of upmarket leisure accommodation, often converted homes with a handful of en-suite rooms.

In the United States, "guest house" more often means a secondary dwelling on a residential lot — also called a granny flat, in-law unit, or accessory dwelling unit — used to house visitors or relatives rather than commercial guests.

Distinctions

A guest house differs from a bed and breakfast mainly in scale and formality, though usage overlaps; from an inn in that it historically does not serve alcohol or full meals to the public; and from a hostel in that rooms are private rather than dormitory-style. In Japan, the minshuku is a comparable family-run lodging, while in Thailand and Cambodia guesthouses form the backbone of backpacker accommodation.
