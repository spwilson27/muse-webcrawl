Serial ATA (SATA, stylized as SATA; abbreviated from Serial AT Attachment) is a computer bus interface that connects host bus adapters to mass storage devices such as hard disk drives, solid-state drives, and optical drives. Serial ATA succeeded the earlier Parallel ATA (PATA) standard, offering several advantages including reduced cable size and cost, native hot swapping, faster data transfer through higher signaling rates, and more efficient transfer through an optional I/O queuing protocol.

History

SATA was announced in 2000 by the Serial ATA Working Group, led by Intel with partners including Seagate and Maxtor, and the SATA 1.0 specification was released in 2003. The first generation operated at 1.5 Gbit/s; SATA revision 2.0 doubled throughput to 3 Gbit/s and added native command queuing; revision 3.0 (2009) reached 6 Gbit/s. The standard is now maintained by the Serial ATA International Organization (SATA-IO).

Features

Unlike parallel ATA's shared master/slave bus, SATA uses point-to-point links: each drive connects to its own port with a slim seven-conductor cable up to one metre long, improving airflow and simplifying installation. SATA uses low-voltage differential signaling and 8b/10b encoding in its first three revisions. Drives share the 15-pin power connector design, and the interface remains software-compatible with the ATA command set via AHCI, enabling broad operating-system support. Variants include eSATA for external drives, mSATA, and the compact SATA Express and M.2 and U.2 connectors that can also carry PCIe/NVMe traffic.
