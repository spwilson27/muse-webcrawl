Celestial spheres

The celestial spheres, or celestial orbs, were the fundamental entities of the cosmological models developed by Plato, Eudoxus, Aristotle, Ptolemy, Copernicus and others. In these models, the apparent motions of the fixed stars and planets were accounted for by treating them as embedded in rotating spheres made of an aethereal, transparent fifth element (quintessence).

History

Eudoxus of Cnidus devised a system of homocentric spheres whose combined rotations reproduced planetary motions, and Aristotle incorporated the spheres into his physics as real material shells of aether, moved by unmoved movers and ultimately by the Prime Mover. Ptolemy's Almagest used eccentrics and epicycles that medieval astronomers, both Islamic and Christian, reified into solid nested orbs, culminating in elaborate schemes with nine or more spheres, the outermost being the primum mobile and beyond it the Empyrean.

Decline

The solid spheres were undermined in the late sixteenth century: Tycho Brahe showed that the comet of 1577 moved through the supposed planetary spheres, and his geo-heliocentric system required intersecting orbits impossible for solid shells. Kepler replaced circular machinery with elliptical orbits, and Newton later supplied a physical cause in universal gravitation, completing the spheres' disappearance from serious astronomy — though their music, the musica universalis or "harmony of the spheres", survived in cultural imagination.
