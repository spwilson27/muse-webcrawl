André-Gustave Citroën (5 February 1878 – 3 July 1935) was a French industrialist and the founder of the French automobile manufacturer Citroën. He is remembered chiefly for the innovative marketing campaigns and production methods he introduced to the European motor industry.

Career

Born in Paris into a family of Dutch-Jewish diamond merchants, Citroën graduated from the École polytechnique in 1900. In Poland he discovered the chevron-shaped (herringbone) gear, whose double-helical teeth became the emblem of his companies. During the First World War he organised a vast munitions factory on the Quai de Javel in Paris, applying American mass-production techniques inspired by Henry Ford.

In 1919 he converted the plant to car production, launching the Type A, the first mass-produced European car. Citroën pioneered the all-steel body, low-price sales, dealer networks, road signage and spectacular publicity, including writing the company's name across the Paris sky in aeroplane smoke and lighting the Eiffel Tower with 250,000 bulbs spelling "Citroën". The innovative Traction Avant of 1934 — front-wheel drive, unitary construction, independent front suspension — was a triumph of engineering but its development costs bankrupted the company, which was taken over by Michelin. Citroën died of stomach cancer in 1935.
