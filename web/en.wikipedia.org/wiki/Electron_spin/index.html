Electron spin is an intrinsic form of angular momentum carried by electrons. In quantum mechanics, spin is a fundamental property of elementary particles that has no exact classical analogue: the electron does not literally rotate like a spinning ball, yet it behaves as if it possesses angular momentum with a fixed magnitude and only two possible orientations relative to any chosen axis. Spin was proposed by George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit in 1925 to explain features of atomic spectra.

Discovery

The Stern–Gerlach experiment of 1922 showed that a beam of silver atoms passing through an inhomogeneous magnetic field split into two discrete components, implying a quantised two-valued angular momentum. Wolfgang Pauli introduced a two-component "two-valuedness" into his theory and formulated the exclusion principle in 1925. Ralph Kronig, and then Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit, suggested the electron itself carries spin. Paul Dirac's relativistic equation of 1928 predicted electron spin naturally, together with the g-factor of approximately 2.

Properties

The electron has spin quantum number s = 1/2, making it a fermion. Its spin angular momentum has magnitude √[s(s+1)]ħ = (√3/2)ħ, while the component along any axis can only be +ħ/2 or −ħ/2. Associated with the spin is a magnetic moment, which is responsible for much of the magnetism of materials and for fine structure in atomic spectra. The Pauli exclusion principle — no two electrons in an atom may share all quantum numbers, including spin projection — governs the structure of the periodic table.

Applications

Electron spin is exploited in electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy, in spintronics devices that use spin currents rather than charge currents, and in quantum computing, where single electron spins in quantum dots or donors serve as qubits. Spin-polarised currents also underlie giant magnetoresistance read heads in hard disk drives.
