LORAN

LORAN, short for long range navigation, is a hyperbolic radio navigation system developed in the United States during the Second World War. It allows a receiver to determine its position by listening to low-frequency radio signals transmitted by fixed land-based radio beacons. LORAN was first used for ship convoys crossing the Atlantic in 1943 and was valued especially for its long range and its usefulness when celestial navigation was impractical.

Principle of operation

LORAN works by measuring the time difference between signals received from pairs of transmitters. Each constant time difference defines a hyperbola on the Earth's surface; the intersection of two or more hyperbolas gives the receiver's position. Stations are organised into chains, with a master station and several secondary stations transmitting precisely timed pulses.

LORAN-C

The most widely used version, LORAN-C, operated in the 90–110 kHz band and offered accuracy of roughly 0.25 nautical miles (460 m), with much better repeatability. Chains covered the North Atlantic, the North Pacific, the Mediterranean, and the coasts of North America. The Soviet Union operated a similar system called Chayka, and several other nations ran compatible chains.

Decline and eLORAN

The spread of satellite navigation, especially GPS, led to the shutdown of most LORAN-C chains in the 1990s and 2000s; the United States and Canada terminated their service in 2010. Concerns about the vulnerability of satellite systems to jamming and spoofing have sustained interest in eLORAN (enhanced LORAN), a modernised version using data channels and improved receivers as a backup to GNSS. South Korea and the United Kingdom have pursued eLORAN development, and China continues to operate its own chains.
