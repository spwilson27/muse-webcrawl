Embrace, extend, and extinguish

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

"Embrace, extend, and extinguish" — also rendered "embrace, extend, and exterminate" — is a phrase describing a strategy for entering a market with a standards-based product: first embrace a widely used standard, then extend it with proprietary features incompatible with rivals' implementations, and finally, once the extensions dominate, extinguish competitors by marginalising the original standard. The phrase became widely known during the United States antitrust case United States v. Microsoft Corp., where it was attributed to Microsoft's internal descriptions of its approach to software standards.

Origin

The earliest known use in this form is attributed to Microsoft executive Paul Maritz in the 1990s; in testimony and documents produced at trial, the strategy was described in relation to technologies including the Java platform — in which Microsoft's Java virtual machine added Windows-specific extensions — Internet protocols, and web standards. Microsoft maintained that it merely added value to standards and that the phrase mischaracterised ordinary product competition, but internal emails describing plans to "extend" standards until Microsoft's implementations became the de facto standard figured prominently in the proceedings.

The three phases

  1. Embrace: announce support for a standard and develop a compatible product, encouraging adoption.
  2. Extend: add proprietary capabilities that competitors' products lack, so documents, files, or services created with the product depend on the extensions, creating lock-in.
  3. Extinguish: once the extended version is the market norm, competitors implementing only the original standard are effectively excluded.

Usage of the term

The phrase has since been applied — by critics and commentators — to alleged strategies of other technology companies and to debates over web browsers, document formats such as Office Open XML, instant messaging protocols, and open-source ecosystems. Supporters of the companies involved typically dispute the characterisation, arguing that extension is a legitimate and often standard-sanctioned part of how technologies evolve. The term remains a staple of discussions about standards, network effects, and platform competition.
