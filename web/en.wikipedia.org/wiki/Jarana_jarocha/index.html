The jarana jarocha is a guitar-shaped, five-stringed fretted instrument from the state of Veracruz, Mexico. It is the rhythmic backbone of son jarocho, the regional folk music of the jarochos (people of Veracruz), where it plays together with the four-string requinto jarocho (which carries the melody) and the arpa jarocha (harp).

Description

The jarana is typically carved from a single block of wood — traditionally Spanish cedar — with a separate soundboard glued on, though modern instruments may be built up from staves. It measures roughly 60–80 cm in length. Its five strings are arranged in five single courses, usually tuned G–C–E–A–G (in various octaves), and struck with a long plectrum or the fingers in rapid, syncopated strumming patterns called rasgueos. Sizes vary from the small jarana mosquito through primera, segunda and tercera to the deep-voiced jaranón.

Role in son jarocho

In a fandango — the community celebration at the heart of son jarocho — jaranas provide the harmonic and rhythmic compás over which the requinto improvises melodies, singers trade improvised verses (coplas), and dancers stomp out percussive rhythms (zapateado) on a wooden platform, the tarima. The best-known son jarocho piece is "La Bamba", popularised internationally by Ritchie Valens in 1958 and by Los Lobos in 1987; contemporary groups such as Los Cojolites and Son de Madera have renewed interest in the jarana tradition.

Construction

Luthiers in communities such as Santiago Tuxtla and Los Tuxtlas region carve jaranas by hand. Traditional construction hollows the body from one piece of cedar, adds a soundboard of the same or spruce, and inlays decorative sound-hole rosettes. Interest in the instrument grew with the movimiento jaranero revival beginning in the late 1970s, when musicians reconstructed older playing styles from elderly masters.
