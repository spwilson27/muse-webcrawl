Codex Sinaiticus

The Codex Sinaiticus (Shelfmark: London, British Library, Add MS 43725), also called the Sinai Bible, is a handwritten copy of the Christian Bible in Greek, made between about 330 and 360 AD. It is one of the two oldest nearly complete Bibles in existence, along with the Codex Vaticanus, and is among the most important witnesses to the text of the Septuagint and the Greek New Testament. The codex is written in uncial script on parchment in four columns per page.

Contents

The codex originally contained the whole of both Testaments. Roughly half of the Septuagint survives; the New Testament is complete, and the volume also preserves two early Christian works not in the modern canon, the Epistle of Barnabas and part of the Shepherd of Hermas. Notable textual features include the absence of the longer ending of Mark and of the pericope of the woman taken in adultery in John.

Discovery and history

The German scholar Constantin von Tischendorf encountered the manuscript at Saint Catherine's Monastery at the foot of Mount Sinai in 1844, 1853, and 1859, removing leaves first to Leipzig and then, under the patronage of Tsar Alexander II, to St. Petersburg. In 1933 the Soviet government sold the main portion to the British Museum for £100,000, raised by public subscription. Tischendorf's account of monks burning leaves as fuel in 1844 remains disputed. Leaves found in the monastery in 1975 added further folios.

Digital reunification

An international project by the four holding institutions published a complete digital facsimile and transcription online in 2009, virtually reuniting the dispersed codex for scholars and the public.
