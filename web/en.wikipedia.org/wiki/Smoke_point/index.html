Smoke point

The smoke point, also referred to as the burning point, is the temperature at which a cooking oil or fat begins to produce a continuous plume of blue-grey smoke that becomes clearly visible, under specified conditions. Smoke point values can range from 325 °F (163 °C) for unrefined oils and butter to over 520 °F (271 °C) for highly refined oils such as avocado oil. Past the smoke point are the flash point and the fire point, at which vapours can ignite.

Factors affecting smoke point

Smoke point depends mainly on the content of free fatty acids and other volatile compounds, and on the degree of refining: refining removes impurities and raises the smoke point. Heating oil repeatedly degrades it and lowers its smoke point, as does prolonged exposure to air and light. The smoke is a sign of decomposition of the oil; compounds such as acrolein form, giving burnt, bitter flavours, and many cooks regard oil heated past its smoke point as unsuitable for use.

Selected smoke points

Fat or oilSmoke point (approx.)
Butter150 °C / 302 °F
Extra virgin olive oil (unrefined)190 °C / 375 °F
Lard190 °C / 374 °F
Refined canola oil204 °C / 400 °F
Ghee (clarified butter)250 °C / 482 °F
Refined peanut oil232 °C / 450 °F
Refined avocado oil271 °C / 520 °F

High smoke point oils are preferred for deep frying and searing; delicate unrefined oils are better suited to dressings and low-temperature cooking.
