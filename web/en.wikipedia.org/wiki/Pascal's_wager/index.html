Pascal's wager is a philosophical argument presented by the French mathematician and philosopher Blaise Pascal (1623–1669) in his posthumous Pensées. Pascal argued that human beings bet with their lives on whether God exists, and that reason cannot settle the question.

The argument

If God exists and you believe, you gain infinite reward; if God exists and you do not believe, you risk infinite loss; if God does not exist, belief costs comparatively little. Therefore, Pascal concluded, a rational person should cultivate belief. Critics object that the argument assumes a particular God (the 'many gods' objection), that genuine belief cannot simply be willed, and that it reduces faith to self-interest. Pascal, co-founder of probability theory with Fermat, framed the wager in decision-theoretic terms centuries before the field existed.
