The varieties of Chinese form the Sinitic branch of the Sino-Tibetan language family, spoken by about 1.3 billion people, predominantly in China and Taiwan and in overseas Chinese communities. The varieties differ from one another in phonology, vocabulary and grammar to an extent comparable to the Romance languages; many are mutually unintelligible in speech, so linguists often treat them as separate languages, while Chinese tradition — reinforced by a shared writing system and political unity — calls them dialects (fāngyán).

Classification

The traditional scheme recognises seven major groups: Mandarin (官话), spoken across the north and southwest and the basis of the national standard; Wu (吴语), centred on Shanghai and Zhejiang, including Shanghainese; Yue (粤语), including Cantonese of Guangdong and Hong Kong; Min (闽语), the most internally diverse group, of Fujian and Taiwan, including Hokkien; Hakka (客家话), scattered through the southern hills; Xiang (湘语) of Hunan; and Gan (赣语) of Jiangxi. Modern classifications add Jin, Huizhou and Pinghua as groups of their own.

Features

All varieties are tonal and largely analytic, with syllable structure constrained by a (consonant)–vowel–(nasal/stop) pattern. They differ sharply in tone inventories — from four in Standard Mandarin to six or more in Cantonese — and in how much of the final consonant system of Middle Chinese they preserve. Min varieties preserve features lost elsewhere, having split off before Middle Chinese.

Standard Chinese and writing

The national standard, Putonghua ("common speech") in China, Guoyu in Taiwan and Huayu in Singapore, is based on the Mandarin of Beijing. Since the early 20th century, written Chinese has been based on this vernacular (baihua), replacing Classical Chinese; written Cantonese exists but is largely informal. Because characters encode meaning more than sound, literate speakers of different varieties can usually read the same standard text while pronouncing it quite differently.
