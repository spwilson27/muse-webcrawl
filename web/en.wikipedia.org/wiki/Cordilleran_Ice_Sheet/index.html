Cordilleran Ice Sheet

The Cordilleran ice sheet was a major ice sheet that periodically covered large parts of North America during the Quaternary period, centred on the western mountain ranges (the North American Cordillera). At its maximum extent during the Last Glacial Maximum (about 20,000 years ago), it covered most of Canada west of the Rocky Mountains, the Alaska panhandle, and parts of the northern United States, including much of Washington state and parts of Idaho and Montana.

Extent and behaviour

The ice sheet grew from coalescing mountain glaciers and ice caps in the Coast Mountains, Columbia Mountains, and Rocky Mountains, reaching thicknesses of up to about 2,000 metres in places; peaks protruded above the ice as nunataks. Its lobes advanced into lowlands — the Puget Lobe reached as far south as present-day Olympia, Washington, and the Okanagan Lobe dammed valleys in the interior of British Columbia.

To the east, the Cordilleran ice sheet at times merged with the much larger Laurentide Ice Sheet, closing off an ice-free corridor between them. The timing of the opening of this corridor after the last glaciation — around 13,000–14,000 years ago — is important to debates about the first peopling of the Americas, since it offered an interior migration route from Beringia once it became passable (an older alternative being a Pacific coastal route).

Deglaciation and floods

As the ice sheet retreated, it dammed large proglacial lakes. The Cordilleran ice dammed glacial Lake Missoula in Montana; repeated failures of that ice dam released the catastrophic Missoula Floods that scoured the Channeled Scablands of Washington. Melting of the Cordilleran and Laurentide ice sheets contributed substantially to post-glacial sea-level rise.
