Kerr metric

Placeholder image: horizons and ergosphere of a Kerr black hole.

The Kerr metric, or Kerr geometry, is an exact solution of the Einstein field equations of general relativity describing the spacetime around a rotating, uncharged, axisymmetric mass. It was discovered by New Zealand mathematician Roy Kerr in 1963 and generalises the Schwarzschild metric to rotating bodies. Because astrophysical black holes are expected to spin, the Kerr metric is the standard model of a realistic black hole.

Structure

The metric is characterised by two parameters: the mass M and the angular momentum per unit mass, a. A Kerr black hole has two event horizons (outer and inner) and an outer ergosphere, a region in which spacetime itself is dragged around so strongly (an effect called frame-dragging) that no observer can remain stationary relative to distant stars. The ring-shaped singularity lies in the equatorial plane.

Extremal limit and the Penrose process

When a exceeds the extremal bound (a = M in geometric units), the horizons would disappear, leaving a naked singularity; Roger Penrose's cosmic censorship hypothesis holds that this cannot occur physically. Within the ergosphere, the Penrose process allows rotational energy to be extracted from the black hole, making spinning holes potential engines for energetic astrophysical phenomena such as relativistic jets.

Observational relevance

Measurements of accretion flows, gravitational waves from merging black holes, and the Event Horizon Telescope images of M87* and Sagittarius A* are routinely interpreted using the Kerr solution.
