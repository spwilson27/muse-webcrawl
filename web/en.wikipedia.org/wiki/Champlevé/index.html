Champlevé

A 12th-century Limoges champlevé enamel plaque.

Champlevé is an enamelling technique in the decorative arts, in which troughs or cells are carved, engraved, etched or cast into the surface of a metal object, and filled with vitreous enamel. The piece is then fired until the enamel fuses, and when cooled the surface is filed and polished flat. The name is French for "raised field": the metal surface rises between the sunken cells of enamel. The technique differs from cloisonné, in which the cells are built up from added wires or strips rather than hollowed out of the base metal.

History

Champlevé
Technique: Enamelling on metal
Name origin: French, "raised field"
Peak: 12th–13th centuries, Limoges
Typical metals: Copper, bronze, gold

Champlevé was known in Celtic and Roman metalwork, and was used in the early Middle Ages on brooches and mounts. It reached its height in Romanesque art, above all in the workshops of Limoges in south-western France, where from about 1100 to the 13th century large quantities of champlevé enamel on gilt copper — reliquary chests (châsses), crosses, altarpieces, book covers and liturgical vessels — were produced and exported across Europe. Mosan workshops in the Meuse valley also produced major champlevé work, often combined with other techniques. Because the cells could be large and the copper base was cheap relative to gold cloisonné, Limoges champlevé suited serial production, and its characteristically deep blue enamel became famous.

Technique

The design was transferred to a metal plate, usually copper, and the areas to receive enamel were hollowed with chisels and gravers, leaving raised lines and fields that would appear as the gilded outlines of the design. Powdered glass enamel in the required colours was packed into the hollows, sometimes in several firings, and the object was heated to about 800 °C until the enamel melted. After cooling, the surface was ground smooth with abrasive stones, polished, and the exposed metal was often mercury-gilded. Figures frequently had their heads and other details executed separately in relief and applied to the enamelled plaque.
