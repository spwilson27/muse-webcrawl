Video game crash of 1983

The video game crash of 1983 (known in Japan as the Atari shock) was a large-scale recession in the video game industry that lasted from 1983 to 1985, primarily in North America. Revenues for home video games fell from a peak of about $3.2 billion in 1983 to around $100 million by 1985, a collapse of some 97 percent, bankrupting many console and software companies and nearly ending the home game market in the United States.

Retailers slashed prices as unsold games flooded the market

Causes

Several factors combined. The market was flooded with consoles and, because Atari had lost a court fight over third-party publishing, an uncontrolled glut of poor-quality cartridges — many produced by start-ups competing on price rather than quality. Retailers lost confidence, discounted aggressively and cut orders. High-profile failures, notably the rushed Atari 2600 port of Pac-Man and the notoriously poor E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial (millions of unsold copies of which Atari buried in a New Mexico landfill, a story long treated as legend until a 2014 excavation confirmed it), symbolised the bubble. Home computers such as the Commodore 64, promoted with price wars as better value for families, undercut the consoles further.

Effects and recovery

Atari was sold off by Warner Communications in 1984; Magnavox, Coleco and Mattel exited or were crippled, and most third-party publishers collapsed. The industry recovered only in 1985–86 with the American launch of the Nintendo Entertainment System, whose tight licensing controls, quality seal and lockout chip were designed specifically to prevent a repeat of the glut. The crash also permanently shifted the centre of the home-console industry from the United States to Japan, where the market — dominated by Nintendo's Famicom — had remained comparatively healthy.
