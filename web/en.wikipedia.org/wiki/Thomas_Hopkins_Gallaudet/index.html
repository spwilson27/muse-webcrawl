Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet

Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet (December 10, 1787 – September 10, 1851) was an American educator and a pioneering figure in the education of the deaf. Along with Laurent Clerc and Mason Cogswell, he co-founded the first permanent school for the deaf in North America—the American School for the Deaf in Hartford, Connecticut, which opened in 1817. He is consequently remembered as a founding figure of deaf education in the United States.

Life and work

Gallaudet graduated from Yale in 1805 and studied theology at Andover before ill health forced him to abandon plans for the ministry. His interest in deaf education began after meeting his Hartford neighbour Mason Cogswell's young deaf daughter, Alice, and attempting to teach her to communicate. Community leaders raised funds to send Gallaudet to Europe in 1815 to study methods of deaf instruction. In Britain he met with limited cooperation from the oralist Braidwood schools, but in Paris he studied at the famous school of the Abbé Sicard and persuaded its deaf teacher Laurent Clerc to return with him to America.

Gallaudet served as the first principal of the Hartford school (the American Asylum for the Deaf and Dumb) from 1817 until his resignation in 1830, teaching in a combination of French Sign Language–derived signs and English that became the basis of American Sign Language (ASL). He married one of his former pupils, Sophia Fowler. His son Edward Miner Gallaudet later became the first president of the college for the deaf in Washington, D.C., chartered in 1864 and now named Gallaudet University in honour of Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet.

Legacy

Gallaudet is commemorated by statues at Gallaudet University—Daniel Chester French's 1889 bronze shows him seated with Alice Cogswell, spelling the letter "A" into her hand—and his name is borne by schools and buildings across the United States. His work established the enduring American tradition of sign-language-based deaf education.
