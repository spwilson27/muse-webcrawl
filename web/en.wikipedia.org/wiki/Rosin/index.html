Rosin

Rosin, also called colophony or Greek pitch, is a solid form of resin obtained from pines and other conifers, produced by heating fresh liquid resin to vapourise the volatile terpenes. It is semi-transparent, varies in colour from yellow to black, and consists chiefly of abietic acid and related resin acids.

Production

Rosin is the non-volatile residue left after distilling turpentine from crude oleoresin tapped from living pine trees, or extracted from pine stumps (wood rosin) or as a by-product of kraft pulping (tall oil rosin).

Uses

Rosin is rubbed on the bows of stringed instruments to provide friction against the strings. It is used in soldering fluxes, printing inks, varnishes, adhesives, soaps, paper sizing, and as a grip aid by dancers, gymnasts, baseball pitchers and weightlifters. Its derivatives, ester gums, appear in chewing gum and food additives.

Properties

Rosin is brittle and friable at room temperature, softens around 70–80 °C, and is soluble in alcohol, ether and many organic solvents but not in water. Prolonged skin contact can cause contact dermatitis; colophony is a common allergen.
