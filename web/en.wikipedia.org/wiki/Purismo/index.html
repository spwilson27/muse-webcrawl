Purismo


Italian Purismo painting, inspired by the early Renaissance "primitives".

Purismo was an Italian cultural movement of the 19th century, beginning in literature and extending to art and music, which sought a return to the purity of earlier, "primitive" models. In art the term describes a movement in Italian painting of the 1830s–1860s inspired by the Nazarenes and looking back to early Renaissance masters such as Fra Angelico, Perugino, and the young Raphael.

Origins

The literary theory of Purismo was championed by the scholar Antonio Cesari, who advocateded a return to the language of 14th-century Tuscan writers. In painting, the movement's manifesto, Del purismo nelle arti (1843), was written by the critic Antonio Bianchini and signed by the painter Tommaso Minardi, the sculptor Pietro Tenerani, and the Nazarene Friedrich Overbeck. The Purists valued clarity of drawing, calm expression, and morally edifying—often religious—subject matter, rejecting what they saw as the empty virtuosity of academic classicism and Baroque excess.

Artists and works

Tommaso Minardi was the movement's leading painter and teacher in Rome. Related tendencies appeared across Italy; in Tuscany the influence of the Purists touched early work associated with the Macchiaioli milieu, though the Macchiaioli soon moved in a very different, realist direction. Purismo's emphasis on sincerity and pre-Raphaelite clarity paralleled the roughly contemporary Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood in England (founded 1848), with which it shared models and ideals.

Legacy

Although sometimes dismissed as derivative and nostalgic, Purismo formed part of the broader 19th-century medieval revival and helped redirect Italian art away from late academic formulas toward direct observation and spiritual seriousness.
