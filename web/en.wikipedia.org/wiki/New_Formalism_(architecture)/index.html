New Formalism (architecture)

New Formalism is an architectural style that emerged in the United States during the mid-1950s and flourished in the 1960s. Buildings in the style exhibit many classical elements, such as highly stylized or simplified classical columns and entablatures, a smooth stone or marble finish, and a strong sense of symmetry and monumentality, combined with modern construction and often a raised podium or stylobate. The term is associated with architects including Edward Durell Stone, Minoru Yamasaki, and Philip Johnson.

Characteristics

  • Emphasis on symmetry and formal axial planning
  • Smooth wall surfaces, often of marble, travertine, granite, or polished concrete
  • Highly stylized, simplified classical ornament and columns
  • Buildings raised on a podium or platform (stylobate)
  • Rational, grid-like façades with delicate screens or grilles
  • A sense of monumentality suited to civic, cultural, and institutional buildings

History and examples

The style arose as a reaction against the austerity of the International Style, seeking to restore elegance, ornament (albeit abstracted), and classical dignity to modern architecture. Edward Durell Stone's United States Embassy in New Delhi (1954–1959), with its gridded screen walls and classical proportions, is considered an early landmark. Other prominent examples include Stone's original Museum of Modern Art building façade and his design for the Kennedy Center in Washington, D.C.; Minoru Yamasaki's McGregor Memorial Conference Center (1958) at Wayne State University and the US Science Pavilion at the 1962 Seattle World's Fair; Lincoln Center in New York (Philip Johnson, Max Abramovitz, and others); and the Los Angeles Music Center.

New Formalism was widely used for embassies, museums, performing arts centers, universities, and corporate headquarters during the 1960s, but fell out of favor in the 1970s as tastes shifted toward late modernism and postmodernism. Interest in the style revived in the 21st century as many of its buildings reached the age of historic preservation evaluations.
