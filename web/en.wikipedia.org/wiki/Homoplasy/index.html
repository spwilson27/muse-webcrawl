Homoplasy

Homoplasy, in biology and phylogenetics, is the term for a character shared by a set of species but not present in their common ancestor — that is, a trait that evolved independently in separate lineages. A homoplastic trait is the product of convergent evolution, parallel evolution, or evolutionary reversal.

Examples

Classic examples include the camera-type eyes of vertebrates and cephalopods, the wings of insects, birds and bats, and the streamlined bodies of sharks, dolphins and ichthyosaurs. In plants, the evolution of succulent, water-storing stems in American cacti and African euphorbias is a textbook homoplasy.

Distinction from homology

Homoplasy contrasts with homology, similarity due to common descent. Because homoplastic traits can mislead classification, phylogenetic methods attempt to minimise the influence of homoplasy when reconstructing evolutionary relationships, for example through parsimony analysis or probabilistic models.

Causes

Homoplasies arise because similar selective pressures act on unrelated organisms, because developmental pathways are constrained in similar ways, or because a trait is lost and later re-evolves (or vice versa). Molecular studies have revealed extensive homoplasy at the genetic level as well.
