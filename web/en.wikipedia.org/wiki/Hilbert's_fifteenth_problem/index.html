Hilbert's fifteenth problem

Hilbert's fifteenth problem, posed by David Hilbert in his 1900 list of problems for the new century, asks for a rigorous foundation of the Schubert calculus — the enumerative geometry of Hermann Schubert, which counted geometric configurations (such as the number of lines meeting four given lines in space) using a formal "principle of conservation of number" that lacked rigorous justification.

The problem

Hilbert asked that Schubert's calculus of enumerative geometry be put on a firm axiomatic footing, so that the numbers it produced could be proved correct and the limits of its validity determined. The question drove much of twentieth-century algebraic geometry: Francesco Severi and the Italian school took it up, and Bartel van der Waerden and André Weil contributed foundational approaches.

Resolution

The modern resolution rests on intersection theory: Schubert varieties have classes in the Chow ring (or cohomology ring) of Grassmannians, and enumerative numbers are computed as intersection numbers with rigorous foundations. In the later twentieth century, work by Steven Kleiman on transversality and by William Fulton on intersection theory established the desired rigour for the classical cases, and the Schubert calculus continues in modern form in Gromov–Witten theory and quantum cohomology.
