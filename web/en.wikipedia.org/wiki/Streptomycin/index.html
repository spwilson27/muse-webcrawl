Streptomycin, the first effective drug against tuberculosis.

Streptomycin is an aminoglycoside antibiotic derived from the soil bacterium Streptomyces griseus. Discovered in 1943 in the laboratory of Selman Waksman at Rutgers University — the isolation is credited to graduate student Albert Schatz — it was the first antibiotic remedy for tuberculosis and the first effective treatment against Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Waksman received the 1952 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery, an award later shadowed by a dispute and legal settlement with Schatz over credit.

History

On 19 October 1943, Schatz isolated streptomycin from strains of Streptomyces griseus. Working with Elizabeth Bugie and Waksman, the team showed activity against Gram-negative bacteria and the tubercle bacillus. The first patient with pulmonary tuberculosis was treated in November 1944 at the Mayo Clinic, and wartime production was undertaken by Merck & Co. A landmark 1948 British Medical Research Council trial of streptomycin in tuberculosis became a landmark of the modern randomized controlled trial.

Medical uses

Streptomycin is given by intramuscular injection because it is poorly absorbed from the gut. Current uses include:

  • Tuberculosis, as a second-line or injectable agent in multidrug regimens, and historically a core first-line drug.
  • Plague (Yersinia pestis), for which it has been a treatment of choice.
  • Tularemia, brucellosis (with doxycycline), and infective endocarditis caused by susceptible organisms (with penicillin for enterococci).
  • Bacterial infections resistant to other agents, in combination therapy.

Mechanism

Streptomycin binds to the S12 protein and 16S rRNA of the bacterial 30S ribosomal subunit, causing misreading of mRNA codons and inhibiting protein synthesis; at sufficient concentrations it is bactericidal. Resistance arises through ribosomal mutations, drug-modifying enzymes, and reduced uptake.

Adverse effects

Streptomycin's use is limited by toxicity: it can damage the inner ear (ototoxicity — especially vestibular dysfunction and hearing loss) and the kidneys (nephrotoxicity), so courses are kept short and monitored. It is also used as a pesticide (bactericide) against fire blight in orchards.
