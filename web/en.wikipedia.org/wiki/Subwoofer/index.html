Subwoofer

A subwoofer (or sub) is a loudspeaker designed to reproduce low-pitched audio frequencies known as bass and sub-bass — typically from about 20 to 200 Hz — which are lower than those a conventional full-range loudspeaker or woofer can adequately reproduce. Subwoofers use one or more large drivers, commonly 8 to 21 inches (20 to 54 cm) in diameter, mounted in an enclosure tuned to move large volumes of air.

History

Early subwoofers were developed in the 1960s for high-end home stereo systems; Ken Kreisel of Miller & Kreisel Sound is credited with one of the first dedicated products in the 1970s. The format became mainstream with home theatre in the 1990s, as the ".1" low-frequency effects (LFE) channel of Dolby Digital 5.1 surround sound created demand for dedicated bass reproduction in living rooms.

Design

Enclosures may be sealed, ported (bass reflex), bandpass, passive-radiator or horn-loaded designs, each trading extension, efficiency and output. Most modern subwoofers are active, with a built-in power amplifier and an electronic crossover (low-pass filter) that feeds the unit only low frequencies, while passive subwoofers require an external amplifier. Because low bass is nearly non-directional to human hearing, a single subwoofer can serve a whole system, though multiple units are often used to smooth room modes.

Applications

Beyond home audio and cinema, subwoofers are used in car audio, concert sound reinforcement, cinemas, nightclubs, and in bass-heavy music production. Very large arrays power the lowest registers of electronic dance music, where infrasonic content below 20 Hz is felt physically as much as heard.
