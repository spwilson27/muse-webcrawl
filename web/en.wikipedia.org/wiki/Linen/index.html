Linen

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Linen is a textile made from the fibres of the flax plant, Linum usitatissimum. Linen is very strong and absorbent and dries faster than cotton. Because of these properties, linen is comfortable to wear in hot weather and is valued for use in garments, bed sheets, tablecloths, towels and canvases. It is one of the oldest textiles in the world: dyed flax fibres found in a cave in the Caucasus date to about 36,000 years ago, and linen wrapping was used for Egyptian mummies.

Production

Producing linen is labour-intensive. Flax stems are harvested and then retted — rotted in water or dew to loosen the bast fibres from the woody core. The stalks are then broken, scutched (scraped to remove woody fragments) and hackled (combed) before spinning. The long, smooth fibres give linen yarn its characteristic strength and slight lustre. The word "linen" survives in phrases such as "bed linen" and the linen cupboard even for goods now made of cotton.

Properties

Linen fibres are 2 to 3 times as strong as cotton, absorb up to 20% of their weight in moisture before feeling damp, and conduct heat away from the body, which is why linen is prized for summer clothing and bedding. The fabric creases readily — a trait long treated as a mark of its authenticity — and becomes softer with washing. Major historic production centres included Ireland (Irish linen), Belgium, and Egypt; today most flax is grown in France, Belgium and China.

Uses

Beyond apparel and household textiles, linen is used for artists' canvas (often oil-primed), bookbinding, banknotes (blended with cotton), industrial sewing thread and composites. Flaxseed and linseed oil are the seed products of the same plant.
