In mathematics, the Wiener process (also called Brownian motion) is a real-valued continuous-time stochastic process named in honour of Norbert Wiener for his investigations of the mathematical properties of one-dimensional Brownian motion. It is the mathematical idealisation of the random motion of particles suspended in a fluid, observed by Robert Brown in 1827 and modelled by Albert Einstein in 1905.

Definition

A Wiener process Wt is a stochastic process satisfying: W0 = 0; it has independent increments; increments Wt − Ws are normally distributed with mean 0 and variance t − s; and its sample paths are continuous in t, almost surely. The paths are, with probability one, nowhere differentiable, and the process has quadratic variation equal to t.

Applications

The Wiener process is fundamental to stochastic calculus, including the Itô integral, and underlies the Black–Scholes model in mathematical finance (through geometric Brownian motion), diffusion models in physics, and random-walk limits given by Donsker's invariance principle.
