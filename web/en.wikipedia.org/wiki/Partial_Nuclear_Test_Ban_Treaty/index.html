Partial Nuclear Test Ban Treaty

Signed5 August 1963, Moscow
Effective10 October 1963
Original signatoriesUnited Kingdom, Soviet Union, United States
Full nameTreaty Banning Nuclear Weapon Tests in the Atmosphere, in Outer Space and Under Water

The Partial Test Ban Treaty (PTBT), formally the Treaty Banning Nuclear Weapon Tests in the Atmosphere, in Outer Space and Under Water, prohibits all test detonations of nuclear weapons except those conducted underground. It was signed in Moscow on 5 August 1963 by the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union and the United States, and entered into force on 10 October 1963.

Background

Public alarm over radioactive fallout from atmospheric testing — dramatised by the contamination of the Japanese fishing vessel Lucky Dragon by the Castle Bravo test in 1954 — created pressure for a ban. Years of negotiations foundered on verification of underground tests; after the Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962, Washington, London and Moscow settled for the partial ban, which could be verified by national technical means without on-site inspection.

Terms

Parties undertake to prohibit and not to carry out any nuclear weapon test explosion, or any other nuclear explosion, in the atmosphere, beyond territorial limits including outer space, or under water, and also not to cause, encourage or participate in such explosions anywhere. Underground tests remained permitted provided they did not vent radioactive debris beyond the testing state's borders.

Effects

More than 120 states eventually joined the treaty, though France and China, then emerging nuclear powers, refused and continued atmospheric testing until 1974 and 1980 respectively. The treaty sharply reduced global fallout and was a landmark of early Cold War arms control, but it drove testing underground rather than ending it. It was a precursor of the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968) and the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (1996).
