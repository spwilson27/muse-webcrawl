Semiconservative replication describes the mechanism of DNA replication in which the two strands of the double helix separate, and each serves as a template for the synthesis of a new complementary strand. As a result, each daughter DNA molecule consists of one original (parental) strand and one newly synthesized strand — the parental molecule is thus "half conserved".

Evidence

When Watson and Crick proposed the double helix in 1953, they noted that the base-pairing rules suggested a copying mechanism, but three models were conceivable: conservative (the parental molecule is fully preserved and an entirely new molecule is made), semiconservative, and dispersive (parental material is scattered through both strands of both daughters).

The Meselson–Stahl experiment of 1958, often called "the most beautiful experiment in biology", used nitrogen isotopes (15N and 14N) and density-gradient centrifugation in Escherichia coli to show that after one generation all DNA was of hybrid density, and after two generations half was hybrid and half light — exactly as the semiconservative model predicts, ruling out both alternatives.

Mechanism

Replication proceeds from origins of replication, where helicase unwinds the helix; primase lays down RNA primers, DNA polymerases synthesize new strands in the 5′→3′ direction (continuously on the leading strand, in Okazaki fragments on the lagging strand), and ligase seals the nicks. Because each parental strand is retained in a daughter molecule, the process preserves genetic information with high fidelity.
