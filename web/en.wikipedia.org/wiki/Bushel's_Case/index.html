Bushel's Case

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bushel's Case (1670) 124 E.R. 1006 is a famous English decision establishing the independence of juries: jurors may not be punished — fined or imprisoned — for returning a verdict contrary to the judge's direction or the evidence as the judge sees it. At the Old Bailey trial of the Quakers William Penn and William Mead for unlawful assembly, the jury refused to convict despite judicial pressure; the jurors were fined and imprisoned for their “against the evidence” acquittal, and juror Edward Bushell sued out habeas corpus. Chief Justice Vaughan, in the Court of Common Pleas, ordered his release: the jury are the judges of fact, and a verdict cannot be “false” in a way punishable by the court, else the jury trial is a formality.

The case is a foundation-stone of the common-law jury: it secured the acquittal's finality in practice and underlies the modern law of jury independence and (controversially) the space in which jury nullification operates — juries can acquit against the evidence, though judges instruct them they must apply the law, and systems differ on how much of Bushel's logic they acknowledge aloud. Penn's trial and Bushel's stand became set-pieces of Whig and then American constitutional memory; the case is still cited whenever courts contemplate disciplining jurors for their verdicts.
