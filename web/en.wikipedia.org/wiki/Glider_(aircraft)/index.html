Glider (aircraft)

A glider or sailplane is a type of aircraft used in the sport of gliding. It is a heavier-than-air craft supported in flight by the dynamic reaction of the air against its lifting surfaces, with no dependence on an engine for sustained flight (some gliders, called motor gliders, carry small engines for self-launching or sustaining flight). Gliders are characterized by long, slender, high-aspect-ratio wings and extremely low drag, giving glide ratios that in the best modern designs exceed 60:1.

Flight

Because a glider continually descends relative to the surrounding air, cross-country and soaring flight depend on finding rising air: thermals, ridge lift along hillsides, mountain wave lift, and convergence zones. Launched by towplane (aerotow) or by a ground winch, the pilot climbs in lift and then glides to the next source, a technique that has carried sailplanes over distances of more than 3,000 kilometres and to altitudes above 15,000 metres in wave conditions.

History and types

Systematic glider experiments by pioneers such as Otto Lilienthal in the 1890s laid the groundwork for powered flight. After the First World War, soaring flourished in Germany, and modern composite sailplanes emerged from the 1960s onward. Competition classes range from club-class trainers to the 18-metre and open classes flown at world gliding championships. Distinct categories include hang gliders and paragliders, in which the pilot's body forms part of the control system, and military assault gliders of the Second World War, such as the Waco CG-4A and the Airspeed Horsa, which were towed to their targets and released.
