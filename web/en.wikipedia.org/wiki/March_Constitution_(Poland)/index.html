March Constitution (Poland)

The March Constitution, adopted 17 March 1921

The March Constitution (Polish: Konstytucja marcowa) was the first constitution of the Second Polish Republic, adopted on 17 March 1921 by the Legislative Sejm. It established Poland as a democratic parliamentary republic modelled largely on the constitution of the French Third Republic.

Background

After Poland regained independence in November 1918 following the First World War, the Legislative Sejm was elected in January 1919 to draft a permanent constitution. An interim "Small Constitution" of 20 February 1919 governed the state until the full document could be completed. Drafting was slowed by disagreement between the nationalist right, which favoured a strong presidency, and the left and centre, which wanted power concentrated in the legislature.

Provisions

The constitution declared that sovereignty rested with the nation and made the Sejm the dominant organ of state. The President of the Republic was elected for a seven-year term by the National Assembly (the Sejm and Senate sitting together) and held mostly representative functions. The government, headed by the Prime Minister, was responsible to the Sejm. Citizens were guaranteed broad civil liberties, equality before the law, freedom of religion and the press, and the right to private property, although land reform was contemplated.

Amendment and replacement

After Józef Piłsudski's May Coup of 1926, the constitution was amended by the August Novelization (nowela sierpniowa) of 2 August 1926, which strengthened the executive, allowed the president to issue decrees with the force of law and to dissolve parliament. The March Constitution was ultimately replaced by the April Constitution of 1935, which concentrated power in the presidency.

Legacy

Although superseded, the March Constitution remained an important reference point for Polish democrats. After 1989 it influenced the constitutional debates of the Third Polish Republic, and the 1997 Constitution drew on some of its democratic principles.
