Ziran (Chinese: 自然; pinyin: zìrán; lit. 'self-so; so of itself') is a key concept in Taoism that literally means "self-so" or "self-such," and is commonly translated as "nature," "naturalness," or "spontaneity." It describes the way things are when they follow their own intrinsic tendencies without external interference, and stands at the heart of Taoist philosophy alongside concepts such as wu wei (non-action) and the Tao itself.

In the Tao Te Ching

The term appears prominently in chapter 25 of the Tao Te Ching, which concludes: "Man follows the earth; the earth follows heaven; heaven follows the Tao; and the Tao follows ziran" (道法自然). Here ziran is not something separate from the Tao but the Tao's own mode of being — the Tao models itself on nothing but its own self-so-ing. Chapter 17 describes the ideal ruler whose people say of their accomplishments, "we did it ziran" — by ourselves, naturally.

Interpretation

Commentators such as Wang Bi understood ziran as the absence of deliberate contrivance: things realizing their nature fully when nothing forces them otherwise. In later Chinese thought the word came to denote "nature" in the modern sense — the natural world as opposed to the human — and it is this meaning that passed into Japanese (shizen) and Korean (jayeon) usage, eventually serving to translate the Western concept of "Nature."

Relation to wu wei

Ziran and wu wei are closely linked: the sage ruler practises wu wei (effortless, non-coercive action) so that the people and things under their care can be ziran — unfolding according to their own nature. Ziran thus functions both as a description of reality at its most authentic and as a normative ideal for human conduct and governance.
