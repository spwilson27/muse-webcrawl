Fasciolosis

Fasciolosis is a parasitic worm infection caused by the liver flukes Fasciola hepatica (the common liver fluke) and Fasciola gigantica. These trematodes primarily infect livestock such as sheep and cattle, causing major economic losses, but also infect humans who consume raw aquatic plants such as watercress bearing the flukes' encysted larvae. The World Health Organization classifies fasciolosis as a neglected tropical disease, with millions of people infected or at risk, notably in the Andean highlands, Egypt, Iran and Southeast Asia.

Specialty:Infectious disease, parasitology
Cause:Fasciola hepatica, F. gigantica
Transmission:Eating raw aquatic plants with metacercariae
Treatment:Triclabendazole

Life cycle and infection

Eggs shed in faeces hatch in water into miracidia that infect lymnaeid snails; after development in the snail, cercariae emerge and encyst as metacercariae on aquatic vegetation. When a mammal eats the plants, the young flukes excyst in the intestine, burrow through the gut wall and liver tissue, and mature in the bile ducts, where adults can live for years.

Symptoms and diagnosis

The acute (migratory) phase can cause fever, right upper abdominal pain, hepatomegaly and marked eosinophilia as larvae traverse the liver. The chronic (biliary) phase may be asymptomatic or cause biliary colic, cholangitis and anaemia. Diagnosis rests on finding eggs in stool or bile, serological tests, and imaging. Triclabendazole is the treatment of choice, being effective against both immature and adult flukes.
