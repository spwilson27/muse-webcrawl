Volhynia

Volhynia (Ukrainian: Волинь, Volyn; Polish: Wołyń; Russian: Волынь) is a historic region in Central and Eastern Europe, in the western part of Ukraine. It is situated in the watersheds of the Bug and Pripyat rivers, between Galicia to the southwest and Polesia to the northeast. Its main modern cities are Lutsk, Rivne, and Zhytomyr, and it corresponds roughly to today's Volyn, Rivne, and northern parts of Ternopil and Khmelnytskyi oblasts.

History

Volhynia was settled by Slavic tribes, including the Dulebes and Volhynians, and was incorporated into Kievan Rus' by the 10th century. After the fragmentation of Rus', it formed the core of the Principality of Volhynia, which in 1199 was united with Galicia under Roman the Great and his son Daniel, who was crowned King of Rus' in 1253. Following the Mongol invasion, the region passed to the Grand Duchy of Lithuania in the 14th century and, after the Union of Lublin (1569), to the Polish Crown.

The partitions of Poland transferred most of Volhynia to the Russian Empire, where it became Volhynia Governorate. Between the world wars the western part belonged to Poland (Wołyń Voivodeship) and the eastern part to the Ukrainian SSR. During the Second World War, Volhynia was the scene of the Volhynian massacres of 1943–44, in which the Ukrainian Insurgent Army killed tens of thousands of Polish civilians — an event that remains a sensitive point in Polish-Ukrainian relations. After 1945 the entire region became part of Soviet Ukraine.

Geography

The region is a rolling plain rising to the Volhynian Upland in the south, drained by the Styr, Horyn, and Sluch rivers. Its fertile black soils made it a major grain-producing area, and its forests and marshes extend north into Polesia.
