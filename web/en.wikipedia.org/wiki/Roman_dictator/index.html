Roman dictator

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A dictator in the Roman Republic was an extraordinary magistrate appointed in emergencies, normally for a maximum of six months, to restore the state — typically to command in war or to conduct elections or religious rites when the consuls could not. The dictator was nominated by a consul, appointed a deputy (magister equitum, master of the horse), and held imperium superior to all other magistrates, but within the constitution: the office was a legal institution of the Republic, not a usurpation of it.

The model case was Cincinnatus, summoned (in the traditional story) from his plough to defeat an enemy and returning to private life within weeks. Over the Republic's history some ninety dictatorships were appointed, increasingly for narrow tasks. The institution was transformed at the end of the Republic: Sulla (82–81 BC) was made dictator without time limit to remake the constitution, and Julius Caesar became dictator perpetuo — dictator in perpetuity — in 44 BC, shortly before his assassination. After Caesar, the office was abolished and the word “dictator” acquired its modern meaning of an absolute, unconstitutional ruler.

Legacy

The Roman office long served as the respectable ancestor invoked by later “constitutional dictatorship” debates: emergency powers temporally limited and legally granted. Modern scholarship (and political usage after the twentieth century's dictatorships) treats the late-Republic perversions — Sulla and Caesar — as the point where the emergency office consumed the Republic it was meant to save.
