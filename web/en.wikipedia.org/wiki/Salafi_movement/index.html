The Salafi movement or Salafism is a revivalist movement within Sunni Islam that advocates a return to the traditions of the "pious predecessors" (al-salaf al-ṣāliḥ)—the first three generations of Muslims, comprising the Prophet Muhammad's companions and their immediate successors. Salafis regard the beliefs and practices of these generations as the purest form of Islam and reject later innovations (bidʿah) and much of the accumulated apparatus of medieval theology and jurisprudence.

Salafism centres on the Qur'an and hadith, interpreted as the salaf understood them.

Beliefs and method

Salafis emphasise tawḥīd (the absolute oneness of God) in its three classical categories, insist on strict adherence to the Qur'an and Sunnah according to the understanding of the salaf, and follow a generally literalist approach to scriptural texts, particularly on theological questions where they are closely associated with the Athari school. Many reject blind adherence (taqlīd) to the four legal schools, although in practice Salafis vary widely. Core reference points include the Hanbali scholar Ibn Taymiyyah (1263–1328) and, in the modern era, the 18th-century Arabian reformer Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab, whose movement became the religious establishment of Saudi Arabia—though many Salafis distinguish their position from the "Wahhabi" label applied by critics.

Varieties

Scholars commonly divide Salafis into three broad tendencies. Purists (or quietists) focus on education, preaching and personal piety and avoid politics and violence. Activists engage in political organisation and contest elections, as Salafi parties did in Egypt after 2011. Jihadist groups—such as al-Qaeda and the Islamic State—embrace armed struggle; they are a minority, and most Salafi scholars condemn them, though analysts debate the theological continuities. The global spread of Salafism from the 1960s onward was aided by Saudi-funded publishing, scholarships and mosque-building, as well as by influential 20th-century hadith scholarship, notably that of Muhammad Nasiruddin al-Albani.

History of the term

The label "Salafi" was also used around 1900 by modernist reformers such as Muhammad Abduh and Rashid Rida, who sought to reconcile Islam with modernity—a usage quite different from today's predominantly literalist movement, which consolidated in the later 20th century.
