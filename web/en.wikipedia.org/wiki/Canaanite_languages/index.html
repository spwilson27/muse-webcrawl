Canaanite languages

The Canaanite languages are a sub-branch of the Semitic languages, comprising the closely related dialects spoken by the ancient peoples of the Canaan region in the Levant, including the Israelites and the Phoenicians. They belong to the Northwest Semitic group, alongside the Aramaic languages and Ugaritic. The principal Canaanite languages are Hebrew, Phoenician, Moabite, Ammonite, Edomite, and Punic (the later form of Phoenician spoken in Carthage).

Characteristics

The Canaanite languages are defined by several shared sound changes that distinguish them from other Northwest Semitic languages. The most famous is the so-called "Canaanite shift," in which long ā became long ō (as in Hebrew shalom from an earlier *salām). Other features include the development of the definite article with a prefixed h-, and characteristic shifts of the diphthongs ay and aw in certain dialects.

Attestation

Canaanite is first attested in the second millennium BCE: glosses in the Amarna letters (14th century BCE) preserve Canaanite words within Akkadian diplomatic correspondence, and alphabetic inscriptions appear from the early first millennium BCE. The Phoenician alphabet, developed around the 11th century BCE, became the ancestor of the Greek alphabet and ultimately of most alphabets in use today. Major inscriptions include the Mesha Stele in Moabite, the Siloam inscription in Hebrew, and the sarcophagus inscriptions of Ahiram and Eshmunazar in Phoenician.

Later history

All Canaanite languages except Hebrew became extinct in antiquity, displaced by Aramaic and later Arabic. Punic survived in North Africa into late antiquity and was reportedly still spoken in the time of Saint Augustine. Hebrew ceased to be a vernacular in late antiquity but survived as a liturgical and literary language and was revived as a spoken language in the 19th and 20th centuries, becoming the official language of the State of Israel in the form of Modern Hebrew.
