Margin (finance)

Margin
MeaningCollateral deposited with a broker or exchange to cover credit risk of leveraged positions

Margin in finance is collateral that a holder of a financial instrument must deposit to cover some or all of the credit risk the holder poses for a broker or exchange. In its best-known form, buying on margin means borrowing money from a broker to purchase securities: the investor opens a margin account, deposits a fraction of the purchase price, and borrows the rest, with the securities themselves serving as collateral for the loan. Margin allows investors to leverage their capital, amplifying both gains and losses.

Margin accounts

Under the US Federal Reserve's Regulation T, investors may borrow up to 50 per cent of the purchase price of marginable stocks (the initial margin). The account must thereafter maintain equity of at least 25 per cent of the position's market value under FINRA rules (the maintenance margin), though brokers commonly require more. If the equity falls below the maintenance requirement, the broker issues a margin call demanding additional funds or securities; if the investor does not comply, the broker may liquidate positions without notice. Interest is charged on the borrowed balance. Margin debt contributed to the speculation of the 1920s and to forced selling in the crash of 1929, which led to the modern regulatory framework in the Securities Exchange Act of 1934.

Futures and derivatives margin

In futures markets, margin is not a loan but a performance bond: both buyer and seller post an initial margin with the clearing house and settle gains and losses daily through variation margin (marking to market). Similar margining applies to options, contracts for difference and centrally cleared swaps, where additional concepts — such as portfolio margining and margin of safety in value investing (a distinct, metaphorical use of the term popularised by Benjamin Graham) — should not be confused with collateral margin proper.

Risks

Because losses are computed on the full position while the investor's stake is only a fraction, a margin investor can lose more than the initial deposit and still owe the broker money. Sudden price falls can trigger cascades of margin calls and forced liquidation, amplifying market declines — a mechanism implicated in several financial crises.
