The Fujiwara clan (藤原氏, Fujiwara-shi) was a powerful family of imperial regents in Japan, descending from the Nakatomi clan and, as legend held, through them from the god Ame-no-Koyane. The clan originated when the founder, Nakatomi no Kamatari (614–669), a key ally of the future Emperor Tenji in the Taika Reforms, was rewarded with the surname Fujiwara ("wisteria field") in 669.

During the Heian period (794–1185), the Fujiwara — particularly the Northern House (Hokke) — dominated the imperial court through the regency system (sekkan seiji). They monopolised the posts of sesshō (regent for a child emperor) and kampaku (regent for an adult emperor) and cemented their power by repeatedly marrying their daughters to emperors, becoming the imperial family's maternal grandfathers and uncles.

Rise and regency

The clan's ascendancy began with Fujiwara no Yoshifusa, who became the first sesshō from outside the imperial family in 866. His adopted son Mototsune took the title kampaku. The system reached its height under Fujiwara no Michinaga (966–1027), who was the father of four empresses and grandfather of three emperors; his era was celebrated in court literature, including Murasaki Shikibu's The Tale of Genji, produced under Fujiwara patronage.

Decline

Fujiwara dominance was undermined in the late 11th century by the rise of rule by retired emperors (insei), beginning with Emperor Go-Sanjō, who had no Fujiwara mother. After the Hōgen and Heiji rebellions and the Genpei War, political power shifted to the warrior class and the Kamakura shogunate, though the Fujiwara-descended Five Regent Houses (go-sekke) retained the right to supply sesshō and kampaku, and Fujiwara daughters continued to become imperial consorts, into the 19th century.
