Spatial cognition

Spatial cognition is the acquisition, organization, utilization, and revision of knowledge about spatial environments — the mental processes by which people and other animals perceive, remember, and reason about the positions, distances, directions, and movements of objects, including their own bodies. It underlies everyday abilities such as navigation, wayfinding, mental rotation, and reading maps.

Key concepts

Edward Tolman's experiments with rats in the 1940s introduced the idea of a cognitive map, an internal representation of an environment that supports novel shortcuts. Later work distinguished landmark knowledge, route knowledge, and survey (map-like) knowledge in the development of environmental understanding. Neuroscience has identified specialized neural machinery for spatial representation: place cells in the hippocampus, grid cells in the entorhinal cortex, head-direction cells, and boundary cells — discoveries recognized by the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine awarded to John O'Keefe and May-Britt and Edvard Moser.

Individual and group differences

Spatial abilities vary widely among individuals and are studied through tasks such as mental rotation tests, perspective taking, and wayfinding in real and virtual environments. Research has examined developmental changes, the effects of experience and video games, average sex differences on some mental-rotation measures, and impairments in conditions such as developmental topographical disorientation and Alzheimer's disease, in which spatial disorientation is often an early symptom. Spatial cognition is an active field spanning psychology, neuroscience, geography, robotics, and human–computer interaction.
