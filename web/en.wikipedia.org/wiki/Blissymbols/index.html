Blissymbols or Blissymbolics is a constructed, ideographic writing system composed of several hundred basic symbols, each representing a concept, which can be combined to form new symbols and sentences. It was created by Charles K. Bliss (1897–1985), an Austrian-born chemical engineer, and published in his book Semantography in 1949.

History

Bliss, a refugee from Nazi Europe who spent time in Shanghai, was inspired by Chinese characters to design a universal written language that could be understood across linguistic barriers and, he hoped, prevent the misunderstandings that lead to war. His system initially attracted little attention. In 1971, however, educators at the Ontario Crippled Children's Centre in Toronto began using Blissymbols to teach communication to children with cerebral palsy who could not speak. The system proved remarkably effective in augmentative and alternative communication (AAC).

Structure

Blissymbols are built from simple geometric shapes drawn on a grid with a "sky line," "earth line," and midline. Symbols are either pictographic (resembling their referents), ideographic (abstract), or arbitrary. Indicators placed above symbols mark parts of speech—for example, a caret-like mark denotes a verb. Combining symbols generates compound meanings: "eye" plus "water" suggests tears or weeping.

Governance

Use of the system is coordinated internationally by Blissymbolics Communication International (BCI), which maintains the authorized vocabulary of several thousand symbols.
