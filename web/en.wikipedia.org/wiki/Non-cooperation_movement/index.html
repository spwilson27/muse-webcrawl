Non-cooperation movement

The non-cooperation movement was a mass political campaign launched by Mahatma Gandhi in 1920–22, the first nationwide satyagraha movement in the Indian independence struggle against British rule. It urged Indians to withdraw cooperation from the colonial state: boycotting foreign cloth, government schools, law courts, titles and legislatures, and promoting hand-spun khadi and national institutions.

Background

Gandhi launched the movement in response to the Rowlatt Acts, the Jallianwala Bagh massacre of 1919, and the Khilafat issue, with which it was allied. The Indian National Congress adopted the programme at its Nagpur session in December 1920, demanding swaraj (self-rule) within a year.

Course and end

The movement drew in millions: students left government schools, lawyers such as C. R. Das and Motilal Nehru gave up their practices, and foreign cloth was publicly burned. Gandhi called the movement off in February 1922 after the Chauri Chaura incident, in which a mob killed 22 policemen; Gandhi judged that the country was not yet disciplined enough for mass civil disobedience and undertook a fast in atonement. He was arrested shortly afterwards and imprisoned for sedition.

Significance

Although it did not achieve swaraj, the movement transformed Congress into a mass organisation and established nonviolent non-cooperation as the central method of the independence struggle, paving the way for the later civil disobedience movement of 1930–34.
