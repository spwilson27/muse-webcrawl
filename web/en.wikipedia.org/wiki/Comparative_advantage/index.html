Comparative advantage

[Image: two workshops side by side, one weaving cloth and one making wine barrels, in Ricardo's classic example]

Comparative advantage is the principle that trade benefits parties who specialise in producing what they can make at lower opportunity cost and exchange for the rest — even if one party is better at everything (absolute advantage). Stated by David Ricardo in 1817, it is the fundamental argument for Free trade and one of the oldest and least intuitive results in Economics.

The classic example

Ricardo imagined England and Portugal producing cloth and wine. Even if Portugal makes both more cheaply, if England's disadvantage is smaller in cloth, both gain when England specialises in cloth and Portugal in wine and they trade. The modern Heckscher–Ohlin model grounds comparative advantage in factor endowments (land, labour, capital); 'new trade theory' (Paul Krugman) added scale economies, and value-chain analysis notes that comparative advantage now applies to tasks and components within global supply chains. The principle does not say trade is painless — displaced workers in import-competing industries bear real costs — but that the gains exceed the losses, leaving room, in principle, to compensate the losers.
