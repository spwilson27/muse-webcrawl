Fracture zone

A fracture zone is a linear oceanic feature — often hundreds, even thousands of kilometres long — resulting from the action of offset mid-ocean ridge axis segments. They are a consequence of plate tectonics. Lithospheric plates on either side of an active transform fault move in opposite directions; here, strike-slip activity occurs in the transform fault. Fracture zones extend past the transform faults, away from the ridge axis; typically inactive, they can exhibit evidence of past transform fault activity, primarily in the different ages of the crust on opposite sides of the zone.

Formation and structure

At mid-ocean ridges, new crust is created at offset ridge segments. The transform fault between segments is seismically active, but beyond the ridge–ridge intersection, the fault becomes an inactive fracture zone as the two plates on either side move in the same direction. Fracture zones can be marked by great escarpments, troughs and ridges on the seafloor, with crust of different ages juxtaposed across them. Because older, colder crust sinks deeper, fracture zones often correlate with gravity anomalies and are useful tracers of past plate motions.
