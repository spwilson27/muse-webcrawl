Neapolitan chord

In music theory, a Neapolitan chord (or Neapolitan sixth chord) is a major chord built on the lowered (flattened) second scale degree, most often used in first inversion. It is notated as N, ♭II, or, when in first inversion, N6. The chord is strongly associated with the Neapolitan school of opera composers of the 17th and 18th centuries, including Alessandro Scarlatti, from which it takes its name.

Structure

In C major or C minor, the Neapolitan chord consists of the notes D♭–F–A♭. Although the chord can appear in root position, it is far more commonly found in first inversion (with the third of the chord, F, in the bass), which gives it the figured-bass designation of a "sixth chord." The flattened supertonic is a chromatic alteration in major keys but is diatonic to the Phrygian mode and borrowed from the parallel minor or Neapolitan scale in minor keys.

Function

The Neapolitan chord typically functions as a predominant (subdominant-function) chord, resolving to the dominant (V) or to a cadential six-four chord (I64–V). In voice leading, the flattened second degree usually descends to the leading tone or tonic, and the chord's third is commonly doubled. The chord heightens the drive toward the dominant because of the semitone pull of ♭2 toward the tonic and its resolution through the cadence.

Usage

The Neapolitan chord became a hallmark of the Baroque and Classical styles and remained in use through the Romantic era. Composers such as Handel, Mozart, Beethoven, Schubert, and Chopin used it for expressive effect, often at dramatic moments of harmonic tension. In popular and jazz harmony, the ♭II chord appears in altered forms, and the "Neapolitan" tritone-substitution dominant shares its root though not its function.
