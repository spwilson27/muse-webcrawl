Slaughter-House Cases

The Slaughter-House Cases, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36 (1873), were landmark United States Supreme Court decisions consolidating several cases concerning a Louisiana slaughterhouse monopoly. They were the first Supreme Court interpretation of the Fourteenth Amendment, adopted in 1868.

Slaughter-House Cases
CourtSupreme Court of the United States
DecidedApril 14, 1873
Decision5–4, monopoly upheld
MajoritySamuel F. Miller

Background

Louisiana, citing pollution of New Orleans' water supply by slaughterhouse waste, granted the Crescent City Live-Stock Landing and Slaughter-House Company a 25-year monopoly, requiring butchers to operate at its facility for a fee. Butchers sued, arguing the law violated the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments.

Decision

In a 5–4 decision, Justice Miller's majority narrowly read the Privileges or Immunities Clause, distinguishing state from federal citizenship and holding most rights remained state-protected. This drained the clause of much force for over a century; Fourteenth Amendment protections were later developed instead through the Due Process Clause (incorporation and substantive due process). Justices Field, Bradley, Swayne and Chief Justice Chase dissented.
