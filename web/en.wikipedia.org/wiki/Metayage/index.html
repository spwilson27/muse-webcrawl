Metayage

The metayage system is the cultivation of land for a proprietor by one who receives a proportion of the produce, as a kind of sharecropping. In France the cultivator of land so held is known as a métayer, and the land as a métairie. The system has equivalents across Europe: the Italian mezzadria, the Spanish mediería and aparcería, and similar arrangements in Portugal and elsewhere.

Description

Under metayage the landowner typically supplies the land and often the buildings, implements, seed and livestock (cheptel), while the métayer supplies labour. The harvest is divided between them in agreed proportions — classically one half to each party, hence the Italian name mezzadria ("halving") — though shares of a third or two-thirds to the proprietor also occur. The métayer is generally free to manage day-to-day work, but major decisions, particularly about planting and the sale of produce, involve the landlord.

History

Metayage has Roman origins and spread widely in early modern Europe, particularly in regions of poor soil, scarce capital and uncertain harvests, where sharing output also shared risk. It predominated in much of south-western and central France, Italy south of the Po valley, and parts of Spain. Adam Smith discussed the system critically in The Wealth of Nations, arguing that a métayer, who keeps only a fraction of the produce, has less incentive to improve the land than a tenant farmer paying fixed rent — though he acknowledged it was gentler than slavery. John Stuart Mill defended aspects of the system, pointing to the stability of tenure it often afforded.

Decline

Metayage declined sharply in the 20th century as mechanisation raised the capital needed for farming and as rural populations left the land. In France it was regulated by statute following the Second World War, and most métayers became tenant farmers (fermiers) paying money rent. The Italian mezzadria was formally abolished by legislation converting contracts to leases in 1964 and finally prohibited for new agreements in 1982.
