National Museum of Denmark

Danish nameNationalmuseet
Established1849 (collections from 1807)
LocationPrince's Palace, Copenhagen, Denmark
TypeNational cultural history museum

The National Museum of Denmark (Danish: Nationalmuseet) in Copenhagen is Denmark's largest museum of cultural history, encompassing Danish prehistory, the Middle Ages and later centuries, together with ethnographic collections from around the world. It is housed in the Prince's Palace (Prinsens Palais), a rococo mansion beside Frederiksholm Canal.

Collections

The museum's Danish antiquities are among Europe's finest: the Neolithic and Bronze Age galleries hold the Trundholm sun chariot, the Egtved Girl's oak coffin and costume, the Gundestrup cauldron (found in a Jutland peat bog), the Golden Horns of Gallehus (reconstructions; the originals were stolen and melted down in 1802), Viking Age runestones and treasures, and medieval church art. The well-preserved bog bodies and weapons sacrifices of the Iron Age, such as those from Illerup Ådal, are highlights of the prehistory displays. Ethnographic galleries cover Greenland and Inuit culture, the former Danish colonies, and world cultures, while the museum also runs the Children's Museum and administers sites including the Viking fortress of Trelleborg and the open-air Frilandsmuseet north of Copenhagen.

History

The museum descends from the Royal Cabinet of Curiosities; its modern founder was Christian Jürgensen Thomsen, who in the early nineteenth century organised the antiquities using his famous Three-Age System — Stone, Bronze and Iron Ages — a framework still fundamental to archaeology. In 1849 the collections opened to the public in the Prince's Palace, where they have remained.
