Standpoint theory

Standpoint theory, also called standpoint epistemology, is a theory for analyzing inter-subjective discourses. It proposes that an individual's perspectives are shaped by their social and political experiences: standpoints are, in essence, neither readily available nor objectively accessible, but are achieved through critical reflection on one's social position.

Origins

The theory draws on Hegel's master–slave dialectic and on Marxist accounts of the proletariat's standpoint, and was developed as a feminist theory in the 1970s and 1980s by thinkers such as Nancy Hartsock, Sandra Harding, Dorothy Smith, and Patricia Hill Collins. Harding's notion of "strong objectivity" argues that knowledge production should start from marginalized lives in order to produce less partial accounts.

Key claims

Standpoint theorists hold that knowledge is socially situated; that marginalized groups are socially situated in ways that can make them more aware of social structures; and that research should begin from the standpoints of the marginalized. Collins's work on Black feminist thought emphasizes intersecting oppressions and the "outsider within" position. Standpoints are not automatic consequences of identity — they are achieved through collective critical consciousness.

Criticism

Critics have raised concerns about essentialism, the problem of identifying a single standpoint for heterogeneous groups, and the thesis's compatibility with scientific objectivity; defenders reply that standpoint theory is a method for improving, not abandoning, objectivity.
