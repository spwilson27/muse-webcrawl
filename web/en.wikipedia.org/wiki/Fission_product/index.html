Fission products

OriginNuclear fission of heavy nuclei (e.g. uranium-235, plutonium-239)
Mass rangeApproximately A = 70–160
Notable examplesCaesium-137, strontium-90, iodine-131, xenon-135

Fission products are the atomic species produced by the nuclear fission of a heavy element such as uranium or plutonium. When a heavy nucleus splits, it typically divides into two lighter nuclei — the fission fragments — which are usually radioactive and further decay through chains of beta decays toward stable isotopes. The cumulative distribution of these products over mass number is double-humped: because fission is usually asymmetric, yields peak for products with mass numbers around 95 (such as strontium-90 and molybdenum-99) and around 140 (such as caesium-137 and cerium-144), and are low for products near half the mass of the parent nucleus.

Formation and decay

The initial fission fragments are neutron-rich and highly excited; they shed prompt neutrons and gamma rays, then progress through beta decay chains (isobaric decay chains of constant mass number) until stable nuclei are reached. Fission products accordingly contribute the majority of the radioactivity of spent nuclear fuel on timescales from seconds up to a few hundred years, and their decay generates the decay heat that continues after a reactor is shut down.

Important fission products

Long-lived fission products include technetium-99 (half-life 211,000 years), iodine-129 (15.7 million years), caesium-135, zirconium-93, palladium-107, tin-126, and selenium-79. Medium-lived products of radiological concern include caesium-137 and strontium-90, each with half-lives of about 30 years. Short-lived products include iodine-131 (8 days), important in reactor accidents because it accumulates in the thyroid, and xenon-135, a potent neutron absorber that affects reactor control (“xenon poisoning”).

Behaviour in reactors and the environment

Some fission products absorb neutrons strongly and so act as reactor poisons, influencing reactivity and fuel burnup calculations. Volatile products such as noble gases (krypton and xenon) and iodine can be released from damaged fuel in accidents. In the environment, fission products from weapons testing and from accidents such as Chernobyl and Fukushima have been distributed globally, with caesium-137 widely used as a marker of contamination. Fission products also have beneficial uses: molybdenum-99 (and its daughter technetium-99m) underpins much of diagnostic nuclear medicine.
