Chemist

A chemist (from Greek chēm(ía) alchemy; replacing chymist from Medieval Latin alchemist) is a graduated scientist trained in the study of chemistry, or an officially enrolled student in the field. Chemists study the composition of matter and its properties. Chemists carefully describe the properties they study in terms of quantities, with detail on the level of molecules and their component atoms. Chemists carefully measure substance proportions, chemical reaction rates, and other chemical properties.

In Commonwealth English, pharmacists are often called chemists, and a pharmacy may be called a "chemist's shop".

Work

Chemists work in a variety of fields, including research and development, quality control, manufacturing, environmental protection, and forensics. Common specializations include analytical chemistry, biochemistry, inorganic chemistry, medicinal chemistry, organic chemistry, and physical chemistry.

Employers of chemists include universities, government laboratories, and the chemical, pharmaceutical, food, and petroleum industries. Chemists typically use laboratory equipment such as spectrometers, chromatographs, and balances, and rely on computational methods to model molecular behaviour.

Education

Jobs for chemists usually require at least a bachelor's degree in chemistry or a related field, while research positions generally require a master's degree or a doctorate (PhD). Professional recognition may be granted by bodies such as the Royal Society of Chemistry in the United Kingdom, which awards Chartered Chemist (CChem) status.

History

The roots of chemistry lie in alchemy, practised for millennia in many cultures. The modern profession emerged in the 18th and 19th centuries as chemistry separated from alchemy and pharmacy, with figures such as Antoine Lavoisier establishing quantitative methods. The term "chemist" in British usage for a pharmacist dates from the 19th century, when chemists and druggists sold medicines alongside other goods.
