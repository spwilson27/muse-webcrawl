Triangulation (topology)

In mathematics, a triangulation of a topological space is a way of representing the space as a simplicial complex: the space is divided into simplices — points, segments, triangles, tetrahedra, and their higher-dimensional analogues — glued together along their faces in a controlled way. A space admitting such a decomposition is called triangulable.

History

Triangulations were central to early combinatorial topology, where invariants such as Euler characteristic and homology were computed from a chosen decomposition. A major question, the Hauptvermutung ("main conjecture"), asked whether any two triangulations of a space admit a common subdivision; it was disproved in the 1960s by Milnor for general complexes and by Kirby and Siebenmann in the manifold setting. Nevertheless, every smooth manifold admits a triangulation (Whitehead), and work of Moise showed that all topological 3-manifolds are triangulable, while in dimension four and above there exist manifolds, such as Freedman's E8 manifold, with no triangulation at all.

Uses

Triangulations allow topological problems to be translated into finite combinatorial data, enabling computation of homology and fundamental groups, normal surface theory in 3-manifold topology, and algorithmic results in computational topology and geometry processing.
