The Crystal Palace was a cast iron and plate glass structure, originally built in Hyde Park, London, to house the Great Exhibition of 1851, the first international exhibition of manufactured products. Designed by Joseph Paxton, a gardener and architect, the enormous glass house — 1,848 feet (563 m) long and enclosing some 990,000 square feet — was a triumph of prefabricated construction, assembled in months from standardized parts.

More than 14,000 exhibitors from around the world displayed their goods in the palace during the exhibition, which drew some six million visitors and was opened by Queen Victoria on 1 May 1851.

Relocation and later life

After the exhibition closed, the building was dismantled, purchased by a private company, and re-erected in an enlarged and modified form on Sydenham Hill in south London, reopening in 1854. Surrounded by parkland with gardens designed by Paxton, fountains, and the famous life-size dinosaur models sculpted under the direction of Richard Owen, the palace became a popular venue for concerts, exhibitions, festivals, and sporting events. The surrounding district took its name, Crystal Palace, from the building, as did the football club founded in 1861.

Destruction and legacy

On the night of 30 November 1936 the palace was destroyed by fire; despite the efforts of 89 fire engines and nearly 450 firefighters, the building was consumed within hours, the glow visible across London. Winston Churchill remarked that it was "the end of an age". The site is now Crystal Palace Park; the terraces, sphinxes, and the dinosaur models survive, and the palace is remembered as a pioneering landmark of modular, industrialized architecture.
