Man-in-the-middle attack

In cryptography and computer security, a man-in-the-middle (MITM) attack is a cyberattack in which the attacker secretly relays and possibly alters the communications between two parties who believe they are directly communicating with each other. The attacker must be able to intercept, send and receive messages to both victims while remaining undetected.


An attacker interposed between two communicating parties.

Examples

Common forms include ARP spoofing and DNS spoofing on local networks, rogue Wi-Fi access points ("evil twins"), SSL stripping that downgrades HTTPS connections, and compromised routers or certificate authorities. Historical precursors include the "bucket brigade" or fire-brigade attack against telegraph and radio communications.

Defences

Defences rely on authentication of the communicating parties: public-key infrastructure and TLS certificates, certificate pinning, HSTS, mutual authentication, and out-of-band verification of keys (as in secure messaging "safety numbers"). Perfect forward secrecy limits the damage from later key compromise but does not by itself prevent an active MITM without authentication.

Notable cases

In 2011 the compromise of the Dutch certificate authority DigiNotar allowed fraudulent certificates (including for Google domains) to be used in interception attacks. Lenovo's 2014 pre-installation of the Superfish adware, which injected a self-signed root certificate, created a widespread MITM vulnerability on consumer laptops.
