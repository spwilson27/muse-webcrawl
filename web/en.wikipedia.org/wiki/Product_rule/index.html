Product rule

In calculus, the product rule (or Leibniz rule) is a formula used to find the derivatives of products of two or more functions. For two differentiable functions f and g, the derivative of their product is:

(f · g)′ = f′ · g + f · g′

In Leibniz's notation this is written:

d(uv)/dx = u · dv/dx + v · du/dx

The rule may be extended to products of more functions, to higher derivatives (the general Leibniz rule), and to other contexts such as derivatives of bilinear operations.

Examples

Suppose one wants to differentiate f(x) = x² sin(x). Using the product rule, f′(x) = 2x sin(x) + x² cos(x), since the derivative of x² is 2x and the derivative of sin(x) is cos(x).

Proof

A standard proof follows from the definition of the derivative as a limit: writing h(x) = f(x)g(x), the difference h(x+h) − h(x) is decomposed by adding and subtracting f(x)g(x+h), then dividing by h and taking the limit using the differentiability (hence continuity) of f and g. A geometric proof interprets the product as the area of a rectangle whose sides change.

Generalizations

The general Leibniz rule gives the nth derivative of a product as a sum resembling the binomial theorem, with binomial coefficients weighting products of derivatives of each factor. Analogues exist for the quotient rule, for derivatives of triple products, and in abstract algebra for derivations. See also Quotient rule, Chain rule, and Derivative.
