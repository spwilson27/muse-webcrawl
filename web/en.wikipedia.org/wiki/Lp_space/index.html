In mathematics, the Lp spaces are function spaces defined using a natural generalization of the p-norm for finite-dimensional vector spaces. They are central to functional analysis and measure theory. For 1 ≤ p < ∞, Lp consists of measurable functions whose p-th power is Lebesgue integrable; the space L∞ consists of essentially bounded functions.

Definition

Given a measure space (S, Σ, μ) and 1 ≤ p < ∞, the Lp norm of a measurable function f is defined as the p-th root of the integral of |f|p. Functions that agree almost everywhere are identified, making Lp a complete normed vector space — a Banach space. The triangle inequality for the norm is Minkowski's inequality, and Hölder's inequality governs products of functions from dual spaces.

Important cases

L2 is a Hilbert space, with inner product given by the integral of f times the conjugate of g, and is fundamental in quantum mechanics and Fourier analysis. L1 is the space of integrable functions, and L∞ of essentially bounded functions. For sequences, the corresponding spaces are written ℓp.

Duality and reflexivity

For 1 < p < ∞, the dual of Lp is Lq, where 1/p + 1/q = 1, and these spaces are reflexive. The dual of L1 is L∞, though the dual of L∞ is larger. The Lp spaces were introduced by Frigyes Riesz in 1910 and remain indispensable in partial differential equations, probability theory, and harmonic analysis.
