Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution (1791) provides: “Excessive bail shall not be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor cruel and unusual punishments inflicted.” Drawn nearly verbatim from the English Bill of Rights of 1689, its cruel-and-unusual-punishments clause is the principal constitutional text governing capital punishment and prison conditions in the United States.

The Supreme Court reads the clause by “evolving standards of decency” (Trop v. Dulles, 1958) and proportionality: it has barred execution of juveniles (Roper v. Simmons, 2005) and of persons with intellectual disability (Atkins v. Virginia, 2002), limited life-without-parole for juveniles, and struck down mandatory death-penalty statutes while upholding guided-discretion capital sentencing (Furman, 1972; Gregg, 1976). Conditions of confinement, deliberate indifference to medical needs (Estelle v. Gamble, 1976), and excessive fines (Timbs v. Indiana, 2019, incorporating the fines clause against the states) fall under the same amendment. Bail jurisprudence is comparatively undeveloped, though pretrial-detention and cash-bail reform debates invoke it.
