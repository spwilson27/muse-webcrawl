Defixio

A defixio (plural defixiones), in English usually called a curse tablet, was a small inscribed sheet, most often of lead, used in the ancient Greek and Roman worlds to invoke gods, spirits, or the dead to harm or control another person. The Greek term was katadesmos (κατάδεσμος), "a binding spell". The Latin defixio derives from defigere, "to fasten down", reflecting the practice of piercing the tablet with a nail to fix the curse upon its victim.

Practice

The text of the curse was scratched into a thin lead sheet, which was then folded or rolled, sometimes pierced with a nail, and deposited in a place thought to give access to the powers of the underworld: graves (especially of those who died young or violently), wells, springs, sanctuaries of chthonic deities such as Hermes, Hecate, Demeter, and Persephone, or the homes of victims. More than 1,600 tablets survive, dating from the 5th century BC to late antiquity, from across the Mediterranean world. Important finds include the tablets from the sanctuary of Demeter at Selinunte in Sicily, from Athens, from the sacred spring at Bath in Roman Britain, and from Mainz in Germany.

Types of curses

Scholars conventionally group defixiones by purpose:

  • Judicial curses — against opponents in lawsuits, binding their tongues and minds.
  • Commercial curses — against rival tradespeople, workshops, and taverns.
  • Athletic curses — against rival chariot teams, gladiators, and athletes.
  • Erotic curses — intended to compel a desired person to love the client, or to separate lovers.
  • Theft curses — "prayers for justice" dedicating a stolen object and its unknown thief to a deity, prominent among the tablets from Bath.

Texts often list the victim's name (ideally including the mother's name, for identification) and enumerate body parts to be "bound". Later tablets add magical words (voces magicae), symbols (charakteres), and invocations of Jewish, Egyptian, and other deities, reflecting the syncretism of late antique magic.
