Beta movement

The beta movement is an optical illusion of apparent motion in which a single object appears to move smoothly across the visual field when two or more static images of it are shown in rapid succession at different positions. Described by the Gestalt psychologist Max Wertheimer in his 1912 experimental studies of apparent motion, it is one of the perceptual phenomena underlying cinema and animation.

Description

In the beta movement, stimuli are typically presented with inter-stimulus intervals and spatial separations such that the observer perceives one object gliding from the first position to the second, passing through the intermediate space. This contrasts with the phi phenomenon, in which motion is perceived without a moving object being seen — a kind of "pure" motion between two flashing lights.

Relation to film

Beta movement is often cited as the basis of motion pictures: a film projector presents a rapid sequence of still frames (traditionally 24 per second), and the visual system constructs continuous motion. Textbooks long attributed the effect to "persistence of vision", but modern accounts treat apparent motion as an active computation of the motion-detection system, of which beta movement is the canonical laboratory example.
