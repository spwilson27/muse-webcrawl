Benign violation theory

Benign violation theory is a theory of humour developed by Peter McGraw and Caleb Warren at the University of Colorado Boulder. The theory proposes that humour occurs when three conditions are met simultaneously: something is appraised as a violation of how the world ought to be; the violation is appraised as benign; and both appraisals occur at the same time.

Explanation

A violation can threaten a person's worldview, a social or cultural norm, or a physical expectation. It seems benign when it is interpreted as normal or acceptable, when the perceiver feels psychologically distant from it, or when a competing norm suggests the situation is acceptable. Tickling, slapstick, puns and many jokes are explained on this account: a pun violates expectations of language but is harmless, while a pratfall violates physical well-being yet is benign when the victim is unhurt or distant.

Research and applications

Experiments inspired by the theory have examined humour reactions to moral violations, disasters ("tragedy plus time"), and taboo words, finding that humour peaks at an intermediate psychological distance. The theory has been applied in marketing and comedy writing to explain why some edgy material amuses audiences while other material merely offends, and it is a prominent modern rival to incongruity, superiority and relief theories of humour.
