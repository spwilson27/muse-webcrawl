Alfred Wallis (18 August 1855 – 29 August 1942) was a British fisherman and artist, celebrated for his naïve paintings of ships and Cornish harbours. He began painting in old age, from memory, and became a central influence on the St Ives artists' colony.

Life

Born in Devonport, Wallis went to sea as a boy, working in the North Atlantic fishing trade and later as a fisherman out of Penzance and Newlyn. In the 1890s he settled at St Ives, where he ran a marine salvage and rag-and-bone business. After his wife's death in 1922, he began painting "for company," using ship's paint on scraps of cardboard and wood.

Discovery and style

In 1928 the artists Ben Nicholson and Christopher Wood encountered Wallis's work in St Ives and championed it in London artistic circles. His paintings ignore conventional perspective — the size of a ship reflects its importance to the artist rather than its distance — and are dominated by greys, browns, and dark greens of household and boat paint. Despite the interest of the modernists, Wallis sold little and died in the Madron workhouse in 1942.

Legacy

Wallis's grave in Barnoon cemetery, St Ives, bears a tile monument decorated with a lighthouse, made by potter Bernard Leach. His work is held by Tate and by Kettle's Yard, Cambridge, where collector Jim Ede was an early patron. He is regarded as one of Britain's most important self-taught artists.
