Mouthpiece (brass)

On a brass instrument, the mouthpiece is the part placed against the player's lips; its vibration of the lips, coupled with the air column of the instrument, produces the sound. Mouthpieces are almost always removable, allowing players to choose one suited to their embouchure and the style of music. They are usually made of brass plated with silver (or occasionally gold), and are also made in plastic for outdoor and cold-weather use.

Anatomy

A brass mouthpiece consists of the rim, the rounded edge that contacts the lips; the cup, the bowl whose depth and diameter shape the tone; the throat, the narrowest point of the bore; the backbore, the tapering channel behind the throat; and the shank, which inserts into the instrument's leadpipe. Trumpet and cornet mouthpieces, though similar in appearance, have different shanks and are not interchangeable in sound or fit.

Size and effect on playing

Mouthpiece dimensions vary by instrument, from the small, shallow cups of trumpets and French horns to the large, deep cups of tubas. A wider rim diameter generally suits larger lip area and gives more control at volume; a deeper cup produces a darker, mellower tone and aids the low register, while a shallower cup brightens the sound and eases the high register, at some cost to fullness below. Because of this trade-off, players often keep several mouthpieces for different repertoire. Numbering systems differ between manufacturers — Vincent Bach's widely used scheme numbers trumpet mouthpieces from large (1) to small (7 and beyond), with letters denoting cup depth — and are not standardised across brands.

History

Early brass instruments used simple integral or detachable mouthpieces, and historical instruments such as the natural trumpet used deep, funnel-like mouthpieces quite different from modern designs. The modern system of interchangeable, precisely machined mouthpieces developed in the 19th and 20th centuries alongside valved instruments and mass manufacture.
