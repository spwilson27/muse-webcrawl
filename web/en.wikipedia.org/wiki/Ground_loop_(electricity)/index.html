Ground loop (electricity)

In an electrical system, a ground loop or earth loop occurs when two points that are supposedly at the same ground potential have a difference in potential between them. This difference causes an unwanted current to flow in ground conductors — most famously producing mains-frequency hum (50 or 60 Hz) in audio equipment and rolling bars in video signals.

Cause

When interconnected devices are plugged into different power outlets, their chassis grounds may sit at slightly different voltages because of voltage drops and leakage currents in the building wiring. The interconnecting signal cable shield then completes a loop through which current circulates, and this current couples noise into the signal path. Magnetic induction into the loop from nearby transformers and power wiring worsens the effect, since the loop acts as a single-turn coil.

Symptoms

In audio systems, a low hum or buzz that changes when equipment is touched or cables moved; in analogue video, horizontal "hum bars" drifting through the picture; in instrumentation, noisy or drifting measurements; and in severe cases, overheated cable shields or shocks.

Solutions

Remedies include powering interconnected equipment from a single outlet or circuit (star grounding), using balanced audio connections that reject common-mode interference, inserting ground-loop isolators (audio transformers) or DI boxes with ground lifts, optical or transformer isolation of data links, and common-mode chokes. Safety earth connections must never be removed to break a loop — dangerous "cheater plug" practice — since protective grounding prevents electric shock.
