In the mathematical field of numerical analysis, spline interpolation is a form of interpolation where the interpolant is a special type of piecewise polynomial called a spline. That is, instead of fitting a single, high-degree polynomial to all of the values at once, spline interpolation fits low-degree polynomials to small subsets of the values, for example, fitting nine cubic polynomials between each of the pairs of ten points, instead of fitting a single degree-nine polynomial to all of them.

Motivation

Spline interpolation is often preferred over polynomial interpolation because the interpolation error can be made small even when using low-degree polynomials. It avoids the problem of Runge's phenomenon, in which oscillation at the edges of an interval occurs when interpolating using high-degree polynomials. The name comes from the flexible drafting tool — a spline — a thin strip of wood or metal bent through control points to draw smooth curves.

Cubic splines

The most commonly used form is the cubic spline, where each piece is a cubic polynomial. The pieces are joined at points called knots in such a way that the spline and its first and second derivatives are continuous, giving a curve that is both smooth and, among interpolating functions, minimises an approximation to the total bending energy. Boundary conditions distinguish variants: a natural spline sets the second derivative to zero at the ends, while a clamped spline fixes the end slopes.

Applications

Spline interpolation is used in data fitting, computer graphics and animation (smoothing keyframe motion), typography and font design, trajectory planning in robotics, and engineering design tools such as computer-aided design packages.
