The Anglo-Scottish border separates England and Scotland within the island of Great Britain. It runs for 96 miles (154 km) between Marshall Meadows Bay on the North Sea, just north of Berwick-upon-Tweed, and the Solway Firth in the west. For most of its length it follows the River Tweed and the line of the Cheviot Hills. Since the Union of the Crowns (1603) and the Acts of Union (1707) it has been an internal boundary of the United Kingdom, today also an administrative and legal boundary between Scotland and England.

History

The line was first fixed in recognisable form by the Treaty of York (1237), which settled the frontier between the kingdoms of England and Scotland along roughly the modern route, though Berwick changed hands repeatedly — finally becoming English in 1482 — and the "Debatable Lands" in the west remained lawless until divided in 1552 (marked by the Scots' Dike). For centuries the border was a militarised frontier of castles, peel towers, and March wardens, raided by the Border reivers. James VI and I, after 1603, pacified the "Middle Shires"; the border counties retained distinct traditions of surnames, ballads, and rugby and football.

The border today

There are no border controls; the boundary is marked by signs and by flags at crossings such as Carter Bar on the A68 and the A1 near Berwick. Since Scottish devolution (1999), differences in law, education, and health policy between Scotland and England have given the internal border renewed practical significance, and it featured prominently in the 2014 Scottish independence referendum debate.
