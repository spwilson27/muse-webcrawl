Quarter tone

Quarter tones divide each semitone in half.

A quarter tone is a pitch halfway between the usual notes of a chromatic scale — an interval equal to half a semitone, or 50 cents. In 24 equal temperament (24-TET), the octave is divided into 24 equal quarter-tone steps. Quarter tones are a standard feature of many Middle Eastern maqam melodies, where neutral intervals (such as the neutral third of roughly 350 cents) are characteristic, and have been used by Western composers in microtonal music since the early 20th century.

In Western composition

The Czech composer Alois Hába, inspired by Moravian folk singing, built quarter-tone pianos and harmoniums and composed systematically in the system; his students spread the technique. Charles Ives's Three Quarter-Tone Pieces for two pianos tuned a quarter tone apart date from the 1920s. Julián Carrillo in Mexico developed his Sonido 13 theory with still finer divisions. Ivan Wyschnegradsky and Mildred Couper were other notable pioneers. Special notation — arrows, reversed flats, and half-sharps (𝄲, 𝄳) — was devised to write the extra pitches.

In Middle Eastern music

Arab and Turkish art musics have long employed intervals smaller than the semitone. Theorists such as Mikhail Mishaqa described an octave of 24 quarter tones; although actual practice uses unequal neutral intervals, quarter-tone notation remains the standard framework for describing maqam and makam scales.

Instruments

Voices, strings, and trombones can inflect pitch freely; fixed-pitch instruments need modification — extra frets on guitars, special flutes, or pairs of pianos tuned apart. Modern electronic instruments and software make quarter-tone performance straightforward.
