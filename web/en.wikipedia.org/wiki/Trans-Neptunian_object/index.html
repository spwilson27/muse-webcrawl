Trans-Neptunian object

A trans-Neptunian object (TNO), also written transneptunian object, is any minor planet in the Solar System that orbits the Sun at a greater average distance than Neptune, which has an orbital semi-major axis of 30.1 astronomical units (AU).

Classification

Typically, TNOs are divided into the classical and resonant objects of the Kuiper belt, the scattered disc, and detached objects such as Sedna. Resonant objects, including the plutinos (in a 3:2 resonance with Neptune, like Pluto itself), are locked in stable orbital resonances. Classical Kuiper belt objects, or cubewanos, orbit at roughly 42–48 AU without resonance. Scattered disc objects have highly eccentric, inclined orbits that interact with Neptune, while detached objects have perihelia so large that Neptune barely influences them.

Discovery and exploration

Pluto, discovered in 1930, was the first TNO identified, though it was classified as a planet until 2006. The second, 15760 Albion, was found in 1992. As of the 2020s, more than 3,000 TNOs are known. The largest are the dwarf planets Pluto, Eris, Haumea, and Makemake. In 2015 the New Horizons spacecraft flew past Pluto, and in 2019 it visited the classical Kuiper belt object 486958 Arrokoth, the most distant object ever explored by a spacecraft.

Composition and color

TNOs are composed largely of water, methane, and ammonia ices mixed with rock. Their surfaces show a wide range of colors, from neutral gray to deep red, thought to result from irradiation of organic compounds over billions of years.
