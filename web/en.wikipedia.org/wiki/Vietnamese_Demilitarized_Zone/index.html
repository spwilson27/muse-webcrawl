The Vietnamese Demilitarized Zone was a demilitarized zone established by the 1954 Geneva Accords as the temporary dividing line between North and South Vietnam. Running roughly along the 17th parallel and the Bến Hải River, the DMZ was intended as a provisional military demarcation pending nationwide elections planned for 1956. When those elections never took place, the DMZ hardened into a de facto international border and became one of the most heavily fought-over areas of the Vietnam War.

Geography

The zone stretched from the coast of the South China Sea westward to the Laotian border, roughly 100 km long and about 10 km wide (5 km either side of the demarcation line). The Bến Hải River formed most of the line's eastern course, crossed by the Hiền Lương Bridge, which was painted in two different colours on its northern and southern halves. Major features included the US Marine base at Con Thien, the Rockpile, Khe Sanh to the south-west, and the coastal plain around Đông Hà.

Vietnam War

Despite its name, the DMZ was anything but demilitarized during the war. North Vietnamese forces infiltrated across and around it (including via the Ho Chi Minh Trail through Laos), while the United States built the McNamara Line — a barrier of sensors, mines, and strongpoints — just south of the zone in 1967–68. Heavy fighting occurred around Con Thien ("the Hill of Angels") in 1967, and during the 1972 Easter Offensive North Vietnam's army attacked directly across the DMZ, capturing Quảng Trị Province. The zone's vegetation was largely destroyed by bombing, artillery, and herbicides such as Agent Orange.

Aftermath

The DMZ ceased to function after the fall of Saigon in April 1975 and the formal reunification of Vietnam in 1976. Today the Hiền Lương Bridge and Bến Hải River are preserved as a national relic site, with museums and monuments on both banks, and the surrounding area remains affected by unexploded ordnance.
