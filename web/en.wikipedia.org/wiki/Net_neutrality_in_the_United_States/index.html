Net neutrality in the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Net neutrality in the United States concerns the application of the principle of net neutrality — that internet service providers should treat all data on the internet equally — to American law and regulation, principally by the Federal Communications Commission (FCC). The issue has produced a long-running policy battle between advocates of open-internet rules and providers favoring deregulation.

Background

The term "net neutrality" was coined by Columbia law professor Tim Wu in 2003. The FCC's classification of broadband — as a lightly regulated "information service" under Title I of the Communications Act, or as a "telecommunications service" under Title II subject to common-carrier obligations — determines the legal basis for any rules.

Timeline

After early enforcement actions (such as the 2008 Comcast/BitTorrent case) and court rulings striking down rules not grounded in Title II (Verizon v. FCC, 2014), the FCC under Chairman Tom Wheeler adopted the 2015 Open Internet Order, reclassifying broadband under Title II and banning blocking, throttling, and paid prioritization. Under Chairman Ajit Pai, the FCC's 2017 Restoring Internet Freedom Order repealed those rules, effective June 2018; several states, led by California, enacted their own net neutrality laws. In April 2024 the FCC voted to restore federal net neutrality rules, but in January 2025 the Sixth Circuit struck them down, holding that broadband is an information service — leaving the federal rules uncertain.

Arguments

Supporters argue net neutrality protects free expression, competition, and innovation by preventing ISPs from favoring affiliated content. Opponents argue the rules discourage network investment and that antitrust and consumer-protection law are sufficient safeguards.
