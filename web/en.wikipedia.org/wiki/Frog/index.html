Frog

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A frog is any member of the order Anura, the tailless amphibians: short-bodied, tailless as adults, with long hind legs for jumping, protruding eyes and glandular skin. Frogs are the most diverse amphibian group, with over 7,000 species, found on every continent except Antarctica and in habitats from rainforest canopy to desert burrows (toads are frogs with dry, warty skin, not a separate scientific group).

Most frogs have a biphasic life: eggs laid in water hatch into aquatic tadpoles with gills and tails, which metamorphose — under thyroid hormone control — into air-breathing, legged adults. Their permeable skin, which supplements breathing, also makes them sensitive indicators of environmental health.

Decline

Amphibians are the most threatened vertebrate class: habitat loss, pollution, climate change and especially the chytrid fungi have driven declines and extinctions worldwide since the 1980s (the golden toad is the emblematic loss). Conservation breeding and fungal-probiotic research are active responses.

Biology and culture

Frogs call with vocal sacs to attract mates; some are brilliantly toxic (poison dart frogs); others freeze solid through winter (wood frog) or estivate underground through dry years. Frogs anchor folklore and science alike — from fairy-tale princes to the African clawed frog of early pregnancy tests and developmental biology.
