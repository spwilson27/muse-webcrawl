Auxiliary verb

An auxiliary verb (abbreviated aux) is a verb that adds functional or grammatical meaning to the clause in which it occurs, so as to express tense, aspect, modality, voice, emphasis, etc. Auxiliaries usually accompany an infinitive verb or a participle, which respectively provide the main semantic content of the clause. An example is the verb have in the sentence I have finished my lunch. Here, the auxiliary have helps to express the perfect aspect along with the participle, finished.

Basic examples

Below are some sentences that contain representative auxiliary verbs from English, German and Spanish:

  • Do you want tea? — do is an auxiliary accompanying the infinitive want.
  • She has finished. — has is an auxiliary accompanying the past participle finished.
  • Ich werde das machen. — German: "I will do that."
  • Estoy comiendo. — Spanish: "I am eating."

Terms

The auxiliary verb is also called a helping verb, helper verb, or simply auxiliary. Generally, auxiliaries are classified according to the grammatical meaning they contribute. In English, auxiliaries include verbs such as be, have and do, as well as the modal verbs can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will and would.

Characteristics of auxiliaries across languages

Auxiliary verbs typically help to mark certain grammatical categories: tense (e.g., future, past), aspect (e.g., perfect, progressive), modality (e.g., possibility, necessity, obligation), polarity (positive or negative statements) and voice (e.g., passive). Some auxiliaries also help to mark questions. In many languages, auxiliaries belong to a closed class, and auxiliary constructions develop historically out of ordinary verbal constructions through grammaticalization.
