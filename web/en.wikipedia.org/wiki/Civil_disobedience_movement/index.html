Civil disobedience movement

The civil disobedience movement in India was a mass campaign of non-violent resistance to British colonial rule, launched by Mahatma Gandhi in 1930. It followed the Indian National Congress's declaration of Purna Swaraj (complete independence) at its Lahore session in December 1929.

The Salt March

The movement began with the Salt March: on 12 March 1930 Gandhi set out from his Sabarmati Ashram with 78 followers and walked some 240 miles to the coastal village of Dandi, where on 6 April he broke the law by making salt from seawater. The British salt tax and monopoly were a burden on every Indian, and the march dramatised the injustice of colonial rule for a world audience.

Spread and repression

The act sparked nationwide defiance: Indians manufactured salt, boycotted foreign cloth and liquor, refused taxes and resigned from government posts. Women participated in unprecedented numbers, and leaders such as Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan in the north-west organised parallel movements. The British responded with mass arrests — more than 60,000 people were imprisoned, including Gandhi himself. The movement was suspended under the Gandhi–Irwin Pact of March 1931, resumed after the failure of the Second Round Table Conference, and was finally called off in 1934. It transformed the independence struggle into a genuinely mass movement and set the stage for the Quit India Movement of 1942.
