A T cell is a type of lymphocyte, a white blood cell that plays a central role in cell-mediated immunity. T cells are one of two primary types of lymphocytes—the other being B cells—and are distinguished by the presence of a T-cell receptor (TCR) on their surface. They derive their name from the thymus, the organ in which they mature; the letter "T" stands for thymus.

Unlike B cells, which secrete antibodies, T cells act directly on other cells: cytotoxic T cells kill infected or cancerous cells, helper T cells coordinate other immune cells by releasing cytokines, and regulatory T cells restrain the immune response to prevent autoimmunity.

Development

All T cells originate from haematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow. Progenitor cells migrate to the thymus, where they are known as thymocytes. In the thymic cortex, developing cells rearrange their T-cell receptor genes and undergo positive selection, surviving only if their receptors can weakly recognise self major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules. In the medulla they undergo negative selection: cells that react strongly to self antigens are eliminated, establishing central tolerance. Fewer than 5% of thymocytes survive this process; the survivors leave the thymus as naïve T cells and circulate through the blood and secondary lymphoid organs.

Types

Helper T cells

Helper T cells (TH cells) express the CD4 co-receptor and recognise antigen presented on MHC class II molecules. Once activated, they proliferate and differentiate into subsets—including TH1, TH2, TH17 and T follicular helper cells—each secreting characteristic cytokines that direct the immune response against intracellular bacteria, helminths, fungi, or that support B-cell antibody production.

Cytotoxic T cells

Cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) express CD8 and recognise antigen on MHC class I molecules, which are present on nearly all nucleated cells. They kill target cells by releasing perforin and granzymes, or through the Fas ligand pathway, and are essential for eliminating virus-infected and malignant cells.

Regulatory T cells

Regulatory T cells (Tregs), characterised by the transcription factor FOXP3, suppress the activity of other immune cells. They maintain tolerance to self antigens and prevent autoimmune disease; mutations in FOXP3 cause the severe autoimmune disorder IPEX.

Memory T cells

After an infection clears, a fraction of the expanded T-cell population persists as memory T cells. These cells—divided into central memory, effector memory and tissue-resident subsets—respond more rapidly and vigorously on re-encounter with the same antigen, forming the basis of long-lived cellular immunity after infection or vaccination.

Other subsets

γδ T cells possess a distinct receptor made of gamma and delta chains and are enriched in mucosal tissues. Natural killer T (NKT) cells recognise lipid antigens presented by CD1d. Mucosal associated invariant T (MAIT) cells respond to microbial riboflavin metabolites.

Activation

Naïve T cells require two signals for full activation: engagement of the TCR by peptide–MHC on an antigen-presenting cell such as a dendritic cell (signal 1), and co-stimulation, principally CD28 binding CD80 or CD86 (signal 2). Activated cells produce interleukin-2, undergo clonal expansion, and differentiate into effector cells. Checkpoint molecules such as CTLA-4 and PD-1 later restrain the response; antibodies blocking these checkpoints are widely used in cancer immunotherapy.

Clinical significance

T-cell counts fall dramatically in advanced HIV infection, which destroys CD4+ helper cells and leaves patients vulnerable to opportunistic infections. Engineered T cells expressing chimeric antigen receptors (CAR-T cells) are an approved treatment for several B-cell leukaemias and lymphomas. T-cell defects underlie several primary immunodeficiencies, including severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID).
