Fu Jian (Chinese: 苻堅; 337–385), courtesy name Yonggu (永固) or Wenyu (文玉), formally Emperor Xuanzhao of (Former) Qin, was the emperor of the Di-led Chinese Former Qin dynasty. Under his rule, Former Qin unified northern China in 376 and appeared poised to conquer the Eastern Jin dynasty in the south and reunify the whole country. His catastrophic defeat at the Battle of Fei River in 383, however, shattered his empire, which fragmented within a few years.

Early life and accession

Fu Jian was a nephew of Fu Jiàn (苻健, note the different character), the founding emperor of Former Qin. After the tyrannical reign of his cousin Fu Sheng, Fu Jian deposed and killed him in 357 and took the throne, initially with the title Heavenly King. Guided by his brilliant Han Chinese advisor Wang Meng, he reformed the administration, curbed the Di aristocracy, promoted Confucian learning, and strengthened the state's finances and army.

Unification of the north

With Wang Meng directing campaigns, Former Qin destroyed Former Yan in 370, took the Sichuan region from Eastern Jin in 373, conquered Dai in 376, and annexed Former Liang the same year. By 376 Fu Jian ruled all of northern China — the first time the north had been unified since the fall of Western Jin. After Wang Meng's death in 375, Fu Jian became increasingly determined to conquer the south despite the misgivings expressed by several advisors and by his brother Fu Rong.

Battle of Fei River and collapse

In 383 Fu Jian launched an enormous invasion of Eastern Jin, boasting that his army was so large its whips, thrown into the Yangtze, would dam the river. At the Fei River, a smaller Jin force under Xie An and Xie Xuan routed the Qin vanguard; a tactical withdrawal degenerated into a panicked rout, and Fu Jian himself was wounded by an arrow. The defeat triggered rebellions across the north: Murong Chui refounded Yan, Yao Chang founded Later Qin, and Former Qin shrank to a small territory around Chang'an.

Death

In 385, with Chang'an besieged and starving, Fu Jian fled to Mount Wujiang, where he was captured by Yao Chang's forces. When he refused to surrender the imperial seal and insulted Yao, Yao Chang had him strangled on 16 October 385. Former Qin lingered under his son Fu Pi and other successors until 394.

Assessment

Historians have generally regarded Fu Jian as a capable and humane ruler — unusual for his tolerance of defeated enemies and promotion of scholars — whose fatal overconfidence in 383 undid one of the most successful unification projects of the Sixteen Kingdoms period. The Chinese idiom "a panic-stricken rout in which every bush and tree looks like an enemy soldier" (草木皆兵) and "throwing a cupped hand at the wind" derive from accounts of the Fei River campaign.
