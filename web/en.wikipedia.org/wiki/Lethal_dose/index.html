Lethal dose

A lethal dose (LD) is an indication of the lethality of a given substance or type of radiation. Because resistance varies from one individual to another, the lethal dose represents a statistical quantity: the amount of a substance that will kill a specified proportion of a test population.

Median lethal dose (LD50)

The most commonly used measure is the median lethal dose, or LD50 — the dose required to kill half the members of a tested population over a specified period. First proposed by J. W. Trevan in 1927, it is expressed in milligrams of substance per kilogram of body weight (mg/kg) and is the standard index for comparing the acute toxicity of substances, from table salt (LD50 about 3,000 mg/kg in rats) to caffeine (about 192 mg/kg) to botulinum toxin (about 0.000001 mg/kg, among the most toxic substances known). Related measures include the LDLo (lowest published lethal dose), and, for gases and radiation, the lethal concentration (LC50) and median lethal dose of radiation.

Use and controversy

LD50 values underpin hazard classifications, safety data sheets, and the Globally Harmonized System (GHS) of chemical labelling. The classical animal test has been criticised on ethical grounds and for poor reproducibility; the OECD deleted its traditional LD50 guideline (Test Guideline 401) in 2001 in favour of alternatives — the fixed-dose procedure, acute toxic class method, and up-and-down procedure — that use fewer animals and estimate ranges rather than exact values.
