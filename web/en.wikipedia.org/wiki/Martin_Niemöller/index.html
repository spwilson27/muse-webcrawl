Martin Niemöller

Friedrich Gustav Emil Martin Niemöller
Born: January 14, 1892, Lippstadt, German Empire
Died: March 6, 1984 (aged 92), Wiesbaden, West Germany
Occupation: Lutheran pastor, theologian
Known for: "First they came…" confession; resistance to Nazism

Martin Niemöller (January 14, 1892 – March 6, 1984) was a German Lutheran pastor and theologian. A decorated U-boat commander in the First World War and initially a conservative nationalist, he became a leading figure in the Confessing Church's resistance to Nazi control of the Protestant churches, was imprisoned in concentration camps from 1937 to 1945, and after the war became a prominent advocate of repentance, peace, and disarmament.

Early life and war service

Niemöller joined the Imperial German Navy as a cadet in 1910 and commanded the submarines U-73 and U-39 during the First World War, winning the Iron Cross, First Class. After the war he studied theology at Münster and was ordained in 1924, becoming pastor of the Dahlem parish in Berlin in 1931.

Resistance and imprisonment

Although he initially welcomed aspects of the Nazi rise to power, Niemöller broke with the regime over its interference in church affairs and the Aryan paragraph. He co-founded the Pastors' Emergency League with Dietrich Bonhoeffer and others in 1933. Arrested in July 1937, he was held in Moabit prison and then in Sachsenhausen and Dachau concentration camps as Hitler's personal prisoner until liberated in 1945.

"First they came…"

Niemöller is best remembered internationally for his postwar confession, delivered in various versions from 1946 onward, which begins in its best-known form: "First they came for the socialists, and I did not speak out — because I was not a socialist…" The text, inscribed at memorials including the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, has become a universal warning against silence in the face of persecution.

Later life

After the war Niemöller helped draft the Stuttgart Declaration of Guilt, served as president of the Evangelical Church in Hesse and Nassau, and became a leading voice in the World Council of Churches and the peace movement, receiving the Lenin Peace Prize in 1967.
