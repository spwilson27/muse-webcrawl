Semi-presidential system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A semi-presidential system is a system of government in which a president exists alongside a prime minister and a cabinet, with the latter two responsible to the legislature. It differs from a parliamentary system in that the president is directly elected and holds significant powers, and from a presidential system in that the cabinet, though named by the president, can be dismissed by the legislature. The term was coined by Maurice Duverger to describe the French Fifth Republic (1958).

Subtypes and operation

Scholars distinguish premier-presidential systems (the prime minister and cabinet are accountable exclusively to parliament — e.g. France, Poland, Portugal) from president-parliamentary systems (the cabinet is accountable to both president and parliament — historically Weimar Germany, and Russia's 1993 constitution in strong form). France's experience of "cohabitation" (1986–88, 1993–95, 1997–2002), where president and prime minister came from opposed camps, showed power shifting towards the prime minister when the president lacks a parliamentary majority — and constitutional amendments shortening the presidential term to five years (2000) made cohabitation less likely. Dozens of countries use variants, with outcomes depending more on party systems and presidential restraint than on the formal model alone.
