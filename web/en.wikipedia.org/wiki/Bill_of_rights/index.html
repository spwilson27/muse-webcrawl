Bill of rights

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A bill of rights, sometimes called a declaration of rights or a charter of rights, is a list of the most important rights to the citizens of a country. The purpose is to protect those rights against infringement from public officials and private citizens. Bills of rights may be entrenched (part of a constitution, amendable only by special procedures) or ordinary statutes, and are a hallmark of modern constitutionalism.

History and examples

The genre descends from documents such as England's Bill of Rights 1689 (parliamentary rights against the Crown), the Virginia Declaration of Rights (1776), France's Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789), and the United States Bill of Rights (1791) — the first ten amendments, guaranteeing speech, religion, arms, due process and other liberties against the federal government (applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment in the 20th century). Later exemplars include the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982), South Africa's Bill of Rights (1996, unusually including socio-economic rights) and the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights (2009). The United Kingdom's Human Rights Act 1998 incorporates European Convention rights in statute form, and periodic proposals for a British bill of rights revisit the model's form.

Debates

Bills of rights are defended as pre-commitments protecting minorities and structuring judicial review; critics in the parliamentary-sovereignty tradition object that they transfer contested moral questions to courts and freeze contemporary values — arguments rehearsed in debates from Jeremy Waldron's case against strong judicial review to popular-constitutionalism responses.
