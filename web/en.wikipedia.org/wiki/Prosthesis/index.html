Prosthesis

A prosthetic leg for sport, using a carbon-fibre blade.

In medicine, a prosthesis (from Ancient Greek prosthesis, "addition, application, attachment"), or a prosthetic implant, is an artificial device that replaces a missing body part, which may be lost through trauma, disease, or a condition present at birth (a congenital disorder). Prostheses are intended to restore the normal functions of the missing body part.

Types

Prostheses range from limb prostheses — including transradial (below-elbow) and transfemoral (above-knee) devices — to joint replacements such as artificial hips and knees, heart valves, cochlear implants, dental prostheses, and ocular prostheses (artificial eyes). Limb prostheses combine mechanical components such as pylons, sockets and joints with cosmesis, a covering intended to mimic the natural limb.

Control and technology

Modern upper-limb prostheses may be body-powered, moved by cables and harnesses, or myoelectric, controlled by electrical signals generated when the user contracts remaining muscles. Research devices use targeted muscle reinnervation and implanted electrodes to give finer control and even sensory feedback. Lower-limb devices use energy-storing carbon-fibre feet; microprocessor-controlled knees adjust resistance in real time for safer walking.

History

Prostheses are ancient: an artificial toe from around 950–710 BC was found on an Egyptian mummy, and Roman accounts describe an iron hand made for the general Marcus Sergius. The wars of the 20th century, and later landmine injuries and diabetes, drove large-scale development, while Paralympic sport has popularised high-performance designs such as the blades worn by Oscar Pistorius.
