Screen printing

Screen printing is a printing technique where a mesh is used to transfer ink onto a substrate, except in areas made impermeable to the ink by a blocking stencil. A blade or squeegee is moved across the screen to fill the open mesh apertures with ink, and a reverse stroke then causes the screen to touch the substrate momentarily along a line of contact, transferring the ink. One colour is printed at a time, so separate screens are used to produce a multi-coloured image.

History

Stencil and mesh printing has ancient roots in China and was refined in Japan, where silk screens were used for textile designs. Screen printing in recognisable modern form appeared in England and France in the early 20th century, patented by Samuel Simon in 1907, and was rapidly adopted for advertising posters, flags and commercial graphics. Photo-emulsion stencils, developed by the 1910s–1930s, made photographic reproduction possible. In fine art, the technique — promoted under the term serigraphy — was embraced in the 1930s–40s and achieved iconic status in the 1960s through Pop artists such as Andy Warhol, whose silkscreen portraits defined the movement's look.

Technique

The screen is a frame (wood or aluminium) stretched with fine polyester or, historically, silk mesh. A stencil is created by coating the mesh with light-sensitive emulsion, exposing it through a film positive, and washing out the unexposed image areas. Mesh count, ink type (plastisol, water-based, UV-curing, solvent) and squeegee pressure and angle determine print quality. Screen printing deposits a thicker ink layer than most printing methods, giving vivid, opaque colours, and it works on an unusually wide range of substrates: textiles (especially T-shirts and garments), paper, plastics, glass, metal, wood, and electronic circuit boards, where conductive inks print circuitry and membrane switches.

Applications

Garment printing is the most visible use; screen printing dominates decorated apparel and is also used for posters, signage, decals, bottles, balloons, medical devices, solar cell contacts, and printed electronics. Although digital methods (direct-to-garment, inkjet) have taken short-run work, screen printing remains unmatched for long runs, spot colours, and specialty effects such as puff, glitter, metallic and high-density inks.
