Covert operation


Plausible deniability is a hallmark

A covert operation or undercover operation is a military or intelligence operation planned and executed so as to conceal the identity of its sponsor, or to permit plausible denial if it is exposed. Covert operations differ from clandestine operations, in which the operation itself is concealed but the sponsor may be acknowledged: in a covert operation, the sponsoring state or organisation also remains hidden.

United States law

Under United States law, covert action is defined in the National Security Act of 1947 (as amended) as activity meant to influence political, economic, or military conditions abroad while the role of the United States is not apparent or acknowledged publicly. Since the Hughes–Ryan Amendment of 1974, such operations require a presidential "finding" and notification to Congressional intelligence committees; the Iran–Contra affair of the 1980s prompted further reforms.

Examples

Historical examples include the 1953 Iranian coup d'état (Operation Ajax), the 1954 Guatemalan coup, and numerous Cold War paramilitary and political-action programmes conducted by the CIA and other services. Law enforcement agencies also conduct undercover operations against organised crime and terrorism, in which officers conceal their identities to infiltrate criminal groups.
