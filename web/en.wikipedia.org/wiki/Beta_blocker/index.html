Beta blockers, also spelled β-blockers and formally called beta-adrenergic blocking agents, are a class of medications that block the effects of the catecholamines adrenaline and noradrenaline at beta-adrenergic receptors. They are used chiefly to manage abnormal heart rhythms, to protect the heart after a heart attack, and to treat high blood pressure and heart failure.

Mechanism and uses

By antagonising β1 receptors in the heart, beta blockers reduce heart rate, contractility and conduction velocity; blockade of β2 receptors affects blood vessels, airways and metabolic tissues. Non-selective agents such as propranolol block both β1 and β2 receptors, while cardioselective agents such as metoprolol and atenolol mainly target β1. Uses include hypertension, angina pectoris, post-myocardial infarction care, heart failure (bisoprolol, carvedilol, metoprolol succinate), rate control in atrial fibrillation, glaucoma eye drops, essential tremor, and the physical symptoms of anxiety; propranolol is also used to prevent migraine. The first clinically successful beta blocker, propranolol, was developed by Sir James Black in the 1960s, work that contributed to his Nobel Prize.

Adverse effects

Common adverse effects include fatigue, cold extremities, slow heart rate and dizziness. Because β2 blockade can provoke bronchospasm, beta blockers are used cautiously in asthma. They can mask the warning symptoms of hypoglycaemia in diabetes, and abrupt withdrawal after long-term use can trigger rebound tachycardia and ischaemia, so doses are tapered. Beta blockers are banned in certain precision sports, such as shooting and archery, because they reduce tremor and performance anxiety.
