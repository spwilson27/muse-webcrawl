Wind chill

Wind chill (popularly wind chill factor) is the sensation of reduced temperature on exposed skin caused by the flow of air. The wind chill temperature is always lower than the air temperature for values where the formula applies, because wind strips away the thin insulating boundary layer of warmed air at the skin's surface, increasing the rate of heat loss by convection.

Explanation

A surface loses heat through conduction, convection, evaporation and radiation. Wind increases convective heat transfer: the faster the air moves, the faster a warm object cools toward air temperature — though wind chill can never cool an inanimate object below the actual air temperature. For people, exposed skin can freeze much faster in wind, raising the risk of frostbite and hypothermia.

History and formula

The concept was developed by Paul Siple and Charles Passel during Antarctic expeditions in the 1940s, based on how quickly water bottles froze at different wind speeds. In 2001 the United States, Canada and the United Kingdom adopted a revised wind chill index derived from human-subject trials conducted by the Joint Action Group for Temp Indices. The North American formula is:

Twc = 13.12 + 0.6215T − 11.37V0.16 + 0.3965TV0.16

where T is air temperature in °C and V is wind speed in km/h at 10 m height. It applies at temperatures of 10 °C or below and wind speeds above about 4.8 km/h.
