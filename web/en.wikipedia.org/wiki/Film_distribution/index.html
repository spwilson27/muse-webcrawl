Film distribution is the process of making a movie available for viewing by an audience. It is usually the task of a professional film distributor, who determines the marketing strategy for the film, the media by which a film is to be exhibited or made available for viewing, and who may set the release date and other matters.

Release windows

Films are traditionally released in a sequence of windows: first a theatrical run in cinemas, then premium video on demand and home video (formerly VHS, DVD and Blu-ray), then pay television, streaming services and finally free-to-air television. The theatrical window historically lasted about 90 days but has shortened considerably, and since the 2010s major studios have experimented with day-and-date releases on their own streaming platforms.

Release patterns

  • Wide release: opening simultaneously on hundreds or thousands of screens, supported by mass advertising.
  • Platform release: opening in a few theatres and expanding gradually on the strength of reviews and word of mouth.
  • Limited release: a restricted run, often to qualify for awards or serve niche audiences.

Economics

Under a typical distribution deal the exhibitor keeps roughly half of the box-office gross and the distributor takes the remainder ("rentals"), from which the distributor recoups its fee and prints-and-advertising (P&A) costs before profits flow to the producers. Marketing costs for major releases can rival the production budget. Film festivals and markets such as Cannes, Berlin and Toronto are where independent films are sold to distributors territory by territory.
