Prior Analytics

A medieval manuscript of the Analytica Priora.

The Prior Analytics (Ancient Greek: Ἀναλυτικὰ Πρότερα, Analytika Protera; Latin: Analytica Priora) is a work by Aristotle, written around 350 BC, on deductive reasoning and the science of demonstration. It is the first surviving systematic treatise on logic in the Western tradition and the founding text of the theory of the syllogism. Together with the Posterior Analytics and the other logical works it forms part of the Organon.

Contents

Book I develops the syllogistic: the definition of the syllogism, the three figures, conversion rules, the enumeration and proof of the valid moods (such as Barbara and Celarent), and Aristotle's method of reducing imperfect syllogisms to the perfect moods of the first figure. Later chapters treat modal syllogisms — arguments mixing necessity and possibility — which scholars still find difficult. Book II examines the powers and defects of syllogisms: false conclusions from true premises, circular reasoning, begging the question, and the relation of induction and example to deduction.

Aristotle's notation

Aristotle used schematic letters for terms — the first known use of variables in logic — and distinguished assertoric, apodeictic (necessary), and problematic (possible) premises. His system dominated Western and Islamic logic for more than two thousand years; commentators from Alexander of Aphrodisias to Averroes and Aquinas elaborated it, and Kant could still claim that logic had not advanced beyond Aristotle.

Influence

The treatise was translated into Syriac, Arabic, and Latin (the logica vetus came through Boethius), shaping medieval scholastic method. Modern scholars, notably Jan Łukasiewicz and John Corcoran, have reconstructed Aristotle's system as a rigorous natural-deduction or axiomatic calculus, sparking debate about its exact logical structure.
