Ottoman Albania

Ottoman Albania (Albanian: Shqipëria Osmane) refers to the period in the history of Albania from the late 14th century, when Ottoman forces first gained a foothold in the region, until 1912, when Albania declared independence from the Ottoman Empire. Over the course of roughly five centuries, the territories inhabited by Albanians were incorporated into the Ottoman administrative system, first through the timar system and later as part of the sanjaks and vilayets of Rumelia.

Ottoman conquest

Ottoman forces first entered Albanian-inhabited territories in the 1380s, defeating a coalition of Balkan lords at the Battle of Savra in 1385. Following the Battle of Kosovo in 1389, Ottoman suzerainty extended over most of the region. The conquest proceeded in stages: Shkodra fell in 1479 after two sieges, and the Venetian possessions along the coast were gradually absorbed. By the end of the 15th century, nearly all Albanian lands were under direct Ottoman rule.

Resistance and Skanderbeg

The most famous episode of resistance was led by Gjergj Kastrioti Skanderbeg, a former Ottoman commander who defected in 1443 and united the Albanian principalities in the League of Lezhë (1444). For 25 years Skanderbeg's forces repelled repeated Ottoman campaigns, including sieges led personally by sultans Murad II and Mehmed II. After Skanderbeg's death in 1468, resistance continued until the fall of Krujë in 1478 and Shkodra in 1479.

Classical period

In the 16th and 17th centuries Albania was fully integrated into the Ottoman system. The lands were divided among sanjaks such as Shkodra, Elbasan, Ohrid, Vlorë, and Prizren. Albanians rose to the highest ranks of the empire: numerous grand viziers, including members of the Köprülü family, were of Albanian origin, and Albanian soldiers and administrators served throughout the Balkans and the Middle East. The timar system gradually gave way to tax farming and large estates (çiftliks).

Islamization

Over the Ottoman centuries, a majority of Albanians gradually converted to Islam, a process driven by taxation incentives, urbanization, and the spread of Sufi orders such as the Bektashis, though substantial Catholic populations remained in the north and Orthodox Christians in the south. This confessional diversity later shaped the Albanian national movement, whose leaders famously subordinated religion to national identity.

The era of the pashaliks

In the late 18th and early 19th centuries, weakening central authority allowed local magnates to carve out semi-autonomous domains known as pashaliks. The most powerful were Kara Mahmud Bushati of Shkodra and Ali Pasha of Ioannina, whose rule extended over much of southern Albania and Greece until his defeat and death in 1822.

National awakening and independence

The 19th century saw Tanzimat reforms and the rise of the Albanian National Awakening (Rilindja Kombëtare). The League of Prizren (1878) resisted the partition of Albanian-inhabited lands among neighboring states after the Russo-Turkish War. Revolts in 1910–1912, combined with the Balkan Wars, led to the Albanian Declaration of Independence at Vlorë on 28 November 1912, ending more than four centuries of Ottoman rule.
