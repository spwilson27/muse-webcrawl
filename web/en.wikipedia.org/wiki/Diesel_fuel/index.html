Diesel fuel, also called diesel oil or simply diesel, is any liquid fuel designed for use in a diesel engine, in which fuel ignition takes place through compression heating rather than a spark. The most common type is a specific fractional distillate of petroleum — petrodiesel — though alternatives not derived from petroleum, such as biodiesel and synthetic (gas-to-liquids or biomass-to-liquids) diesel, are increasingly used. The fuel is named after Rudolf Diesel, inventor of the compression-ignition engine.

Petroleum diesel

Petrodiesel is produced by fractional distillation of crude oil between roughly 200 °C and 350 °C at atmospheric pressure, yielding a mixture of hydrocarbons mostly in the C10–C20 range. Its willingness to auto-ignite is measured by the cetane number: higher cetane gives shorter ignition delay, smoother cold starting and less engine knock. Because diesel has a higher volumetric energy density than petrol (gasoline), diesel vehicles typically achieve better fuel economy.

Modern road diesel in Europe and North America is ultra-low-sulfur diesel (ULSD), with sulfur capped at 10–15 parts per million, allowing exhaust after-treatment systems such as diesel particulate filters (DPF) and selective catalytic reduction (SCR) with urea (AdBlue/DEF) to cut particulate and NOx emissions. Winter grades are blended or additised to prevent wax crystals from blocking filters in cold weather.

Biodiesel and renewable diesel

Biodiesel consists of fatty acid methyl esters (FAME) made by transesterification of vegetable oils or animal fats; it is commonly blended with petrodiesel (e.g. B7, B20). Hydrotreated vegetable oil (HVO), or renewable diesel, is a paraffinic fuel chemically similar to petrodiesel and usable as a drop-in replacement. Fischer–Tropsch synthesis can also convert natural gas, coal or biomass into synthetic diesel.

Uses

Diesel powers most trucks, buses, trains, construction and agricultural machinery, many ships (as marine diesel oil or heavier fuel oils), military vehicles, and a substantial share of passenger cars, especially in Europe. Stationary diesel generators provide standby and remote power.

Environmental and health issues

Diesel exhaust contains fine particulate matter and nitrogen oxides; the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified diesel engine exhaust as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) in 2012. Per litre, diesel combustion emits slightly more CO2 than petrol, but the greater efficiency of diesel engines usually means lower CO2 per kilometre. The 2015 "Dieselgate" scandal, in which Volkswagen and others fitted defeat devices to cheat emissions tests, accelerated the shift away from diesel cars in many markets.

Taxation

Fuel taxes on diesel differ widely: in the United States diesel is taxed somewhat more heavily per gallon than gasoline at the federal level, while many European countries historically taxed diesel less than petrol, encouraging high diesel-car market shares.
