Retrograde amnesia

Retrograde amnesia (RA) is a loss of memory of events and information that occurred, or was learned, before an injury or the onset of a disease. It contrasts with anterograde amnesia, the inability to form new memories after the causative event; the two frequently occur together following damage to the medial temporal lobe, diencephalon or related structures.

Characteristics

Retrograde amnesia is usually temporally graded: in accordance with Ribot's law (1881), memories from just before the injury are most vulnerable, while remote memories from childhood are relatively spared, a pattern attributed to systems consolidation, in which memories gradually become independent of the hippocampus. The deficit typically affects episodic (autobiographical) memory more than semantic knowledge or procedural skills. In traumatic brain injury, the period of retrograde loss often shrinks during recovery, though a gap for events immediately around the trauma is commonly permanent.

Causes

Causes include head injury, stroke, encephalitis, Korsakoff syndrome (from thiamine deficiency), electroconvulsive therapy, and neurodegenerative disease. Extensive, ungraded retrograde amnesia covering a person's whole past is rare and more often has a psychogenic (dissociative) origin. Famous studied cases include patients K.C. and H.M., whose amnesias shaped modern theories of memory consolidation.
