Keto–enol tautomerism

Keto–enol tautomerism is a chemical equilibrium between a keto form (a ketone or aldehyde) and an enol (an alcohol with a carbon–carbon double bond adjacent to the hydroxyl group). The enol and keto forms are said to be tautomers of each other. The interconversion involves the movement of a proton and the shifting of bonding electrons; hence, the isomerism qualifies as prototropic tautomerism.

The equilibrium

For most simple carbonyl compounds, the equilibrium lies far toward the keto form: in acetone, for example, only about one molecule in ten million is in the enol form at any moment. Removal of an α-hydrogen (or addition of a proton to the α-carbon of the enol) interconverts the forms, and the process is catalysed by both acids and bases. Compounds with two carbonyl groups separated by one carbon (β-dicarbonyls) such as acetylacetone have much higher enol content — around 15–25% in water and more in nonpolar solvents — because the enol is stabilised by conjugation and intramolecular hydrogen bonding. Phenols are aromatic enols whose "keto" forms are negligible.

Enolates and reactivity

Deprotonation at the α-carbon gives an enolate, a resonance-stabilised anion that is a key intermediate in organic synthesis. Reactions proceeding through enols or enolates include α-halogenation of ketones, the aldol reaction, racemisation at α-stereocentres, and deuterium exchange at α-positions. In biochemistry, enol intermediates appear in glycolysis (phosphoenolpyruvate) and enol–keto tautomerism of nucleobases can cause rare mispairing during DNA replication, a proposed source of spontaneous mutations.
