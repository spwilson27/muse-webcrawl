Liberal democracy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Liberal democracy is a form of government combining liberal political ideology with democracy: representative government by free and fair elections under universal suffrage, together with the protection of civil liberties — speech, press, religion, association, property — the Rule of law, an independent judiciary, and limits on the power of any majority or executive. Its opposite poles are illiberal democracy (elections without liberal protections) and liberal autocracy (rights without elections).

Historically, liberalism (rights, constitutions, markets) preceded mass democracy in most countries, and the two were fused only in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries as suffrage expanded — a fusion some theorists consider tense: democratic majorities may vote away liberal rights, and liberal courts may override democratic choices. After 1945 liberal democracy became the dominant model in Western Europe, North America and Japan, spread widely after 1989, and has faced a period of backsliding and populist challenge in the twenty-first century.

Features and measurement

Institutional hallmarks include competitive parties, alternation in power, free media, civilian control of the military and horizontal accountability between branches. Indices such as Freedom House's Freedom in the World and the V-Dem project measure liberal democracy's presence and decline; their findings of a long democratic recession since roughly the mid-2000s are debated in detail but broadly accepted as identifying a real trend. Fareed Zakaria's 1997 essay on “illiberal democracy” framed much of the subsequent discussion.
