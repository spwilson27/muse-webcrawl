Angiosperms

Angiosperms, commonly called flowering plants, are the largest and most diverse group of land plants. Their defining feature is that their ovules are enclosed within a carpel, which typically develops into a fruit after fertilisation. Flowers, double fertilisation and highly reduced gametophytes also distinguish them from gymnosperms.

Evolution and diversity

The earliest widely accepted angiosperm fossils date to the Early Cretaceous, after which the group diversified rapidly and came to dominate most terrestrial ecosystems. Living angiosperms number roughly 300,000 described species, from duckweeds and grasses to oaks, orchids and cacti. Major lineages include monocots, eudicots, magnoliids and several early-diverging groups.

Reproduction

A typical flower contains stamens, which produce pollen, and carpels, which contain ovules. Pollination may be by wind, water or animals. After fertilisation, one sperm cell fuses with the egg to form the embryo, while another commonly fuses with central-cell nuclei to form endosperm, a nutritive tissue in many seeds.

Importance

Angiosperms provide most human food crops, many medicines, timber, fibres and ornamentals, and they form the structural basis of forests, grasslands and wetlands.
