Weimar culture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Weimar culture was the emergence of the arts and sciences in Germany during the Weimar Republic (1919–1933), centred above all in Berlin. In the years of relative stability after 1924 — the "Golden Twenties" — Germany became a leading centre of the avant-garde, influential in painting, architecture, cinema, theatre, literature, philosophy and science, even as the Republic itself remained politically fragile.

Art, design and architecture

The Bauhaus, founded by Walter Gropius in Weimar in 1919, reshaped modern design, joining fine art, craft and industrial production; its teachers included Wassily Kandinsky and Paul Klee. Painting movements included the dying embers of Expressionism and the cooler, satirical New Objectivity (Neue Sachlichkeit) of artists such as Otto Dix and George Grosz.

Cinema and theatre

Weimar cinema produced classics of German Expressionism and beyond: The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920), Nosferatu (1922), Fritz Lang's Metropolis (1927) and M (1931), and The Blue Angel (1930), which made Marlene Dietrich a star. In theatre, Bertolt Brecht and Kurt Weill created The Threepenny Opera (1928), while director Max Reinhardt dominated the Berlin stage.

Science and thought

Berlin and Göttingen were world centres of physics during the development of quantum mechanics, home to Albert Einstein, Max Planck, Werner Heisenberg and others. The era also saw the Frankfurt School of social theory, Magnus Hirschfeld's pioneering sexology, and a famous cabaret and nightlife scene — later romanticised abroad in works such as Cabaret.

End

The culture was extinguished after 1933: the Nazi regime denounced much of it as "degenerate", and thousands of artists and intellectuals fled into exile, carrying Weimar modernism to Britain, Palestine and the United States.
