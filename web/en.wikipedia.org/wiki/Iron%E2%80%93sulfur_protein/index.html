Iron–sulfur protein

Iron–sulfur proteins are proteins characterised by the presence of iron–sulfur clusters: assemblies of iron and sulfide ions coordinated by cysteine residues. The commonest clusters contain two, three, or four iron atoms (written [2Fe–2S], [3Fe–4S], and [4Fe–4S]). These proteins are found in virtually all organisms and are among the most ancient of biological cofactors.

Functions

Iron–sulfur clusters chiefly serve in electron transfer. Chains of them carry electrons through the respiratory complexes of mitochondria and the photosynthetic apparatus of chloroplasts and cyanobacteria; familiar examples include the ferredoxins and the Rieske proteins. Clusters also perform catalytic roles, as in aconitase, serve as sensors of iron, oxygen, and nitric oxide, and participate in the radical chemistry of enzymes such as those in the radical SAM superfamily.

Biogenesis

Because free iron and sulfide are toxic, cells assemble clusters on dedicated scaffold machinery (the ISC, SUF, and related systems) and deliver them to target proteins. Defects in human cluster-biogenesis proteins cause diseases including Friedreich's ataxia and multiple mitochondrial dysfunctions syndrome.
