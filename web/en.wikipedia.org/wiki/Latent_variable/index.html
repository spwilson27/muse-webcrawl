Latent variable

In statistics, latent variables (from Latin latens, "hidden") are variables that can only be inferred indirectly through a mathematical model of other observable variables that can be directly observed or measured. Latent variable models aim to explain observed variables in terms of latent variables. Latent variables are commonly contrasted with "manifest variables," which are directly measurable. Familiar examples include intelligence, happiness, quality of life, and economic confidence: none can be measured directly with an instrument, but each can be inferred from multiple observable indicators, such as test scores, survey responses, or spending behaviour.

A latent variable (circle) inferred from its observable indicators (squares).

Examples

Psychology

Many psychological constructs are latent variables. Charles Spearman proposed that performance on diverse mental tests reflects a single latent factor, general intelligence or g. Personality traits in the Big Five model are likewise treated as latent dimensions inferred from questionnaire items.

Economics

Economists use latent variables for constructs such as expected inflation, utility, quality of life, and the "true" value of an economic quantity measured with error. In discrete choice models, the observed choice is modelled as the outcome of an unobserved latent utility comparison.

Medicine

In medicine, a disease state may be treated as latent when no perfect diagnostic test exists; latent class analysis is then used to estimate the accuracy of several imperfect tests against the unobserved true status.

History

The concept originates with Spearman's 1904 work on factor analysis. Latent structure analysis was formalised by Paul Lazarsfeld in the mid-twentieth century, and the general theory of latent variable modelling was consolidated in the structural equation modelling tradition associated with Karl Jöreskog and others in the 1970s.

Common latent variable models

  • Factor analysis and principal component models
  • Latent class analysis and latent profile analysis
  • Item response theory (Rasch and related models)
  • Hidden Markov models
  • Structural equation models
  • Latent growth curve models

Inference

Because latent variables are unobserved, statistical inference typically proceeds by integrating (marginalising) them out of the likelihood, often with the expectation–maximisation algorithm, or by Bayesian methods that sample or approximate their posterior distribution. The interpretation of a latent variable depends on the model's assumptions: it may be regarded as a real but unmeasured attribute of the subject, or merely as a convenient mathematical device that summarises patterns in the data.
