Foley catheter
InventorFrederic Foley (1929)
PurposeIndwelling urinary drainage
RetentionInflatable balloon (usually 5–30 mL)
SizesFrench gauge (Fr), commonly 12–24 Fr

A Foley catheter is a flexible tube passed through the urethra into the urinary bladder to drain urine. It is the most common type of indwelling urinary catheter. The catheter is held in place by a small balloon inflated with sterile water just inside the bladder, and the external end drains into a collection bag.

Design and use

Foley catheters are made of latex, silicone-coated latex, or pure silicone, and are sized by the French scale (1 Fr = 1/3 mm of outer diameter); sizes from 12 to 24 Fr are typical for adults. Most have two channels—one for drainage and one for balloon inflation—while three-way catheters add a channel for bladder irrigation, used after prostate or bladder surgery. They are inserted under sterile technique with lubricant, and the balloon is inflated only after urine flow confirms placement in the bladder.

Clinical applications

Indications include urinary retention or obstruction, accurate measurement of urine output in critically ill patients, healing of perineal wounds in incontinent patients, and drainage during and after surgery. Foley balloons are also used outside urology—for cervical ripening in labor induction and for tamponade of epistaxis.

Complications

The chief complication of indwelling catheterization is catheter-associated urinary tract infection (CAUTI), one of the most common healthcare-associated infections; risk increases with each day of catheterization, so guidelines recommend removing catheters as soon as they are no longer needed. Other complications include urethral trauma, bladder spasm, encrustation, and blockage.

History

The device is named after the American urologist Frederic Foley, who designed the self-retaining balloon catheter in 1929; it was first marketed by the C. R. Bard company in 1934.
