Charlotte Brontë (21 April 1816 – 31 March 1855) was an English novelist and poet, the eldest of the three Brontë sisters who survived into adulthood and whose works are classics of English literature. Writing under the pen name Currer Bell, she published Jane Eyre in 1847, a revolutionary novel incorporating elements of social criticism, Gothic fiction and an intense first-person psychological realism.

Life

Charlotte was born at Thornton, near Bradford, the third child of the Irish clergyman Patrick Brontë. The family moved to the parsonage at Haworth on the Yorkshire moors, where the children, after their mother's early death and a disastrous period at the Clergy Daughters' School at Cowan Bridge (reflected in Lowood in Jane Eyre), created elaborate imaginary worlds — Charlotte and her brother Branwell chronicled the kingdom of Angria in tiny hand-written books.

She worked as a teacher and governess, and studied French in Brussels in 1842–43, where she fell in love, unrequited, with the school's director Constantin Héger — an experience transmuted in The Professor and Villette. In 1846 she published, at the sisters' expense, Poems by Currer, Ellis, and Acton Bell. Jane Eyre (1847) was an immediate success; Emily's Wuthering Heights and Anne's Agnes Grey followed.

Later years

Within eight months of 1848–49 Charlotte lost Branwell, Emily and Anne. She continued to write — Shirley (1849) and Villette (1853) — and became a figure in London literary society, befriending her publisher's reader and her biographer Elizabeth Gaskell. In 1854 she married her father's curate, Arthur Bell Nicholls; she died the following March, aged 38, probably of complications of pregnancy (hyperemesis gravidarum), though tuberculosis has also been proposed.
