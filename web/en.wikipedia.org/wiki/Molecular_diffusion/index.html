Molecular diffusion

Molecular diffusion, often simply called diffusion, is the thermal motion of all particles in liquids, gases and solids — atoms, molecules or larger suspended particles — that results in the net transport of a substance from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, with no bulk motion of the medium. Diffusion is driven by the tendency toward uniform mixing inherent in random molecular motion, and it proceeds until concentration is everywhere equal. It is the mechanism behind such everyday phenomena as a drop of dye dispersing in water, the spread of a scent through a room, and the exchange of gases across the alveoli of the lungs.

Fick's laws

The quantitative description of diffusion is given by Fick's laws, formulated by Adolf Fick in 1855. The first law states that the diffusive flux J is proportional to the negative of the concentration gradient: J = −D ∂c/∂x, where D is the diffusion coefficient. The second law, the diffusion equation, describes how concentration changes in time: ∂c/∂t = D ∇²c. Typical values of D are around 10−9 m²/s for small molecules in water and 10−5 m²/s in air.

Microscopic picture

At the molecular level, diffusing particles undergo Brownian motion: a ceaseless random walk caused by collisions with surrounding molecules. Einstein's 1905 analysis of Brownian motion showed that the mean squared displacement grows linearly with time, ⟨x²⟩ = 2Dt in one dimension, and relates D to temperature and viscosity through the Stokes–Einstein relation. This connection provided some of the earliest direct evidence for the existence of atoms.

Applications

Diffusion governs transport at the cellular scale in biology — oxygen and nutrient delivery, neurotransmitter spread across synapses — because distances are small enough for its square-root-of-time scaling to be fast. It is exploited or combated across engineering: dialysis and membrane separations, doping of semiconductors, case hardening of steel, drug delivery, and the dispersion of pollutants. In solids, diffusion proceeds by vacancy and interstitial mechanisms and is exponentially temperature-dependent (Arrhenius behaviour).
