Copy number variation

Copy number variation (CNV) is a phenomenon in which sections of the genome are repeated and the number of repeats in the genome varies between individuals. The term, along with the discovery that healthy individuals carry many large deletions and duplications, arose from genome-wide studies in 2004 that revealed an unexpectedly large amount of structural variation in the human genome. Copy number variations are a type of structural variation: specifically, they are a type of duplication or deletion event that affects a considerable number of base pairs.

Scale and prevalence

CNVs range from about one kilobase to several megabases in size. Roughly two-thirds of the human genome may be composed of repeats, and about 4.8–9.5% of the genome can be classified as copy number variations. They arise from mechanisms including non-allelic homologous recombination, non-homologous end joining, and replication errors.

Role in disease and evolution

Many CNVs are benign, but others are associated with susceptibility or resistance to disease, including associations reported with autism, schizophrenia, Crohn's disease, rheumatoid arthritis, obesity and HIV. Copy number changes in genes such as those encoding amylase (AMY1) and the CCL3L1 chemokine have been linked to dietary adaptation and pathogen resistance respectively. In cancer, somatic copy number alterations of oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes are common.

Detection

CNVs are detected using comparative genomic hybridization arrays, SNP genotyping arrays, and increasingly by analysis of DNA sequencing read depth.
