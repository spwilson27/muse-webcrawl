In measure theory, the Lebesgue measure, named after French mathematician Henri Lebesgue, is the standard way of assigning a measure — a generalisation of length, area or volume — to subsets of Euclidean space. It is used throughout real analysis, in particular to define the Lebesgue integral.

Definition

Lebesgue first defines an outer measure for any set E ⊆ ℝ by covering E with countably many open intervals and taking the infimum of the sums of their lengths:

λ*(E) = inf { Σ length(Ik) : E ⊆ ∪ Ik }.

A set E is called Lebesgue measurable if it splits every test set A cleanly, in the sense of Carathéodory's criterion: λ*(A) = λ*(A ∩ E) + λ*(A ∩ Ec) for all A. The restriction of λ* to the measurable sets is the Lebesgue measure λ. On intervals it agrees with ordinary length: λ((a, b)) = b − a; in ℝn it assigns to boxes the product of their side lengths.

Properties

  • Translation invariance: shifting a set does not change its measure.
  • Countable additivity: the measure of a countable disjoint union is the sum of the measures.
  • Completeness: every subset of a null set is measurable with measure zero.
  • It extends the Riemann notion of size to far more sets; for example, the rationals have measure zero, and there exist measurable sets that are not Borel, as well as (assuming the axiom of choice) non-measurable sets such as a Vitali set.

History

Lebesgue introduced the measure in his 1901 note and 1902 doctoral thesis Intégrale, longueur, aire, resolving problems left by the theories of Jordan content and Borel measure and laying the foundations of modern integration and probability theory.
