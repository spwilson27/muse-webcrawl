A discotheque (French: discothèque) is an entertainment venue at which patrons dance to recorded music played by a disc jockey, rather than to a live band. The word combines disque (record) with bibliothèque (library) — a "library of records" — and came to be applied to the clubs themselves.

History

The format emerged in Paris during the Second World War, when, under the German occupation, live jazz was restricted and basement clubs such as Paul Pacini's La Discothèque (opened 1941 on the rue de la Huchette) played records for dancers instead. After the war, Régine Zylberberg, working at the Whisky à Gogo, is credited with replacing the jukebox with twin turntables and a dance floor with coloured lights — the template of the modern club. Discothèques spread through Europe in the 1960s and to the United States, where venues such as New York's Le Club (1960) and Arthur (1965) catered to fashionable crowds.

The disco era

In the 1970s the discotheque became the centre of disco culture, exemplified by Studio 54 (opened 1977) with its celebrity door policy and theatrical design. DJs such as Francis Grasso and Larry Levan developed techniques of beat-matching and long mixes, turning record-playing into an art form, while purpose-built sound systems and lighting made the club itself an instrument.

Legacy

After the disco backlash around 1979–80 (symbolised by Disco Demolition Night), the word "discotheque" fell out of fashion in English, replaced by "club" or "nightclub", but the format — DJ, dance floor, recorded music — became the default model of nightlife worldwide, feeding into house, techno and rave culture.
