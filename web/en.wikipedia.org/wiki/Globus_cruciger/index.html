Globus cruciger

TypeItem of regalia / Christian symbol
MeaningChrist's dominion over the world; the ruler's God-given authority

The globus cruciger (Latin for "cross-bearing orb"), also known as the orb and cross, is an orb surmounted by a Christian cross. It has been a Christian symbol of authority since the Middle Ages, used on coins, in iconography and as part of royal regalia. It symbolises Christ's dominion over the world and, when held by a monarch, represents the ruler as God's representative on Earth, ruling by divine sanction; in images of Christ himself (as Salvator Mundi, "Saviour of the World") it signifies his heavenly sovereignty.

History

The motif derives from the Roman practice of depicting emperors holding a globe representing universal rule — sometimes topped by the goddess Victory. With the Christianisation of the empire, the cross replaced Victory: a globus cruciger appears in the hand of the emperor on coinage from the early 5th century, in the reign of Theodosius II, and became standard on Byzantine coins and imperial imagery. Early medieval Western rulers adopted the symbol in turn; it appears on Carolingian and Ottonian depictions and became, alongside the crown, sceptre and throne, a core item of European coronation regalia.

In regalia and heraldry

Famous surviving examples include the Imperial Orb of the Holy Roman Empire (made in the later 12th century, now in Vienna's Imperial Treasury), the orbs of the Danish, Swedish, Norwegian and Hungarian regalia, and the Sovereign's Orb made in 1661 for Charles II as part of the British Crown Jewels, which is still carried by the monarch at coronations. In heraldry the globus cruciger — called a "mound" in English blazon when part of a crown or crest (from the French monde) — tops many European crowns and coats of arms. The Unicode symbol ☩ and variants are also used in cartography to mark church sites in some national map series.
