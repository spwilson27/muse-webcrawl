Lumbar puncture

A lumbar puncture (LP), also known as a spinal tap, is a medical procedure in which a needle is inserted into the spinal canal, most commonly to collect cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) for diagnostic testing. It is performed in the lower back, usually between the L3/L4 or L4/L5 vertebrae — below the level where the spinal cord ends — so that the needle passes into the subarachnoid space among the roots of the cauda equina.

Uses

Lumbar puncture is principally used to diagnose conditions affecting the central nervous system, above all suspected meningitis and other infections, subarachnoid haemorrhage not seen on imaging, inflammatory conditions such as multiple sclerosis and Guillain–Barré syndrome, and meningeal malignancy. The procedure allows measurement of the opening pressure of the CSF, and analysis of its cell count, protein, glucose, and microbiology. It can also be therapeutic: draining CSF in idiopathic intracranial hypertension, or administering drugs, including chemotherapy and spinal anaesthesia.

Procedure and risks

The patient typically lies on their side in a flexed position (or sits leaning forward) to open the spaces between the vertebrae. Under local anaesthetic and sterile technique, a spinal needle is advanced until CSF flows, the opening pressure is measured with a manometer, and samples are collected in sterile tubes. The most common complication is a post-dural-puncture headache, caused by continued leakage of CSF; other risks include local bleeding, infection, and — rarely and mainly in patients with raised intracranial pressure from a mass lesion — brain herniation, which is why imaging or fundoscopy is considered first when such lesions are suspected. The procedure was introduced by Heinrich Quincke in 1891.
