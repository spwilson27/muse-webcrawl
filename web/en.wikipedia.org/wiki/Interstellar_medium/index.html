Interstellar medium

Interstellar medium
Composition~99% gas (mostly hydrogen and helium), ~1% dust by mass
Temperature range~10 K (cold clouds) to 106 K (hot ionised medium)
Share of galaxy massRoughly 10–15% of the Milky Way's baryonic disc mass

In astronomy, the interstellar medium (ISM) is the matter and radiation that exist in the space between the star systems in a galaxy. This matter includes gas in ionic, atomic and molecular form, as well as dust and cosmic rays; it fills interstellar space and blends smoothly into the surrounding intergalactic space. The ISM plays a central role in astrophysics: it is the reservoir from which stars form and into which stars return material through winds and supernovae.

Phases

The ISM exists in several thermal phases in approximate pressure equilibrium: cold, dense molecular clouds (tens of kelvin, the sites of star formation); cold and warm neutral atomic hydrogen, observed through the 21-cm line; warm ionised gas around hot stars (H II regions); and a hot, tenuous ionised medium (around a million kelvin) created by supernova shocks. Interstellar dust — silicate and carbonaceous grains — absorbs and reddens starlight, re-radiating the energy in the infrared.

Observation and effects

The ISM is studied across the electromagnetic spectrum and through interstellar absorption lines, extinction and polarisation of starlight. Locally, the Sun sits inside the low-density Local Bubble and the Local Interstellar Cloud. Interstellar scintillation and dispersion of pulsar signals also probe its structure, and spacecraft such as Voyager 1 have crossed the heliopause to sample the very local ISM directly.
