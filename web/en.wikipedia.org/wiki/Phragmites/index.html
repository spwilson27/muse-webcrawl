Phragmites is a genus of large perennial wetland grasses found in temperate and tropical regions worldwide. The best known species is Phragmites australis, the common reed, one of the most widely distributed flowering plants on Earth, growing in marshes, lake margins, riverbanks and estuaries on every continent except Antarctica.

Description and ecology

The common reed grows 2–6 metres tall, with hollow stems, long flat leaves and large feathery purple-brown flower plumes that turn silvery as the seeds ripen. It spreads vigorously by rhizomes and by runners (stolons), forming dense reedbeds that shelter birds such as the Eurasian reed warbler and bittern, filter water and protect banks from erosion. Reedbeds are an important habitat and are used in constructed wetlands for wastewater treatment.

Invasiveness

In North America an introduced Eurasian haplotype of P. australis has become highly invasive, forming near-monocultures in tidal and freshwater marshes and displacing native plants such as cattails. The native North American subspecies, P. australis subsp. americanus, is comparatively non-aggressive and is itself being crowded out. Management by herbicide, mowing, burning and flooding is a major wetland-conservation activity.

Uses

Reeds have been used by humans for millennia: for thatching roofs, fencing, mats and baskets, as a source of cellulose, and for musical instruments, including reed pipes and the reeds of some folk flutes. Young shoots and seeds are edible, and the plant has a long history in traditional medicine.
