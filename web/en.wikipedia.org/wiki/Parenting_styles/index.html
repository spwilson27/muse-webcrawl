Parenting styles

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A parenting style is a pattern of behaviours, attitudes, and approaches that a parent uses when interacting with and raising their child. The study of parenting styles is based on the idea that certain methods affect children's development and outcomes across domains including social skills, academic performance, and mental health.

Psychologist Diana Baumrind's research in the 1960s identified three styles, later reorganised by Eleanor Maccoby and John Martin (1983) into four styles based on two dimensions: demandingness (control) and responsiveness (warmth).

The four styles

StyleDemandingnessResponsivenessCharacteristics
AuthoritativeHighHighSets clear rules and expectations while being warm, responsive, and willing to explain decisions; encourages independence.
AuthoritarianHighLowStrict obedience expected, rules enforced with discipline, little explanation or warmth.
PermissiveLowHighWarm and accepting but few rules or limits; children regulate their own behaviour.
Neglectful (uninvolved)LowLowLittle control and little warmth; minimal involvement in the child's life.

Outcomes

Research in many Western samples associates authoritative parenting with children who are more socially competent, self-reliant and academically successful. Authoritarian parenting has been associated with lower self-esteem and poorer social skills, permissive parenting with impulsivity and difficulty with self-control, and neglectful parenting with the poorest outcomes overall. These findings are correlational, vary across cultures and contexts, and are moderated by child temperament and socioeconomic circumstances.

Criticism and cultural variation

Scholars such as Ruth Chao have argued that Baumrind's categories, developed largely on white middle-class American families, may misdescribe parenting in other cultures — for example, the Chinese concept of "training" combines control with devotion in ways that do not map neatly onto "authoritarian". Parenting styles also interact with neighbourhood, parental stress and children's own behaviour (bidirectional effects).
