Folk Catholicism

Folk Catholicism is any of the various regional expressions of Roman Catholicism shaped by local culture, pre-Christian traditions and indigenous or diaspora religions. Folk Catholic practices — devotion to local saints and apparitions of the Virgin Mary, fiestas and processions, pilgrimages, home altars, healing rituals and confraternities — vary enormously between communities and may exist alongside, or in tension with, the official liturgy and doctrine of the Church.

Forms

In Latin America, centuries of evangelisation blended Catholic practice with indigenous and African traditions, producing such phenomena as the cult of Our Lady of Guadalupe in Mexico, the Day of the Dead (Día de los Muertos), Afro-Brazilian Candomblé's correspondence between orixás and saints, and devotion to folk saints not canonised by Rome, such as Santa Muerte in Mexico, Jesús Malverde, and Argentina's Gauchito Gil and Difunta Correa. In the Philippines, folk Catholicism produced the Aglipayan-influenced devotions, Santo Niño fiestas (Sinulog), the Black Nazarene procession in Manila and Passion plays. In Europe, examples include local pilgrimage shrines, calendar customs and saints' cults; in Ireland and elsewhere, folk Catholic practice also appears in some New Age and neopagan contexts.

Attitudes of the Church

Church authorities have variously tolerated, absorbed ("inculturated") or condemned folk practices. The Second Vatican Council encouraged legitimate local adaptation of the liturgy, while bishops have warned against superstition and against cults judged incompatible with Catholic faith, notably Santa Muerte, condemned by the Vatican in 2013. Anthropologists and theologians increasingly study folk Catholicism as a genuine lived religion rather than a corruption of an official norm.
