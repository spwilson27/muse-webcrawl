Lysozyme

Lysozyme
Also called: Muramidase
EC number: 3.2.1.17
Discovered: 1922, Alexander Fleming
Found in: Tears, saliva, mucus, egg white

Lysozyme (EC 3.2.1.17) is an antimicrobial enzyme that damages bacterial cell walls by catalyzing the hydrolysis of the β-(1,4) linkages between N-acetylmuramic acid and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine in peptidoglycan. It is part of the innate immune system and is abundant in secretions such as tears, saliva, human milk, and mucus, as well as in egg white.

History

Lysozyme was discovered in 1922 by Alexander Fleming, who noticed that nasal mucus dissolved colonies of bacteria — the observation that led him, six years later, to penicillin. Hen egg-white lysozyme was the first enzyme to have its structure solved by X-ray crystallography, by David Phillips in 1965, and the first for which a detailed catalytic mechanism was proposed.

Function

By cleaving peptidoglycan, lysozyme is especially effective against Gram-positive bacteria, whose cell walls are exposed; against Gram-negative bacteria it acts together with other factors that breach the outer membrane. In phagocytes it contributes to killing engulfed microbes.

Uses

Lysozyme is used as a food preservative (E1105), notably in cheese and wine, and in some pharmaceutical and diagnostic applications. Recombinant human lysozyme has been produced in transgenic goats and rice for research into therapeutic uses.
