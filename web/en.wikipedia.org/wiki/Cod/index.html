Cod

Cod (pl.: cod) is the common name for the demersal fish genus Gadus, belonging to the family Gadidae. Cod is also used as part of the common name for a number of other fish species, and one species that belongs to genus Gadus is commonly not called cod (Alaska pollock, Gadus chalcogrammus). The two most commercially important true cods are the Atlantic cod (Gadus morhua), living in the colder waters and deeper sea regions of the North Atlantic, and the Pacific cod (Gadus macrocephalus) of the North Pacific.

Characteristics

Species in the genus Gadus have three rounded dorsal fins and two anal fins, a prominent barbel on the chin, small scales, and a pale lateral line. They are bottom-feeding (demersal) predators of molluscs, crustaceans, and smaller fish, and form large spawning aggregations in winter and early spring, when a single large female may release several million eggs.

Fisheries and cuisine

Cod has been an important economic commodity for over a thousand years: dried and salted cod (stockfish and klippfisk) sustained Viking trade and later the economies of Iceland, Norway, and Newfoundland, whose Grand Banks fishery was among the richest in the world. Cod flesh is mild, white, and flaky, popular in dishes such as fish and chips and Portuguese bacalhau; cod liver is processed into cod liver oil, a source of vitamins A and D.

Conservation

Decades of overfishing caused the dramatic collapse of the northern cod stock off Newfoundland in 1992, prompting a moratorium that threw tens of thousands out of work, and severe depletion in the North Sea and Baltic. Several cod populations remain listed as vulnerable, and recovery has been slow and uneven despite quotas and closed areas. Disputes over cod fishing rights also caused the Cod Wars between the United Kingdom and Iceland.
