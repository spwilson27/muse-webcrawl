The elixir of life, also known as the elixir of immortality, is a potion in mythology and alchemy that supposedly grants the drinker eternal life and/or eternal youth. It was also said to cure all diseases. Alchemists in various ages and cultures sought the means of formulating the elixir, alongside the philosopher's stone, which could transmute base metals into gold.

History by region

China

In ancient China, emperors and nobles sought elixirs of immortality, often based on cinnabar, mercury, gold and jade — many of them poisonous. The Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor, famously sent expeditions in search of the elixir and is believed to have died from mercury poisoning in pills intended to make him immortal. External alchemy (waidan) later gave way to internal alchemy (neidan), which reinterpreted the elixir as something refined within the practitioner's own body.

India

Indian tradition speaks of amrita, the nectar of immortality churned from the ocean of milk by gods and demons, and of soma, the sacred drink of the Vedas. Ayurvedic rasayana practices sought rejuvenation through mineral and herbal preparations, including mercury-based compounds.

Europe

European alchemists linked the elixir with the philosopher's stone: the stone, dissolved or prepared as a tincture, was the universal medicine. Figures such as Nicolas Flamel and the Count of St. Germain acquired legends of extraordinary longevity. During the 16th and 17th centuries the search shaded into early chemistry and medicine, with Paracelsus emphasising mineral remedies.

In fiction and modern culture

The elixir appears throughout literature and popular culture — from the fountain of youth legends associated with Ponce de León to modern fantasy novels and video games — as a symbol of the human desire to escape mortality. Contemporary "longevity" research is sometimes compared, journalistically, to the ancient quest.
