The 26 cantons are the federal states of the Swiss Confederation.

The 26 cantons of Switzerland are the federal states of the Swiss Confederation. Each canton has its own constitution, legislature, government and courts, and retains all powers not delegated to the federal level. The cantons range in population from about 16,000 (Appenzell Innerrhoden) to more than 1.5 million (Zurich), and in area from 37 km² (Basel-Stadt) to over 7,100 km² (the Grisons).

History

The confederation began in 1291 with the alliance of the three forest cantons — Uri, Schwyz and Unterwalden — and grew by stages to thirteen cantons by 1513. After the Helvetic Republic (1798–1803), Napoleon's Act of Mediation raised the number to nineteen; the Congress of Vienna settlement of 1815 set it at twenty-two. The former half-cantons each carry half a vote in the Council of States, the upper house of the federal parliament, though the distinction has little practical significance today. The newest canton, Jura, separated from Bern in 1979 after a series of plebiscites.

List

The cantons are: Aargau, Appenzell Ausserrhoden, Appenzell Innerrhoden, Basel-Landschaft, Basel-Stadt, Bern, Fribourg, Geneva, Glarus, Grisons, Jura, Lucerne, Neuchâtel, Nidwalden, Obwalden, Schaffhausen, Schwyz, Solothurn, St. Gallen, Thurgau, Ticino, Uri, Valais, Vaud, Zug and Zurich. Four official languages are represented among them: German (17 cantons), French (4), Italian (1, Ticino), and Romansh alongside German and Italian in the trilingual Grisons; Bern, Fribourg and Valais are bilingual.

Government

Cantonal governments are typically collegial executives of five or seven members elected directly by the people. Several cantons historically practised the Landsgemeinde, an open-air assembly of all voters; it survives today only in Appenzell Innerrhoden and Glarus.
