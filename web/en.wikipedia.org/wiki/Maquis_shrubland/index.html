Maquis (French: maquis; Italian: macchia) is a shrubland biome in the Mediterranean region, typically consisting of densely growing evergreen shrubs. It is characterised by plants such as the strawberry tree (Arbutus unedo), tree heath (Erica arborea), mastic (Pistacia lentiscus), wild olive, juniper, myrtle, cistus and aromatic herbs including lavender and rosemary, often forming an almost impenetrable thicket two to four metres high.

Formation

Maquis generally develops on acidic, siliceous soils where the original Mediterranean evergreen forest — dominated by holm oak and cork oak — has been degraded by logging, grazing and repeated fire. On limestone soils the equivalent formation is the more open, lower garrigue. Many maquis species are adapted to fire, resprouting from lignotubers or germinating from heat-stimulated seed, and the formation is highly flammable in the dry summer season. Similar Mediterranean-climate shrublands occur worldwide: chaparral in California, matorral in Chile, fynbos in South Africa and kwongan in Australia.

Ecology and human history

Despite its origin in degraded forest, maquis is rich in biodiversity, supporting endemic plants, reptiles, small mammals and numerous bird species, and its aromatic shrubs have long furnished honey, herbs, charcoal and cork. The word acquired a second meaning during the Second World War, when the dense Corsican and southern French maquis sheltered resistance fighters, who took the name Maquisards — "those who take to the maquis".
