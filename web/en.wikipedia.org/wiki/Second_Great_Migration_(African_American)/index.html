Second Great Migration (African American)

Migrants arriving in Chicago during the Second Great Migration

The Second Great Migration was the mass movement of more than five million African Americans from the rural South of the United States to the North, Midwest and — for the first time in large numbers — the West, lasting roughly from 1941 to 1970. It followed and far exceeded the First Great Migration (1916–1930) and completed the transformation of Black America from an overwhelmingly southern, rural population to a predominantly urban, national one.

Causes

The Second World War created enormous demand for labour in defence industries and shipyards, while Executive Order 8802 (1941) and the Fair Employment Practices Committee nominally barred discrimination in war work. In the South, the mechanisation of cotton agriculture — especially the spread of the mechanical cotton picker after 1944 — destroyed the sharecropping economy, and Jim Crow segregation, racial violence and disfranchisement continued to push Black families out. Northern and western employers, once again short of labour, actively recruited in the South.

Destinations

Established destinations such as Chicago, Detroit, Cleveland, New York and Pittsburgh drew hundreds of thousands, but the war boom also made the West Coast a major new pole: Los Angeles, Oakland, Richmond, San Francisco, Seattle and Portland grew large Black communities almost overnight, drawn by shipbuilding and aircraft industry jobs. Chain migration along family and hometown networks directed newcomers to particular neighbourhoods, churches and workplaces.

Impact

The migration reshaped American cities and politics. Black voters became a decisive constituency in northern states, underpinning post-war civil rights politics; the migrants' children and churches supplied much of the leadership and mass base of the Civil Rights Movement. At the same time, newcomers met restrictive covenants, redlining, job discrimination and white flight, producing the segregated metropolitan geography that persists in altered form. Culturally, the migration carried southern music, foodways and religion northward and westward, fuelling developments from Chicago blues and Motown to the Black Arts Movement.

End and reversal

The movement slowed after 1970 as deindustrialisation eroded northern manufacturing employment, southern conditions slowly improved after the civil rights victories, and Sun Belt opportunities grew. From the 1970s onward, demographers have noted a "New Great Migration" of Black Americans — many of them the descendants of the earlier migrants — moving back to the South, especially to metropolitan Atlanta, Charlotte, Dallas and Houston.
