Dissociative

Dissociative describes phenomena involving dissociation — a disconnection or lack of integration between a person's thoughts, memories, feelings, actions, or sense of identity. Dissociation exists on a continuum: mild, everyday experiences such as daydreaming or "highway hypnosis" are common and non-pathological, while severe forms, often linked to trauma, constitute the dissociative disorders in psychiatry.

Dissociative disorders

The DSM-5 groups the dissociative disorders together: dissociative identity disorder (formerly multiple personality disorder), characterised by two or more distinct identity states and recurrent amnesia; dissociative amnesia, the inability to recall important autobiographical information beyond ordinary forgetting; and depersonalization-derealization disorder, persistent feelings of detachment from one's own body or mind, or of the world seeming unreal. Dissociative symptoms also occur in post-traumatic stress disorder, which has a recognised dissociative subtype.

Theory and treatment

Dissociation is commonly understood as a defensive response that allows a person to distance themselves from overwhelming experience, especially in childhood trauma; structural dissociation theory describes how personality may divide into apparently normal and emotional parts. The concept originated with Pierre Janet in the late nineteenth century, fell from favour, and revived with renewed attention to trauma in the late twentieth century, amid continuing debate about the role of suggestion and sociocognitive factors in some presentations. Treatment is usually phased psychotherapy focused on safety and stabilisation, trauma processing, and integration; no medication directly treats dissociation itself, though comorbid conditions are treated pharmacologically.
