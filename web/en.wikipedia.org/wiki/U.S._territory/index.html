The territories of the United States are sub-national administrative divisions overseen by the federal government that are not part of any U.S. state. The United States currently administers 14 territories, of which five are permanently inhabited: Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa, and the Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands. The remainder are small uninhabited islands, mostly grouped as the United States Minor Outlying Islands.

Classification

Territories are classified by the Supreme Court's Insular Cases as incorporated or unincorporated, and by Congress as organized (with an organic act establishing a local government) or unorganized. Puerto Rico and the Northern Mariana Islands hold commonwealth status. Residents of most territories are U.S. citizens at birth; American Samoans are U.S. nationals unless they naturalize. Territorial residents cannot vote in presidential elections and are represented in Congress only by non-voting delegates.

History

The United States acquired most of its current territories in the 19th and early 20th centuries: Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines from Spain in 1898 (the Philippines later became independent), the Virgin Islands from Denmark in 1917, and the Northern Marianas after the Second World War. Historically, incorporated organized territories such as the Northwest Territory were the standard pathway to statehood for what is now the continental United States.
