Beginning of pregnancy controversy

Fertilisation and implantation are the two candidate starting points of pregnancy.

The beginning of pregnancy controversy concerns disagreement over when pregnancy begins. In mainstream medical usage, pregnancy is generally dated from implantation — the point at which the blastocyst embeds in the uterine wall — whereas many other traditions and some medical bodies have defined it from fertilisation (conception). The question has consequences for the classification of contraceptives and for abortion law and ethics.

Medical definitions

Bodies such as the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists define pregnancy as beginning at implantation, in part because a large proportion of fertilised eggs never implant or are lost shortly afterward, and because pregnancy cannot be detected before implantation produces the hormone hCG. Gestational age, by contrast, is conventionally counted from the first day of the last menstrual period, often about two weeks before fertilisation.

Points of dispute

Those who hold that pregnancy (or human life deserving protection) begins at fertilisation argue that a genetically distinct organism exists from that point. The definitional dispute affects whether methods that may act after fertilisation — such as some intrauterine devices or emergency contraception — are described as contraceptives or abortifacients; scientific evidence indicates that commonly used emergency contraception acts primarily by preventing ovulation. Surveys of physicians show the controversy is partly professional and partly cultural, and definitions vary between jurisdictions and institutions.
