Air draft

Air draft (or air draught) is the distance from the surface of the water to the highest point on a vessel. It is the vertical clearance a ship requires to pass safely beneath a bridge, overhead power lines, or other fixed structures spanning a waterway. Air draft is the aerial counterpart of a ship's draft, which measures how deeply the hull extends below the waterline.

Air draft determines whether a vessel can safely pass beneath a bridge.

Measurement and variation

A vessel's air draft is not a fixed number. It depends on the ship's loading condition: a fully laden ship sits lower in the water and so presents a smaller air draft, whereas the same ship in ballast rides higher and may be unable to clear a bridge it could pass under when loaded. Air draft also varies with the height of tide, river level, water density, and any list or trim. Sailing vessels and tall ships must consider the height of their masts, and some ships are fitted with hinged or telescoping masts and funnels specifically so they can reduce air draft for bridge transits.

Significance

Bridge clearances dictated by air draft have directly shaped port development. The Bayonne Bridge in the Port of New York and New Jersey, for example, was originally built with about 151 feet (46 m) of clearance; the enlargement of container ships after the 2016 expansion of the Panama Canal required a major project to raise its roadway to 215 feet (about 65.5 m) so that the largest new container ships could reach the port's terminals. Conversely, new bridges are designed with air drafts matched to the largest vessels expected to use a channel, and charts and bridge tables publish the vertical clearance at a defined reference water level, usually mean high water.

Failure to allow for air draft has caused serious accidents, including collisions of ships and cranes with bridges — tall cargo-handling cranes being transported as deck cargo are a recurrent problem — as well as strikes on overhead cables.
