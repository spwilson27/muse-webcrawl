The Giralda (Spanish: La Giralda) is the bell tower of Seville Cathedral in Andalusia, Spain. It was built as the minaret of the city's great Almohad mosque between 1184 and 1198 and, after the Christian reconquest of Seville, was converted into a bell tower, crowned in the sixteenth century by a Renaissance belfry and a great revolving weathervane figure — el Giraldillo — from which the tower takes its name (girar, "to turn").

History

The square brick minaret, modelled on the Koutoubia Mosque in Marrakech, is ascended by a series of ramps — wide enough, tradition says, for a rider on horseback — rather than stairs. After an earthquake destroyed the original gilded spheres in 1356, the tower was adapted as a belfry; between 1558 and 1568 the architect Hernán Ruiz II added the Renaissance upper stages with their belfry, lantern and the 4-metre bronze figure of Faith, mounted as a weathervane in 1568.

Significance

The Giralda, about 104 metres tall, has been Seville's most emblematic landmark for centuries and inspired imitations, including towers in Kansas City and the former Wrigley and Madison Square Garden buildings. Together with the cathedral and the Alcázar it forms a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
