Stanislaw Ulam

Stanisław Marcin Ulam (Polish: [staˈɲiswaf ˈmart͡ɕin ˈulam]; April 13, 1909 – May 13, 1984) was a Polish-American mathematician and nuclear physicist. He participated in the Manhattan Project, originated the Teller–Ulam design of thermonuclear weapons, discovered the concept of the cellular automaton, and invented the Monte Carlo method of computation. He is also credited with ideas that led to nuclear pulse propulsion (Project Orion).

Life and career

Ulam was born in Lwów (then in Austria-Hungary, now Lviv, Ukraine) into a wealthy Polish Jewish family. He studied mathematics at the Lwów Polytechnic under Stefan Banach and became part of the celebrated Lwów School of Mathematics, whose members worked in the Scottish Café and recorded problems in the famous "Scottish Book." He obtained his doctorate in 1933 with work on measure theory.

Invited by John von Neumann, Ulam visited Princeton in the mid-1930s and accepted positions at Harvard and the University of Wisconsin–Madison before joining the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos in 1943. There he worked on the hydrodynamics of implosion for the plutonium bomb. After the war he returned to Los Alamos, where in 1951 he and Edward Teller arrived at the breakthrough configuration — radiation implosion, using the X-rays of a fission primary to compress a physically separate fusion secondary — that made practical hydrogen bombs possible. The first test, Ivy Mike, followed in 1952.

The Monte Carlo method arose in 1946 while Ulam, convalescing from brain surgery, pondered the probabilities of winning the card game Canfield solitaire and realised that statistical sampling on the new electronic computers could solve difficult problems in neutron diffusion and elsewhere; he developed the method with von Neumann and Nicholas Metropolis. With von Neumann he also pioneered the study of cellular automata, including the idea of self-reproducing automata, and in pure mathematics he proved (with Borsuk) the Borsuk–Ulam theorem's milieu of results, devised the Ulam spiral of prime numbers (1963), worked on the Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou problem in nonlinear physics, and contributed to set theory and ergodic theory. His memoir, Adventures of a Mathematician (1976), is a classic of scientific autobiography. He died in Santa Fe in 1984.
