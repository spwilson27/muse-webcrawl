History of Ireland

The history of Ireland stretches from prehistoric settlement and Celtic culture through Christianisation, Viking and Norman invasions, centuries of English and British rule, the Great Famine, the struggle for independence, and the partition of the island in 1921 into the Republic of Ireland and Northern Ireland.

Prehistoric and early Christian Ireland

Mesolithic hunter-gatherers arrived after the last Ice Age, and Neolithic farmers built monumental passage tombs such as Newgrange (c. 3200 BC). Celtic languages and culture took hold in the Iron Age. In the 5th century AD Saint Patrick's mission Christianised the island, whose monasteries became centres of learning that preserved Latin culture and sent missionaries across Europe.

Vikings, Normans and English rule

Viking raiders founded Dublin and other coastal towns from the 9th century. The Anglo-Norman invasion of 1169 began centuries of English involvement; over time English control was extended through the Tudor conquest, the Plantations of Ulster and other provinces, and the Cromwellian and Williamite wars. The Acts of Union 1800 abolished the Irish Parliament and joined Ireland to the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland.

Famine and nationalism

The Great Famine (1845–52), caused by potato blight, killed about one million people and drove another million to emigrate, beginning a long population decline. Nationalist movements — from Daniel O'Connell's campaign for Catholic Emancipation and Repeal to the Fenians and the Home Rule movement — pressed for self-government. The Easter Rising of 1916 and the War of Independence (1919–21) led to the Anglo-Irish Treaty, the Irish Free State in 1922, and a civil war over the treaty.

Modern Ireland

The Free State became the Republic of Ireland in 1949, while six counties of Ulster remained in the UK as Northern Ireland. From the late 1960s, the Troubles in Northern Ireland claimed more than 3,500 lives before the Good Friday Agreement of 1998. The Republic joined the EEC in 1973 and experienced rapid "Celtic Tiger" growth in the 1990s–2000s.
