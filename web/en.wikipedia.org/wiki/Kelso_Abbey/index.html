Kelso Abbey is a ruined Scottish abbey in the town of Kelso in the Scottish Borders, beside the River Tweed. Founded in the 12th century by monks of the Tironensian order invited by King David I, it was one of the great Border abbeys, celebrated for its wealth, its library and the distinctive architecture of its church, whose surviving west tower and transept form an unusual double-transept plan.

History

The community was first established at Selkirk in 1113 — the first Tironensian house in Britain — and moved to Kelso in 1128, closer to David I's royal burgh and castle at Roxburgh. The abbey church was dedicated in 1243. Because of its location near the English border, Kelso suffered repeatedly in the Anglo-Scottish wars: it was attacked and damaged in the 14th century, burned by the Earl of Hertford's forces in 1545 during the Rough Wooing, and formally dissolved in 1559 on the eve of the Scottish Reformation. The last monks were driven out, and much of the stone was later quarried for buildings in the town.

Architecture

The church was built in the Romanesque style with an unusual plan featuring transepts at both the east and west ends. The massive western tower, intended never to be completed as a crossing, dominates the fragment that survives: the west front, part of the north-west transept and a section of the south transept. The cloister was rebuilt in the 1930s as a memorial cloister in the medieval style, incorporating the burial place of the Dukes of Roxburghe.
