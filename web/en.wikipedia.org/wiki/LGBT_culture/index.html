LGBT culture

LGBT culture is a culture shared by lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender and queer people. It is sometimes referred to as queer culture or gay culture, although the latter term may apply more specifically to gay men's culture. LGBT culture encompasses a wide variety of customs, creative works, social institutions and political activities, and varies greatly by geography and by the identities involved; many of its elements — such as Pride parades, drag performance, the rainbow flag and chosen-family structures — developed within marginalised communities and have since entered mainstream awareness.

History

Historians trace recognisable urban queer subcultures to early-modern and 19th-century cities, including the molly houses of London and the balls and bars of Berlin, Paris and New York. In the United States, the harassment of gay bars culminated in the Stonewall riots of June 1969 in New York City, a watershed that launched the modern gay liberation movement and is commemorated annually by Pride marches. The AIDS crisis of the 1980s and 1990s reshaped the culture profoundly, spurring activist groups such as ACT UP, new forms of community care and memorial art such as the NAMES Project AIDS Memorial Quilt.

Elements

  • Pride events — parades, marches and festivals held worldwide, usually in June.
  • Symbols — the rainbow flag (designed by Gilbert Baker in 1978), the pink triangle (reclaimed from its Nazi-era use), and a growing family of identity flags.
  • Drag — performance traditions including drag queens and kings, rooted in venues such as Harlem's ball culture, which also produced the categories and houses depicted in the documentary Paris Is Burning.
  • Media and arts — literature, film, theatre and music by and for LGBT audiences, from early physique magazines to contemporary mainstream television.
  • Spaces — gay villages or "gayborhoods", bars, bookstores and community centres that historically offered safety and social infrastructure.

Language

LGBT communities have developed distinctive slang, including Polari in Britain and lexicons drawn from ball culture (such as "shade" and "reading") that have entered general usage. Terminology for identities continues to evolve, and the acronym itself has expanded over time, with many variants in use.
