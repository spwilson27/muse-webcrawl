Cancer Alley

Cancer Alley is the regional nickname given to an 85-mile (137 km) stretch of land along the Mississippi River between Baton Rouge and New Orleans in Louisiana, which contains over 200 petrochemical plants and refineries. The region accounts for a substantial share of the United States' petrochemical production. Environmental activists coined the name because residents in the predominantly African American communities along the corridor are exposed to elevated levels of air pollution and, according to US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) analyses, face some of the highest estimated cancer risks from air toxics in the nation.

History

Industrial development along the river began in the early 20th century, attracted by deepwater shipping, abundant oil and natural gas, fresh water, and tax incentives. Many plants were built on former plantation land, adjacent to historically Black "fenceline" communities whose residents were descendants of enslaved people. In the 1980s and 1990s, residents and environmental lawyers began documenting elevated cancer and respiratory illness rates, leading to the nickname "Cancer Alley" and a long-running environmental justice campaign centred on towns such as Reserve, Norco, and St. Gabriel.

Health studies and disputes

The EPA's National Air Toxics Assessment has repeatedly identified census tracts in the corridor with the highest modeled cancer risk in the United States, driven by emissions of chemicals such as chloroprene and ethylene oxide. Industry groups and Louisiana state health studies have disputed claims of statistically elevated cancer incidence, noting that state cancer registry data does not always show higher overall rates than elsewhere in Louisiana; community advocates counter that registry data masks local hot spots and cumulative exposure burdens. In 2022–2023, the EPA opened civil rights investigations into Louisiana's permitting practices under Title VI of the Civil Rights Act, and federal regulators ordered emission reductions at a neoprene plant in St. John the Baptist Parish.

Environmental justice

Cancer Alley is frequently cited as a paradigmatic case of environmental racism and has drawn international attention, including from United Nations human rights experts and during the Biden administration's Justice40 initiative, which directed federal investment toward disadvantaged communities burdened by pollution.
