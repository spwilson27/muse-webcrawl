Reversible reaction

A reversible reaction is a chemical reaction in which the products, as soon as they are formed, react to produce the original reactants. In other words, the reaction can proceed in both the forward and the reverse directions. Such reactions are commonly written with a double-headed arrow:

aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD

Many reactions are reversible to some degree, although in practice a reaction may be effectively irreversible if one product escapes as a gas, precipitates out of solution, or if the equilibrium lies overwhelmingly on one side.

Dynamic equilibrium

As a reversible reaction proceeds in a closed system, the rates of the forward and reverse reactions eventually become equal. The system is then in chemical equilibrium: the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant over time, even though both reactions continue to occur. This state is called dynamic equilibrium. The position of equilibrium is described by the equilibrium constant K, and can be shifted by changes in concentration, pressure, or temperature, in accordance with Le Châtelier's principle.

Examples

The Haber process for ammonia synthesis, N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3, is an industrially crucial reversible reaction. The synthesis of esters from carboxylic acids and alcohols, and the weak ionisation of acetic acid in water, are also textbook examples. In biochemistry, many enzyme-catalysed steps operate near equilibrium and are readily reversible.

History

The concept grew out of early nineteenth-century observations, notably by Claude Louis Berthollet, who noticed that reactions in Egyptian salt lakes could run in either direction depending on conditions, challenging the idea that reactions always go to completion.
