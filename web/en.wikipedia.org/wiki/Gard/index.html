Gard

Gard (French pronunciation: [ɡaʁ]; Occitan: Gard) is a department in southern France, located in the region of Occitanie. It is named after the river Gardon (also called the Gard), a tributary of the Rhône. Its prefecture is Nîmes, and its population is roughly 760,000. INSEE and postal code 30 identify the department.

Geography

Gard stretches from the Cévennes mountains in the northwest — including Mont Aigoual, one of the wettest places in metropolitan France — through the garrigue limestone hills and the Nîmes plain, to a short Mediterranean coastline in the south around Le Grau-du-Roi and Aigues-Mortes, on the edge of the Camargue. The Rhône forms its eastern border with Provence. The climate is Mediterranean, with hot dry summers and episodes of violent autumn rainfall (épisodes cévenols) that can cause severe flash flooding.

History

The area is rich in Roman remains: Nîmes (ancient Nemausus) preserves an amphitheatre and the Maison Carrée temple, and the Pont du Gard — the great aqueduct bridge near Remoulins — is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The department was created in 1790 from part of the province of Languedoc. Gard was a stronghold of Protestantism in France: the Cévennes saw the Camisard revolt of 1702–1704 after the revocation of the Edict of Nantes, and the region's history is told at the Musée du Désert. In the 19th century, denim cloth ("serge de Nîmes") from the department gave its name to the fabric.

Economy and culture

Agriculture (wine — including Costières de Nîmes and Tavel appellations — fruit, olives and Camargue rice and salt), tourism, and industry around Nîmes, Alès and the Rhône valley (including the Marcoule nuclear site in the northeast) form the economy. The ferias of Nîmes, with bullfighting in the Roman arena, are major cultural events. Uzès, Aigues-Mortes and the medieval walled towns of the interior draw visitors, as do the Cévennes National Park, partly within the department.
