Endothermic reaction

In thermochemistry, an endothermic process is any thermodynamic process with an increase in the enthalpy H (or internal energy U) of the system. In such a process, a closed system usually absorbs thermal energy from its surroundings, which is heat transfer into the system. Thus, an endothermic reaction generally leads to a decrease in the temperature of the surroundings.

In an endothermic reaction the products have higher enthalpy than the reactants; ΔH is positive.

Examples

Examples include photosynthesis, the melting of ice, evaporation of liquids, cracking of alkanes, and the thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate. The classic classroom demonstration is the reaction between barium hydroxide octahydrate and ammonium chloride, which absorbs so much heat that it can freeze a wet surface beneath the flask.

Contrast with exothermic processes

The opposite of an endothermic process is an exothermic process, one that releases energy, usually in the form of heat. The terms derive from the Greek endo- ("within") and exo- ("outside") combined with thermē ("heat").
