Contour line

A contour line (also isoline, isopleth, or isarithm) of a function of two variables is a curve along which the function has a constant value, so that the curve joins points of equal value. In cartography, a contour line joins points of equal elevation (height) above a given level, such as mean sea level. A map showing such lines — a contour or topographic map — reveals the shape of the terrain: closely spaced contours indicate steep slopes, widely spaced contours gentle ones.

Terminology and types

Specialized isolines include isobars (equal pressure), isotherms (equal temperature), isohyets (equal rainfall), isobaths (equal water depth) and isogonic lines (equal magnetic declination). On topographic maps, heavier index contours are labelled with their elevation, intermediate contours are thinner, and depressed areas may be marked with hachured contours whose ticks point downhill.

Rules and interpretation

Contour lines never cross one another (except in rare cases such as an overhanging cliff), form closed curves or terminate at the map edge, and make V-shapes pointing upstream when crossing valleys. The vertical distance between adjacent contours is the contour interval, chosen according to the map scale and terrain. From contour patterns a reader can identify hills, ridges, saddles, valleys, cliffs and slopes, and can construct elevation profiles.

History

Lines of equal value were first used for magnetic declination by Edmond Halley (1701) and for water depth in Dutch charts of the 1730s–40s; contour lines for land elevation appeared in the later 18th century and became standard on national topographic surveys in the 19th. Today contours are generated automatically from digital elevation models by GIS software.

Mathematics

Mathematically, a contour line is a level set of a scalar function f(x, y): the set of points where f(x, y) = c. The gradient of the function is everywhere perpendicular to its contour lines, a fact used in optimization and physics (equipotential lines, streamline analysis).
