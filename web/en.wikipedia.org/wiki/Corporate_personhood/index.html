Corporate personhood

Corporate personhood is the legal doctrine by which a corporation is recognised as a legal person distinct from its shareholders, directors and employees, capable of holding rights and duties of its own. As legal persons, corporations can own property, enter contracts, incur debt, sue and be sued, and — depending on the jurisdiction — claim certain constitutional or human rights protections. The doctrine underlies the modern business corporation, whose separate personality and limited liability allow assets to be pooled for large enterprises while insulating investors' personal wealth from most corporate debts.

Legal foundations

The separate personality of the corporation was definitively affirmed in English law by the House of Lords in Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897], which held that a duly incorporated company is a person distinct from its incorporators even when one individual effectively controls it. Courts may nevertheless "pierce the corporate veil" in limited circumstances, such as fraud or undercapitalisation, to hold shareholders personally liable.

United States

In the United States, corporations were treated as "persons" under the Fourteenth Amendment following the Supreme Court's 1886 decision in Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad, in which the court reporter's headnote recorded the justices' view that the amendment applied to corporations. Subsequent jurisprudence extended various rights to corporations: Fourth Amendment protection against unreasonable searches, Fifth Amendment protection against double jeopardy and takings, and First Amendment speech rights. The latter became especially prominent in Citizens United v. FEC (2010), which held that the government may not suppress independent political expenditures by corporations and unions, and Burwell v. Hobby Lobby Stores (2014), which recognised religious-exercise claims by closely held corporations under federal statute. Corporations do not possess all personal rights — they have no Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination, for example, and cannot vote.

Other jurisdictions

Most legal systems recognise corporate personality but differ on rights. In Europe, companies can invoke certain guarantees of the European Convention on Human Rights, including fair trial, property and freedom of expression protections. In India, corporations are not "citizens" for some constitutional purposes but may challenge state action through their shareholders and enjoy fundamental rights in other respects.

Criticism and debate

Critics argue that extending constitutional rights — particularly speech and religious rights — to corporations distorts democratic politics and lets artificial entities claim protections meant for human beings, inspiring movements to amend constitutions to specify that rights belong to natural persons. Defenders respond that corporations are associations of people whose collective rights would be meaningless if the entity itself could not assert them, and that personhood is a practical legal technique rather than a claim that corporations are human.
