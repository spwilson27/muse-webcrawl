Karl Dönitz (16 September 1891 – 24 December 1980) was a German naval commander who directed the U-boat campaign against Allied shipping during the Second World War, became Commander-in-Chief of the Kriegsmarine in 1943, and briefly succeeded Adolf Hitler as head of state of Nazi Germany in May 1945.

Career

Dönitz joined the Imperial German Navy in 1910 and commanded UB-68 in the First World War, being captured after his boat was sunk in the Mediterranean in 1918. Between the wars he championed the rebuilt U-boat arm, developing the "wolfpack" tactic of group attacks on convoys. As Befehlshaber der U-Boote (Commander of U-boats) from 1939, he directed the Battle of the Atlantic; his boats sank millions of tons of Allied shipping before Allied air cover, radar and code-breaking turned the tide in 1943 ("Black May"). He succeeded Erich Raeder as Commander-in-Chief of the navy in January 1943.

Succession and trial

In his political testament Hitler named Dönitz his successor as Reichspräsident. From Flensburg, Dönitz headed a short-lived government that sought partial surrenders to the Western Allies before authorising the unconditional surrender signed on 7–8 May 1945; his government was dissolved and its members arrested on 23 May.

At the Nuremberg trials Dönitz was convicted of crimes against peace and war crimes and sentenced to ten years' imprisonment, served at Spandau Prison. He later wrote memoirs, Memoirs: Ten Years and Twenty Days, and lived quietly until his death in 1980.
