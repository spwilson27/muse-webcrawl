Cupping therapy

TraditionsTraditional Chinese medicine; Middle Eastern hijama; Greek and European folk medicine
FormsDry cupping, wet cupping, fire cupping, suction cupping

Cupping therapy is a form of alternative medicine in which a therapist places cups on the skin and creates a local suction, traditionally by heating the air inside the cup (fire cupping) or by means of a mechanical pump. The suction draws the skin upward into the cup, leaving characteristic circular marks. Wet cupping (in Islamic tradition, hijama) adds small incisions so that blood is drawn out. Mainstream medical bodies regard cupping as a pseudoscientific practice without convincing evidence of benefit beyond placebo effects.

History

Cupping is one of the oldest recorded medical practices: it appears in the Egyptian Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BC), was described by Hippocrates, and was practised in ancient China, Greece and Rome, usually in the context of humoral medicine and bloodletting. It remained common in European and American medicine into the 19th century before falling out of use, while persisting in traditional Chinese medicine and in Middle Eastern practice, where hijama is recommended in Islamic tradition and typically performed on specified days of the lunar month.

Practice

In dry cupping, cups of glass, bamboo, silicone or plastic are applied to the back, shoulders or limbs for several minutes; oils may allow the cups to be slid over the skin ("massage cupping"). Practitioners claim the treatment improves circulation, relieves pain and removes "stagnation" or toxins. The practice gained wide public attention at the 2016 Olympic Games, when swimmers including Michael Phelps competed with prominent cupping marks.

Evidence and safety

Systematic reviews have found low-quality, small studies suggesting possible short-term pain relief, but the overall evidence is insufficient to support the claims made for cupping for any condition. Adverse effects include burns from fire cupping, bruising, skin infection, scarring and, with wet cupping, blood-borne infection and anaemia from repeated blood loss. Regulators and science writers commonly list cupping among treatments whose effects are attributable to placebo and natural recovery.
