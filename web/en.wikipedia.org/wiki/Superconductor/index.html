Superconductivity

Superconductivity is a set of physical properties observed in certain materials where electrical resistance vanishes and magnetic flux fields are expelled from the material below a characteristic critical temperature. A material exhibiting these properties is called a superconductor. The phenomenon was discovered by Dutch physicist Heike Kamerlingh Onnes in 1911 in solid mercury cooled to about 4.2 kelvin.

Properties

In addition to zero resistance, superconductors exhibit the Meissner effect, the active expulsion of magnetic fields during the transition to the superconducting state, which allows a magnet to levitate above a superconductor. Conventional superconductivity is explained by the BCS theory, formulated in 1957, in which electrons form bound Cooper pairs that condense into a coherent quantum state.

High-temperature superconductors

In 1986, ceramic cuprate materials were found to superconduct at temperatures above 77 kelvin, the boiling point of liquid nitrogen; these high-temperature superconductors remain incompletely explained by theory.

Applications

Superconductors are used in powerful electromagnets for MRI scanners and particle accelerators, in maglev trains, in sensitive magnetometers called SQUIDs, and in prototype power cables and quantum computing circuits.
