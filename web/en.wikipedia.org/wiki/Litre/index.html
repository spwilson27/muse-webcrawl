Litre

The litre (also spelled liter in American English; SI symbol L or l) is a metric unit of volume. It is not an SI unit but is accepted for use with the SI. One litre is exactly equal to one cubic decimetre: 1 L = 1 dm³ = 1,000 cm³ = 0.001 m³. A litre of water has a mass of almost exactly one kilogram under standard conditions.

History

The litre was introduced in France in 1795 as one of the new "republican units of measurement", defined as one cubic decimetre. The name derives from the older French unit litron, ultimately from Latin and Greek words for a pound.

In 1901 the litre was redefined as the volume occupied by 1 kg of pure water at its maximum density, which differed from the cubic decimetre by about 28 parts per million. The 1964 CGPM abrogated that definition and restored the litre as exactly one cubic decimetre, while noting that the word is a "special name" for the unit.

Symbol

Because the lowercase letter l is easily confused with the numeral 1 or the capital letter I, the CGPM in 1979 also allowed the capital letter L as a symbol for the litre — an exception to the usual SI rule that unit symbols named after people are capitalised. Capital L is standard in the United States, Canada and Australia; lowercase l predominates in much of Europe.

Subdivisions and usage

  • Millilitre (mL) — 0.001 L = 1 cm³; used for drinks, medicine doses and laboratory work.
  • Centilitre (cL) — 0.01 L; common on European beverage labels.
  • Decilitre (dL) — 0.1 L; used in cooking in some countries.
  • Hectolitre (hL) — 100 L; used in brewing and agriculture.
  • Kilolitre (kL) — 1,000 L = 1 m³; used for water supply volumes.

The litre is the everyday unit of volume in most of the world, used for beverages, fuel (fuel consumption is often expressed in litres per 100 km), engine displacement (a "2.0-litre engine"), and backpack and container capacities. In the United States the litre is common for soft drinks (the 2-litre bottle) while most other consumer volumes are in US customary units.
