Audio engineer

An audio engineer (also known as a sound engineer or recording engineer) helps to produce a recording or a live performance by using technology and technical skill to balance and adjust sound sources. The role combines knowledge of acoustics, electronics and signal processing with an ear for music and performance.

Roles

The term covers several specialisations. A recording engineer operates the console and recording medium during studio sessions. A mixing engineer combines multitrack recordings into a finished stereo or surround mix. A mastering engineer prepares the final mix for distribution. In live sound, a front-of-house (FOH) engineer mixes what the audience hears, while a monitor engineer mixes what performers hear on stage. Broadcast, film and game audio each employ engineers for dialogue, music and effects capture and reproduction.

Work and training

Engineers select and place microphones, set levels, apply equalization, compression and effects, and manage playback and recording systems, increasingly centred on digital audio workstations. Training routes include degree and diploma programmes in audio engineering and music technology, apprenticeships in studios, and professional bodies such as the Audio Engineering Society (AES), founded in 1948.

History

The profession emerged with electrical recording in the 1920s and grew with multitrack tape recording in the 1950s and 1960s, when engineers such as Tom Dowd and the staff of Abbey Road Studios became integral creative collaborators. Notable engineer-producers include George Martin, Rudy Van Gelder, Alan Parsons and Sylvia Massy.
