Burletta

A burletta (from the Italian, meaning "little joke", sometimes burla or burlettina) is a brief comic opera, largely a phenomenon of the 18th and early 19th centuries. In Italy and Germany it denoted a comic intermezzo, while in England the term acquired a specific legal significance connected with the licensing of theatres.

In England

Under the Licensing Act 1737, only the patent theatres in London (Drury Lane and Covent Garden, and later the Haymarket for opera) were permitted to perform "regular" spoken drama. The "minor" theatres evaded the restriction by presenting burlettas: short pieces — typically in three acts, rhymed, and accompanied by music — which legally counted as musical entertainments rather than tragedies or comedies. The distinction depended on the proportion of music and on the piece being performed with songs, choruses, and recitative throughout; magistrates sometimes ruled on whether a work was a true burletta or an illegal play in disguise.

Notable English burlettas include Kane O'Hara's Midas (1760), an early and influential example of the genre. The legal dodge shaped the development of English comic opera, melodrama, and pantomime. The distinction lost importance after the Theatres Act 1843 abolished the patent monopoly, though the term continued to be used loosely for short comic operatic pieces.
