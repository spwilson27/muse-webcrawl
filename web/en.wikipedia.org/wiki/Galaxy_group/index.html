A galaxy group or group of galaxies is an aggregation of galaxies comprising about 50 or fewer gravitationally bound members, each at least as luminous as the Milky Way. Collections of galaxies larger than groups that do not meet the conditions for galaxy clusters are called galaxy groups as well, though the terminology overlaps. Galaxy groups are the most common structures of galaxies in the universe, comprising at least 50% of the galaxies in the local universe.

Galaxy groups have a range of masses from about 1013 solar masses, similar to the mass of a single large galaxy. Their diameters are typically 1 to 2 megaparsecs. The Local Group, which contains the Milky Way and the Andromeda Galaxy, is an example.

Characteristics

Groups are distinguished from galaxy clusters by their smaller size, lower mass, and lower velocity dispersion. Many groups are dominated by one or two large spiral or elliptical galaxies surrounded by dwarf satellite galaxies. Because groups are so common, they are thought to represent the environment in which most galaxies form and evolve.

Compact groups

A compact group is a sub-type consisting of a small number of galaxies separated by small distances relative to their sizes. Stephan's Quintet and the Hickson Compact Groups are well-known examples. Compact groups show strong galaxy interactions and mergers, and some astronomers have questioned whether they are truly distinct systems or chance alignments within looser groups.

Proto-groups

At high redshift, astronomers observe protoclusters and proto-groups, overdense regions in the early universe that will eventually collapse into today's groups and clusters under gravity as cosmic structure grows hierarchically.
