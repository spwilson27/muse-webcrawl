Theobald Wolfe Tone

Theobald Wolfe Tone (20 June 1763 – 19 November 1798), posthumously known as Wolfe Tone, was a leading Irish revolutionary figure and one of the founding members of the United Irishmen. He is regarded as the father of Irish republicanism.

Life

Born in Dublin to a Protestant family, Tone studied law at Trinity College Dublin and the Middle Temple. Influenced by the American and French Revolutions, he argued in pamphlets — notably his Argument on behalf of the Catholics of Ireland (1791) — for an alliance of Irish Protestants, Catholics and Dissenters to break the connection with England. In October 1791 he helped found the Society of United Irishmen in Belfast. Driven into exile in 1795, he travelled to America and then to France, where he persuaded the revolutionary government — including Lazare Carnot — to send expeditions to Ireland. A large French fleet carrying 15,000 men under General Hoche reached Bantry Bay in December 1796 but was scattered by storms. In 1798, the year of the United Irish rising, Tone accompanied a smaller French force under General Hardy that was intercepted off County Donegal; Tone was captured aboard the frigate Hoche, recognised, tried by court-martial in Dublin and sentenced to hang. Denied a soldier's death by firing squad, he cut his own throat in prison and died a week later, on 19 November 1798.

Legacy

Tone's memoirs and journals, published by his widow, became scripture for later Irish nationalism. He is honoured annually at his grave in Bodenstown, County Kildare, and his name is borne by streets, monuments and republican organisations, including the Wolfe Tones GAA clubs and the Wolfe Tone Societies.
