The Shire is a fictional region in J. R. R. Tolkien's Middle-earth, the homeland of the Hobbits, appearing in The Hobbit, The Lord of the Rings and related writings. It is an inland, agricultural country in Eriador, bounded by the River Baranduin (Brandywine) to the east, and divided into four Farthings. Tolkien modelled its landscape and society on the rural England of his childhood, describing it as a well-ordered, peaceful land of small farmers, largely ignored by the great powers of Middle-earth.

Description

The Shire measures roughly 40 leagues (about 120 miles) from the Far Downs in the west to the Brandywine Bridge in the east. Hobbits live in villages of houses and hobbit-holes (smials) dug into hillsides, the grandest being Bag End at Hobbiton, home of Bilbo and Frodo Baggins. The country has no government to speak of beyond the hereditary Thain, the Mayor of Michel Delving, a small body of Shirriffs, and a postal service; its inhabitants farm, brew ale, smoke pipe-weed, and are famously fond of parties and regular meals.

History in the legendarium

The Shire was founded in the year 1601 of the Third Age, when the brothers Marcho and Blanco led hobbits across the Brandywine into land granted by King Argeleb II of Arthedain. Apart from the Battle of Greenfields (2747), in which Bandobras "Bullroarer" Took repelled a goblin invasion, its history was quiet until the War of the Ring. In its closing phase, the Shire was occupied and industrialised by Saruman ("Sharkey") and his ruffians; Merry, Pippin, Sam and Frodo's return led to the Battle of Bywater and the Scouring of the Shire, after which Sam Gamgee directed its restoration, using Galadriel's gift of earth to replant its gardens and trees.

Calendar and language

The Hobbits used the Shire Reckoning, a calendar of twelve 30-day months with five (six in leap years) extra days at the solstices and equinoxes, and a seven-day week in which the year always began on the same weekday. Tolkien "translated" their names and language conventions into English forms derived from Old English and rural English usage.
