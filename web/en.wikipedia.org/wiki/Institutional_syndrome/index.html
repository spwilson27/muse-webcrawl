Institutional syndrome
Also called institutional neurosis; related to institutionalization.

Institutional syndrome — called institutional neurosis in British psychiatrist Russell Barton's classic 1959 book of that name — refers to deficits and disabilities in social and personal functioning that result not from a person's illness but from long-term life in an institution such as a psychiatric hospital, prison, or residential home. Its features include apathy, loss of initiative, loss of interest, submissiveness, inability to plan for the future, and a loss of individuality and social skills.

Causes

Barton attributed the syndrome to features of institutional life itself: loss of contact with the outside world, enforced idleness, the authority of ward staff, loss of friends, possessions, and personal events, the effects of medication and ward atmosphere, and the loss of prospects outside. Sociologist Erving Goffman's Asylums (1961) described the parallel process in "total institutions", and John Wing and George Brown's comparative study of three mental hospitals (1970) showed that a poverty of the social environment measurably worsened patients' clinical state, and that enriching the environment could improve it.

Consequences and reform

Recognition of institutional syndrome contributed to deinstitutionalization, rehabilitation psychiatry, and design standards favoring small, normalized, community-based settings with meaningful occupation and personal possessions. Analogous effects have been described in children raised in deprived orphanages (hospitalism), in long-stay elderly care, and in prisoners, where "institutionalization" can impede re-entry to society.
