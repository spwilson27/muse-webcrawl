Sealant

A sealant is a substance used to block the passage of fluids, air, dust or sound through openings in materials, joints and surfaces. Sealants typically have lower strength than adhesives and remain flexible after curing, accommodating movement between the surfaces they join. Common applications include construction joints, glazing, plumbing, automotive gaskets, aerospace fuel tanks, and dental fissure sealing.

Types

Sealant chemistries include silicone (widely used for glazing and bathrooms, with excellent weather and temperature resistance), polyurethane (tough and paintable, common in construction and automotive work), polysulfide (fuel- and solvent-resistant, used in aircraft and insulated glass units), acrylic latex (inexpensive and paintable for interior joints), butyl rubber, and hot-melt and epoxy systems. Dental sealants are resin-based coatings applied to the chewing surfaces of molars to prevent decay.

History

Natural sealants are ancient: pitches and bitumen waterproofed boats and roofs in antiquity, and mixtures of beeswax, tar and natural resins were used for centuries. Caulking of wooden ships with oakum and pitch is a historical antecedent of modern joint sealing. Synthetic polymer sealants developed rapidly after the Second World War with the growth of curtain-wall construction and aerospace manufacture.
