Intermarium

The Intermarium concept envisioned a federation stretching "between the seas".

Intermarium (Polish: Międzymorze, Polish pronunciation: [mʲɛnd͡zɨˈmɔʐɛ]) was a post–World War I geopolitical plan conceived by Józef Piłsudski for a federation or alliance of Central and Eastern European states situated between the Baltic Sea and the Black Sea — the name derives from the Latin for "between the seas".

The plan

Piłsudski, leader of the newly re-established Polish state after 1918, sought to recreate in a new form the old Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, grouping Poland with Lithuania, Belarus, Ukraine, and potentially other states of the region such as Latvia, Estonia, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Yugoslavia, and Finland. The federation was intended as a counterweight both to Germany in the west and to Russia — whether tsarist, White, or Bolshevik — in the east.

Failure

The plan was never realized. Many of the intended member states regarded Poland itself as an imperialist power; Lithuania, for example, was in conflict with Poland over Vilnius, and Ukraine's alliance with Poland in 1920 against the Bolsheviks collapsed after the Treaty of Riga (1921). The concept also faced opposition from the Soviet Union and from Western powers that preferred bilateral arrangements. Piłsudski's related "Promethean" policy sought instead to weaken Russia by supporting independence movements among its non-Russian peoples.

Legacy

The Intermarium idea has been revived in modified forms since the Cold War. The Three Seas Initiative, launched in 2015–2016 by the presidents of Poland and Croatia, groups twelve (later thirteen) European Union member states between the Baltic, Adriatic, and Black seas for infrastructure and energy cooperation, and is sometimes described as a modern echo of the Intermarium concept, although it is an economic initiative rather than a federation.
