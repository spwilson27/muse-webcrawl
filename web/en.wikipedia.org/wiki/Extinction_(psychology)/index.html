Extinction (psychology)

Response strength declines as reinforcement is withheld.

In the psychology of learning, extinction is the gradual weakening and eventual disappearance of a conditioned response when the reinforcement that maintained it is no longer delivered. In operant conditioning, extinction occurs when a previously reinforced behaviour no longer produces reinforcing consequences. In classical conditioning, it occurs when the conditioned stimulus is repeatedly presented without the unconditioned stimulus.

Phenomena

Extinction does not erase the original learning; rather, it overlays it with new inhibitory learning. Evidence for this includes:

  • Spontaneous recovery — the reappearance of the extinguished response after a rest interval.
  • Renewal — recovery of the response when the context changes from the extinction context.
  • Reinstatement — recovery after re-exposure to the reinforcer or unconditioned stimulus alone.
  • Extinction burst — a temporary initial increase in the frequency and intensity of the behaviour when reinforcement is first withheld.

Clinical significance

Extinction is the principle behind exposure therapy for phobias, anxiety disorders, and PTSD, in which feared stimuli are presented without harmful consequences until fear diminishes. Because extinction is context-sensitive new learning rather than unlearning, relapse after therapy is common and is managed with booster sessions and extinction in multiple contexts. Partial reinforcement during training makes a behaviour more resistant to extinction — the partial reinforcement extinction effect.
