Apes are a clade of Old World simian primates native to sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia. They are distinguished from monkeys most obviously by the absence of a tail. The living apes comprise two families: the Hylobatidae, the "lesser apes" or gibbons of Southeast Asian forests, and the Hominidae, the "great apes", which include orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees, bonobos, and humans. Apes are among the most intelligent animals, with large brains relative to body size, complex social lives, and, in several species, documented tool use and elements of culture.

Characteristics

Apes have broad chests, mobile shoulder joints adapted for swinging and suspension, no external tail, and an appendix; their molar teeth show a Y-5 cusp pattern. Great apes range from the bonobo and chimpanzee (about 40–60 kg) to the male gorilla, the largest living primate at up to about 200 kg. Gibbons are much smaller and famously monogamous and acrobatic, moving by brachiation.

Evolution and conservation

The hominoid lineage diverged from the Old World monkeys roughly 25–30 million years ago, and apes were once far more diverse and widespread than today. Molecular evidence shows humans are most closely related to chimpanzees and bonobos, sharing a common ancestor about 6–7 million years ago. Nearly all non-human ape species are now endangered or critically endangered because of habitat loss, hunting, and disease.
