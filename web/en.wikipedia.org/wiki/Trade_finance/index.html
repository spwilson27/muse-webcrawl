Trade finance

Trade finance is the branch of finance concerned with funding and de-risking the exchange of goods and services across borders. It encompasses the financial instruments and products used by companies to facilitate international trade and commerce — bridging the gap between an exporter's wish to be paid promptly (ideally before shipment) and an importer's wish to pay only after goods are received and inspected. The World Trade Organization has estimated that a large majority of world trade — often cited as up to 80–90% — relies on some form of trade finance or credit insurance.

Intermediaries such as banks and financial institutions mitigate the risks of international trade — payment risk, currency risk, political and country risk, and performance risk — through instruments including letters of credit, documentary collections, bank guarantees, export credit insurance, factoring, and supply-chain finance.

Instruments

Letter of credit

A letter of credit (LC, or documentary credit) is a bank's undertaking, issued at the importer's request, to pay the exporter against presentation of stipulated documents (typically a bill of lading, commercial invoice, and insurance certificate) that comply with the credit's terms. Governed worldwide by the International Chamber of Commerce's Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits (UCP 600, 2007), the LC substitutes the bank's creditworthiness for the buyer's and is the classic risk-mitigation instrument for first-time or high-risk trading relationships. Variants include confirmed, revolving, transferable, back-to-back, and standby letters of credit.

Documentary collection

In a documentary collection, the exporter's bank forwards shipping documents to the importer's bank, which releases them against payment (documents against payment, D/P) or against acceptance of a time draft (documents against acceptance, D/A). Collections (governed by ICC URC 522) are cheaper than LCs but offer less protection, since the banks give no payment undertaking.

Open account and supply-chain finance

Most trade among established partners in developed markets moves on open account terms (payment 30–90 days after shipment). Supply-chain finance (reverse factoring) lets a buyer approve invoices so that its suppliers can discount them at the buyer's bank at rates keyed to the buyer's credit rating, extending the buyer's payment terms while paying suppliers early.

Factoring and forfaiting

Factoring is the sale of receivables at a discount to a factor, often with credit protection and collection services. Forfaiting is the non-recourse purchase of medium-term export receivables (promissory notes or bills of exchange), typically for capital-goods exports.

Export credit agencies and insurance

Government-backed export credit agencies (ECAs) — such as the Export–Import Bank of the United States, UK Export Finance, and Euler Hermes acting for Germany — provide loans, guarantees, and insurance against commercial and political risks, supporting national exporters especially in large infrastructure and aircraft deals. Private credit insurers cover short-term receivables against buyer default.

Risks addressed

  • Payment/default risk — the buyer fails or refuses to pay.
  • Currency risk — exchange-rate movement between contract and settlement, hedged with forwards.
  • Political/country risk — war, expropriation, exchange controls, or sanctions preventing payment.
  • Performance risk — goods not shipped, late, or not to specification, mitigated by inspection certificates and performance bonds.
  • Documentary risk — discrepancies in documents, a leading cause of delayed payment under LCs.

Market and the trade finance gap

Estimates by the Asian Development Bank and other institutions have placed the global trade finance gap — rejected requests for trade finance, concentrated among small and medium-sized enterprises in developing economies — in the trillions of US dollars annually (around US$2 trillion in ADB surveys of the early 2020s). Contributing factors include compliance costs of anti-money-laundering and know-your-customer rules ("de-risking" by correspondent banks), Basel capital treatment, and weak legal frameworks. Trade finance contracted sharply during the 2008–09 financial crisis, prompting G20 and WTO initiatives; official support helped trade rebound. Digitalisation efforts — electronic bills of lading, distributed-ledger platforms, and the UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Transferable Records (2017) — aim to replace the paper-heavy documentary system.

History

Trade finance is ancient: Mesopotamian merchants used credit contracts in the second millennium BCE, and medieval instruments such as the bill of exchange, developed by Italian bankers and spread through the Champagne fairs, financed European commerce for centuries. The modern documentary system grew with 19th-century steam shipping, the telegraph, and standardised documents such as the marine bill of lading; the ICC, founded in 1919, progressively harmonised practice through the UCP (first issued 1933) and Incoterms (1936).
