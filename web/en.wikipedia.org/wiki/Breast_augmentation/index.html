Breast augmentation

[Image placeholder: breast implant diagram]

Breast augmentation and augmentation mammoplasty are terms for a surgical procedure that aims to increase the size, change the shape, or alter the texture of the breasts. It is usually performed using breast implants — silicone shells filled with silicone gel or saline solution — or, in some cases, by fat grafting. Breast augmentation is one of the most commonly performed cosmetic surgical procedures worldwide.

Types

The two principal categories of implant are saline-filled and silicone gel-filled. Saline implants are filled with sterile salt water after insertion and can be adjusted in volume; if the shell leaks, the saline is absorbed by the body. Silicone gel implants are pre-filled with a cohesive silicone gel that tends to feel more like natural breast tissue. Implants may be placed under the breast tissue (subglandular) or under the chest muscle (submuscular), and incisions may be made in the fold under the breast, around the areola, in the armpit, or through the navel.

Fat-transfer breast augmentation uses liposuction to harvest fat from other parts of the body and inject it into the breasts; it produces more modest increases in size but avoids foreign material.

Indications

Augmentation is performed for cosmetic reasons (primary augmentation), to reconstruct the breast after mastectomy or injury (reconstruction), to revise earlier surgery, and as part of gender-affirming surgery. Surgeons assess patients' expectations and psychological readiness before operating.

Risks and complications

Possible complications include capsular contracture (hardening of scar tissue around the implant), implant rupture or leakage, infection, changes in nipple or breast sensation, and the need for further surgery. A rare cancer, breast implant-associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma (BIA-ALCL), has been linked mainly to certain textured implants, and "breast implant illness" is a term used for a range of systemic symptoms some patients report. Implants are not considered lifetime devices and may need replacement after 10–20 years.

History

Early attempts in the late 19th and early 20th centuries used paraffin, glass, ivory, and fat grafts with poor results. The modern silicone gel implant was developed by Thomas Cronin and Frank Gerow and first implanted in 1962. In 1992 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration restricted silicone gel implants amid safety concerns; after extensive study, they were re-approved for cosmetic use in 2006.
