Disjunctive normal form

In Boolean logic, a formula is in disjunctive normal form (DNF) if it is a disjunction of one or more conjunctions of literals; that is, it is an OR of ANDs, where the AND terms contain no ORs inside them. A literal is either a variable or its negation.

Examples

The formula (A ∧ ¬B ∧ ¬C) ∨ (¬D ∧ E ∧ F) is in DNF. Every Boolean formula can be converted into an equivalent DNF, for example by using a truth table and forming one conjunction for each row in which the formula is true; this yields the canonical (or full) DNF.

Properties and uses

DNF is useful in automated theorem proving, logic circuit design, and artificial intelligence (for example in covering algorithms for rule learning). Checking whether a DNF formula is satisfiable is easy (it suffices that one conjunction be consistent), whereas checking tautology for DNF is computationally hard; the dual situation holds for conjunctive normal form. A DNF formula consisting of a single conjunction is called a term, and the smallest number of terms needed relates to two-level circuit complexity.
