Saltatorial locomotion
TypeTerrestrial locomotion
CharacteristicMovement by leaping or hopping
ExamplesKangaroos, grasshoppers, fleas, frogs, jerboas

Saltatorial locomotion is a form of movement characterised by leaping or hopping, in which an animal propels itself through the air using enlarged, powerful hind limbs. The term derives from the Latin saltare, "to jump." Animals specialised for this mode of locomotion are called saltators, and typically possess greatly elongated hind legs, long feet, and reduced forelimbs.

Anatomy and mechanics

Saltatorial species share a suite of anatomical features: a lengthened hind foot (metatarsals and toes) that acts as an extra lever segment, large extensor muscles in the thigh and ankle, and elastic tendons — notably the Achilles tendon — that store and release energy like a spring, reducing the metabolic cost of each hop. The centre of mass is positioned so that landing forces are transmitted through the hind limbs, and the tail often serves as a counterbalance or, in kangaroos at rest, a fifth support.

Examples across taxa

Saltation has evolved independently many times. Among mammals, kangaroos and wallabies hop bipedally, ricocheting along with both hind feet moving together, while smaller forms such as jerboas, kangaroo rats, and springhares do the same at a smaller scale. Rabbits and hares combine saltatorial hind limbs with a galloping gait. Among insects, grasshoppers, crickets, fleas, and froghoppers achieve jumps many times their body length using catapult mechanisms that store energy in resilin or in cuticular springs. Frogs are the classic saltatorial amphibians, and a number of birds, such as sparrows, hop on the ground with both feet together.

Ecology

Hopping is an efficient way to cross open ground at moderate speeds and can be energetically cheaper than running for large kangaroos, whose energy cost per distance actually falls as speed increases within their hopping range. For small prey animals, unpredictable leaping also serves as an escape strategy, making the animal harder to target. The disadvantages are poor manoeuvrability at very low speed and unsuitability for cluttered or arboreal environments, where scansorial or arboreal locomotion prevails.
