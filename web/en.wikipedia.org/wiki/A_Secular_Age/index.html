A Secular Age

AuthorCharles Taylor
Published2007 (Harvard University Press)
Pages874
SubjectSecularization, philosophy of religion

A Secular Age is a 2007 book by the Canadian philosopher Charles Taylor, based on his Gifford Lectures. It examines how Western society moved from a condition in 1500 in which belief in God was nearly unchallenged to one in which belief is one option among others — the defining feature, for Taylor, of a "secular age." The work won the Templeton Prize-associated recognition and is considered Taylor's magnum opus.

Argument

Taylor rejects "subtraction stories" that portray secularity as what remains when religion is stripped away. Instead he traces the construction, within Latin Christendom, of an "immanent frame" — a disenchanted, natural order understood without reference to transcendence — and of the "buffered self" that experiences itself as insulated from spirits and cosmic forces, in contrast to the "porous self" of the enchanted world. Key stages include medieval reform movements, the rise of providential Deism, and the modern moral order centered on mutual benefit and discipline. Crucially, the immanent frame can be "spun" either closed (toward exclusive humanism) or open (toward transcendence), and modern unbelief and belief alike are "cross-pressured" by the other.

Reception

The book was widely reviewed and debated across philosophy, sociology, and theology; James K. A. Smith's How (Not) to Be Secular (2014) popularized its argument. Critics have questioned its focus on elite intellectual history and its Latin Christian scope, while admirers credit it with reframing the secularization debate.
