Universal health care

Universal health care (also called universal health coverage, universal coverage, or universal care) is a health care system in which all residents of a particular country or region are assured access to health care. It is generally determined by three dimensions: who is covered, what services are covered, and how much of the cost is covered. The World Health Organization describes universal health coverage as the goal of ensuring that all people obtain the health services they need without suffering financial hardship.

Most high-income countries provide universal health coverage

Models

  • Beveridge model — health care is provided and financed by the government through tax payments, as in the United Kingdom's National Health Service.
  • Bismarck model — insurance is provided by largely non-profit "sickness funds" financed by employers and employees, as in Germany and Japan.
  • National health insurance model — a single public insurer pays private providers, as in Canada.
  • Multi-payer universal systems — a mix of public and private insurers with mandatory coverage and subsidies, as in Switzerland and the Netherlands.

History

Germany established the first national social insurance system under Chancellor Otto von Bismarck in 1883. The United Kingdom created the National Health Service in 1948 under the Beveridge model. The World Health Organization's 2010 World Health Report identified universal coverage as a central goal of health systems, and the United Nations included universal health coverage in the Sustainable Development Goals (target 3.8) adopted in 2015.

Debate

Supporters point to better population health outcomes, financial protection against medical bankruptcy, and lower administrative costs in many universal systems. Critics raise concerns about waiting times, rationing, taxation levels, and the responsiveness of public bureaucracies. The United States is the notable exception among wealthy nations without a universal system, relying instead on a mixture of private insurance and public programs such as Medicare and Medicaid, and it spends a substantially larger share of GDP on health care than peer countries.
