Fritz Müller

Johann Friedrich Theodor Müller (31 March 1821 – 21 May 1897), better known as Fritz Müller, was a German naturalist who emigrated to Brazil, where he became an early and important supporter and correspondent of Charles Darwin. He is chiefly remembered for describing the form of mimicry now called Müllerian mimicry.

Life and work

Müller studied medicine and natural science in Berlin and Greifswald but, holding atheist and liberal views, left Germany in 1852 for the German colony of Blumenau in southern Brazil. There he farmed, taught, and conducted wide-ranging research on the crustaceans, insects and plants of the Atlantic forest.

Contributions

His book Für Darwin (1864), translated as Facts and Arguments for Darwin, presented embryological evidence from crustaceans supporting evolution. In 1878–79 he explained how two unpalatable butterfly species benefit by sharing the same warning pattern, since predators learn to avoid the pattern faster — the phenomenon Henry Walter Bates's rival form of mimicry complements. Darwin called him the "Prince of Observers".
