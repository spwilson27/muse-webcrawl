Patriarch

A patriarch (from Greek patriárkhēs, "chief or head of a family") is, in the broadest sense, the male head of a family or tribe, and more specifically a high-ranking bishop in several Christian churches. The term is used for the biblical forefathers of Israel — Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob are known as the Patriarchs — and for senior ecclesiastical office-holders in Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Catholic, and some other traditions.

Biblical and historical usage

In the Hebrew Bible, the patriarchs are Abraham, his son Isaac, and Isaac's son Jacob (Israel), whose twelve sons became the ancestors of the Twelve Tribes. In Judaism the term also applies to the Nasi, the president of the Sanhedrin in Roman-era Judea, known in Greek sources as the patriarch.

Ecclesiastical office

In the early church, the bishops of the most important sees came to be styled patriarchs. The Council of Chalcedon (451) recognised the authority of the five great sees of the Pentarchy: Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem. After the East–West Schism, the title remained central in Eastern Orthodoxy, where autocephalous churches such as those of Constantinople, Moscow, Serbia, and Bulgaria are headed by patriarchs. The Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople is regarded as first among equals among Orthodox patriarchs.

In the Catholic Church, the Pope was historically styled Patriarch of the West (a title Benedict XVI discontinued in 2006), and several Eastern Catholic Churches are headed by patriarchs. Some smaller churches, including the Latter Day Saint movement in its early period, have also used the office of patriarch.

Other uses

By extension, "patriarch" can denote the oldest or founding figure of a movement, family, or organisation, and is the male counterpart to "matriarch".
