Structure mapping theory

Structure mapping theory is a theory of analogical reasoning developed by cognitive scientist Dedre Gentner, first presented in 1983. The theory holds that analogy involves a mapping of knowledge from one domain (the base) into another (the target) such that a system of relations that holds among the base objects also holds among the target objects.

Principles

According to the theory, an analogy maps:

  • Object correspondences between base and target elements, which are placed in correspondence by
  • Relational structure: relations between objects (such as CAUSE, GREATER-THAN, or ATTRACTS) are preserved, while simple object attributes (such as color or size) are typically dropped.

The systematicity principle states that people prefer mappings that preserve deeply interconnected systems of relations — governed by higher-order relations such as causal chains — over mappings that preserve only isolated relations. Thus, in the analogy between the solar system and the atom, the fact that the sun is hotter than the planets is discarded, while the relational structure of attraction and revolution is kept.

Structure Mapping Engine

The theory was implemented computationally in the Structure Mapping Engine (SME), developed by Brian Falkenhainer, Kenneth Forbus, and Gentner. SME constructs mappings by finding locally consistent matches and merging them into globally consistent structures, scoring candidate mappings by their systematicity.

Applications

Structure mapping theory has been applied to metaphor comprehension, scientific discovery, children's cognitive development (including the "relational shift" from object-based to relation-based responding), and the design of educational materials that highlight relational structure.
