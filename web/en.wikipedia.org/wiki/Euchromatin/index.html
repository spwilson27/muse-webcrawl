Euchromatin

Euchromatin (also called "true chromatin") is a lightly packed, lightly staining form of chromatin in the cell nucleus that is enriched in genes and is generally transcriptionally active. Under the electron microscope it appears as loosely coiled nucleosome fibres (roughly 10 nm), in contrast to the densely packed heterochromatin, which stains darkly and is mostly transcriptionally silent.

Structure and function

Euchromatin is composed of DNA wrapped around histone octamers (nucleosomes) but with looser higher-order packing, allowing access for transcription factors and RNA polymerase. It is typically characterized by histone modifications such as acetylation (e.g. H3K9ac, H3K27ac at active enhancers) and trimethylation of H3K4 at active promoters. Euchromatic regions replicate early in S phase and show high rates of recombination.

Roughly the great majority of the human genome is euchromatic in terms of genomic span, although only a minority of genes are expressed in any given cell type. The distinction between euchromatin and heterochromatin, first drawn by Emil Heitz in 1928 on the basis of staining patterns in moss nuclei, remains foundational to epigenetics: shifts between the two states help regulate development and cell identity.
