Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), also called corticotropin, is a peptide hormone produced and secreted by the anterior pituitary gland. It is a key component of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis: ACTH stimulates the adrenal cortex to produce and release glucocorticoids, principally cortisol, in response to stress and circadian signals.

Production and regulation

ACTH is cleaved from the precursor protein proopiomelanocortin (POMC) in corticotroph cells of the anterior pituitary. Its release is driven by corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) from the hypothalamus and is suppressed by negative feedback from cortisol. Secretion follows a circadian rhythm, peaking in the early morning.

Clinical significance

Excess ACTH — most often from a pituitary adenoma (Cushing's disease) or from ectopic production by a tumour — causes Cushing's syndrome through cortisol overproduction. Deficient ACTH leads to secondary adrenal insufficiency. Measuring plasma ACTH helps distinguish pituitary from adrenal causes of cortisol disorders, and a synthetic fragment of ACTH (cosyntropin/tetracosactide) is used in the ACTH stimulation test for adrenal insufficiency. Because POMC is also the precursor of melanocyte-stimulating hormone, chronic high ACTH (as in Addison's disease) can cause skin hyperpigmentation.
