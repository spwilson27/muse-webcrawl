Adipose tissue

SystemEndocrine / connective tissue
Main cell typeAdipocytes
TypesWhite, brown, beige

Adipose tissue, commonly known as body fat, is a loose connective tissue composed primarily of adipocytes (fat cells). Its main roles are to store energy in the form of lipids, cushion and insulate the body, and — increasingly recognised — to act as an endocrine organ secreting hormones such as leptin and adiponectin.

Types

White adipose tissue stores energy in large single lipid droplets and is distributed beneath the skin (subcutaneous fat) and around internal organs (visceral fat); excess visceral fat is strongly associated with metabolic disease. Brown adipose tissue, abundant in infants and hibernating mammals, contains many mitochondria rich in the uncoupling protein UCP1 and burns lipids to generate heat (non-shivering thermogenesis). Beige adipocytes arise within white fat depots and can adopt a heat-producing phenotype.

Physiology

Adipose tissue expands by hypertrophy (enlargement of cells) and hyperplasia (recruitment of new cells). It releases free fatty acids during fasting and takes up glucose and lipids after meals under the control of insulin. Its secreted products — leptin, which signals satiety to the brain, adiponectin, resistin, and inflammatory cytokines — link fat mass to appetite, insulin sensitivity, and immunity.

Health

Both excess adiposity (obesity) and its absence (lipodystrophy) cause metabolic dysfunction, illustrating that adipose tissue is essential for safely buffering the body's energy supply.
