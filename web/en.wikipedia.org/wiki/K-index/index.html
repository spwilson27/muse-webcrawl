K-index

The K-index quantifies disturbances in the horizontal component of Earth's magnetic field over a three-hour interval, on an integer scale from 0 (quiet) to 9 (extreme storm). It is derived from magnetometer data at a given observatory and used to characterise geomagnetic activity and its effects on radio propagation, power grids, and aurora.

Definition

Introduced by Julius Bartels in 1939, the K-index is computed from the maximum fluctuation (after removing the regular daily variation) of the horizontal field during each 3-hour UT interval, converted to the quasi-logarithmic scale using station-specific thresholds — a low-latitude station needs a much smaller fluctuation in nanoteslas to register a given K than an auroral-zone station.

Planetary index

The planetary Kp-index is the average of K values from a network of 13 mid-latitude observatories coordinated by GFZ Potsdam, and is the standard global measure. Ap/aa indices are linear derivatives. NOAA's G-scale for geomagnetic storms maps roughly to Kp: G1 (minor) corresponds to Kp 5, up to G5 (extreme) at Kp 9.

Uses

Space-weather forecasters use Kp to predict auroral visibility — aurora is typically visible at lower latitudes when Kp reaches 6–7 — HF radio blackouts, satellite drag, and geomagnetically induced currents in power transmission systems.
