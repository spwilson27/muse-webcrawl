Borosilicate glass

Borosilicate glass is a type of glass with silica and boron trioxide as the main glass-forming constituents. Borosilicate glasses are known for having very low coefficients of thermal expansion (around 3 × 10−6 K−1 at 20 °C), making them more resistant to thermal shock than any other common glass. Such material is less subject to thermal stress and is commonly used for the construction of reagent bottles and flasks, as well as lighting, electronics, and cookware.

Borosilicate glass
Type: Glass
Main constituents: Silica, boron trioxide
Thermal expansion: ~3.3 × 10−6/K
Density: ~2.23 g/cm³

History

Borosilicate glass was first developed by German glassmaker Otto Schott in the late 19th century. In 1915, Corning Incorporated introduced borosilicate glass under the brand name Pyrex, which became a household name for ovenware and laboratory glassware alike.

Composition and properties

The most famous borosilicate composition is roughly 80% silica, 13% boric oxide, 4% sodium oxide, and 2–3% alumina. Because of its low thermal expansion, borosilicate glass can withstand rapid temperature changes of around 165 °C without breaking, whereas ordinary soda-lime glass tolerates only about 37 °C.

Uses

Borosilicate glass is used extensively in scientific laboratories for beakers, test tubes, and distillation apparatus. It is also used for high-quality cookware, aquarium heaters, telescope mirrors, flashlight lenses, and the glass beads used in reflective road paint. During the space age it coated the tiles of heat protection systems, and it remains a standard material for storing nuclear waste in vitrified form.

Manufacture

Like other glasses, borosilicate is made by melting the raw materials together at temperatures above 1,500 °C. Because of the high melting point, special gas/oxygen burners are used. The formed glass is then annealed to relieve internal stresses.
