Journalism ethics and standards

Journalism ethics and standards comprise the principles of ethics and good practice applicable to the specific challenges faced by journalists, including truthfulness, accuracy, objectivity, impartiality, fairness, and public accountability. They are embodied in professional codes of conduct adopted by news organizations and journalists' associations around the world.

Common standards

Most codes share several elements. Journalists should seek truth and report it: verify information before publication, use original sources where possible, and never deliberately distort facts or context. They should minimize harm, showing sensitivity to sources and subjects, especially children and victims, and balancing the public's right to know against privacy. They should act independently, avoiding conflicts of interest and refusing gifts or favors that compromise integrity. And they should be accountable and transparent, admitting and correcting mistakes promptly.

Codes of ethics

The Society of Professional Journalists' Code of Ethics, first adopted in the 1970s and revised several times, organizes these duties under four headings: seek truth and report it; minimize harm; act independently; be accountable and transparent. Internationally, the International Federation of Journalists' Declaration of Principles (1954) makes respect for truth the journalist's first duty. In the United Kingdom, editors operate under the IPSO Editors' Code of Practice, while many individual newspapers, such as The New York Times and The Guardian, maintain their own detailed standards and a corrections process overseen by standards editors or readers' editors.

Issues and debates

Recurring ethical questions include the use of anonymous sources, undercover reporting and deception, paying sources ("checkbook journalism"), plagiarism and fabrication scandals, the naming of crime suspects and suicide reporting guidelines, and the handling of graphic images. Digital media have added debates about speed versus verification, the permanence of online archives ("right to be forgotten"), sponsored content, and the ethics of aggregation and social-media sourcing.
