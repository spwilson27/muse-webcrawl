The Perkin reaction is an organic reaction developed by the English chemist William Henry Perkin, used to make α,β-unsaturated aromatic acids by the aldol condensation of an aromatic aldehyde and an acid anhydride in the presence of an alkali salt of the acid, which acts as a base catalyst. In the classic example, benzaldehyde reacts with acetic anhydride and sodium acetate to give cinnamic acid after aqueous workup.

History

Perkin described the reaction in 1868, building on his earlier work that had made him famous with the synthesis of the dye mauveine in 1856. He applied the reaction in 1877 to a celebrated synthesis of coumarin from salicylaldehyde, and the condensation went on to be used industrially in early routes to synthetic perfumes and to indigo.

Mechanism

Under the basic conditions, the anhydride forms an enolate at its α-carbon, which adds to the carbonyl carbon of the aromatic aldehyde. The resulting alkoxide is acetylated by further anhydride, and elimination gives a mixed anhydride of the unsaturated acid; hydrolysis during workup liberates the α,β-unsaturated carboxylic acid. Several details of the mechanism — in particular the sequence of acylation and elimination steps — were debated in the literature before the generally accepted pathway was established.

Scope and variations

The reaction works best with aromatic and heteroaromatic aldehydes and with anhydrides bearing α-hydrogens. Because high temperatures (often around 170–180 °C) and long reaction times are required, yields with sensitive substrates can be modest, and the Perkin condensation has largely been supplanted in the laboratory by milder methods such as the Knoevenagel condensation and the Heck reaction for preparing cinnamic acid derivatives.

Related variants include the Perkin-type condensation of succinic anhydride with aldehydes (a form of the Stobbe condensation) and the use of the reaction to close rings, as in the classic coumarin synthesis, in which the intermediate o-hydroxycinnamic acid lactonizes.

Applications

The reaction has been used in the synthesis of cinnamic acids (precursors to fragrances, flavours, and sunscreens), coumarins, stilbenes, and the natural product resveratrol analogues; historically it provided one of the first practical syntheses of coumarin and played a role in the early synthetic dye and perfume industry.
