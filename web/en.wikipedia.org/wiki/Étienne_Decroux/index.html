Étienne Decroux

Decroux developed corporeal mime into a rigorous, codified theatre technique.

Étienne Decroux (19 July 1898 – 12 March 1991) was a French actor and mime, often described as the father of modern mime. He developed corporeal mime (mime corporel), a technique that treats the actor's body — rather than the face or hands alone — as the principal instrument of expression.

Life and work

Decroux came from a working-class background and held many jobs before studying at Charles Dullin's Atelier and working with Jacques Copeau's circle at the Vieux-Colombier. Dissatisfied with the subordination of movement to text in conventional theatre, he spent decades researching how the trunk and limbs could articulate thought and emotion, developing a vocabulary of walks, inclinations, dynamo-rhythms and "figures of style".

Among his students were Jean-Louis Barrault and Marcel Marceau; Decroux appeared as Baptiste's father in the 1945 film Les Enfants du paradis. He taught in Paris for many years, spent a period in the United States in the 1950s, and continued teaching in Boulogne-Billancourt until late in life. His writings include Paroles sur le mime (1963).

Legacy

Corporeal mime influenced physical theatre worldwide, and Decroux's analytical approach to movement is still taught in theatre schools. His insistence that the body be trained like a musical instrument reshaped 20th-century mime from pantomimic illustration into an autonomous art.
