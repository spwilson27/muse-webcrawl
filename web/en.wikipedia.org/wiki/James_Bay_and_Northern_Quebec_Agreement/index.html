The James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement (JBNQA; French: Convention de la Baie-James et du Nord québécois) is an Aboriginal land claim settlement, signed on November 11, 1975, by the government of Quebec, the government of Canada, Hydro-Québec, the Grand Council of the Crees, and the Northern Quebec Inuit Association. It is commonly regarded as Canada's first modern treaty — the first comprehensive land claims agreement since the early-20th-century numbered treaties.

Background

In April 1971, Premier Robert Bourassa announced the "Project of the Century": the James Bay hydroelectric project, which would dam and divert major rivers flowing into James Bay and Hudson Bay. The Cree and Inuit of northern Quebec, who had never signed a treaty surrendering their rights to the territory, challenged the project in court. In November 1973 Justice Albert Malouf of the Quebec Superior Court granted an interlocutory injunction halting construction, finding the Indigenous plaintiffs had a strong case; although the Quebec Court of Appeal overturned the injunction a week later, the ruling forced the governments and Hydro-Québec to negotiate seriously.

Terms

Under the agreement, the Cree and Inuit ceded claims to most of the territory in exchange for:

  • Financial compensation of C$225 million;
  • A three-category land regime: Category I lands (about 14,000 km²) for exclusive Indigenous use and local governance, Category II lands with exclusive hunting, fishing, and trapping rights, and Category III public lands with preferential harvesting rights;
  • Creation of Indigenous governments and institutions, including the Cree Regional Authority (now the Cree Nation Government), the Kativik Regional Government for Nunavik, school boards, and health boards;
  • Environmental and social impact assessment regimes for future development;
  • Hunting, fishing, and trapping rights and income-support programs for harvesters.

Legacy and later agreements

The JBNQA became the template for modern comprehensive claims across Canada and was protected under section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982. Implementation disputes produced further litigation and agreements, including the 2002 Paix des Braves between Quebec and the Cree Nation, which settled implementation issues and Cree consent for the Eastmain–Rupert hydroelectric diversion in exchange for revenue sharing over 50 years. The Naskapi of Kawawachikamach adhered through the Northeastern Quebec Agreement of 1978.
