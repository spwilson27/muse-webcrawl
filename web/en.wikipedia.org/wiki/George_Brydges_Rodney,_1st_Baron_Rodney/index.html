George Brydges Rodney, 1st Baron Rodney, KB (bap. 13 February 1718 – 24 May 1792) was a British naval officer. He is best known for his service in the American War of Independence, particularly for the British victory over the French at the Battle of the Saintes in 1782, in which his fleet famously "broke the line" of the French formation. This victory restored British naval supremacy in the Caribbean and helped Britain negotiate peace from a stronger position.

Early career

Rodney entered the Royal Navy at fourteen and rose steadily, seeing action at the Second Battle of Cape Finisterre (1747) during the War of the Austrian Succession. He served as governor of Newfoundland (1749–51) and, during the Seven Years' War, took part in the capture of Louisbourg (1758) and commanded the expedition that seized Martinique (1762), along with St Lucia, Grenada, and St Vincent.

American War of Independence

Promoted and sent to command in the Leeward Islands, Rodney intercepted French and Spanish shipping and in January 1780 inflicted a heavy defeat on a Spanish squadron at the Battle of Cape St. Vincent (the "Moonlight Battle"), relieving Gibraltar. In the Caribbean he fought a series of indecisive actions against the Comte de Guichen in 1780. His seizure of the Dutch island of St Eustatius in 1781, and his lingering there to secure prize goods, became controversial and was blamed for missed opportunities elsewhere.

On 12 April 1782, near the Îles des Saintes between Dominica and Guadeloupe, Rodney's fleet met the French under the Comte de Grasse. A shift of wind opened gaps in the French line, through which Rodney's ships passed, raking the French from both sides — whether by Rodney's design or his captains' initiative remains debated. The French flagship Ville de Paris and four other ships of the line were captured, de Grasse himself taken prisoner, and the planned Franco-Spanish invasion of Jamaica was abandoned.

Later life and legacy

Rodney was raised to the peerage as Baron Rodney in 1782 and granted a parliamentary pension. He died in London in 1792. Several Royal Navy warships have borne the name HMS Rodney, including the Nelson-class battleship launched in 1925.
