Centering prayer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Centering prayer is a popular method of Christian contemplative prayer that places a strong emphasis on interior silence. The modern movement was developed in the 1970s at St. Joseph's Abbey in Spencer, Massachusetts, principally by the Trappist monks Thomas Keating, William Meninger and Basil Pennington, who sought to present the contemplative tradition in a form accessible to lay people. Its roots are claimed in earlier Christian contemplative practice, including the anonymous 14th-century work The Cloud of Unknowing, the writings of John of the Cross and Teresa of Ávila, and the practice of lectio divina.

Method

The method consists of sitting comfortably in silence for a period usually of twenty minutes, twice a day. The practitioner chooses a "sacred word" as a symbol of consent to God's presence and action within. Whenever the practitioner notices engagement with a thought, feeling, or sensation, they gently return to the sacred word. The practice ends with a slow recitation of the Lord's Prayer. Keating described centering prayer not as an end in itself but as a disposition toward contemplative prayer, and emphasized its fruits in daily life rather than experiences during the prayer period.

Spread

Contemplative Outreach, founded in 1984, promotes the practice through workshops, retreats and local groups. Centering prayer has been adopted by many Catholic parishes and by some Protestant communities. It has also been a subject of theological discussion: a 1989 letter from the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith addressed aspects of Christian meditation, and commentators differ on how centering prayer relates to traditional contemplative doctrine and to Eastern meditation techniques, a comparison its proponents generally resist.
