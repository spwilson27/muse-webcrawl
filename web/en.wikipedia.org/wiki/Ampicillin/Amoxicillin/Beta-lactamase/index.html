Beta-lactamase

Type: Enzyme (EC 3.5.2.6)
Function: Hydrolyses the beta-lactam ring
Effect: Resistance to penicillins and cephalosporins

Beta-lactamases are enzymes produced by bacteria that break open the beta-lactam ring of penicillin-class antibiotics such as ampicillin and amoxicillin, rendering the drugs inactive. They are the most important mechanism of bacterial resistance to beta-lactam antibiotics: an organism producing a suitable beta-lactamase can survive treatment with drugs that would otherwise kill it. The first beta-lactamase ("penicillinase") was described even before penicillin came into wide clinical use.

Classification and spectrum

Beta-lactamases are divided, in the Ambler classification, into classes A, B, C and D by their protein structure. Class A includes the common TEM and SHV penicillinases and the extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) that also destroy modern cephalosporins; class B comprises the metallo-beta-lactamases, which require zinc and include the feared New Delhi enzyme NDM-1; class C (AmpC) enzymes are typically chromosomal cephalosporinases; and class D includes the OXA enzymes. Carbapenemases — enzymes that destroy even carbapenem antibiotics — occur in several classes and are a major public-health concern.

Inhibitors

Medicine counters beta-lactamases with inhibitor drugs that sacrifice themselves to protect the companion antibiotic: clavulanic acid (paired with amoxicillin), sulbactam (with ampicillin), tazobactam, and newer agents such as avibactam and vaborbactam. Resistance genes are frequently carried on plasmids, spreading rapidly between bacteria, which makes beta-lactamase production a central problem in antimicrobial stewardship.
