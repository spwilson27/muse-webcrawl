Ampicillin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ampicillin, a widely used aminopenicillin antibiotic.

Ampicillin is a beta-lactam antibiotic of the aminopenicillin family, in use since 1961. Developed by Beecham, it extended the activity of penicillin to many Gram-negative bacteria as well as Gram-positive organisms, and it remains on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Medical uses

Ampicillin is used for respiratory tract infections, meningitis (notably listeria meningitis in newborns and the immunocompromised), urinary tract infections, gastrointestinal infections such as salmonellosis, and endocarditis caused by susceptible enterococci. It crosses into the cerebrospinal fluid when the meninges are inflamed better than many alternatives. Combined with the beta-lactamase inhibitor sulbactam, it regains activity against many beta-lactamase-producing strains.

Mechanism and resistance

Like all penicillins, ampicillin inhibits bacterial cell-wall synthesis by binding penicillin-binding proteins, blocking the cross-linking of peptidoglycan and causing cell lysis. Resistance arises mainly through beta-lactamase enzymes, altered binding proteins, and reduced outer-membrane permeability in Gram-negative bacteria.

Adverse effects

Common effects include rash — famously, a non-allergic maculopapular rash when given during infectious mononucleosis — diarrhea, and nausea. True allergic reactions range from urticaria to rare anaphylaxis, and ampicillin can precipitate Clostridioides difficile colitis like other broad-spectrum agents. It is available orally and intravenously; its close relative amoxicillin is better absorbed by mouth and often preferred for oral therapy.
