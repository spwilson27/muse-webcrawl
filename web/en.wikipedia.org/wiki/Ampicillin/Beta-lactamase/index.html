Beta-lactamase

Beta-lactamase
TypeEnzyme (hydrolase)
SubstrateBeta-lactam antibiotics (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems)
ReactionHydrolysis of the β-lactam ring
Clinical significanceMajor mechanism of antibiotic resistance

Beta-lactamases are enzymes produced by bacteria that break down beta-lactam antibiotics — the penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems and monobactams — by hydrolysing the four-membered beta-lactam ring that is essential to their antibacterial activity. They are the most important mechanism of resistance to this major class of antibiotics.

Classification

Hundreds of beta-lactamases are known. The Ambler classification groups them by amino-acid sequence into classes A, C and D (serine enzymes, with an active-site serine) and class B (metallo-beta-lactamases, which require zinc ions). Clinically important groups include the TEM, SHV and CTX-M extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs), which inactivate most penicillins and cephalosporins; the AmpC enzymes; and the carbapenemases such as KPC, NDM, VIM and OXA-48, which destroy even the carbapenem antibiotics of last resort.

History

The first beta-lactamase — a penicillinase from Escherichia coli — was described by Edward Abraham and Ernst Chain in 1940, before penicillin came into clinical use, foreshadowing the perpetual contest between antibiotic development and bacterial resistance. The genes for many beta-lactamases are carried on plasmids, allowing rapid spread between bacteria.

Inhibitors

Beta-lactamase inhibitors such as clavulanic acid, sulbactam, tazobactam and the newer avibactam and vaborbactam are combined with beta-lactam antibiotics to protect them from hydrolysis, as in the combinations amoxicillin/clavulanic acid and piperacillin/tazobactam. The continuing evolution of new enzymes drives an ongoing search for antibiotics and inhibitors that can overcome them.
