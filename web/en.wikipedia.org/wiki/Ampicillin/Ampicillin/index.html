Ampicillin is a beta-lactam antibiotic in the aminopenicillin family used to treat a variety of bacterial infections. Developed by Beecham and introduced in 1961, it was the first penicillin with useful activity against many Gram-negative bacteria as well as Gram-positive organisms, and it remains on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Medical uses

Ampicillin is used for respiratory tract infections, urinary tract infections, meningitis, salmonellosis, endocarditis and infections caused by Listeria monocytogenes, for which (often combined with gentamicin) it remains a treatment of choice. It is also used intrapartum to prevent early-onset group B streptococcal disease in newborns. In the laboratory, ampicillin resistance genes are widely used as selectable markers in molecular biology.

Mechanism and resistance

Like other penicillins, ampicillin kills bacteria by inhibiting the synthesis of the peptidoglycan cell wall, binding to penicillin-binding proteins so that the wall weakens and the cell lyses. Many bacteria have become resistant by producing beta-lactamase enzymes that hydrolyse the drug's beta-lactam ring; this led to combinations such as ampicillin/sulbactam, in which a beta-lactamase inhibitor protects the antibiotic.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include rash — a non-allergic ampicillin rash is particularly frequent in patients with infectious mononucleosis — nausea and diarrhoea. Serious allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis, can occur in people with penicillin allergy.
