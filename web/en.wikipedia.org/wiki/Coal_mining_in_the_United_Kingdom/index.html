Coal mining in the United Kingdom

Coal mining in the United Kingdom dates back to Roman times and occurred on a large scale from the 18th century onward, when coal fuelled the Industrial Revolution. At its peak in 1913, Britain produced 292 million tonnes of coal and employed some 1.1 million miners, making it the world's largest coal producer and exporter. The industry was nationalised in 1947 under the National Coal Board, but output and employment declined steadily through the second half of the 20th century because of cheaper imports, the switch to natural gas and other fuels, and the exhaustion of accessible reserves. The last deep coal mine in the UK, Kellingley Colliery in North Yorkshire, closed in December 2015.

History

Growth

Coal had been mined in Britain from shallow pits and outcrops since prehistoric times, but large-scale deep mining developed with the steam engine, which both demanded coal and powered the pumps and winding gear that made deep shafts practical. Coalfields in South Wales, the North East, the Midlands, Yorkshire, Lancashire, Scotland, and Kent supplied steam coal for railways and shipping, coking coal for iron and steel, and household coal for heating.

Nationalisation and decline

On 1 January 1947 the industry was nationalised and placed under the National Coal Board. Mechanisation raised productivity but employment fell from about 700,000 in the late 1940s to under 300,000 by 1970. The 1970s saw major strikes by the National Union of Mineworkers in 1972 and 1974, the latter contributing to the fall of Edward Heath's government.

The year-long miners' strike of 1984–85, over a programme of colliery closures, ended in defeat for the union and was followed by a rapid rundown of the industry. British Coal was privatised in 1994. Output continued to shrink as power stations switched to gas ("the dash for gas") and imported coal undercut domestic production. Kellingley's closure in 2015 ended centuries of deep mining, although small drift mines and opencast extraction continued on a minor scale, and the last coal-fired power station, Ratcliffe-on-Soar, closed in 2024.

Coalfields

The principal British coalfields were the South Wales Coalfield, the Northumberland and Durham Coalfield, the Yorkshire Coalfield, the Lancashire Coalfield, the North and South Staffordshire coalfields, the Central Belt coalfields of Scotland (Fife, Lanarkshire, Ayrshire), and the small but strategically important Kent Coalfield, worked from the early 20th century.

Legacy

Coal mining shaped the landscape, communities, and politics of much of industrial Britain. Former mining areas suffered persistent economic difficulties after the closures, while the industry's heritage is preserved in museums such as the National Coal Mining Museum for England at Caphouse Colliery and Big Pit in Blaenavon, Wales.
