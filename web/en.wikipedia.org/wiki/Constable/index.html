Constable

A constable is a person holding a particular office, most commonly in law enforcement. The office developed in medieval England from the comes stabuli — count of the stable — a high officer of royal and noble households, and the word survives in many countries and contexts with differing duties.

United Kingdom

In the United Kingdom, a constable is a police officer of the lowest rank in most territorial forces, and more fundamentally the holder of an original public office: every sworn police officer, whatever their rank, holds the office of constable and exercises original (not delegated) authority. A special constable is a part-time volunteer with the same powers. Historically, parish constables were unpaid local officials responsible for law and order, a system replaced by professional forces during the 19th century. The chief officer of most forces holds the title Chief Constable.

Other countries

In Canada, constable is the entry-level rank in municipal police services and the Royal Canadian Mounted Police; "special constables" enforce specific laws for transit agencies and universities. In Australia and New Zealand it is likewise the basic police rank. In parts of the United States — notably Pennsylvania, Texas and some other states — constables are elected peace officers who serve court papers, execute warrants and provide courtroom security. In Finland and (historically) several other European states, forms of the title were used for senior military or court offices, such as the Constable of France, commander of the king's armies until the office was abolished in 1627.

Etymology

The word derives through Old French conestable from Late Latin comes stabuli, "count (officer) of the stable," the master of the horse in late Roman and Frankish courts, whose responsibilities expanded into military command and constabulary duties.
