Hyperinflation

Devalued paper money during the German hyperinflation of 1923.

In economics, hyperinflation is a very high and typically accelerating inflation. It quickly erodes the real value of the currency, as the prices of all goods increase. This causes people to minimise their holdings in that currency as they usually switch to more stable foreign currencies. When measured in stable foreign currencies, prices typically remain stable.

Economist Phillip Cagan's classic definition begins at a monthly inflation rate of 50 percent. Unlike ordinary inflation, which is associated with the business cycle, hyperinflation is usually associated with some kind of political or social crisis, war or its aftermath, where a government begins printing money to pay for its spending.

Causes

Hyperinflation typically arises when a government finances large deficits by printing money, often after a collapse in tax revenue or a surge in obligations such as war reparations or foreign-currency debt. As the public loses confidence and spends the currency ever faster — a rise in velocity — prices spiral even as the money supply grows. Expectations become self-fulfilling: workers demand payment daily and spend wages within hours.

Historical examples

The most studied case is the Weimar Republic in 1923, when the German Papiermark fell from hundreds to trillions per US dollar and prices doubled every few days; a loaf of bread came to cost 200 billion marks. Hungary in 1946 suffered the worst hyperinflation recorded, with prices doubling roughly every 15 hours at the peak and the pengő replaced after denominations reached 1021. Zimbabwe in 2008 and Venezuela in the late 2010s produced similar collapses, with 100-trillion-dollar notes and mass emigration respectively.

Ending hyperinflation

Episodes typically end with drastic reforms: a new currency (often redenominated by many zeros), an independent central bank barred from financing the treasury, fiscal austerity, and sometimes dollarisation or a currency board to restore credibility.
