Meaning"Really serious harm" to the body

Grievous bodily harm (GBH) is a term used in English criminal law — and in other common-law jurisdictions influenced by it — for really serious physical injury inflicted on a victim. In England and Wales the offences are created by the Offences against the Person Act 1861: section 18 covers unlawfully and maliciously wounding or causing grievous bodily harm with intent, and section 20 covers the same acts without that specific intent. Case law (notably DPP v Smith [1961]) established that "grievous" means simply "really serious" bodily harm; it need not be life-threatening or permanent, and recognised psychiatric illness can qualify.

The offences

Section 18, the graver offence, requires proof that the defendant intended to cause really serious harm (or to resist lawful arrest); it carries a maximum sentence of life imprisonment. Section 20 requires only that the defendant foresaw that some physical harm might result ("maliciousness" in the sense of R v Cunningham recklessness) and carries a maximum of five years. Both offences may be committed by wounding — breaking all layers of the skin — or by inflicting/causing other really serious injury; charging standards issued by the Crown Prosecution Service list injuries typically charged as GBH, such as major fractures, injuries requiring extensive treatment, and serious psychiatric injury.

Related offences and other jurisdictions

GBH sits at the top of the ladder of non-fatal offences below it: assault occasioning actual bodily harm (section 47) and common assault. Scots law does not use the term, covering the same ground with the common-law crime of assault to severe injury. Variants of GBH offences exist across Commonwealth jurisdictions, including Australia and Canada-influenced systems, generally following the 1861 Act's structure.
