Observable universe

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of the observable universe as nested shells of lookback time centred on Earth, out to the cosmic microwave background]
The observable universe: a sphere of lookback time around us.

The observable universe is a spherical region of the universe consisting of all matter and energy that can be observed from Earth — all light (or other signals) that has had time to reach us since the expansion began. Because the universe is 13.8 billion years old but has been expanding, the current radius of the observable universe is about 46.5 billion light-years (diameter ~93 billion light-years): the most distant objects whose light we now receive have since receded far beyond the distance their light travelled.

Estimates of its contents: roughly two trillion galaxies (revised estimates vary), about 10⁸⁰ particles, with composition about 5% ordinary matter, 27% Dark matter and 68% Dark energy. The observable universe is not the whole universe: beyond the particle horizon lie regions whose light has not yet arrived (and, with accelerated expansion, some never will); whether space is finite or infinite, and what its global topology is, remain open questions — the whole may be vastly larger, or literally infinite, with our observable patch a vanishing sample.

Horizons

Cosmology distinguishes the particle horizon (the farthest distance light has travelled to us), the event horizon (the farthest distance light emitted now will ever reach us — about 16 billion light-years), and the Hubble sphere (where recession reaches light speed — objects beyond it are nevertheless observable, since the expansion rate changes). Every observer in the universe has their own observable universe, centred on themselves — Copernican humility written into geometry.
