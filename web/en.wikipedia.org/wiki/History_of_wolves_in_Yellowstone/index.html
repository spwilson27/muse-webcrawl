History of wolves in Yellowstone

The history of wolves in Yellowstone describes the eradication of the grey wolf from Yellowstone National Park by the 1920s and its controversial, celebrated reintroduction in 1995–96, one of the most famous wildlife restoration projects in the world.

Wolves before eradication

Grey wolves were native predators of the Yellowstone ecosystem, hunting elk, deer and bison alongside grizzly bears and cougars. When the park was established in 1872, federal policy nevertheless favoured predator control to protect game animals. The US Army and later the National Park Service trapped, poisoned and shot predators; the last known Yellowstone wolves were killed in 1926 near Soda Butte.

The wolf-free decades

Without wolves, elk populations irrupted, heavily browsing willow, aspen and cottonwood along streams. Scientists later linked this to declines in beaver, songbirds and streambank stability — a "trophic cascade" hypothesis popularised by the reintroduction's results. From the 1930s, biologists such as Adolph Murie questioned predator extermination, and the Endangered Species Act of 1973 listed the grey wolf, obliging recovery planning for the northern Rockies.

Reintroduction, 1995–1996

After years of environmental review and litigation, 14 wolves captured in Alberta were released into Yellowstone in January 1995, followed by 17 more from British Columbia in 1996. The population grew rapidly, reaching around 100 in the park within a decade, and wolves were delisted in the northern Rockies in 2011 (with litigation continuing).

Ecological and social effects

Wolves culled and redistributed elk, coinciding with regrowth of riparian willows, returning beavers and scavenger subsidies from carcasses. Claims of a dramatic wolf-driven cascade remain debated, as climate, bears and human hunting also changed. Ranchers' livestock losses, state hunting seasons outside the park, and famous individuals such as wolf 06 (832F) keep the Yellowstone wolves at the centre of American wildlife politics.
