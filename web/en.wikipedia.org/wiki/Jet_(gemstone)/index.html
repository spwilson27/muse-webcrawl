Jet (gemstone)

Jet is a type of lignite, a precursor to coal, that has been used as a gemstone since prehistoric times. It is derived from the compressed wood of ancient trees — particularly araucarian conifers — that were buried and mineralised in marine sediments. Jet is soft enough to carve easily yet takes a high polish, producing the deep, glossy black from which the expression "jet black" derives.

Properties and sources

Geologically, jet forms from driftwood swept into the sea and buried in mud during the Jurassic period, some 180 million years ago. The most famous source is the coast around Whitby in Yorkshire, England, where jet occurs in the shale cliffs of the Whitby Mudstone Formation and can be collected on the beaches after storms. Other sources include northern Spain (Asturias), France, Germany, Poland and the United States. "Hard jet", formed in salt water, is more durable than the "soft jet" formed in fresh water.

History of use

Jet beads and pendants appear in Neolithic and Bronze Age burials in Britain, and the Romans carved it into rings, hairpins and amulets, believing it had protective and medicinal powers. Its greatest fashion came in the 19th century: after Prince Albert died in 1861, Queen Victoria wore jet mourning jewellery, and Whitby's jet industry boomed, employing over a thousand men at its peak in the 1870s. The industry declined with changing fashions after the First World War. Imitations in French jet (black glass), vulcanite and later plastics are common; genuine jet can be identified because it is warm to the touch, lightweight, and produces a brown streak and an electrostatic charge when rubbed.
