Modernist cuisine

Modernist cuisine is a culinary movement that applies scientific understanding and new technology to cooking, emphasising precision, experimentation and the deliberate manipulation of texture, temperature and presentation. It is closely related to — and often synonymous with — molecular gastronomy, a term popularised in the 1990s for the scientific study of cooking, though many chefs prefer "modernist" because their work is a creative cuisine rather than laboratory science.

Origins

The physics of cooking had interested scientists since the 18th century, but the movement coalesced around 1988–1992 when physicist Nicholas Kurti and chemist Hervé This began workshops on "molecular and physical gastronomy" at Erice, Sicily. Chef Ferran Adrià at elBulli in Spain, Heston Blumenthal at The Fat Duck in England, and later Grant Achatz at Alinea in Chicago became the public faces of the style, serving long tasting menus of novel constructions: hot and cold foams, edible "spheres" made by spherification, savoury ice creams, and dishes cooked for exact times at exact temperatures.

Techniques and tools

Signature techniques include sous-vide water-bath cooking with immersion circulators; spherification and gelification with hydrocolloids such as agar, alginate, carrageenan and xanthan gum; foams from whipping siphons; liquid nitrogen freezing; rotary evaporators and centrifuges; precision scales and thermocouples; and dehydration, smoking guns and freeze-drying. The five-volume, 2,400-page cookbook Modernist Cuisine (2011) by Nathan Myhrvold and colleagues codified the movement's techniques with extensive laboratory testing and cross-section photography.

Criticism and legacy

Critics have accused modernist cooking of gimmickry, elitism and joyless technophilia, and some diners found the tasting-menu format theatrical to a fault; elBulli itself closed in 2011. Yet many of its methods — precise temperature control, hydrocolloid stabilisation, centrifuged stocks — have been absorbed into ordinary professional and home cooking, and its emphasis on understanding why recipes work has permanently changed culinary education.
