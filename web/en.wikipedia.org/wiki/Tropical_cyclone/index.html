Tropical cyclone

Regional namesHurricane (Atlantic/NE Pacific), typhoon (NW Pacific), cyclone (Indian Ocean/South Pacific)
FormationWarm ocean water (≥26.5 °C), moist air, low wind shear
EffectsStorm surge, flooding, extreme wind, tornadoes

A tropical cyclone is a rapidly rotating storm system characterised by a low-pressure centre, a closed low-level atmospheric circulation, strong winds and a spiral arrangement of thunderstorms that produce heavy rain and squalls. Depending on its location and strength, a tropical cyclone is called a hurricane, typhoon or simply a cyclone.

Structure

A mature tropical cyclone has a calm central eye, typically 30–65 km across, surrounded by the eyewall where the strongest winds and heaviest rain occur, and outer spiral rainbands. Warm air rises through the storm, releasing latent heat that powers the system; air sinks in the eye, suppressing clouds there.

Formation

Tropical cyclones form over warm ocean waters near the equator where sea surface temperatures are at least 26.5 °C, the atmosphere is moist and unstable, vertical wind shear is low, and the Coriolis effect is sufficient to impart rotation — which is why they rarely form within about 5° of the equator. On the Saffir–Simpson scale, Atlantic and Northeast Pacific hurricanes are classified from Category 1 to Category 5 by sustained wind speed.

Impacts

Tropical cyclones can cause catastrophic damage through storm surge, inland flooding, extreme winds and spawned tornadoes. Among the deadliest were the 1970 Bhola cyclone in East Pakistan (now Bangladesh) and the 1780 Great Hurricane; among the costliest are Hurricanes Katrina (2005) and Harvey (2017). Climate change is expected to increase the intensity and rainfall rates of the strongest storms.
