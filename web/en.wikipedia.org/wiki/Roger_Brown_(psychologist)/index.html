Roger William Brown (April 14, 1925 – December 11, 1997) was an American psychologist renowned for his work on the psychology of language and children's language development. A professor at Harvard University for most of his career, his classic 1973 book A First Language: The Early Stages, based on intensive longitudinal studies of three children ("Adam, Eve, and Sarah"), founded the modern field of child language research.

Career

Brown served in the U.S. Navy during World War II and studied at the University of Michigan, receiving his PhD in psychology in 1952. He taught at Harvard (1952–1957), then at MIT, and returned to Harvard in 1962, where he remained. His 1958 book Words and Things was an early, influential text of psycholinguistics and the cognitive revolution.

Contributions

Brown identified five stages of children's grammatical development measured by mean length of utterance (MLU), and documented the consistent order in which English-speaking children acquire 14 grammatical morphemes (such as -ing, plural -s, and articles). He also coined the term flashbulb memory (with James Kulik, 1977) for vivid, confident memories of the circumstances in which one learned of shocking public events. His social psychology work included studies of why names and forms of address shift (with Marguerite Ford, on pronouns of power and solidarity) and memorable experiments on the "tip of the tongue" state (with David McNeill). His memoir Against My Better Judgment (1996) described his life as a gay man and the historical context of secrecy and stigma.

Recognition

Brown was a member of the National Academy of Sciences and received the American Psychological Association's Distinguished Scientific Contribution Award. He died in 1997, aged 72.
