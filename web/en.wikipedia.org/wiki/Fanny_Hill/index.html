Fanny Hill: or, the Memoirs of a Woman of Pleasure is an erotic novel by English novelist John Cleland, first published in London in 1748–1749. Written while Cleland was in a debtors' prison, it is often considered the first original English prose pornography, and one of the most prosecuted and banned books in history.

Plot

The novel is written as a series of letters by Frances "Fanny" Hill, a country girl orphaned and brought to London, where she is drawn into a brothel. It follows her sexual education and career, her love for a young man named Charles, her experiences in fashionable society, and ultimately her reunion with Charles and ascent to comfortable respectability. The text famously avoids crude vocabulary, relying instead on elaborate metaphor and circumlocution.

Legal history

Cleland and his publisher were prosecuted for obscenity on the book's appearance, and it was suppressed in Britain, circulating in pirated editions. In the United States, an 1821 Massachusetts case became the first American obscenity prosecution of a book. The novel remained banned until landmark decisions — including Memoirs v. Massachusetts (1966), in which the U.S. Supreme Court held the book not obscene — cleared the way for open publication.

Literary significance

Despite its notoriety, Fanny Hill is recognized for its stylish prose and its place in the development of the English novel, and has been repeatedly adapted for film and the stage.
