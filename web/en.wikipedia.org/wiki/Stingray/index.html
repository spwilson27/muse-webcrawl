Stingrays are a group of sea rays, which are cartilaginous fish related to sharks. They are classified in the suborder Myliobatoidei of the order Myliobatiformes and consist of eight families. Most stingrays have one or more barbed stingers on the tail, which are used exclusively in self-defense.

The stinger may reach a length of about 35 cm (14 in), and its underside has grooves with venom glands. Stingrays are common in coastal tropical and subtropical marine waters throughout the world; there are also freshwater species in South America, Africa and Asia. They are generally docile, bottom-dwelling animals that feed on molluscs, crustaceans and small fish, and most injuries to humans occur when a ray is stepped on.

Anatomy and behaviour

Stingrays have flattened, disc-shaped bodies formed by joined pectoral fins, with eyes and spiracles on top and mouth and gill slits underneath. They swim by undulating or flapping their discs and breathe by drawing water through the spiracles while buried in sand. Reproduction is aplacental viviparous: embryos develop inside the mother, nourished by yolk and later by uterine secretions.

Stings

The venomous, serrated spine can cause severe lacerations and painful envenomation; immersion in hot water is the standard first aid. Fatalities are rare but have occurred, most famously the death of Australian naturalist Steve Irwin in 2006, when a stingray barb pierced his chest.
