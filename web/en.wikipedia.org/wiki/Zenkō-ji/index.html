Zenkō-ji (善光寺) is a Buddhist temple in the city of Nagano, Japan. Founded in the seventh century, the city of Nagano itself grew up around the temple. Zenkō-ji is famous throughout Japan as a pilgrimage destination open to all — men and women, of every sect and none — and it enshrines what tradition holds to be the first Buddhist statue ever brought to Japan, an image of Amida Buddha kept permanently hidden from view.

History

According to temple tradition, the merchant Yoshimitsu Honda enshrined the Amida triad here in 642; the image is said to have arrived in Japan from Korea in 552. The temple was moved to its present site in 664. During the Sengoku period the image and the temple became caught up in the wars between Takeda Shingen and Uesugi Kenshin, whose forces fought repeatedly over the surrounding plain of Kawanakajima; the statue was moved for safekeeping several times, and at one point was taken by Toyotomi Hideyoshi before being returned to Nagano in 1598. The present main hall dates from 1707 and is one of the largest wooden structures of the Edo period.

The hidden Buddha and Gokaichō

The principal image is a hibutsu — an absolutely hidden Buddha — not shown even to the temple's chief priests; a replica (maedachi honzon) is displayed instead during the Gokaichō festival, held roughly every six to seven years (most recently in 2022), when millions of pilgrims visit to touch a pillar connected by a cord to the image. Below the main hall runs the pitch-dark kaidan meguri corridor, where visitors grope along the wall seeking the "key to paradise" mounted beneath the altar.

Joint administration

Unusually, Zenkō-ji is administered jointly by the Tendai school (at Daihongan) and the Jōdo-shū Pure Land school (at Daikanjin), and is served by nuns as well as monks. Its long-standing welcome of female pilgrims — at a time when many sacred sites excluded women — underlay the saying that everyone should visit Zenkō-ji once in their life.
