John Scotus Eriugena, also known as Johannes Scotus Erigena (c. 800 – c. 877), was an Irish theologian, Neoplatonist philosopher and poet of the Carolingian Renaissance. Head of the palace school of the Frankish king Charles the Bald, he translated the Greek works of Pseudo-Dionysius and other Greek Fathers into Latin and composed the Periphyseon (On the Division of Nature), the most ambitious speculative system produced in the Latin West between Augustine and the twelfth century.

Life

Little is known of Eriugena's life in Ireland; his name combines Scotus ("the Irishman") and Eriugena ("born in Ireland"). By about 845 he was in Francia, directing the court school of Charles the Bald. Commissioned by Archbishop Hincmar, he intervened in the predestination controversy against Gottschalk with De praedestinatione (851), which was condemned for its rationalism. Charles commissioned him to translate Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite from Greek — a rare skill in the ninth-century West — and he also translated Maximus the Confessor and Gregory of Nyssa, deeply absorbing their apophatic and cosmic theology. A later legend says he was invited to England by Alfred the Great and stabbed to death by his own students with their pens.

The Periphyseon

Written as a dialogue between master and pupil, the Periphyseon divides "Nature" — everything that is and is not — into four species: (1) nature that creates and is not created (God as source); (2) nature that is created and creates (the primordial causes or Ideas in the Word); (3) nature that is created and does not create (the spatiotemporal world); and (4) nature that neither creates nor is created (God as the end to which all things return). The system traces an exitus of all things from God and their reditus into God, in a scheme indebted to Greek patristic Neoplatonism rather than to Latin scholasticism.

Reception

Suspect for its pantheistic overtones, the Periphyseon was condemned in 1225, when Pope Honorius III ordered copies burned. It nonetheless influenced later medieval mystics and was admired by nineteenth-century historians as a strikingly original, almost modern work of speculative reason.
