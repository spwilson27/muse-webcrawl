Begriffsschrift (German for "concept-script") is a book on logic by Gottlob Frege, published in 1879, and the formal system it set out. It is generally regarded as the founding work of modern mathematical logic: in it Frege introduced quantifiers and variables, the function–argument analysis of propositions, and a complete axiomatic system for propositional and first-order predicate logic.

Notation

Frege's notation was two-dimensional. A horizontal "content stroke" joined to a vertical "judgement stroke" expressed assertion; negation was a small vertical mark on the content stroke, and the conditional was written by stacking the consequent above the antecedent. The universal quantifier was written as a concavity in the content stroke containing a Gothic (Fraktur) letter. The system presented logical expressions as branching diagrams rather than linear strings — a major reason the book was difficult for contemporaries to read and slow to gain influence.

Key innovations

  • Quantification: The first successful treatment of "every", "some" and nested quantifiers, replacing the subject–predicate analysis of Aristotelian logic.
  • Function and argument: Propositions analysed as functions applied to arguments, allowing general statements about relations.
  • Axiomatic method: Frege gave axioms (six for the propositional and predicate calculus in modern assessment) and explicit inference rules, notably modus ponens and generalisation; Part III defined the ancestral relation, a construction later central to his logicist programme.
  • Identity and definitions were handled with explicit stipulative rules.

Reception and influence

The work was little read at first; reviews by Ernst Schröder and others were uncomprehending, and Frege complained that his notation was ignored. Its significance was recognised through Giuseppe Peano's different notation, Bertrand Russell's advocacy, and Ludwig Wittgenstein's and Rudolf Carnap's engagement with Frege's ideas. Bertrand Russell's own work and the Principia Mathematica (1910–13) effectively rebuilt Frege's logic in a linear notation. Modern scholarship — notably Jean van Heijenoort's 1967 translation in From Frege to Gödel — treats 1879 as the birth year of modern logic. Frege extended the system in his Grundgesetze der Arithmetik (1893/1903), where the inconsistent Basic Law V led, via Russell's paradox, to the collapse of his original logicist project.
