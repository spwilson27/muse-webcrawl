Segmentation (biology)

Placeholder image: the repeating segments of an annelid worm.

Segmentation in biology is the division of some animal and plant body plans into a linear series of repetitive segments. It is a characteristic feature of the phyla Annelida (e.g. earthworms), Arthropoda (insects, crustaceans, and others), and Chordata, in which the vertebrate spine, ribs, and associated musculature are segmentally organised. Segments may be nearly identical, as in an earthworm, or specialised and fused, as in the head and tagmata of insects.

Development

In vertebrates, segments called somites form sequentially from the front of the embryo rearward under the control of an oscillating "segmentation clock" of gene expression (involving Notch, Wnt, and FGF signalling) interacting with a moving wavefront — the clock-and-wavefront model. In Drosophila, segmentation is established by a cascade of maternal effect, gap, pair-rule, and segment polarity genes, including the Hox genes that give segments their identities. Annelids and many arthropods add segments from a growth zone near the tail.

Significance

Segmentation is considered one of the key innovations in animal evolution: a modular body plan allows segments to specialise for different functions and provides redundancy and flexibility in movement. Whether segmentation evolved once or independently in the annelid, arthropod, and chordate lineages remains debated.
