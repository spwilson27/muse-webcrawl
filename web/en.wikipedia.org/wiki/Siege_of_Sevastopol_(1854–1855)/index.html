Siege of Sevastopol (1854–1855)

Date: October 1854 – September 1855
Location: Sevastopol, Crimea, Russian Empire
Result: Allied victory

The siege of Sevastopol (1854–1855) was the principal operation of the Crimean War. British, French, Ottoman, and later Sardinian forces besieged the Russian naval base of Sevastopol on the Black Sea for eleven months before its fall in September 1855.

Background

After landing in Crimea and defeating the Russians at the Alma in September 1854, the Allies marched around Sevastopol and invested it from the south. The Russians scuttled ships to block the harbour entrance and mounted their guns in improvised earthworks organised by the engineer Franz Todleben. Russian attacks at Balaclava (famous for the Charge of the Light Brigade) and Inkerman failed to break the siege.

The siege

The siege became a grueling contest of trenches, bombardments, and sorties, worsened by a terrible winter storm in November 1854 and rampant disease. The French capture of the Malakoff redoubt on 8 September 1855 made the position untenable; the Russians evacuated the south side of the city that night, blowing up their fortifications.

Aftermath

Sevastopol's fall effectively ended major fighting; the Treaty of Paris (1856) demilitarised the Black Sea. The siege is noted for the pioneering war photography of Roger Fenton, the reporting of William Howard Russell, and Florence Nightingale's nursing reforms.
