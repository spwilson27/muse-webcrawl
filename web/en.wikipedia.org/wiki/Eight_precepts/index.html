Eight precepts

The eight precepts (Sanskrit: aṣṭāṅga-śīla; Pali: aṭṭhaṅga-sīla) is a practice in Buddhism in which lay followers observe additional rules of ethical and ascetic conduct beyond the basic five precepts, typically on observance days (Uposatha) or during periods of retreat and meditation.

The precepts

  1. Refraining from killing living beings.
  2. Refraining from taking what is not given (stealing).
  3. Refraining from all sexual activity (complete celibacy, strengthening the third of the five precepts).
  4. Refraining from false speech.
  5. Refraining from intoxicating drinks and drugs.
  6. Refraining from eating after midday.
  7. Refraining from entertainment such as dancing, singing, music, and shows, and from bodily adornments such as garlands, perfumes, and cosmetics.
  8. Refraining from using high or luxurious beds and seats.

Practice

The eight precepts are modelled on the ten precepts observed by novice monks and nuns, adapted for laypeople for periods of 24 hours. In Theravada countries such as Thailand, Sri Lanka, and Myanmar, lay devotees — especially the elderly — often wear white clothing and stay at temples on Uposatha days to observe them. In East Asian Mahayana traditions they are undertaken on the six fast days of the month and during retreats. The practice is seen as a way to deepen meditation, accumulate merit, and experience something of the monastic life.
