Epidermis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The epidermis is the outermost of the three layers of the skin, the others being the dermis and the hypodermis (subcutaneous tissue). It is a stratified squamous epithelium composed mainly of keratinocytes, providing a protective, waterproof barrier that regulates water loss, blocks pathogens and chemicals, and is continuously renewed as cells produced at its base migrate outward and are shed from the surface.

Cross-section of skin: the epidermis is the thin outer layer above the dermis.

Structure

The epidermis contains no blood vessels; its cells are nourished by diffusion from the dermis below. Its thickness varies by body region, from about 0.05 mm on the eyelids to 1.5 mm or more on the palms and soles. From deepest to most superficial, its layers (strata) in thin skin are:

  • Stratum basale — a single layer of dividing stem cells attached to the basement membrane; also contains melanocytes, which produce the pigment melanin.
  • Stratum spinosum — several layers of keratinocytes joined by desmosomes, giving a spiny appearance in prepared sections; contains Langerhans cells of the immune system.
  • Stratum granulosum — cells accumulate granules of keratohyalin and begin to flatten and die as they lose their nuclei.
  • Stratum corneum — the outermost layer of dead, flattened, keratin-filled cells (corneocytes) embedded in lipids, constantly worn away and replaced.

Thick skin of the palms and soles has an additional layer, the stratum lucidum, between the granulosum and corneum.

Cell types and function

Keratinocytes make up about 90 percent of epidermal cells and produce the structural protein keratin. Melanocytes transfer melanin to keratinocytes, determining skin color and protecting against ultraviolet radiation. Merkel cells in the basal layer function as touch receptors. A keratinocyte takes roughly four weeks to travel from the basal layer to the surface, where it is shed; this turnover slows with age and is dramatically accelerated in diseases such as psoriasis.

Clinical significance

Disorders of the epidermis include eczema (atopic dermatitis), in which barrier function is impaired, often involving mutations in the filaggrin gene; blistering diseases such as epidermolysis bullosa, in which the epidermis separates from the dermis; and skin cancers arising from epidermal cells, including basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma. Melanoma arises from melanocytes. Burns are classified in part by whether they damage only the epidermis (first degree) or extend deeper.
