The Polish Film School (Polish: Polska Szkoła Filmowa) was an informal group of Polish film directors and screenwriters active between approximately 1956 and 1963. Emerging after the political thaw of 1956, its members — many of them graduates of the Łódź Film School — used unusual freedom within the state-run film industry to examine Polish heroism, the Second World War, and Stalinism with a moral complexity forbidden under socialist realism.

Zbigniew Cybulski in Andrzej Wajda's Ashes and Diamonds (1958), an emblematic film of the school.

Themes and major works

Two broad currents are usually distinguished: the romantic, heroic line of Andrzej Wajda (Kanal, 1957; Ashes and Diamonds, 1958), which re-examined national myths of sacrifice, and the anti-heroic, ironic line of Andrzej Munk (Eroica, 1958; Bad Luck, 1960), which mocked those same myths. Jerzy Kawalerowicz's Night Train (1959) and Mother Joan of the Angels (1961) and Wojciech Has's films added psychological and surreal dimensions.

Legacy

The school brought Polish cinema to international festivals and influenced later movements including the "cinema of moral anxiety" of the 1970s. Its achievements remain central to Poland's film heritage and to the curriculum of the Łódź Film School, whose alumni include Roman Polanski and Krzysztof Kieślowski.
