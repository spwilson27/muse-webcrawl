Edward Benjamin Britten, Baron Britten (22 November 1913 – 4 December 1976) was an English composer, conductor and pianist. He was a central figure of twentieth-century British music, best known for his operas — above all Peter Grimes (1945), which re-established English opera on the international stage — and for orchestral works including the War Requiem (1962) and The Young Person's Guide to the Orchestra (1945).

Life

Britten was born in Lowestoft, Suffolk, the son of a dentist and a keen amateur musician mother. He studied composition with Frank Bridge as a schoolboy and later at the Royal College of Music in London with John Ireland. In the 1930s he wrote prolifically for documentary films of the GPO Film Unit, where he met the poet W. H. Auden, a major early collaborator and influence.

A committed pacifist, Britten left England for North America in 1939 with the tenor Peter Pears, who became his lifelong partner and the singer for whom most of his vocal music was written. They returned to England in 1942 and registered as conscientious objectors. A performance in California of Koussevitzky's commission led to Peter Grimes, premiered at Sadler's Wells in June 1945 to acclaim.

Britten settled at Aldeburgh on the Suffolk coast, where in 1948 he, Pears and Eric Crozier founded the Aldeburgh Festival, which became one of Britain's leading musical institutions. The festival's home, the Snape Maltings concert hall, opened in 1967. Britten's later operas include Billy Budd (1951), The Turn of the Screw (1954), A Midsummer Night's Dream (1960), Owen Wingrave (1971) and Death in Venice (1973).

Music

Britten's style combines lyric melodic writing with a keen ear for orchestral colour and an economical, precisely calculated technique. Recurring themes in his operas are the corruption of innocence, the conflict between the individual and society, and the sea — which pervades works such as Peter Grimes and the Four Sea Interludes. His pacifism found its fullest expression in the War Requiem, written for the consecration of the rebuilt Coventry Cathedral in 1962, interleaving the Latin Mass for the dead with the war poems of Wilfred Owen.

He wrote extensively for children and amateur performers — notably the opera Noye's Fludde (1958) — believing music should be useful and communal. His song cycles, including Les Illuminations, the Serenade for Tenor, Horn and Strings and the Hardy cycle Winter Words, set new standards in English song.

Honours and death

Britten was appointed a Companion of Honour in 1953 and admitted to the Order of Merit in 1965. In 1976 he became the first composer to receive a life peerage, as Baron Britten of Aldeburgh. His health failed after heart surgery in 1973; he died at Aldeburgh on 4 December 1976 and was buried in St Peter and St Paul's Church there, close to Pears's later grave. His home, the Red House, is now the headquarters of the Britten Pears Arts charity.
