Corporate tax

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A corporate tax (or corporation tax) is a tax on the profits of companies, levied by national (and sometimes subnational) governments. Rates have fallen worldwide since the 1980s — the OECD average combined rate declined from above 40 per cent to the low 20s by the 2020s — under competitive pressure among states seeking investment, a dynamic critics call a “race to the bottom”.

The tax's economic incidence is disputed: shareholders (lower returns), workers (lower wages) and consumers (higher prices) share the burden in proportions that vary by economy. Multinational profit-shifting to low-tax jurisdictions (see Tax haven) erodes bases; the international response culminated in the OECD/G20 agreement of October 2021 under which more than 130 jurisdictions committed to a 15 per cent global minimum tax on large multinationals (Pillar Two), being implemented from 2024. Corporate tax remains a major revenue source and a symbolically charged one, since it is the main direct tax visibly paid by companies as such.
