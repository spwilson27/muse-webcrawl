Straight line mechanism

A straight line mechanism is a mechanism that converts circular motion into rectilinear motion, or vice versa, generating an exact or approximate straight line. The first such mechanism, the Watt linkage, was invented by James Watt in 1784 and used in his steam engines; Watt described it as one of his most ingenious inventions.

The Watt linkage, an approximate straight-line mechanism.

History

In the late eighteenth century, before milling machines accurate enough to manufacture long straight guides were common, the problem of guiding a piston rod in a straight line using only linkages was of great practical importance. Watt's linkage of 1784 produced an approximate straight line (a flattened figure-eight curve). Later mechanisms included the Chebyshev linkage and the Roberts linkage, both producing approximate straight lines.

Exact mechanisms

The first mechanism known to produce exact straight-line motion was the Peaucellier–Lipkin linkage, described in 1864 by Charles-Nicolas Peaucellier and independently by Yom Tov Lipman Lipkin. It uses an inversor cell based on the geometry of circle inversion. The Hart's inversor (1874) achieves the same with fewer links. Other exact mechanisms include the Sarrus linkage, a spatial mechanism used in some steam engines and later in robotics.
