Financial economics

Subfield of: Economics
Key models: CAPM, Black–Scholes, EMH
Topics: Asset pricing, corporate finance, risk

Financial economics is the branch of economics concerned with the allocation of resources over time in markets, characterised by the intertemporal trade-offs, risk and pricing of financial assets such as stocks, bonds and derivatives.

Asset pricing

Foundational results include the capital asset pricing model (CAPM) of Sharpe, Lintner and Mossin, relating expected return to systematic (beta) risk; arbitrage pricing theory; and the efficient-market hypothesis of Eugene Fama. The Black–Scholes–Merton model (1973) priced options via no-arbitrage replication, earning the 1997 Nobel Prize for Scholes and Merton.

Corporate finance

The Modigliani–Miller theorems analyse capital structure irrelevance under ideal conditions, from which trade-off and pecking-order theories depart. Dividend policy, valuation (discounted cash flow) and agency theory are core topics.

Developments

Behavioural finance challenges rational-expectations models with evidence of bubbles and anomalies, while market microstructure, financial econometrics and risk management grew after the 2008 financial crisis. See Black–Scholes model.
