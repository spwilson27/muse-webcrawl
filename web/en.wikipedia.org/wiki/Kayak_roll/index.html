Kayak roll

Placeholder image: a kayaker mid-roll.

A kayak roll (also known as an Eskimo roll) is the act of righting a capsized kayak by use of body motion and/or a paddle. Typically this is done by quickly flicking the hips while using the paddle to push against the surface of the water. The recovery technique was developed by the Inuit peoples of the Arctic, in particular the Greenlandic Inuit, who depended on their kayaks for hunting and could not afford to exit the boat in frigid waters.

History

The traditional kayak roll, known in Greenlandic as kinngusaarneq and related techniques, formed an essential survival skill for Inuit hunters. Early European explorers recorded dozens of distinct rolling manoeuvres, including rolls performed with the paddle, with a throwing stick (norsaq), with one hand, and even with the arms crossed. These techniques were later adopted by recreational paddlers and whitewater kayakers around the world.

Technique

Although there are many different styles of rolling, the most common techniques share a set of principles: the paddler sets up with the paddle extended along the surface, tucks the head and body toward the deck, sweeps the paddle out to the side, and finishes with a powerful hip flick that rotates the boat upright while the head comes up last. Common variants include the C-to-C roll, the sweep roll, and the combat roll used in moving water.

Types of rolls

  • Standard Greenland roll — performed with a narrow Greenland paddle, finishing with the body leaned forward.
  • Reverse roll — begins with the paddle at the stern.
  • Hand roll — performed without a paddle, relying entirely on hip snap and body English.
  • Storm roll — a low, quick roll designed for rough conditions.
