The OIF brings together French-speaking states and governments on five continents.

The Organisation internationale de la Francophonie (OIF; sometimes rendered in English as the International Organisation of La Francophonie) is an international organization representing countries and regions where French is a lingua franca or customary language, where a significant proportion of the population are francophones, or where there is a notable affiliation with French culture.

History

The organization traces its origins to the Agency for Cultural and Technical Co-operation (ACCT), founded in Niamey, Niger, on 20 March 1970, championed by figures such as Léopold Sédar Senghor of Senegal, Habib Bourguiba of Tunisia, Hamani Diori of Niger, and Norodom Sihanouk of Cambodia. The first summit of heads of state and government was held in Paris in 1986; the agency was transformed into the OIF in the 1990s–2000s, with a Secretary General as its political head — the first being former UN Secretary-General Boutros Boutros-Ghali (1997–2002), followed by Abdou Diouf, Michaëlle Jean, and Louise Mushikiwabo.

Mission and activities

The OIF promotes the French language and linguistic and cultural diversity, peace, democracy and human rights, education and research, and economic cooperation and sustainable development. It deploys election observation missions, supports French-language media such as TV5Monde, and runs the quadrennial Jeux de la Francophonie. Its members hold biennial summits.

Membership

The organization counts 93 states and governments, including full members, associate members, and observers, spanning Europe, Africa, the Americas, Asia, and the Pacific; not all members are majority French-speaking. Several members have been suspended following coups d'état.
