The coast, also known as the coastline or seashore, is the land adjacent to a sea or ocean, forming the boundary between the land and the water. Coasts are shaped by tides, waves, currents, wind and the geology of the land, and they change constantly through erosion and deposition.

Formation and features

Wave action erodes cliffs and headlands, while eroded material is transported by longshore drift and deposited as beaches, spits and barrier islands. Where rivers meet the sea they form estuaries and deltas; in the tropics, mangrove forests and coral reefs fringe many coasts. Coastlines are fractal-like, so their measured length depends on the scale of measurement — the coastline paradox.

Human use

A large share of the world's population lives near coasts, which host major ports, fisheries, tourism and industry. Coastal zones are vulnerable to storms, flooding and sea-level rise, and are protected by defences such as sea walls, groynes and managed retreat. Coastal ecosystems — salt marshes, dunes and seagrass beds — provide flood protection, nurseries for fish and carbon storage.
