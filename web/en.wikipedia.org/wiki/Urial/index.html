Urial

Conservation statusVulnerable (IUCN)
Scientific nameOvis vignei
FamilyBovidae
RangeCentral and South Asia

The urial (Ovis vignei) is a wild sheep native to mountainous and arid regions of Central and South Asia, from Iran and Turkmenistan through Afghanistan and Pakistan to northwestern India (Ladakh). Also called the arkars or shapo, it is a medium-sized sheep closely related to the mouflon and is considered by many taxonomists to belong to the same species complex as the domestic sheep's wild ancestors.

Description

Urials are sexually dimorphic. Rams stand about 80–90 cm at the shoulder and bear large, homonymous horns that curve outward and forward, reaching up to a metre in length along the curve; ewes have much smaller horns. Males of several subspecies develop a prominent white saddle patch and, in winter, a long beard and neck ruff. The coat is reddish-brown in summer, greyer in winter. Recognised subspecies include the Ladakh urial, the Punjab urial, the Afghan urial, and the Transcaspian urial (arkar).

Behaviour and habitat

Urials inhabit open, rolling hills and mountain foothills, generally between 600 and 3,600 metres elevation, avoiding dense forest and very steep cliffs. They graze on grasses and browse shrubs, gathering in herds that segregate by sex outside the autumn rut. The mating season peaks in late autumn, and ewes give birth to one or two lambs in spring after a gestation of about five months.

Conservation

The species is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN, with a decreasing population estimated in the low tens of thousands. Threats include poaching, trophy hunting pressure, disease and competition from domestic livestock, and habitat degradation. Urials are legally protected in much of their range and occur in several national parks, including Khunjerab National Park in Pakistan.
