Planetary-mass object

Planetary-mass object
Mass rangeRoughly that of a planet: from bodies massive enough for hydrostatic equilibrium up to the deuterium-fusion limit (~13 Jupiter masses)
IncludesPlanets, dwarf planets, large moons, sub-brown dwarfs, rogue planets
Related termsPlanemo, sub-brown dwarf, free-floating planet

A planetary-mass object (PMO, planemo, or planetary body) is a celestial object whose mass lies within the range of definitions of a planet: massive enough to achieve hydrostatic equilibrium (to be rounded by its own gravity), but not massive enough to sustain core fusion, generally taken to mean below the limit of about 13 Jupiter masses at which deuterium fusion begins.

Usage

The term is used to discuss objects by mass rather than by orbital status, sidestepping the controversies of the IAU planet definition. It therefore encompasses true planets orbiting stars, dwarf planets, large moons that are planetary in character (such as Titan, Ganymede, and Earth's Moon), and free-floating objects that orbit no star at all. Because formation history is usually unknown for isolated objects, the term lets astronomers group together bodies that may have formed like stars (by cloud collapse, in which case objects in this range are called sub-brown dwarfs) or like planets (by accretion in a disk, in which case ejected examples are rogue planets).

Free-floating examples

Young star clusters contain numerous free-floating PMOs, such as those in the Sigma Orionis cluster and the objects Cha 110913−773444 and OTS 44, some of which possess their own accretion disks — suggesting they formed like stars and might even host their own satellites. Microlensing surveys indicate that rogue planets may be at least as numerous as stars in the Milky Way, and the James Webb Space Telescope has discovered candidate Jupiter-mass binary objects ("JuMBOs") in the Orion Nebula whose existence has challenged formation theories.

Significance

Planetary-mass objects blur the traditional boundary between stars and planets, showing that mass and structure form a continuum. Studying them tests theories of planet formation and star formation alike: the minimum mass of cloud fragments constrains the low end of the initial mass function, while ejected planets record the dynamical violence of young planetary systems.
