The domesticated red fox, often called the Russian domesticated fox or silver fox, is a form of the red fox (Vulpes vulpes) bred to be tame in a famous experiment begun in 1959 by the Soviet geneticist Dmitry Belyayev at the Institute of Cytology and Genetics in Novosibirsk, and continued by Lyudmila Trut.

The experiment

Belyayev selected foxes from fur-farm stock solely for tameness — those least fearful and aggressive toward humans. Within about ten generations the foxes began seeking human contact, wagging their tails and whining for attention. Selection for behaviour alone produced correlated physical changes: floppy ears, curled tails, piebald ("star") coat markings, shorter snouts, and altered reproductive timing — a suite of traits paralleling the "domestication syndrome" seen in dogs and other domestic animals.

Significance

The experiment is a landmark demonstration that behaviour can be the leading edge of domestication and that selection on a single behavioural trait can drag along morphological and hormonal changes, possibly through changes in developmental timing and the adrenal system. A contrasting population selected for aggression was also maintained. Domesticated foxes have been sold in small numbers as exotic pets, and research on their genome continues in Novosibirsk and at collaborating institutions.
