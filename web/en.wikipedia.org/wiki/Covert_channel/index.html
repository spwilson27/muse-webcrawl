Covert channel

A covert channel is a type of attack or communication path that transfers information between processes or parties in a way that violates a system's security policy — using a mechanism not intended for communication. The concept was introduced by Butler Lampson in his 1973 paper "A Note on the Confinement Problem". Covert channels are a central concern of multilevel security and the Trusted Computer System Evaluation Criteria (the "Orange Book"), which required systems at higher assurance levels to analyze and limit them.

Types

Covert storage channels convey information through the setting and reading of a shared attribute — for example, the existence of a file, a lock state, or unused bits in a protocol header. Covert timing channels convey information by modulating the timing of events, such as inter-packet arrival times, CPU scheduling delays, or response latencies, which the receiver measures. Examples in networks include encoding data in unused TCP/IP header fields, DNS query patterns, and packet timing; in shared hardware, cache timing channels let one process infer another's behavior, a principle related to side-channel attacks such as Spectre.

Analysis and countermeasures

Covert channels are characterized by their bandwidth: channels below about one bit per second were historically tolerated in high-assurance systems, while higher-bandwidth channels had to be eliminated or audited. Countermeasures include eliminating shared resources, adding noise (fuzzy time), partitioning, and monitoring. Because some sharing of resources is intrinsic to useful computation, complete elimination is generally impossible; evaluators therefore bound the exploitable bandwidth.
