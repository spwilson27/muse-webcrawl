Social democracy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Social democracy is a social, economic and political philosophy that supports political and economic democracy and a gradualist, reformist path to socialism — or, in its dominant modern form, a mixed economy with a comprehensive welfare state, collective bargaining and regulated capitalism. Emerging from the 19th-century workers' movement, it split from revolutionary communism after 1917, and after 1945 social-democratic parties governed much of Western Europe, building universal welfare states, public health and education systems, and (in Scandinavia) the high-equality "Nordic model".

Ideas and evolution

Eduard Bernstein's revisionism (1899) supplied the theoretical turn from revolution to parliamentary reform; Keynesian demand management underpinned post-war settlements. From the 1980s, globalisation, deindustrialisation and the decline of union density weakened social democracy's electoral base; "Third Way" modernisation (Blair, Schröder) accepted markets and fiscal discipline while targeting social investment, and the post-2008 era revived debates about inequality, precarious work, climate policy and the party family's future as new left, green and populist competitors rose.
