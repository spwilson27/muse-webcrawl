Gender role

A gender role, or sex role, is a social role encompassing a range of behaviors and attitudes that are generally considered acceptable, appropriate, or desirable for a person based on that person's sex. Gender roles are culturally specific and vary widely across societies and over time, though many societies associate masculinity and femininity with particular traits, occupations, and family responsibilities.

Traditional gender roles assigned breadwinning to men and homemaking to women

History of the concept

The term "gender role" was coined by sexologist John Money in 1955. In the social sciences, Talcott Parsons developed a model of complementary roles within the nuclear family: an "instrumental" role (provision of income and leadership) and an "expressive" role (emotional support and homemaking). Feminist scholars later criticized such models as naturalizing inequality. Simone de Beauvoir's The Second Sex (1949) famously argued that "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman."

Theories

Social learning theory holds that children acquire gender roles through reinforcement and imitation of models, a process called gender socialization. Cognitive developmental theories emphasize children's active construction of gender schemas. Evolutionary psychologists argue that some role divisions reflect evolved dispositions shaped by reproductive differences, an account contested by many social scientists who stress cultural variability, such as Margaret Mead's studies of the Arapesh, Mundugumor, and Tchambuli peoples.

Change and variation

Gender roles have shifted substantially since the mid-twentieth century, with rising female labor force participation, the entry of women into previously male professions, and greater male involvement in childcare in many countries. Nonetheless, occupational segregation, the gender pay gap, and unequal domestic labor persist in varying degrees. Attitudes toward gender roles correlate with religiosity, political ideology, and economic development.
