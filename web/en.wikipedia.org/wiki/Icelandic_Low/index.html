Icelandic Low

The Icelandic Low is a semi-permanent centre of low atmospheric pressure located between Iceland and southern Greenland, most pronounced in winter. It is one of the main centres of action in the atmospheric circulation of the Northern Hemisphere, together with its counterpart, the Azores High. The pressure difference between the Icelandic Low and the Azores High defines the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO), the dominant pattern of climate variability in the North Atlantic region.

Formation

The Icelandic Low appears in mean sea-level pressure maps as a climatological average. It is not a single stationary cyclone; instead, the North Atlantic storm track steers a continual procession of extratropical cyclones into the region between Iceland and Greenland, where they often slow, deepen, and decay. Strong temperature contrasts between the cold Canadian and Greenland air masses and the relatively warm Atlantic waters (including the North Atlantic Current) promote cyclogenesis, and individual lows can reach very low central pressures — among the deepest recorded North Atlantic storms fell below 920 hPa.

Role in climate

When the Icelandic Low is deep and the Azores High strong, the steepened pressure gradient drives vigorous westerly winds across the Atlantic, funnelling storms into northern Europe and bringing mild, wet, windy winters there, while Greenland and eastern Canada tend to be cold and dry. When the low is weak, the westerlies weaken or shift, allowing cold continental air to dominate parts of Europe and eastern North America.

North Atlantic Oscillation

The NAO index is typically defined as the normalised pressure difference between stations in Iceland (or the Icelandic Low region) and the Azores or Gibraltar area. Positive and negative NAO phases correspond to the strengthened and weakened configurations described above. The NAO strongly influences North Atlantic sea surface temperatures, Arctic sea ice export through the Fram Strait, and winter weather across Europe, North Africa, and the eastern seaboard of North America.

Analogous systems

The North Pacific has a counterpart system in the Aleutian Low, paired with the Siberian High in driving the Pacific–North American pattern. Other semi-permanent lows include the monsoon trough over South Asia in summer.
