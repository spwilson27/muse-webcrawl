Barbary Wars

The Barbary Wars were a series of conflicts fought by the United States, Sweden and the Kingdom of Sicily against the Barbary states — the nominally Ottoman regencies of Tripoli, Algiers and Tunis, and the independent Sultanate of Morocco — between 1801 and 1815. They comprised the First Barbary War (1801–1805) against Tripoli and the Second Barbary War (1815) against Algiers. For centuries the Barbary corsairs had preyed on Mediterranean shipping, exacting tribute from maritime nations in return for immunity; the young United States, which lost British protection after independence and paid heavily in ransom and tribute, chose instead to fight — sending the new United States Navy across the Atlantic in its first overseas wars and giving the Marine Corps its hymn's famous line, "to the shores of Tripoli".

Background

Barbary corsairs had operated from the North African regencies since the sixteenth century, capturing merchant ships and enslaving or ransoming their crews; European powers generally bought peace with tribute rather than suppress them. American ships, protected by the Royal Navy before 1776 and by a purchased treaty with France during the Revolution, were exposed thereafter: Algerine corsairs seized eleven American vessels in 1793 alone. Congress answered with the Naval Act of 1794 — authorising the six original frigates of the US Navy — and with humiliating treaties that by 1800 were costing the United States on the order of a fifth of its federal revenue in payments to the Barbary rulers. In 1801 the Pasha of Tripoli, Yusuf Karamanli, demanding an increased tribute, declared war by having the flagstaff before the American consulate chopped down.

First Barbary War (1801–1805)

President Thomas Jefferson, who had long argued against tribute, sent naval squadrons to the Mediterranean. The war's early years were indecisive blockade and bombardment, and in October 1803 the frigate USS Philadelphia ran aground off Tripoli and was captured with her crew of over 300. In February 1804 Lieutenant Stephen Decatur led a daring night raid into Tripoli harbour and burnt Philadelphia at her moorings — "the most bold and daring act of the age", in Nelson's reported judgement. Commodore Edward Preble's squadron bombarded Tripoli repeatedly through 1804. The war's climax came overland: William Eaton, with the Pasha's exiled brother Hamet and a motley army of a few hundred mercenaries, marched 500 miles from Alexandria and captured Derna in April 1805 — the first time the American flag was raised in battle on foreign soil — threatening Tripoli itself. Yusuf sued for peace: the treaty of June 1805 ended tribute to Tripoli and ransomed the Philadelphia prisoners for $60,000, though Eaton's supporters denounced the abandonment of Hamet.

Second Barbary War (1815)

Algiers had used the distraction of the War of 1812 — during which it was encouraged by Britain — to seize American ships and enslave their crews. As soon as peace with Britain was signed, President James Madison obtained a declaration of war against Algiers (March 1815) and sent Decatur's squadron, which in June captured the Algerine flagship Mashouda and the brig Estedio and appeared before Algiers within weeks. The Dey capitulated on terms dictated at the cannon's mouth: no more tribute, release of captives without ransom, and compensation for seized property. Decatur extracted similar treaties from Tunis and Tripoli. A British-Dutch fleet's bombardment of Algiers in 1816 completed the work; Barbary piracy, though not finally extinguished until the French conquest of Algiers in 1830, never again troubled American commerce.

Legacy

The Barbary Wars established the United States Navy as a permanent institution, provided the service with its first heroes (Preble, Decatur, Somers), carried American arms overseas for the first time, and entered the national mythology — the Marine Corps hymn, Decatur's toast ("Our country... may she always be in the right"), and a tradition of refusing tribute that Americans contrasted proudly with the practice of the European powers.
