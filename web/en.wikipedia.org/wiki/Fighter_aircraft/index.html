Fighter aircraft

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A grey stealth fighter jet in flight against clouds]
A fifth-generation fighter.

A fighter aircraft is a fixed-wing military aircraft designed primarily for air-to-air combat against other aircraft. From First World War biplanes synchronised to fire through the propeller, through the Spitfire and Mustang of the Second World War, to today\'s stealthy, sensor-fused fifth-generation jets (F-35, F-22, J-20, Su-57), fighters contest control of the air on which modern warfare depends.

Generations

Historians group jet fighters into generations: first (1940s–50s subsonic, e.g. F-86, MiG-15), second (supersonic, early missiles), third (multi-role maturity), fourth (fly-by-wire, pulse-Doppler radar — F-16, Su-27) and 4.5 upgrades, then fifth-generation stealth designs with internal weapons and networked sensors.

Armament and sensors

Modern fighters combine a cannon, radar- and infrared-guided missiles, helmet-mounted sights, AESA radar and electronic-warfare suites; beyond-visual-range combat dominates doctrine, while dogfighting agility remains a design driver (supermanoeuvrability in Russian and latest US types).

Costs and proliferation

An F-35 costs roughly US$80–100 million depending on variant, and over 1,000 have been delivered to many allied air forces — the most widely exported modern fighter programme. Development costs and export politics (ITAR restrictions, rival Chinese and Russian offers) shape air forces worldwide.
