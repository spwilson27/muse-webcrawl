Romanian orphans

Romanian orphans is a term for the generation of children raised in state institutions in Romania during and after the communist regime of Nicolae Ceaușescu. Following the fall of the regime in 1989, journalists and aid workers revealed an estimated 100,000–170,000 children living in orphanages and institutions, often in conditions of severe neglect. Many were not orphans in the literal sense but had been placed in care by families unable to support them.

Origins: Decree 770

In 1966 Ceaușescu issued Decree 770, which banned abortion and severely restricted contraception in an effort to raise the birth rate ("the foetus is the socialist property of the whole society"). Birth rates surged, but economic decline and poverty meant many families could not care for their children, who entered a vast network of state institutions — leagăne (cradles) for infants, orphanages for older children and institutions for children classified as "irrecoverable". Underfunding, overcrowding and low staff ratios produced widespread malnutrition, disease and developmental deprivation.

Exposure and response

After December 1989, international media coverage of the institutions prompted an influx of humanitarian aid and a surge in international adoption. The 1990s also saw abuses within the adoption system. Under pressure from the European Union in the run-up to accession, Romania reformed child protection: a moratorium on international adoptions was imposed in 2001 and made effectively permanent in 2005, large institutions were progressively closed, and care shifted to foster families, small group homes and family support.

Research legacy

The Romanian orphanages became a landmark case in developmental psychology. The Bucharest Early Intervention Project, begun in 2000, randomly assigned institutionalised children to foster care or care as usual and found that early placement in families produced substantial gains in cognitive development, attachment and mental health, demonstrating sensitive periods in early childhood development. Follow-up studies documented the long-term effects of early deprivation on brain development and stress physiology.
