Estimator

In statistics, an estimator is a rule for calculating an estimate of a given quantity based on observed data: thus the rule (the estimator), the quantity of interest (the estimand) and its result (the estimate) are distinguished. For example, the sample mean is a commonly used estimator of the population mean. There are point estimators, which produce a single value, and interval estimators, such as confidence intervals, which produce a range.

Definition

Formally, an estimator is a statistic—a measurable function of the sample—that is used to infer the value of an unknown parameter in a statistical model. Point estimators yield single-valued results, while interval estimators, such as a confidence interval, yield range-valued results.

Properties

Bias

The bias of an estimator is the difference between its expected value and the true value of the parameter. An estimator with zero bias is called unbiased. The sample mean is an unbiased estimator of the population mean, whereas the maximum likelihood estimator of a variance is generally biased (though consistent).

Efficiency and variance

Among unbiased estimators, more efficient ones have smaller variance. The Cramér–Rao bound gives a lower bound on the variance of unbiased estimators of a parameter.

Consistency

An estimator is consistent if it converges in probability to the true parameter value as the sample size grows without bound.

Sufficiency

A sufficient estimator is based on a sufficient statistic, capturing all the information the sample contains about the parameter; the Rao–Blackwell theorem shows how to improve an estimator by conditioning on a sufficient statistic.

Methods of construction

Common methods of finding estimators include maximum likelihood estimation, the method of moments, least squares, and Bayesian estimators derived from posterior distributions.
