ClassificationString instrument (struck zither / hammered dulcimer)
OriginHungary
RangeUp to about 4½ octaves (concert cimbalom)

The cimbalom (Hungarian: [ˈt͡simbɒlom]) is a large hammered dulcimer: a trapezoidal box zither whose steel and copper-wound strings are struck with cotton-wrapped sticks. It is especially associated with Hungarian folk and Romani music, and a developed concert version is used in classical and film music.

Types

Two broad types exist: the small portable folk cimbalom, carried on a strap or set on a table, and the large concert cimbalom developed in Budapest in the 1870s by József Schunda, which stands on legs, has a pedal-operated damper mechanism and a fully chromatic range of more than four octaves.

Repertoire

The instrument is a fixture of Hungarian café and Romani ensembles. In classical music it appears in Kodály's Háry János suite, in works by Bartók, Stravinsky (who championed the player Aladár Rácz), Liszt, Dohnányi and Kurtág, and it colours many film scores evocative of Central and Eastern Europe — John Barry used it prominently in the 1960s. Solo virtuosi such as Rácz established schools of playing, and the cimbalom is taught at the Liszt Academy in Budapest.

Related instruments

The cimbalom belongs to the widespread hammered dulcimer family, alongside the Romanian țambal, the Greek santouri, the Persian santur and the Chinese yangqin.
