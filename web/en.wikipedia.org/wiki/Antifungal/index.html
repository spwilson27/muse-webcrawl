Antifungal

An antifungal medication, also known as an antimycotic medication, is a pharmaceutical fungicide or fungistatic used to treat and prevent mycoses such as athlete's foot, ringworm, candidiasis (thrush), and serious systemic infections such as cryptococcal meningitis. Such drugs are usually obtained by a doctor's prescription, but a few are available over the counter.

Classes

Azoles

Azole antifungals inhibit the enzyme lanosterol 14α-demethylase, which the fungus needs to produce ergosterol, an essential component of its cell membrane. They divide into imidazoles (such as clotrimazole, ketoconazole, and miconazole), used mostly topically, and triazoles (such as fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, and posaconazole), which have broader spectra and are used for systemic infections.

Polyenes

Polyene drugs, including amphotericin B and nystatin, bind directly to ergosterol and form pores in the fungal membrane, causing leakage of cellular contents. Amphotericin B, introduced in the 1950s, remains a mainstay for life-threatening systemic mycoses, though it is notably toxic to the kidneys; lipid formulations reduce this toxicity.

Echinocandins

Echinocandins such as caspofungin, micafungin, and anidulafungin inhibit the synthesis of β-glucan, a key component of the fungal cell wall. Because human cells have no cell wall, these drugs are relatively well tolerated, but they must be given intravenously.

Allylamines and others

Allylamines such as terbinafine inhibit squalene epoxidase, an earlier step in ergosterol synthesis, and are widely used for dermatophyte infections of skin and nails. Other agents include flucytosine, an antimetabolite used in combination therapy, and griseofulvin, an older drug that disrupts fungal mitosis.

Uses

Superficial infections of the skin, nails, and mucous membranes are usually treated with topical preparations such as creams and shampoos. Invasive fungal diseases — including invasive aspergillosis, disseminated candidiasis, and mucormycosis — occur mainly in immunocompromised patients and require systemic therapy, often for weeks or months.

Resistance

Antifungal resistance is a growing concern. Azole-resistant Aspergillus fumigatus, partly attributed to agricultural azole fungicide use, and the multidrug-resistant yeast Candida auris, first described in 2009, have both been identified by health agencies as emerging threats. The World Health Organization published its first fungal priority pathogens list in 2022.
