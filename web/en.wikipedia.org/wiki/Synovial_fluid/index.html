Synovial fluid

Synovial fluid, also called synovia, is a viscous, non-Newtonian fluid found in the cavities of synovial joints. With its egg white–like consistency, its principal role is to reduce friction between the articular cartilage of synovial joints during movement. Synovial fluid is secreted by the synovial membrane (synovium) that lines the joint capsule.

Composition and function

The fluid is an ultrafiltrate of blood plasma enriched with hyaluronic acid (hyaluronan), produced by fibroblast-like synoviocytes, and the glycoprotein lubricin. Hyaluronan gives the fluid its viscosity and elasticity; lubricin provides boundary lubrication at the cartilage surface. Synovial fluid also nourishes the avascular articular cartilage and removes its metabolic waste.

Normal synovial fluid is clear, pale yellow, and highly viscous, forming a long "string" when expressed from a needle. A healthy knee contains only a few millilitres. The fluid behaves as a non-Newtonian fluid: its viscosity falls as the rate of shear increases, protecting the joint across speeds of movement.

Clinical significance

Analysis of aspirated joint fluid (arthrocentesis) is a key diagnostic tool. Changes in volume, colour, viscosity, and white-cell count help distinguish non-inflammatory conditions such as osteoarthritis (clear fluid, low cell counts) from inflammatory arthritis (cloudy fluid, high counts), crystal disease such as gout (needle-shaped urate crystals under polarized light), and septic arthritis (purulent fluid with bacteria), which is a medical emergency.
