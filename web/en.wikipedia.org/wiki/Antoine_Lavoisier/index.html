Antoine-Laurent de Lavoisier (1743–1794) was a French chemist widely regarded as the founder of modern chemistry. He established the law of conservation of mass, showed that combustion and respiration involve oxygen rather than 'phlogiston', and co-wrote the first modern chemical nomenclature.

His Traité élémentaire de chimie (1789) listed 33 'elements' and set chemistry on a quantitative footing of careful weighing. Lavoisier was also a tax farmer for the monarchy, and during the French Revolution he was arrested and guillotined on 8 May 1794.

[Image: Lavoisier and his wife Marie-Anne at work with chemical apparatus]

Work

Working with his wife Marie-Anne (who translated, drew apparatus and kept records), Lavoisier named oxygen and hydrogen, showed water is a compound of the two, helped reform chemical naming with Berthollet and others (1787), and built precision balances and calorimeters. The famous verdict attributed to Lagrange — that it took only a moment to cut off a head that a century might not produce again — captures chemistry's loss.

Legacy

The element-list, nomenclature and mass-balance methods Lavoisier championed are the direct ancestors of modern chemical practice; the phlogiston theory he demolished is the standard case study of a scientific revolution.
