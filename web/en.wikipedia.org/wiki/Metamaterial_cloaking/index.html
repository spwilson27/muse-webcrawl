Metamaterial cloaking

Metamaterial cloaking
Field: Electromagnetics, optics
Key concept: Transformation optics
First microwave demo: 2006

Metamaterial cloaking is the use of metamaterials in an invisibility cloak. Metamaterials are artificial materials engineered to have properties not found in nature, arising from their repeating microstructures rather than their chemical composition. In a cloak, the metamaterial is designed to guide electromagnetic waves smoothly around an object so that they emerge on the far side as if the object were not there, rendering the object hidden from detection at selected wavelengths.

Theory

The theoretical foundation is transformation optics, proposed independently in 2006 by John Pendry and collaborators and by Ulf Leonhardt. By treating the coordinates of space as a medium whose electromagnetic properties (permittivity and permeability) vary from point to point, one can bend light rays around a concealed region the way a river flows around a smooth rock, leaving no shadow and no reflection downstream.

Experiments

The first experimental demonstration, in 2006 by David Schurig, David R. Smith and colleagues at Duke University, cloaked a copper cylinder from microwave radiation using concentric rings of split-ring resonators. The device worked at a single frequency and in two dimensions only, but proved the concept workable. Subsequent research has pushed cloaking toward optical (visible-light) frequencies, carpet cloaks that hide objects under a reflective surface, and acoustic analogues that route sound waves.

Limitations

Practical invisibility remains elusive: known cloaks work only over narrow bands of wavelength, are lossy, and are difficult to scale. Theoretical limits relate achievable bandwidth and object size, so a full-spectrum, macroscopic, free-standing cloak is considered far beyond present technology. Nevertheless, metamaterial cloaking research has produced useful spin-offs in antenna design, sensors, imaging and superlenses.
