**James Dewey Watson** (born 6 April 1928) is an American molecular biologist, geneticist and zoologist. In 1953, aged 25, he and Francis Crick proposed the double-helix structure of DNA at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge, England, work for which they shared the 1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Maurice Wilkins.

Watson later led the Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory, was the first director of the US Human Genome Project at the National Institutes of Health, and wrote the influential textbook Molecular Biology of the Gene and the bestselling memoir The Double Helix (1968).

Early life and education

Watson grew up in Chicago and appeared as a teenager on the radio quiz show Quiz Kids. He entered the University of Chicago at 15, studied zoology, and earned his PhD at Indiana University in 1950 under Salvador Luria, researching bacteriophage viruses.

DNA and Harvard

On a postdoctoral fellowship in Europe, Watson moved to Cambridge in 1951 and began working with Francis Crick. Their model of DNA, announced in 1953, explained how genetic information could be stored and copied. Watson joined Harvard's biology department in 1956 and helped build its molecular biology programme.

Cold Spring Harbor and the genome project

Watson became director of Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory in 1968, greatly expanding its research and fundraising. From 1988 to 1992 he headed the NIH's genome effort, championing the sequencing of the human genome, before resigning over patenting disputes. In 2007 he was among the first people to have a full personal genome sequenced and published.

Watson repeatedly attracted public controversy for remarks about race and intelligence, beginning in 2007; in 2019 Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory revoked his honorary titles after he restated those views in a television documentary. His scientific legacy rests on the DNA discovery and on building institutions of modern genetics.
