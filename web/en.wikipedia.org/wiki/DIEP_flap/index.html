DIEP flap

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A DIEP flap is a type of breast reconstruction surgery in which blood vessels called deep inferior epigastric perforators (DIEP), together with the overlying skin and fat of the lower abdomen, are removed and transferred to the chest to reconstruct a breast after mastectomy, without transplanting abdominal muscle. The name is an acronym for the vessels used — the deep inferior epigastric perforators. Because the rectus abdominis muscle is preserved (unlike the older TRAM flap), the DIEP flap reduces the risk of abdominal weakness and hernia at the donor site.

The lower abdominal flap and its perforator vessels.

Procedure

The technique was first described in 1989 by Koshima and Soeda and popularised for breast reconstruction by Robert Allen in 1994. In the operation, a flap of skin and subcutaneous fat from the lower abdomen — similar to the tissue removed in a tummy tuck — is dissected free on one or more perforating vessels, which are carefully separated from the underlying rectus muscle. The tissue is transferred to the chest, where the microsurgeon connects its artery and veins to vessels at the recipient site (usually the internal mammary vessels) using an operating microscope (a free flap). The flap is then shaped into a breast mound, and the abdominal wound is closed.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include reconstruction with the patient's own tissue that looks and ages naturally, long-lasting results, preservation of abdominal muscle function, and less donor-site pain than muscle-taking flaps. Disadvantages include a long and technically demanding operation (often 4–8 hours), the need for microsurgical expertise, a long abdominal scar, and a small risk of total flap loss (around 1%) or partial fat necrosis. Not all patients are candidates: sufficient abdominal tissue, suitable perforator anatomy (often assessed with CT angiography), and absence of certain prior surgeries or risk factors such as smoking are required.

Related techniques

Related autologous reconstructions include the TRAM flap (which takes rectus muscle), the SIEA flap (using the superficial inferior epigastric artery, avoiding dissection through the fascia), muscle-sparing free TRAM, and perforator flaps from other donor sites such as the gluteal (SGAP/IGAP) and inner thigh (PAP/TUG) flaps.
