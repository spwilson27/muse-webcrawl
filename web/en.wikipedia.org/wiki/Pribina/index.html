Pribina

Reignc. 825 – 833 (Nitra); c. 840 – 861 (Lower Pannonia)

SuccessorKočeľ
IssueKočeľ

Pribina (c. 800 – 861) was a Slavic prince who ruled the Principality of Nitra in present-day Slovakia in the 820s and 830s, and later, as a Frankish vassal, the Principality of Lower Pannonia around Lake Balaton in present-day Hungary. He is remembered in Slovak tradition as an early Christian ruler: around 828 he allowed the consecration of a church in Nitra — the first known church in Slavic central Europe — although he himself was probably still a pagan at the time.

Prince of Nitra

Pribina's principality centred on Nitra was, according to the Conversio Bagoariorum et Carantanorum, prosperous and well fortified. About 828 Archbishop Adalram of Salzburg consecrated a church on Pribina's estate at Nitra, an event often taken as the beginning of church organisation among the western Slavs. Around 833 the Moravian prince Mojmir I expelled Pribina, and Nitra was absorbed into Great Moravia. Pribina fled with his son Kočeľ to the Frankish margrave Ratbod and was baptised in East Francia.

Exile and restoration

After quarrelling with Ratbod, Pribina fled to the Bulgars and then to Prince Ratimir of Lower Pannonia, but was eventually reconciled with the Franks. King Louis the German granted him land around the Zala River near Lake Balaton in 840, and in 847 gave it to him in full ownership as a duchy. Pribina built a stronghold at Zalavár (Mosaburg, "Blatograd") and endowed numerous churches, settling Bavarian priests among his Slavic subjects and cooperating with the Archbishopric of Salzburg's missions.

Death and legacy

Pribina was killed in 861 fighting the Moravians of Rastislav, who were expanding against East Francia. His son Kočeľ succeeded him and later hosted the Byzantine missionaries Constantine (Cyril) and Methodius. In Slovakia, Pribina is a figure of national memory: the Order of Pribina is among the country's state honours, and his era is commemorated in Nitra, which celebrates the early church consecration as a foundational moment in Slovak Christian history.
