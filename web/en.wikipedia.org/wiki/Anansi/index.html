Anansi is a famous figure of West African folklore, originating with the Akan people of present-day Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire. Depicted as a spider, a man, or a man with spider-like limbs, he is a trickster and culture hero, celebrated and reproached in the same tales for his cleverness, greed, laziness and wit. So central is he that Akan folktales as a whole are called anansesem — "spider stories".

The stories

In the best-known tale, Anansi buys the sky god Nyame's stories — previously beyond all price — by capturing seemingly impossible creatures (hornets, a python, a leopard, and a fairy), becoming the owner of all the world's stories and the god of storytelling himself. Other tales, endlessly retold, show his schemes against animals larger and stronger than himself, his many sons, and his frequent comeuppance: Anansi is as often the fool as the victor, and the stories carry morals lightly, through comedy.

Diaspora and legacy

Carried across the Atlantic by enslaved Akan people, Anansi survived the Middle Passage and flourished in the Caribbean — as Anancy in Jamaica, where he is a national folk hero of resistance and cunning — and in Suriname, the southern United States ("Aunt Nancy", and through parallel trickster cycles, Br'er Rabbit's milieu) and elsewhere in the Americas. He appears in modern literature and media from Ghanaian children's books to Neil Gaiman's Anansi Boys (2005) and the television adaptation of American Gods, remaining a symbol of how the weak outwit the strong and of storytelling itself as survival.
