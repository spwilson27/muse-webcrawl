La Calavera Catrina

La Calavera Catrina ("The Dapper Skull", "The Elegant Skull") is a zinc etching by Mexican printmaker José Guadalupe Posada, first published around 1910–1913. It depicts a female skeleton wearing only a wide-brimmed, flowered hat in the European style of the time, and was created as a satire of the Mexican upper classes who had embraced French customs during the Porfiriato — the image's message being that death makes everyone equal, rich or poor.

Posada's etching of La Calavera Catrina

History

Posada's image originally appeared in the penny-sheet newspaper La Patria Ilustrada and in the publications of editor Antonio Vanegas Arroyo, accompanied by satirical verses. The etching was originally called La Calavera Catrina in reference to the figure's fashionable appearance (a catrín being a dapper gentleman); some early captions called her "La Calavera de la Catrina". The image remained relatively obscure until the muralist Diego Rivera gave the skeleton a full body and elegant dress in his mural Dream of a Sunday Afternoon in the Alameda Central (1946–1947), placing her beside Posada himself and popularizing the name "La Catrina".

Cultural significance

Today La Catrina is the most recognizable icon of the Mexican Day of the Dead (Día de Muertos). Her image appears in countless forms — figurines, costumes with elaborate skull makeup, parades (mega-procession of Catrinas through Mexico City), and art — and she has become a general symbol of Mexican folk culture and of the country's playful, familiar relationship with death.
