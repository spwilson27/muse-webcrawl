Bloodletting (or blood-letting) is the withdrawal of blood from a patient to prevent or cure illness and disease. Bloodletting, whether by a physician or by leeches, was based on an ancient system of medicine in which blood and other bodily fluids were regarded as "humours" that had to remain in proper balance to maintain health. It was the most common medical practice performed by surgeons from antiquity until the late 19th century, a span of almost 2,000 years.

History

The practice is depicted in ancient Egyptian medicine and was systematised by Greek physicians including Hippocrates and Galen, whose teachings dominated European medicine through the Middle Ages and Renaissance. Blood was drawn by venesection with a lancet or fleam, by cupping, or by the application of medicinal leeches. Barber-surgeons commonly performed the procedure; the red-and-white barber's pole is traditionally said to evoke blood and bandages. In the 18th and early 19th centuries, physicians such as Benjamin Rush advocated copious bleeding for fevers; George Washington's final illness (1799) was treated with the removal of roughly 40 per cent of his blood volume in under a day, which likely hastened his death.

Decline

From the mid-19th century, statistical studies — notably by Pierre Louis in Paris — showed bloodletting did not improve outcomes in pneumonia, and the rise of germ theory and experimental physiology undermined the humoral rationale. By the early 20th century the practice had been largely abandoned in mainstream medicine, though leech therapy retained niche surgical uses.

Modern uses

Therapeutic phlebotomy remains standard treatment for a few specific conditions, including hereditary haemochromatosis (iron overload), polycythaemia vera, and porphyria cutanea tarda, in which controlled removal of blood is genuinely beneficial.
