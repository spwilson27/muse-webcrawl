Beam (nautical)

Beam
MeasurementMaximum width of a vessel's hull
SymbolB
Related termsDraft, freeboard, length overall

The beam of a ship is its width at its widest point. The maximum beam (Bmax) is the distance between planes passing through the outer sides of the ship, while the beam of the hull (BH) only includes parts of the hull that are permanently fixed. Generally speaking, the wider the beam of a vessel, the greater its initial stability, because of a higher metacentric height from the increased second moment of area of the waterplane.

Typical values

Typical length-to-beam ratios (aspect ratios) for vessels range from about 2:1 for some small sailing dinghies to as high as 20:1 for very long, narrow racing shells. Commercial cargo ships commonly have ratios between 5:1 and 8:1. A vessel with a large beam relative to its length is sometimes described as "beamy", while a narrow vessel is described as "fine".

Beam and stability

Beam is a principal factor in the stability of a vessel. Increasing the beam increases the righting moment at small angles of heel, which makes a wide vessel feel "stiff" in a seaway. However, an excessively wide vessel may have a quick, uncomfortable rolling motion, and in monohull sailing vessels excessive beam can reduce the ability to recover from a knockdown at large heel angles.

Regulations

Maximum beam can be a constraint imposed by infrastructure: the Panamax standard limited vessels transiting the original Panama Canal locks to a beam of 32.31 metres (106 feet), while the expanded locks opened in 2016 allow a beam of up to 49 metres. See also draft and ship.
