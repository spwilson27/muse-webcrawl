Civilian Public Service

The Civilian Public Service (CPS) was a program of the United States government that provided conscientious objectors with an alternative to military service during World War II. From 1941 to 1947, nearly 12,000 draftees who refused to bear arms worked in 152 CPS camps and units across the country in soil conservation, forestry, firefighting, agriculture, social services, and mental health care. The program was administered by the Selective Service System in cooperation with the three historic peace churches — the Church of the Brethren, the Society of Friends (Quakers), and the Mennonite Church — whose agencies operated most camps and bore much of the cost. CPS men received no pay and their dependents no allowances.

Background

The Selective Training and Service Act of 1940, the first peacetime draft in American history, recognised conscientious objection for men opposed to war by reason of religious training and belief, and provided that such objectors could be assigned to "work of national importance under civilian direction". Negotiations between the government and the peace churches produced the CPS experiment, directed within Selective Service by Lewis B. Hershey and administered on a day-to-day basis by the National Service Board for Religious Objectors. The first camp opened in May 1941 near Baltimore, Maryland.

Work performed

Many CPS camps were former Civilian Conservation Corps camps, where men planted trees, built fire towers and trails, fought forest fires, and worked on erosion control and irrigation projects. In the western United States, CPS men served as smokejumpers, parachuting to fight remote wildfires from bases such as Camp Paxson in Montana. Others were assigned to detached units: dairy testing, agricultural research stations, and — most consequentially — as attendants in state mental hospitals, where CPS units exposed abusive conditions and helped galvanise post-war mental-health reform.

CPS volunteers also took part in scientific experiments. In the Minnesota Starvation Experiment of 1944–45, conducted by physiologist Ancel Keys at the University of Minnesota, 36 CPS men submitted to semi-starvation diets so researchers could learn how to rehabilitate famine victims in Europe and Asia. Other men volunteered for experiments on hepatitis, malaria, and the effects of high altitude, and some served as "sea cows" guinea pigs in wartime medical research.

Criticism and dissent

The program was criticised from several directions. Because CPS men were unpaid and their families unsupported, the churches and the men themselves effectively financed a government program, and many objectors believed the work was make-work rather than genuinely important. Some absolutists refused CPS entirely and went to prison. In 1943 a work strike and protests over censorship and camp conditions occurred at several camps, and a minority of men walked out. Civil liberties groups objected that the system discriminated in favour of members of the historic peace churches over objectors from other faiths or none.

Legacy

CPS ended in 1947, but it established alternative service as a lasting feature of American conscription, shaping the 1-W service program of the Korean and Vietnam war eras and the work of voluntary agencies such as the Mennonite Central Committee. Experiences in CPS also influenced returning objectors who became leaders in the peace and civil-rights movements; CPS veterans included the artist Ben Shahn's contemporaries in federal service, the poet William Stafford, and future senator and reformers in mental health care.
