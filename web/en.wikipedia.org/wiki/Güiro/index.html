Güiro

ClassificationPercussion instrument, scraper idiophone
OriginLatin America (Taíno and African roots)
Related instrumentsGüira, reco-reco, guayo, washboard

The güiro (Spanish pronunciation: [ˈɡwiɾo]) is a Latin American percussion instrument consisting of an open-ended, hollow gourd with parallel notches cut in one side. It is played by rubbing a stick or tines along the notches to produce a ratchet-like sound. The güiro is commonly used in Cuban, Puerto Rican, and other Latin American musical styles including salsa, son, plena, and merengue, as well as in classical music.

A traditional güiro made from a gourd, with scraper

Construction

Traditionally made from the calabash gourd (higüero), modern güiros are also made from wood or fibreglass with metal or plastic ridges. A typical güiro is about 30–40 centimetres long with a diameter of a handgrip. The player holds it in one hand and scrapes with a thin stick (pua) held in the other, producing both short strokes and long rattling sounds by varying speed and pressure.

History and use

Scraped gourds trace their origins to the indigenous Taíno peoples of the Caribbean and to African instruments brought during the colonial era. In Puerto Rico the güiro (or güícharo) is a symbol of national culture and an essential instrument in música jíbara. In Dominican merengue, the metal güira plays a comparable role. Composers such as Igor Stravinsky (The Rite of Spring) and Heitor Villa-Lobos have also written for the güiro in orchestral works.
