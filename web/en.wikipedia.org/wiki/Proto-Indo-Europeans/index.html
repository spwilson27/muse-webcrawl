The Proto-Indo-Europeans were a hypothetical prehistoric population that spoke Proto-Indo-European (PIE), the reconstructed common ancestor of the Indo-European language family — today the most widely spoken family on earth, encompassing languages from English, Spanish, and Russian to Hindi, Persian, and Bengali. Because the Proto-Indo-Europeans left no writing, everything known about them is reconstructed indirectly: from comparative linguistics, which recovers their vocabulary and, through it, glimpses of their material culture and beliefs; from archaeology of the cultures plausibly identified with them; and, since 2015, from ancient DNA, which has traced the migrations that spread their languages.

Homeland and chronology

The majority scholarly view, the Kurgan hypothesis developed by Marija Gimbutas and refined by David Anthony and others, places the PIE homeland on the Pontic–Caspian steppe north of the Black and Caspian Seas, in the fourth millennium BCE, associating its later phase with the Yamnaya culture (c. 3300–2600 BCE) of wagon-driving pastoralists who buried their dead under mounds (kurgans). Reconstructed vocabulary supports this setting and date: PIE had words for wheeled vehicles (*kʷékʷlos, "wheel"; *h₂éḱs-, "axle"), wool, horses (which they likely domesticated), and a temperate steppe fauna and flora, but apparently no shared words for the sea, olive, or vine — evidence that also underpins the main rival, Colin Renfrew's Anatolian hypothesis (which proposes a much earlier spread with farming from Anatolia), now largely displaced among linguists though debated in refined "hybrid" forms. Large ancient-DNA studies (Haak et al. and Allentoft et al., 2015) confirmed a massive migration of steppe pastoralists into Europe around 3000 BCE, closely matching the archaeological Corded Ware horizon.

Society and culture, as reconstructed

Comparative evidence suggests a patriarchal, patrilineal society of herders organised in clans under chiefs, practising guest-friendship (the root *ghosti- gives both "guest" and "host/enemy" words across the family), cattle raiding, and feasting. Kinship terms, a three-tier social ideology sometimes claimed (priests, warriors, herders-cultivators, per Georges Dumézil's trifunctional hypothesis), and legal-religious concepts of cosmic order have all been reconstructed with varying confidence. Their technology included the domesticated horse, spoked-wheel wagons in later phases, bronze metallurgy, and dairying — lactase persistence and milk residues attest to the centrality of herds. From the steppe, daughter groups migrated west into Europe (ancestors of the Corded Ware and Bell Beaker peoples), east to the Altai (Afanasievo, plausibly early Tocharians), and southeast through Central Asia to Iran and India (Sintashta and Andronovo, associated with Indo-Iranian speakers and the chariot).

Religion

Reconstructed religion centres on a sky father, *Dyēus Ph₂tḗr — the direct ancestor of Zeus, Jupiter (Diespiter), and the Vedic Dyaus Pita — with a dawn goddess (*h₂éwsōs: Eos, Aurora, Ushas), divine twins, and a storm god who slays a serpent, a myth echoed from the Rigveda to Norse tradition. Sacral kingship, horse sacrifice (compare the Vedic ashvamedha and Roman October Horse), and poetic formulas preserved in cognate phrases across Greek, Indic, and Germanic verse allow comparative mythologists to recover fragments of a shared poetic and ritual inheritance.
