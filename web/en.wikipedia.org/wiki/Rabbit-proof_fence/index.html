The rabbit-proof fence is a pest-exclusion fence system built in Western Australia between 1901 and 1907 to keep rabbits and other agricultural vermin out of the state's eastern pastoral and farming districts. The State Barrier Fence, as it is now known, originally comprised three fences; the No. 1 fence alone ran 1,833 km north–south, making it among the longest fences in the world.

Construction and effectiveness

Following the spread of rabbits from the east, the Western Australian government surveyed and built the fences under the supervision of Richard John Anketell. Camels and later vehicles patrolled the barrier. The fence never entirely stopped rabbits, which had already crossed into the state before its completion, but it reduced their incursion and the fence continues in use against emus, wild dogs and other pests.

Cultural significance

The fence is famous as the route followed in 1931 by three Aboriginal girls — Molly, Daisy and Gracie — who escaped from the Moore River Native Settlement and walked some 1,600 km home to Jigalong along the fence. Their story is told in Doris Pilkington Garimara's book Follow the Rabbit-Proof Fence (1996) and the 2002 film Rabbit-Proof Fence.
