Contagious magic

Contagious magic is one of the two main forms of sympathetic magic identified by the anthropologist Sir James George Frazer in The Golden Bough (first published 1890). It rests on the "law of contagion": the belief that things which have once been in contact with each other continue to act on each other at a distance after the physical connection has been severed — that an effect on a part can influence the whole it once belonged to.

Principles and examples

Under the law of contagion, objects that were once part of a person — hair clippings, nail parings, teeth, clothing, footprints, or bodily fluids — retain a sympathetic link to that person, so that magic worked on the object is believed to affect its former owner, for good or ill. This explains widespread practices and taboos: the careful disposal of hair and nail clippings to keep them from witches or sorcerers, the use of a target's possessions in love magic and curses, and the veneration of relics, whose power derives from past contact with a holy person. Frazer contrasted contagious magic with homeopathic (or imitative) magic, governed by the "law of similarity" — like produces like — of which effigy magic is the standard example. In practice the two principles are frequently combined in a single rite.

Later analysis

Later anthropologists criticised Frazer's intellectualist framing but retained the categories as descriptions of ritual logic. Psychologists have found that an intuitive "law of contagion" persists in modern, educated populations: people are reluctant to wear a laundered sweater once worn by a disliked person, or to eat food touched by a sterilised contaminant — findings associated with the work of Paul Rozin and colleagues on disgust and magical thinking.
