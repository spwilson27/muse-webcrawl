Nizari Ismailism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Alamut Castle, seat of the Nizari state

Nizari Ismailism is the largest branch of Isma'ili Shia Islam, with adherents — known as Nizaris — today found in more than 25 countries across Asia, Africa, Europe, and North America. The Nizaris recognise a living, hereditary Imam descended from the Prophet Muhammad through his daughter Fatima and son-in-law Ali; the present (49th) Imam is Prince Shah Rahim al-Husseini, Aga Khan V, who succeeded his father Aga Khan IV in 2025.

Origins

The branch arose from a succession dispute in the Fatimid Caliphate in 1094. On the death of the Imam-caliph al-Mustansir, the Egyptian vizier installed the younger son al-Musta'li, while supporters of the elder son Nizar — led in Persia by Hasan-i Sabbah — upheld Nizar's claim. From the mountain fortress of Alamut, seized in 1090, Hasan built a network of strongholds in Persia and Syria that formed the Nizari state, famous in Crusader-era legend as the "Assassins".

History

The Alamut period ended in 1256 when Hülegü's Mongol armies destroyed the Nizari strongholds. The Imamate survived in concealment (satr), re-emerging publicly in Persia in the 15th century at Anjudan. In the 19th century the 46th Imam, Hasan Ali Shah, received the title Aga Khan from the Qajar shah and later settled in India, where his community — largely Khoja merchants — was reorganised. Under Aga Khan III and Aga Khan IV the community built extensive institutions in education, health, and development.

Beliefs and institutions

Nizaris affirm the central Shia doctrine of the Imamate: the Imam is the authoritative interpreter of the faith. Worship centres on the jamatkhana. The Aga Khan Development Network, founded by the Imam, is one of the world's largest private development organisations, working in health, education, culture, and economic development regardless of the faith of beneficiaries.
