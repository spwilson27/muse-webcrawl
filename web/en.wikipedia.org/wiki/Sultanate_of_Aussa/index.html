The Sultanate of Aussa was a kingdom of the Afar people in the Horn of Africa, centred on the lower Awash River valley in present-day north-eastern Ethiopia. It succeeded the earlier Imamate of Aussa and was ruled by the Mudaito dynasty, whose sultans took the title Amoyta. At its height the sultanate controlled the vital trade route between the Red Sea port of Tajoura and the Ethiopian highlands, levying tolls on the salt and slave caravans that passed through its territory.

History

Origins

The Imamate of Aussa had been founded in 1577 when Muhammad Jasa moved his capital from Harar to Aussa. In 1734 the Mudaito Afar clans overthrew the imamate and established the sultanate, with Kedafu as its first sultan. The new state maintained a standing force of Afar warriors armed with spears, daggers (jile) and later firearms, and its authority extended over the other Afar principalities of the lower Awash and the Danakil Depression.

Nineteenth century

During the 1800s the sultans of Aussa manoeuvred between the Ethiopian emperors, the Egyptians under Muhammad Ali's successors, and European explorers. Sultan Hanfadhe negotiated with Werner Munzinger and other emissaries; the sultanate's control of the Aussa salt plains and caravan routes made it a strategic prize. In 1869 the opening of the Suez Canal raised the value of its Red Sea approaches, and Italy acquired Assab on its doorstep.

Colonial era and after

Italy occupied Aussa in 1936 during the Second Italo-Ethiopian War. In 1943 the British-assisted Ethiopian government restored Sultan Mohammed Yayyo, and in the 1950s–60s Alimirah Hanfadhe, the last reigning sultan, negotiated a degree of autonomy within Haile Selassie's empire. After the 1974 Ethiopian revolution, the Derg abolished the sultanate's autonomy in 1975, and Alimirah went into exile. Today the title survives as a traditional dignity among the Afar of Ethiopia's Afar Region, whose administrative capital, Semera, lies near historic Asaita.
