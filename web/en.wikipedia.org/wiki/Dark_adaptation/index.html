Dark adaptation

Dark adaptation is the process by which the eye adjusts to decreasing levels of illumination, increasing its sensitivity to light by up to several orders of magnitude. It is the opposite of light adaptation. When a person moves from a bright environment into darkness, vision at first is poor but improves progressively over about 20–30 minutes as the photoreceptor system shifts from cone-mediated (photopic) to rod-mediated (scotopic) vision and photopigments regenerate.

Mechanism

Three processes contribute. First, the pupil dilates within seconds, admitting more light — a minor factor given its limited area change. Second, neural circuits in the retina adjust their gain. Third, and most important, photochemical regeneration: bright light "bleaches" the photopigments of rods and cones; in darkness, rhodopsin in the rods is rebuilt through the visual cycle in the retinal pigment epithelium, restoring sensitivity. As rhodopsin regenerates, the absolute threshold of vision falls dramatically.

The adaptation curve

Plotted over time, sensitivity follows a biphasic curve. Cones adapt quickly, reaching their plateau in about 5–10 minutes (the "cone plateau"). The rod–cone break (Kohlrausch kink) occurs around 7–10 minutes, after which rods — far more sensitive — continue improving for 20–30 minutes or more, ultimately detecting light roughly 100,000 times dimmer than the initial threshold. Vitamin A deficiency impairs rhodopsin regeneration and causes night blindness (nyctalopia); conditions such as retinitis pigmentosa also slow or reduce dark adaptation.

Applications

Dark adaptation is exploited in astronomy, where observers avoid white light (using dim red light, which rods barely detect) to preserve night vision; in military and aviation settings; and clinically, where delayed dark adaptation is an early marker of retinal disease, including age-related macular degeneration.
