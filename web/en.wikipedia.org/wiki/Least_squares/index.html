Least squares

The method of least squares is a standard approach in regression analysis to approximate the solution of overdetermined systems — sets of equations in which there are more equations than unknowns — by minimizing the sum of the squares of the residuals made in the results of each individual equation. The most important application is in data fitting, where the best fit is the one that minimises the sum of squared differences between observed values and the values predicted by a model.

History

Adrien-Marie Legendre first published the method in 1805, in work on comet orbits. Carl Friedrich Gauss claimed to have used it since 1795 and published it in 1809 in connection with celestial mechanics, famously applying it to recover the orbit of the asteroid Ceres from limited observations. The priority dispute between them was well known in their lifetimes; the underlying statistical justification — that least squares gives the maximum-likelihood estimate for normally distributed errors — is associated with Gauss and the Gauss–Markov theorem.

Ordinary least squares

In a linear model y = Xβ + ε, the ordinary least squares estimator chooses the coefficient vector β that minimises the sum of squared residuals. When the design matrix has full column rank, the solution is given in closed form by the normal equations, β̂ = (XTX)−1XTy. Under the Gauss–Markov assumptions, this estimator is the best linear unbiased estimator.

Variants and computation

Extensions include weighted least squares (for unequal error variances), total least squares (errors in the independent variables), nonlinear least squares, and regularised forms such as ridge regression. Numerically, least squares problems are solved stably using QR decomposition or the singular value decomposition rather than by forming the normal equations explicitly.
