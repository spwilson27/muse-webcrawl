A super star cluster: an extremely dense, massive young star cluster.

A super star cluster (SSC) is a very massive young star cluster, thought to be the precursor of a globular cluster. These clusters typically contain a very high number of young, massive stars that ionize a surrounding region of ionized hydrogen (an H II region).

Properties

Super star clusters typically have masses of 105 to 106 solar masses packed into a region only a few parsecs across, giving them stellar densities far exceeding those of ordinary open clusters. Their luminosities can rival those of small dwarf galaxies. Because they form in bursts of intense star formation, they are common in starburst galaxies and in interacting or merging galaxy systems, where gas is compressed to high densities.

Formation

SSCs are believed to form when giant molecular clouds collapse under conditions of very high pressure, such as those found in galaxy mergers and the disks of starburst galaxies. The high efficiency of star formation within these clouds allows a large fraction of the gas to be converted into stars before feedback from winds and supernovae disperses the remaining material.

Examples

Well-known super star clusters include Westerlund 1 in the Milky Way, R136 in the Tarantula Nebula of the Large Magellanic Cloud, and the central cluster of NGC 3603. In more distant galaxies, the Antennae Galaxies contain hundreds of young super star clusters formed during their ongoing collision.
