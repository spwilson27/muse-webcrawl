Chemical potential

In thermodynamics, the chemical potential of a species is the energy that can be absorbed or released due to a change of the particle number of the given species, for example in a chemical reaction or phase transition. It is defined as the partial molar Gibbs free energy:

μi = (∂G / ∂Ni)T, P, Nj≠i

The concept was introduced by the American scientist Josiah Willard Gibbs in the 1870s. Chemical potential has units of energy per amount of substance (e.g. joules per mole).

Equilibrium

Particles tend to move from regions of higher chemical potential to regions of lower chemical potential, just as heat flows from hot to cold. At equilibrium between phases, the chemical potential of each component is equal in all phases; this condition underlies phase diagrams, solubility and osmosis. For an ideal solution, the chemical potential varies logarithmically with mole fraction: μ = μ° + RT ln x. The total chemical potential of electrons in a solid is the Fermi level, a central concept in semiconductor physics.

Applications

Chemical potential governs chemical equilibrium (through the Gibbs energy of reaction), electrochemistry (electrode potentials), diffusion, and the thermodynamics of mixtures, and it is generalised in statistical mechanics through the grand canonical ensemble.
