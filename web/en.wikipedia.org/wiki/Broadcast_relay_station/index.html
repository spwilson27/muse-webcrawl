Broadcast relay station

A broadcast relay station, also called a relay transmitter, rebroadcaster or translator, is a broadcast transmitter that repeats the signal of a parent radio or television station in an area where the parent signal is weak, obstructed by terrain, or outside its normal service area. Relay stations extend coverage without requiring separate programme production.

Operation

A relay station receives the parent station's signal — historically off-air, by microwave link, or by satellite — and retransmits it, usually on a different frequency or channel to avoid interference with the received signal. Television relay stations range from full-power transmitters serving entire regions to low-power fill-in transmitters for valleys and shadow areas.

Types

  • Translators (United States): low-power stations that rebroadcast another station, on FM governed by FCC rules; they may not originate programming except in limited circumstances.
  • Boosters: transmitters that repeat a signal on the same channel within the parent station's service area.
  • Satellite stations: full-power stations that rebroadcast a parent station to a separate community, sometimes with local advertising insertion.
  • Single-frequency networks: multiple synchronised transmitters radiating the same signal on one channel, common in digital television (DVB-T) and DAB digital radio.

History

Relay stations were essential to the growth of broadcasting in mountainous countries and large nations. In the United Kingdom, the BBC and ITV networks built chains of main transmitters supplemented by hundreds of relay stations to reach remote valleys in Wales, Scotland and the West Country. Shortwave relay stations, such as the BBC's overseas transmitting sites, beamed international services around the world.
