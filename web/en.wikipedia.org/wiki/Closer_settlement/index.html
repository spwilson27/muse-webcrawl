Closer settlement
RegionAustralia and New Zealand
PeriodLate 19th – mid 20th century
PolicySubdivision of large estates into small farms

Closer settlement was a policy pursued in Australia and New Zealand from the late nineteenth century under which governments purchased or compulsorily acquired large pastoral estates and subdivided them into small farms, which were then sold or leased to individual farmers on favorable terms. The aim was to break up the great squatters' holdings, increase rural population and agricultural production, and create a class of independent yeoman farmers.

Development

Closer settlement followed the selection movement of the 1860s, which had often failed to dislodge pastoralists who used "peacocking" and dummy selectors to keep their runs. New Zealand's Liberal Government under John Ballance and Richard Seddon legislated from 1892 to purchase estates such as Cheviot Hills for subdivision. In Australia, colonies and then states passed Closer Settlement Acts in the 1890s and 1900s; schemes were frequently combined with irrigation development, as in Victoria's Murray districts, and after both world wars with soldier settlement.

Outcomes

The policy dramatically reshaped rural landscapes in districts such as Canterbury, the Waikato, Gippsland, and the Riverina, enabling dairying and mixed farming on family blocks. However, many closer-settlement farms proved too small to be economic, settlers carried heavy debts, and marginal land schemes (especially soldier settlements) suffered high failure rates. By the mid-twentieth century, policy shifted toward farm consolidation as agriculture mechanized.
