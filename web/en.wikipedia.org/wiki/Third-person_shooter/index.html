Third-person shooter

GenreAction / shooter video game
CameraBehind or over the shoulder of the player character
Core mechanicsAiming and shooting, movement, cover, traversal
ExamplesGears of War, Resident Evil 4, Uncharted, Max Payne, Fortnite

A third-person shooter (TPS) is a video-game genre in which the player sees the avatar from an external camera—usually behind the back or over one shoulder—while combat is driven by aiming and firing weapons. The visible character makes movement, melee, dodging, climbing and cover use easier to read than in a first-person view, while the camera introduces its own problems of occlusion, aiming alignment and collision with scenery.

Design characteristics

Modern TPS design often combines free movement with an aim mode that tightens the camera over the shoulder and slows the character. Cover systems, popularized by games in the mid-2000s, let avatars snap to walls and fire around or over obstacles. Because the player can sometimes see around corners that the character cannot, designers tune enemy behaviour, camera offset and level geometry to keep the advantage acceptable.

Third-person perspective supports character identity and animation. Players watch the protagonist roll, sprint, reload and react, which helps action-adventure games blend shooting with platforming and cinematic presentation. It also makes cosmetic customization commercially and expressively important, especially in multiplayer games.

History

Early shooters and action games used side, top-down and isometric views before polygonal 3D made the chase camera practical. Tomb Raider emphasized traversal with shooting as one element; WinBack and Syphon Filter experimented with cover and lock-on. Max Payne (2001) fused over-shoulder gunplay with slow-motion “bullet time”. Resident Evil 4 (2005) refined a close over-shoulder aim that influenced the genre, and Gears of War (2006) standardized stop-and-pop cover combat for many successors.

Later series such as Uncharted, Mass Effect, The Division, Warframe and Fortnite mixed TPS foundations with platforming, role-playing systems, cooperative play and live-service structures. Some games allow switching between first- and third-person views, blurring a boundary that remains useful because camera position changes tactics and feel.

Camera and control problems

The TPS camera must avoid walls, preserve aim direction and communicate threats without making the player fight the controls. Designers choose whether projectiles originate from the character’s weapon or from the camera centre, and how to reconcile reticle accuracy with what the avatar appears to be doing. On gamepads, aim assistance, snap and sensitivity curves are crucial; on mouse and keyboard, precision aiming can make cover and enemy health balance differently.

Criticism and appeal

Players who prefer first-person shooters sometimes criticize third-person views as less immersive or too permissive in multiplayer peeking. Defenders point to spatial awareness, character embodiment and accessibility for players uncomfortable with first-person motion. The genre’s persistence reflects those trade-offs: it is less a compromise than a distinct grammar of action in which seeing the body is part of play.
