Elizabeth Ligon Bjork is an American cognitive psychologist and professor of psychology at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA). Together with her husband and long-time collaborator Robert A. Bjork, she is known for research on human learning and memory, particularly the concept of desirable difficulties — conditions of learning that slow apparent acquisition but enhance long-term retention and transfer.

Career and research

Bjork has spent her career at UCLA, where she has served as chair of the psychology department and has been recognised for her teaching with campus-wide distinctions, including the UCLA Distinguished Teaching Award. Her research concerns how memory works in real-world learning settings: the spacing effect, retrieval practice (the testing effect), interleaving of different problem types, and variation in study conditions.

A central theme of her work is the dissociation between performance during training and durable learning — learners' judgements of their own learning are often misled by fluent, easy study conditions, whereas introducing difficulties such as spacing, testing and interleaving produces superior retention. The Bjorks have termed the framework the "new theory of disuse", developed with Robert Bjork, distinguishing storage strength from retrieval strength.

Selected contributions

Elizabeth Bjork has published extensively on metacognition and self-regulated learning, on the misinterpretation of forgetting, and on applying cognitive psychology to education. She co-founded the UCLA Learning and Forgetting Lab. Her work with Robert Bjork has influenced evidence-based study advice: practise retrieval, space study sessions, and interleave topics rather than blocking them.
