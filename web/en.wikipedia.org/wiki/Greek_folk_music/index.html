Greek folk music

Greek folk music (Greek: παραδοσιακή μουσική, paradosiaki mousiki, "traditional music"; also δημοτική μουσική, dimotiki mousiki, "folk music") comprises the traditional musical traditions of mainland and island Greece. Greek folk music includes a variety of styles, emerging from the cultures of Byzantine, Ottoman, Venetian and indigenous Greek traditions. Traditionally, a distinction is made between the mainland tradition and that of the Greek islands.

Mainland tradition

Mainland folk music is often associated with the clarinet (klarino), which by the 19th century had become the dominant melodic instrument, accompanied by violin, laouto (lute) and percussion. The repertoire includes the slow, heavy dances of Epirus such as the tsamiko, the laments and polyphonic singing of the north-west, the songs of the klephts (heroic ballads of the mountain brigands of the Ottoman period), and the brisk dances of Thessaly, Macedonia and Thrace — many in asymmetrical metres such as 7/8 and 9/8.

Island tradition

Island music (nisiotika) is typically led by violin and laouto and is generally lighter and merrier in character. Crete has its own vigorous tradition centred on the Cretan lyra and laouto, accompanying dances such as the pentozali and improvised rhyming couplets called mantinades. The Aegean islands maintain the tradition of the syrtos and ballos dances, while the Ionian islands developed the serenade-like kantada, influenced by Italian music.

Other forms

Closely related traditions include Byzantine chant, the urban music of rebetiko — which blended refugee music from Asia Minor with the mainland tradition after 1922 — and the ritual anastenaria fire-walking music of northern Greece. Greek folk dances, of which over a hundred are documented, are inextricably bound to the music and are performed at weddings, baptisms and village festivals (panigyria).
