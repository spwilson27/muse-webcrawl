Chelow kebab

Chelow kebab (Persian: چلوکباب, romanized čelo-kabāb) is an Iranian dish consisting of steamed, long-grain basmati rice (chelow) served with kebab. It is widely considered the national dish of Iran.

The chelow is prepared by parboiling long-grain rice, then steaming it so that the grains remain separate and fluffy; a crispy golden crust called tahdig often forms at the bottom of the pot. A pat of butter is commonly placed on top of the hot rice at serving, and the dish is accompanied by grilled tomatoes, raw onion, and the sour spice sumac.

Varieties

The kebab component may take several forms:

  • Kabab koobideh — seasoned minced meat (usually lamb, beef, or a mixture) moulded onto flat skewers and grilled over charcoal.
  • Kabab barg — thin slices of marinated lamb or beef fillet.
  • Jujeh kabab — pieces of chicken marinated in lemon juice, saffron and onion.
  • Soltani ("the sultan's") — a combination of one skewer of barg and one of koobideh on a single plate.

History

The dish in its modern form is associated with the Qajar era of the 19th century. Tradition credits Naser al-Din Shah with popularising the combination, and Tehran's kebab houses (chelow-kababi) became a fixture of Iranian dining culture. The oldest such restaurant in Tehran is often said to date to the early 20th century.

Serving

Chelow kebab is traditionally served with doogh, a salted yoghurt drink, along with fresh herbs (sabzi khordan), raw onion and grilled tomatoes. Sumac is sprinkled over the rice to taste.
