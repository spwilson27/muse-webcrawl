Search warrant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A search warrant is a court order, issued by a judge or magistrate, that authorises law-enforcement officers to search a specified place for specified items and to seize them if found. In the United States the Fourth Amendment requires warrants to issue "upon probable cause, supported by oath or affirmation, and particularly describing the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized" — the particularity requirement answering the writs of assistance and general warrants of colonial grievance.

Practice and exceptions

Most searches in practice occur without warrants under exceptions (consent, search incident to arrest, exigent circumstances, the automobile exception, plain view), so the warrant process — affidavit, neutral magistrate, execution within a set period, knock-and-announce rules, return and inventory — governs the most intrusive planned intrusions, especially of homes, and increasingly of digital devices (the Supreme Court required a warrant for cell-phone searches incident to arrest in Riley v. California, 2014, and for historical cell-site records in Carpenter, 2018). Violations trigger the exclusionary rule subject to good-faith and other exceptions. Other democracies run analogous judicial-authorisation regimes (UK warrants under PACE; German Durchsuchungsbeschluss, with a constitutional home-inviolability guarantee).
