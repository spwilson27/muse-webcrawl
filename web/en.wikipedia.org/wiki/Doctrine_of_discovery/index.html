Doctrine of discovery

Doctrine of discovery
Type: Legal doctrine / colonial law
Key documents: Papal bulls of 1452–1493, notably Inter caetera (1493)
Key case: Johnson v. M'Intosh (1823)

The doctrine of discovery is a disputed legal and religious concept that has been used to justify the colonisation of Indigenous lands by European powers and later the United States, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand. It held that Christian sovereigns acquired title to lands 'discovered' by them, diminishing Indigenous peoples' sovereignty to a mere right of occupancy.

Origins

The doctrine is traced to fifteenth-century papal bulls—Dum Diversas (1452), Romanus Pontifex (1455), and Inter caetera (1493)—which authorised Portugal and Spain to claim non-Christian lands.

In United States law

Chief Justice John Marshall incorporated the doctrine into U.S. law in Johnson v. M'Intosh (1823), holding that Native Americans could not sell land directly to private parties because ultimate title rested with the federal government by 'discovery'.

Criticism and repudiation

Indigenous nations, scholars, and churches have long criticised the doctrine as racist and a basis for dispossession. The Vatican formally repudiated it in March 2023. It remains debated in cases concerning Native American rights and terra nullius.
