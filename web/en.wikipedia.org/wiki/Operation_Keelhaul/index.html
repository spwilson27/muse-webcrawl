Operation Keelhaul was the forced repatriation, under the terms of the Yalta Conference, of Soviet citizens and others — including prisoners of war, forced labourers, collaborators and anti-communist refugees — from Western Europe to the Soviet Union after the Second World War, carried out by British and American forces between 1945 and 1947.

Background

At Yalta in February 1945, the Western Allies agreed to return all Soviet citizens found in their zones to the USSR, in return for the repatriation of Western prisoners liberated by the Red Army. The agreement was applied expansively: it came to include people who had never been Soviet citizens, such as White Russian émigrés who had left after the Russian Civil War, and Cossack formations who had fought alongside Germany.

The repatriations

Repatriations were frequently carried out by force and deception. In May and June 1945 the British handed over some 40,000–50,000 Cossacks, including women and children, at Lienz in Austria; many were killed or sent to the Gulag, and there were suicides among those resisting transfer. In the United States, Soviet prisoners held at Fort Dix, New Jersey, rioted against repatriation in June 1945. In total, more than two million people were returned to Soviet control; returnees faced execution, imprisonment in labour camps, or exile, particularly those accused of collaboration, such as members of the Russian Liberation Army.

Legacy and controversy

The episode remained secret for decades and became a lasting controversy. The term "Keelhaul" was popularised by Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn and by the historian Nikolai Tolstoy, whose book Victims of Yalta (1977) accused British officials of knowingly sending people to their deaths. A 1989 libel trial in London, Aldington v Tolstoy, ended in damages against Tolstoy and continuing historical dispute. Keelhaul is frequently cited in debates over the morality of the Yalta agreements and the principle of non-refoulement.
