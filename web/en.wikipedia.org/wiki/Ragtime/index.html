Ragtime

Sheet music for Scott Joplin's "Maple Leaf Rag" (1899).

Ragtime — also spelled rag-time or rag time — is a musical genre that enjoyed its peak popularity between 1895 and 1919. Its main characteristic is its syncopated or "ragged" rhythm, with a steadily accented bass in the left hand against a syncopated melody in the right.

History

Ragtime evolved in African-American communities in the southern and midwestern United States, combining elements of marches, cakewalks, and African rhythmic traditions. It spread nationally through sheet music and piano rolls. Scott Joplin, its most famous composer, published the "Maple Leaf Rag" in 1899 and composed dozens of rags, including "The Entertainer" and the opera Treemonisha. Other major figures included James Scott and Joseph Lamb, and the bandleader and publisher John Stark.

Characteristics

Classic piano rags are typically written in 2/4 or 4/4 time and follow a multi-strain form borrowed from marches (AABBACCDD). Ragtime was composed music, written down rather than improvised, setting it apart from early jazz.

Legacy

Ragtime strongly influenced early jazz, stride piano, and novelty piano. A major revival in the 1970s, aided by the film The Sting (1973), which used Joplin's music, returned Joplin to popular attention; he was awarded a posthumous Pulitzer Prize in 1976. See also Scott Joplin and jazz.
