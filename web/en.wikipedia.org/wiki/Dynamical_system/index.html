Dynamical system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A swirling Lorenz-style attractor drawn as a luminous butterfly-shaped curve]

A dynamical system is a mathematical model of something that evolves over time according to a fixed rule: a state space of possible configurations, together with a law (an equation or an iterated map) saying how each state changes.

The field began with Henri Poincaré's qualitative study of planetary motion around 1890, which uncovered the seeds of chaos. Modern theory studies stability, periodicity, attractors and bifurcations rather than exact formulas.

Chaos and order

Chaotic systems are deterministic yet practically unpredictable, because tiny errors in the initial state grow exponentially — quantified by Lyapunov exponents. The Lorenz attractor, a butterfly-shaped object in a three-variable weather model, is the classic picture.

Ergodic theory, a deep branch of the subject, asks what fraction of time a system spends in each region of its state space and connects dynamics to probability and number theory.
