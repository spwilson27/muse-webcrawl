Meker burner

A Meker burner (also spelled Méker burner) is a type of laboratory gas burner that produces a hotter, more uniform flame than the common Bunsen burner. It was designed by the French chemist Georges Méker in the early 20th century and is widely used in chemistry laboratories for heating that requires high, even temperatures.

Meker burner

A Meker burner

Inventor: Georges Méker
Fuel: natural gas / LPG
Flame temperature: up to ~1,100–1,200 °C

Design

Unlike a Bunsen burner, whose flame issues from a single open tube, the Meker burner has a wider barrel topped with a metal grid (a honeycomb-like mesh). Gas mixes with air at the base, and the mixture burns across the grid in many small flames, producing a broad, flat, uniform flame with no single hot cone. The grid also prevents flashback. An adjustable air inlet at the base controls the flame, and a needle valve controls gas flow.

Uses

The Meker burner's higher temperature and even heating make it suitable for sterilising inoculating loops, heating crucibles, and experiments requiring sustained high temperatures. It is less commonly used for routine gentle heating, where the Bunsen burner's smaller, more easily adjusted flame is preferred. In modern laboratories, electric hot plates and heating mantles have replaced gas burners for many purposes, but the Meker burner remains standard in teaching laboratories.
