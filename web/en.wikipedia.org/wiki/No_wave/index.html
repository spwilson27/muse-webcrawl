No wave

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The no wave scene centred on downtown Manhattan clubs

No wave was a short-lived avant-garde music and art movement that emerged in downtown New York City in the late 1970s. A punning rejection of the commercial new wave then absorbing punk rock, no wave embraced abrasive atonality, dissonant guitars, funk-derived rhythms stripped to skeletons, performance-art provocation, and a deliberate refusal of conventional musicianship.

History

The movement coalesced around lofts and clubs such as Tier 3 and the Mudd Club, and around artists who were often as involved in film, performance, and visual art as in music. Its defining document is the 1978 compilation No New York, produced by Brian Eno and featuring four bands: the Contortions, Teenage Jesus and the Jerks, Mars, and D.N.A.

Key artists

  • James Chance and the Contortions — confrontational jazz-funk
  • Lydia Lunch's Teenage Jesus and the Jerks
  • Mars and D.N.A. (with Arto Lindsay)
  • Glenn Branca and Rhys Chatham — guitar orchestras bridging no wave and minimalism
  • Swans, Sonic Youth, and Live Skull carried aspects of the sound into the 1980s

Legacy

Though commercially negligible at the time, no wave proved highly influential: it fed into noise rock, post-punk revival, and independent film (the "Cinema of Transgression" and directors such as Jim Jarmusch emerged from the same milieu). Retrospectives and reissues since the 2000s have consolidated its reputation as one of the most uncompromising movements in rock history.
