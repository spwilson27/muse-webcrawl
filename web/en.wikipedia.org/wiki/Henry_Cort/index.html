Henry Cort (c. 1740 – 23 May 1800) was an English ironware producer and ironmaster. During the Industrial Revolution, Cort made significant improvements to the production of iron, especially through his puddling process for converting pig iron into wrought iron using coal rather than charcoal, and his rolling process using grooved rollers to form iron bars. These innovations helped free the British iron industry from dependence on scarce charcoal and expensive imports of high-quality Swedish and Russian bar iron, and they are often credited with helping to launch the mass production of iron that underpinned the railway age.

Early life

Cort was born in Lancaster around 1740, the son of a builder. Little is known of his early life; he became a London agent and money-lender serving Royal Navy officers, accumulating capital. In 1775 he took over an ironworks at Fontley, near Gosport in Hampshire, supplying iron goods to the Portsmouth naval dockyard.

Inventions

In 1783 Cort patented a rolling process using grooved rollers, which could shape iron bars about fifteen times faster than hammering. In 1784 he patented the puddling process: pig iron was melted in a reverberatory furnace fired with coal, and the molten metal was stirred ("puddled") with iron oxide-rich rabbling tools, so that the carbon, silicon, and other impurities oxidised away without the fuel contaminating the iron. The result was a pasty ball of refined iron that could be shingled and rolled into bar iron of good quality — from cheap British pig iron rather than imported bar iron.

The combination of puddling and rolling transformed the economics of ironmaking: output of wrought iron in Britain rose dramatically in the following decades, and Cort's methods, though quickly imitated and improved elsewhere, set the pattern of the nineteenth-century ironworks.

Ruin

Cort's success was destroyed by his business partnership with Adam Jellicoe, the deputy paymaster of the Navy, who had financed the Fontley works with embezzled public funds. When Jellicoe died in 1789 owing the Admiralty a large sum, the government seized Cort's assets, patents, and works to recover the debt. Cort was left effectively bankrupt, and although ironmasters petitioned on his behalf, he received only a modest pension late in life. He died in 1800, while the industry flourished on his inventions, for which other men made fortunes.
