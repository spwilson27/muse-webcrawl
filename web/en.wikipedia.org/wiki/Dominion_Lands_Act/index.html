The Dominion Lands Act (French: Loi des terres fédérales) was Canadian federal legislation enacted in 1872 to encourage the settlement and agricultural development of the prairie West. Modelled on the American Homestead Act, it offered a quarter-section of land — 160 acres (65 hectares) — virtually free to settlers who paid a small registration fee, built a dwelling, and cultivated a portion of the land within three years.

Provisions

Under the Dominion Lands Survey, the prairies were divided into townships of 36 sections, each one square mile. Male heads of household and sole female heads of household over 18 (later 21) could apply for a homestead patent. Railways, the Hudson's Bay Company and school lands received reserved sections, creating the characteristic checkerboard of prairie land ownership.

Impact

The Act underpinned the great wave of immigration that populated Manitoba, Saskatchewan and Alberta between the 1890s and 1914; millions of hectares were patented under it. It also facilitated the displacement of Indigenous peoples and the Métis from lands guaranteed to them, contributing to the tensions behind the Red River and North-West resistances. Administration of the lands passed to the prairie provinces under the Natural Resources Transfer Agreements of 1930, ending the Act's role.
