Brittle (food)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Brittle
TypeConfection
Main ingredientsSugar, nuts (often peanuts), butter, baking soda
VariationsPeanut brittle; sesame brittle; chikki; croquante

Brittle is a type of confection consisting of flat broken pieces of hard sugar candy embedded with nuts such as peanuts, almonds, pecans or pistachios, and sometimes seeds such as sesame. It is made by cooking a sugar syrup to the hard-crack stage and spreading the hot mixture thinly so that it sets into a sheet that can be broken — hence "brittle."

Preparation

Sugar (often with corn syrup or glucose to inhibit crystallisation) is heated with water to about 300 °F (150 °C), the hard-crack stage at which the syrup becomes brittle when cooled. Butter and a small quantity of baking soda are commonly stirred in near the end; the soda reacts with the hot sugar to release tiny bubbles that lighten the texture and give the finished candy its characteristic slight foaminess. The nuts — warmed so they do not seize the syrup — are added last, and the mixture is poured onto a greased marble slab or baking sheet, stretched or rolled thin, cooled and broken into pieces.

Varieties around the world

The best-known version, peanut brittle, is a traditional American candy, popular in the South and Midwest and associated with home candy-making at Christmas; references to it in American cookbooks appear from the 19th century. Similar confections are widespread: Indian chikki is made with jaggery and peanuts or sesame; French croquante uses caramelised sugar and almonds and is used structurally in patisserie; in the Middle East and China, sesame-seed brittles are traditional sweets; Greek pasteli binds sesame with honey. Spanish turrón de Alicante is a close relative made with honey and egg white. Modern variations use other nuts, pumpkin seeds, bacon or spices, and softer microwave recipes have been promoted since the late 20th century.
