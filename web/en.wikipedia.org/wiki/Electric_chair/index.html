The electric chair is a device used to execute a condemned person by electrocution. The prisoner is strapped into a specially built wooden chair and electrodes are attached to the head and leg; a series of high-voltage alternating-current cycles is then passed through the body, intended to cause immediate unconsciousness and death through cardiac arrest and damage to the brain. It was the predominant method of execution in the United States for much of the 20th century before being almost entirely replaced by lethal injection.

History

The chair arose from the "war of the currents" between Thomas Edison's direct current and George Westinghouse's alternating current. Horrified by hangings, New York State sought a more humane method; dentist Alfred P. Southwick promoted electrocution, and Edison's associates worked to associate AC with death. Harold P. Brown, with Edison's quiet backing, conducted public experiments killing animals with alternating current. New York adopted electrocution in 1888, and William Kemmler became the first person executed in the chair at Auburn Prison on 6 August 1890 — a botched, prolonged affair that Westinghouse remarked could have been done better with an axe. Nonetheless, the method spread to many states and to the Philippines, then under US rule.

Decline

More than 4,400 people were executed by electrocution in the United States. Famous cases include Sacco and Vanzetti, Julius and Ethel Rosenberg, and Ted Bundy. Botched executions, such as the flames seen in the cases of Jesse Tafero (1990) and Pedro Medina (1997) in Florida, intensified criticism. From the 1980s lethal injection displaced the chair as the default method; it survives only as an option or fallback in a few states, including Tennessee, South Carolina, Alabama, Florida, Kentucky and Virginia, and has been ruled unconstitutional as a sole method in some jurisdictions. Nebraska's supreme court held in 2008 that electrocution constitutes cruel and unusual punishment.
