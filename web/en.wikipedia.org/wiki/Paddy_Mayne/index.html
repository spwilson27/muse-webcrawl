Paddy Mayne

Lieutenant Colonel Robert Blair "Paddy" Mayne, DSO & Three Bars (11 January 1915 – 14 December 1955) was a British Army officer from Newtownards, County Down, Northern Ireland. A celebrated sportsman before the war — he played rugby union for Ireland and the British Lions and was an amateur boxing champion — he became one of the most decorated British soldiers of the Second World War as a founding member and later commander of the Special Air Service (SAS), leading raids that destroyed hundreds of Axis aircraft in North Africa.

Early life and sport

Mayne was educated at Regent House Grammar School and studied law at Queen's University Belfast, qualifying as a solicitor. A formidable athlete, he won Irish universities boxing titles, played rugby for Ireland six times in 1937–39, and toured South Africa with the 1938 British Lions, where he was rated among the finest forwards of the tour.

Second World War

Commissioned into the Royal Ulster Rifles, Mayne served with No. 11 (Scottish) Commando in the Litani River operation in Syria in 1941 before being recruited by David Stirling into the new SAS. In the desert raids of 1941–42, Mayne personally destroyed scores of German and Italian aircraft on the ground — by some accounts more than a hundred — at airfields such as Tamet, Sirte and Berka. After Stirling's capture in January 1943, Mayne commanded the Special Raiding Squadron in Sicily and Italy and then 1 SAS through France, the Low Countries and Germany in 1944–45.

He was awarded the Distinguished Service Order four times in all (the DSO and three Bars). A recommendation for the Victoria Cross for his actions at Oldenburg in April 1945 was downgraded to a further Bar of the DSO, a decision later campaigners — and the citation's own wording — have questioned; parliamentary campaigns for a posthumous award have repeatedly been mounted.

Post-war life and death

Mayne worked as a solicitor and as secretary of the Law Society of Northern Ireland, and travelled with a British Antarctic survey expedition. Struggling with back pain from his wartime exploits and reportedly with alcohol, he was killed in a road accident in Newtownards in December 1955, aged 40, when his car struck a lorry. He has since been commemorated by a statue in his home town and in popular histories and dramatisations of the SAS.
