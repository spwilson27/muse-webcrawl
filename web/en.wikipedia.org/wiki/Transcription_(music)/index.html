Transcription (music)

In music, transcription is the act of writing down a piece or sound that was previously unwritten — for example, notating an improvisation or a folk song from a recording — or, more broadly, adapting a composed work for a medium different from the one for which it was originally written (in which sense it overlaps with arrangement).

Transcription of recordings

Jazz and ethnomusicology rely heavily on transcription: solos by improvisers such as Charlie Parker have been transcribed note-for-note for study, and field recordings of traditional music are transcribed to preserve and analyse repertories that have no written form. Modern software that slows recordings or visualises pitch assists the process, but skilled listening remains central.

Adaptation between media

Famous examples of the adaptive sense include Franz Liszt's piano transcriptions of Beethoven's symphonies and Schubert songs, which spread orchestral music before recordings existed, and Bach's many reworkings of his own and Vivaldi's music. The boundary between transcription and arrangement is debated: transcription usually aims at fidelity to the source's notes, arrangement at reinterpretation.

Copyright

Transcribing a copyrighted work creates a derivative work; publishing or selling transcriptions of recordings can require permission from rights holders, a question litigated around sheet-music editions of popular songs.
