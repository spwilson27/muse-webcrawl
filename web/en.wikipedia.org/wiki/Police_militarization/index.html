Militarization of police

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The militarization of police (also spelled militarisation) is the use of military equipment and tactics by law enforcement officers. This includes the use of armored personnel carriers (APCs), assault rifles, submachine guns, flashbang grenades, sniper rifles, and SWAT (Special Weapons and Tactics) teams. The militarization of law enforcement is also associated with intelligence agency-style information gathering aimed at the public and political activists, and with a more aggressive style of law enforcement.

History in the United States

The modern era of police militarisation is often traced to the creation of the first SWAT teams in Los Angeles in the late 1960s under Chief Daryl Gates, and accelerated by the federal war on drugs in the 1980s. The National Defense Authorization Act for 1990 and 1997 created and expanded the so-called 1033 Program, under which the Department of Defense transfers surplus military equipment — from rifles and helicopters to mine-resistant ambush-protected (MRAP) vehicles — to civilian police agencies at little or no cost. By the mid-2010s the programme had transferred more than $5 billion worth of equipment to thousands of agencies, including small towns and campus police forces.

Debate

The deployment of heavily armed police in military-style vehicles during the 2014 protests in Ferguson, Missouri brought the issue to national prominence. Critics, including the ACLU, argue that military equipment encourages aggressive tactics, that SWAT teams are deployed overwhelmingly for routine drug search warrants, and that the warrior mindset undermines community policing. Defenders argue the equipment is defensive, protects officers and hostages, and is obtained cheaply. In 2015 President Barack Obama restricted transfers of certain equipment (such as tracked armoured vehicles, bayonets and grenade launchers); the restrictions were rescinded in 2017 under President Donald Trump, and partly reinstated under President Joe Biden.

Similar debates occur in other countries, where gendarmerie-style forces have long blurred the civil–military boundary, and paramilitary police units have expanded in response to terrorism and organised crime.
