Law of excluded middle

In logic, the law of excluded middle (Latin: principium tertii exclusi, "principle of the excluded third") states that for every proposition, either the proposition or its negation is true. Formally, for any proposition P:

P ∨ ¬P

It is one of the traditional "laws of thought", alongside the law of non-contradiction and the law of identity, and is a cornerstone of classical logic. For example, the law holds that either "Socrates is mortal" or "Socrates is not mortal" must be true, with no third possibility.

History

The principle is usually traced to Aristotle, who in Metaphysics and De Interpretatione argued that of any contradictory pair one must be true and the other false — though his discussion of future contingents (the famous sea-battle argument) has led some scholars to think he qualified the law for statements about the future. The law was assumed throughout medieval and modern logic and underlies proof by contradiction (reductio ad absurdum) and the double-negation elimination rule.

Criticisms and alternatives

In the early twentieth century, L. E. J. Brouwer and the intuitionist school rejected the law as applied to infinite domains: for an intuitionist, asserting P ∨ ¬P requires a method for deciding which disjunct holds, which may not exist for statements quantifying over infinitely many numbers. Intuitionistic logic therefore drops excluded middle, and double negation of a proposition does not imply the proposition itself. Many-valued logics, such as Jan Łukasiewicz's three-valued logic, add a third truth value ("possible" or "unknown") and likewise invalidate the law. Fuzzy logic generalises this to a continuum of truth degrees.

The law should be distinguished from the principle of bivalence, the semantic claim that every proposition has exactly one of two truth values. A logic can be bivalent in its semantics yet the excluded-middle schema can still fail in some non-classical systems, and vice versa.

Examples

Proofs that use excluded middle are sometimes non-constructive: a classic illustration shows there exist irrational numbers a and b with ab rational, by considering √2√2 — if it is rational the claim holds with a = b = √2; if not, it holds with a = √2√2 and b = √2 — without determining which case obtains.
