Harmony

Harmony
ElementsChords, chord progressions, consonance and dissonance
Related conceptsMelody, Counterpoint, Tonality

In music, harmony is the concept of two or more notes being played or sung simultaneously, and the study of how chords are constructed and how they follow one another in chord progressions. Harmony is often described as the "vertical" aspect of music, in contrast to melody, the "horizontal" aspect. However, the term can also describe the pleasing effect produced when individual musical parts sound well together.

A key distinction in harmony is between consonance and dissonance: consonant harmonies are perceived as stable and pleasant, while dissonant ones create tension that usually seeks resolution. The rules governing harmony evolved over centuries of Western practice and differ across musical cultures.

History

In the Middle Ages, harmony grew out of organum, in which voices moved in parallel perfect intervals. By the Renaissance, thirds and sixths were treated as consonances, and composers such as Josquin des Prez wrote polyphony of several independent lines. The Baroque era codified basso continuo practice and functional progression; Johann Sebastian Bach's chorales became the model for later harmony teaching. Jean-Philippe Rameau's Treatise on Harmony (1722) systematised chord roots and inversions.

In the nineteenth century, composers such as Wagner stretched tonal harmony toward chromaticism, and in the twentieth century Debussy, the Second Viennese School, and jazz musicians developed new harmonic languages, including quartal harmony, extended chords, and modal interchange.

Types

Diatonic harmony uses only notes of the prevailing scale, while chromatic harmony introduces notes outside it. Functional harmony analyses chords by their roles — tonic, subdominant, and dominant — in establishing a key. Jazz harmony emphasises extended and altered chords and substitutions. Other approaches include parallel harmony, planing, and the harmony of non-Western traditions such as the drone-based music of India.

Perception

The perception of harmony depends partly on the overtone series: intervals whose frequencies stand in simple ratios (such as the octave 2:1 and the perfect fifth 3:2) are heard as consonant by most listeners, though cultural exposure shapes preferences. In the twentieth century, psychologists and acousticians such as Helmholtz studied the roughness produced by beating between nearby partials as a basis for dissonance.
