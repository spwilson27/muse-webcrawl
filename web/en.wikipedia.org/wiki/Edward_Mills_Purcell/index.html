Edward Mills Purcell (August 30, 1912 – March 7, 1997) was an American physicist who shared the 1952 Nobel Prize in Physics with Felix Bloch for their independent discoveries of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) in liquids and solids, a technique that became fundamental to chemistry, materials science, and medical imaging.

Career

Purcell was born in Taylorville, Illinois, and educated at Purdue University (B.S. in electrical engineering, 1933) and Harvard University (Ph.D. in physics, 1938). During the Second World War he worked at the MIT Radiation Laboratory on the development of radar, leading the fundamental research group. Returning to Harvard, in December 1945 he and his co-workers Robert Pound and Henry Torrey detected nuclear magnetic resonance absorption in paraffin — the first observation of NMR in a solid.

Contributions

In 1951 Purcell and his doctoral student Harold Ewen detected the hyperfine 21-centimetre line of interstellar hydrogen, first predicted by Hendrik van de Hulst — a landmark of radio astronomy that allowed astronomers to map the spiral structure of the Milky Way. His name is also attached to the Smith–Purcell effect (radiation from electrons passing a diffraction grating) and to the Purcell effect in quantum optics. His much-loved 1977 essay "Life at Low Reynolds Number" explored the physics of microorganisms swimming in viscous fluids, and his textbook Electricity and Magnetism, part of the Berkeley Physics Course, remains a standard introduction to electromagnetism.

Purcell served as science advisor to Presidents Dwight D. Eisenhower and John F. Kennedy. He died in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1997.
