New Urbanism is an urban design movement that promotes environmentally friendly habits by creating walkable neighbourhoods containing a wide range of housing and job types. It arose in the United States in the early 1980s and has gradually influenced many aspects of real estate development, urban planning, and municipal land-use strategies.

Principles

New Urbanist neighbourhoods are typically designed around a five-minute walk (about a quarter mile) from home to work and shops, with interconnected street networks, narrower streets, buildings oriented to the street with porches and windows, mixed uses, a range of housing types and prices, civic buildings given prominent sites, and parking placed behind buildings or in structures. The movement's Charter, adopted in 1996, sets out principles at the scales of the region, the neighbourhood, and the block.

History

The resort town of Seaside, Florida, designed by Andrés Duany and Elizabeth Plater-Zyberk beginning in 1981, became the movement's emblematic early project. The Congress for the New Urbanism (CNU) was founded in 1993, modelling itself partly on the CIAM congresses of modernist architecture, which New Urbanists criticised for producing automobile-dependent sprawl. Related concepts include transit-oriented development, traditional neighbourhood development (TND), smart growth, and form-based codes, which regulate built form rather than separating land uses.

Criticism

Critics argue that many New Urbanist developments are affluent, aesthetically nostalgic enclaves that remain car-dependent because they are built on greenfield sites at metropolitan edges; that their design codes can be rigid or exclusionary; and that claims for environmental and social benefits outrun the evidence. Supporters respond that the principles adapt to infill and retrofit contexts as well as new towns.
