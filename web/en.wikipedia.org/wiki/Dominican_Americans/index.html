Dominican Americans (Spanish: domínico-americanos) are Americans who trace their ancestry to the Dominican Republic. The Dominican diaspora in the United States is one of the largest Latin American immigrant groups in the country; Dominicans constitute the fourth-largest Hispanic group in the United States, after Mexicans, Puerto Ricans, and Cubans. The population numbered over 2.2 million people in the early 2020s.

The Dominican Day Parade in Manhattan, first held in 1982, is the largest celebration of Dominican culture in the United States.

History

Dominican migration to the United States grew substantially after the death of dictator Rafael Trujillo in 1961 and the political turmoil that followed, including the 1965 civil war and U.S. intervention. Economic crises in the 1980s and 1990s drove further waves of emigration. New York City — particularly the Washington Heights neighborhood of Manhattan, nicknamed "Little Santo Domingo" — became the historic center of the community, with later growth in the Bronx, Queens, and Brooklyn, as well as in Florida, Massachusetts (Lawrence and Boston), New Jersey, and Rhode Island (Providence).

Culture

Dominican American culture blends Caribbean traditions with American influences. Merengue and bachata music are central cultural exports; Spanish-language media, bodegas, and Dominican cuisine — including dishes such as mangú, sancocho, and la bandera — are fixtures of Dominican neighborhoods. Remittances sent to the Dominican Republic amount to billions of dollars annually and are a major share of the Dominican economy.

Notable people

Notable Dominican Americans include fashion designer Oscar de la Renta, actress Zoe Saldaña, author Junot Díaz, baseball players such as David Ortiz and Albert Pujols (Dominican-born U.S. residents), and Congressman Adriano Espaillat, the first formerly undocumented immigrant elected to the U.S. Congress and the first Dominican American member of Congress.
