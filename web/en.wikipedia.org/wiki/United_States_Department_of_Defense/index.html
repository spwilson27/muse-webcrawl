The United States Department of Defense (DoD) is an executive branch department of the federal government charged with coordinating and supervising the six U.S. armed services: the Army, Navy, Air Force, Marine Corps, Space Force, and Coast Guard (the latter normally under the Department of Homeland Security except when transferred to the Navy in wartime). Headed by the Secretary of Defense, a civilian appointed by the President with Senate consent, the DoD is the largest employer in the world.

History

The department was created by the National Security Act of 1947, which merged the Department of War and the Department of the Navy into the National Military Establishment; amendments in 1949 renamed it the Department of Defense and strengthened the Secretary's authority over the military departments. The Goldwater–Nichols Act of 1986 was the most significant reorganization since, making the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff the principal military advisor to the President and clarifying the chain of command from the President through the Secretary to the combatant commanders.

Structure

The DoD comprises three military departments (Army, Navy — which includes the Marine Corps — and Air Force, which includes the Space Force), the Joint Chiefs of Staff, eleven unified combatant commands, and numerous defense agencies and field activities such as the Defense Logistics Agency, the National Security Agency, and the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA). The Office of the Secretary of Defense provides policy oversight.

The Pentagon and budget

The department is headquartered at the Pentagon, completed in 1943 and one of the world's largest office buildings. The United States spends more on defense than any other country — roughly $850–900 billion per year in recent budgets, about 3% of GDP — supporting some 4,800 sites worldwide and about 1.3 million active-duty service members.
