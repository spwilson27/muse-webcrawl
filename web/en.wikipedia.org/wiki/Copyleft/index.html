Copyleft is the practice of granting the right to freely distribute and modify intellectual property with the requirement that the same rights be preserved in derivative works created from that property. It is a form of licensing and is used to maintain copyright conditions for works ranging from software and documents to art. Copyleft licenses use copyright law itself — hence the play on "copyright" and the reversed-C symbol — to guarantee that a work and all its descendants remain free.

History

The word appeared in the 1970s in the Tiny BASIC community ("@COPYLEFT — all wrongs reserved") and in hacker culture, but the concept was formalized by Richard Stallman for the GNU Project. After seeing a program he had shared become proprietary, Stallman wrote the GNU General Public License (GPL), first released in 1989, embedding the idea that recipients may run, study, share and change the software, provided derivatives are passed on under the same terms. Eben Moglen and the Free Software Foundation developed the legal framework further in GPLv2 (1991) and GPLv3 (2007).

How it works and types

Strong copyleft (e.g. GPL, AGPL) requires that combined and derivative works be licensed as a whole under the same licence. Weak copyleft (e.g. LGPL, MPL, EPL) applies the condition only to the licensed files or library, allowing combination with proprietary code under stated conditions. The Creative Commons ShareAlike condition applies copyleft-like reciprocity to creative works; Wikipedia itself is published under CC BY-SA. Opponents in industry sometimes call the effect "viral", while advocates call it "share-alike" reciprocity.

Contrast with permissive licensing

Permissive licences (MIT, BSD, Apache) allow reuse with minimal conditions, including in proprietary products, while copyleft trades that freedom for a guarantee that improvements stay in the commons. The choice between them — copyleft for community protection versus permissive for maximal adoption — is a long-running debate in open-source strategy; major projects on the copyleft side include the Linux kernel (GPLv2), GCC, WordPress and MediaWiki, while much cloud and mobile infrastructure uses permissive terms.

Beyond software

Copyleft principles have been applied to documentation (GNU Free Documentation License), art and music (Free Art License, CC BY-SA), hardware designs, and font licences (SIL Open Font License has a weak-copyleft character). The concept also influenced "open access" and open-data movements.
