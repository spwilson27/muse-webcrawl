Inuit music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Inuit music is the traditional music of the Inuit peoples of the Arctic regions of Alaska, Canada, Greenland, and Chukotka. It is characterized by vocal music with a narrow melodic range, recitative-like delivery, and accompaniment by a shallow frame drum. Inuit music has historically served storytelling, celebration, healing, and social functions, and includes distinctive forms such as throat singing.

Traditional forms

The most widespread traditional form is drum dancing and singing, in which a performer beats a large frame drum (qilaut) while singing songs that may recount hunts, journeys, ancestors, or personal experiences. Songs were traditionally "owned" by individuals or families and composed for specific occasions. In Canadian Inuit culture, katajjaq — throat singing — is performed typically by two women standing face to face, using each other's mouth cavity as a resonator in a rhythmic, competitive game that ends when one laughs or runs out of breath. Greenlandic Inuit (Kalaallit) also practiced song duels, in which grievances were aired and settled through satirical songs before the community. Shamanic (angakkuq) rituals formerly involved special songs and drum rhythms, suppressed in many areas after Christian conversion.

Characteristics

Traditional Inuit melody typically spans a narrow range, uses a small set of tones, and descends in contour toward the end of phrases. Vocal quality is often described as throaty and pulsating. Lyrics are in Inuit languages such as Inuktitut and Kalaallisut.

Modern Inuit music

Contemporary Inuit musicians blend traditional elements with country, folk, rock, and hip hop. Notable artists include the Greenlandic band Nanook; Canadian Inuit performers such as Susan Aglukark, who achieved mainstream success in Canada in the 1990s; throat-singing performer Tanya Tagaq, whose experimental work won the 2014 Polaris Music Prize; and the Inuktitut-language hip hop scene in Nunavut. The Greenlandic choir tradition and hymn singing, introduced by Moravian and Lutheran missions, remain an important part of local music-making.
