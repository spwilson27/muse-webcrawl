Onomatopoeia

Onomatopoeia is a word that imitates, resembles, or suggests the sound it describes. Examples in English include animal sounds such as meow and oink, machine sounds such as honk and whirr, and impact sounds such as boom, splash, and clang. The term derives from the Greek onomatopoiia, "word-making."

Cross-linguistic variation

Although onomatopoeic words imitate real sounds, they are filtered through the phonology of each language, so the same sound is represented differently across languages. A dog's bark, for example, is woof in English, wang wang in Mandarin, ouaf in French, and wan-wan in Japanese. Some languages, such as Japanese, Korean, and many African languages, make rich use of ideophones — expressive sound-symbolic words covering sounds, textures, movements, and states, not only noises.

Uses

Onomatopoeia is used in poetry and literature for vivid effect, in comic books to render sound effects visually, in children's language learning, and in ordinary speech. Poets from Aristophanes — whose frog chorus in The Frogs croaks brekekekex koax koax — to modern writers have exploited its immediacy.

Theory of language origins

One historical hypothesis about the origin of language, nicknamed the "bow-wow theory," proposed that speech began with imitations of natural sounds. Although onomatopoeia is too small a part of vocabulary to explain language as a whole, research on sound symbolism shows that imitative and expressive words may have played a supporting role.
