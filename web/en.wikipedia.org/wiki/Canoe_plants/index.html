Canoe plants

Canoe plants are the plant species carried across the Pacific Ocean by Austronesian voyagers during the settlement of Polynesia and other Pacific islands. Transported deliberately in voyaging canoes, these useful plants — most domesticated in Southeast Asia and New Guinea — formed the agricultural foundation of island societies from Hawai'i to New Zealand and Easter Island.

Principal species

About two dozen species are generally counted among the canoe plants. The most important food crops include:

  • Taro (Colocasia esculenta)
  • Breadfruit (Artocarpus altilis)
  • Banana and plantain (Musa species)
  • Coconut (Cocos nucifera)
  • Yams (Dioscorea species)
  • Sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas), which reached Polynesia from South America
  • Sugarcane (Saccharum officinarum)
  • Ti (Cordyline frutescens) and kava (Piper methysticum)

Significance

Because many canoe plants are propagated vegetatively from cuttings rather than seed, voyagers had to keep living plants alive on long ocean crossings — evidence of the deliberate, planned nature of Polynesian colonisation. The distribution and genetics of canoe plants are used by archaeologists and linguists to reconstruct migration routes across the Pacific, complementing the evidence of the Lapita culture.
