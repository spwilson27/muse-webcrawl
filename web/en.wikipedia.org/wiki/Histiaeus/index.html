Histiaeus

Histiaeus (Greek: Ἱστιαῖος; died 493 BC) was the tyrant of Miletus in the late 6th century BC, under the overlordship of the Persian Empire. He is known chiefly from Herodotus's Histories for his role in Darius I's Scythian campaign and in the origins of the Ionian Revolt.

Career

During Darius's expedition against the Scythians (c. 513 BC), Histiaeus commanded the Milesian contingent guarding the bridge of boats across the Danube. When the Scythians urged the Ionian tyrants to destroy the bridge and abandon Darius, Histiaeus argued against it, pointing out that their own rule depended on Persian power; the bridge was kept, and Darius rewarded him with territory at Myrcinus in Thrace. Later, suspicious of his growing power, Darius detained Histiaeus at his court in Susa under honourable pretexts.

According to Herodotus, Histiaeus, longing to return home, tattooed a message on the shaved head of a trusted slave and sent him to his son-in-law Aristagoras in Miletus, urging revolt once the hair had grown back — an early anecdote of steganography. Whether or not the story is true, Aristagoras launched the Ionian Revolt in 499 BC. Histiaeus was allowed to leave Susa ostensibly to help suppress the revolt, but instead intrigued among the Ionian cities. After the revolt's defeat he turned to piracy in the Aegean, was captured by the Persian commander Harpagus near Miletus, and was crucified; Darius, displeased, had his embalmed head sent to Susa.
