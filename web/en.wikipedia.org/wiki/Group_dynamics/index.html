Group dynamics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A team in discussion — group processes shape outcomes beyond individual abilities

Group dynamics is a system of behaviours and psychological processes occurring within a social group (intragroup dynamics) or between social groups (intergroup dynamics). The study of group dynamics is useful in understanding decision-making behaviour, tracking the spread of diseases in society, creating effective therapy techniques, and following the emergence and popularity of new ideas and technologies.

The field was founded by the German-American social psychologist Kurt Lewin (1890–1947), who established the Research Center for Group Dynamics at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 1945 and coined the term. Lewin proposed that group behaviour could be understood through the relation between a person and their environment (field theory), summarised in his formula B = f(P, E) — behaviour is a function of the person and the environment.

Key processes

Research in group dynamics covers group formation and development (such as Tuckman's stages of forming, storming, norming, performing and adjourning), norms, roles, cohesion, leadership, conformity (the Asch experiments), obedience (the Milgram experiment), groupthink, social facilitation, social loafing, and group polarisation.

Applications

Findings are applied in organisational development, team building, psychotherapy (group therapy), education, community development, and the design of online communities. Practitioners such as Wilfred Bion and William Schutz developed influential frameworks for observing and intervening in group processes.
