Bupivacaine

Bupivacaine, sold under the brand name Marcaine among others, is an amide-type local anaesthetic widely used for surgical anaesthesia and postoperative analgesia. It is known for its long duration of action and its relative separation of sensory block from motor block at low concentrations, which makes it especially valuable in epidural analgesia for labour and in spinal anaesthesia. It appears on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Medical uses

Bupivacaine is administered by local infiltration, peripheral nerve block, spinal (intrathecal) injection — often as the "heavy" hyperbaric solution with glucose — and epidural injection, with or without added epinephrine or opioids. A liposomal formulation (Exparel) provides prolonged release for postoperative infiltration. Onset is slower than lidocaine but duration is much longer, typically several hours.

Pharmacology

Like other local anaesthetics it blocks voltage-gated sodium channels, preventing initiation and propagation of action potentials in nerve fibres. It is highly protein bound (~95%) and is metabolised in the liver, chiefly by CYP3A4, to pipecoloxylidide. The single-enantiomer agents levobupivacaine and ropivacaine were developed to reduce its toxicity.

Adverse effects

Bupivacaine is more cardiotoxic than most other local anaesthetics: inadvertent intravascular injection can cause local anaesthetic systemic toxicity with cardiovascular collapse and arrhythmias that are resistant to resuscitation, a risk that prompted withdrawal of high-concentration (0.75%) solutions from obstetric epidural use in the United States in the 1980s. Intravenous lipid emulsion therapy ("lipid rescue") is now the standard antidote. It was first synthesised in 1957 by Bo af Ekenstam and introduced clinically in 1963.
