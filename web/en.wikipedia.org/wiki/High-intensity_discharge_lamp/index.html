High-intensity discharge lamp

High-intensity discharge lamps (HID lamps) are a type of gas-discharge lamp — an electrical lamp that produces light by means of an electric arc between tungsten electrodes housed inside a translucent or transparent fused quartz or fused alumina arc tube filled with gas and metal salts. The gas facilitates the arc's initial strike; once struck, the arc heats and evaporates the metal salts, forming a plasma that greatly increases the light output.

Types

The main families of HID lamps are mercury-vapour lamps, metal-halide lamps, ceramic discharge metal-halide lamps, sodium-vapour lamps (high-pressure and low-pressure), and xenon arc lamps. Wattages range from a few watts for miniature lamps to tens of kilowatts for industrial units.

Characteristics

HID lamps are more efficient than fluorescent and incandescent lamps because a greater proportion of their radiation is visible light rather than infrared. Metal-halide lamps offer good colour rendering and efficacies of roughly 75–100 lumens per watt; high-pressure sodium lamps are even more efficient but render colours poorly. Drawbacks include a warm-up period of several minutes to reach full brightness, difficulty restarting a hot lamp (restrike time), colour shift over life, and the need for a ballast to regulate current and provide the starting voltage.

Applications

Typical uses include street lighting, stadium and arena floodlighting, warehouse and factory high-bay lighting, greenhouse grow lights, cinema projection (xenon), and automotive headlamps.
