Gallbladder

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The gallbladder is a small, pear-shaped organ tucked under the Liver that stores and concentrates bile, the digestive fluid the liver produces. When fatty food enters the Small intestine, the gallbladder contracts and releases bile through the cystic and common bile ducts to help emulsify fats.

Function

Between meals, bile flows into the gallbladder, which absorbs water and salts from it, concentrating it several-fold. The hormone cholecystokinin, released when fat reaches the duodenum, triggers the gallbladder to contract and the sphincter at the duct's end to relax.

Clinical significance

Gallstones — hardened deposits of cholesterol or bile pigment — are common and can block the ducts, causing biliary colic pain, inflammation of the gallbladder (cholecystitis) or jaundice. Removal of the gallbladder (cholecystectomy), usually by laparoscopic surgery, is one of the most common operations; people digest food adequately without it, since bile still drips from the liver into the intestine.
