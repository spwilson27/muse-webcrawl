Carcinoid

A carcinoid (also known as a well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumor) is a slow-growing type of cancer that arises from cells of the neuroendocrine system. Carcinoids most often develop in the gastrointestinal tract — especially the small intestine, appendix and rectum — and in the lungs. The term was coined in 1907 by Siegfried Oberndorfer, who noted that the tumours looked like carcinomas under the microscope but behaved in a much more benign fashion (karzinoide, "carcinoma-like").

Classification

Modern WHO classification groups these neoplasms as neuroendocrine tumors and grades them by mitotic rate and Ki-67 proliferation index (G1–G3), separating well-differentiated NETs from poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas. "Carcinoid" survives as the customary name for well-differentiated tumors of the gut and lung (typical and atypical carcinoids of the bronchus). Historically they were divided by embryological origin into foregut, midgut and hindgut tumors, which differ in secretory products and behaviour.

Clinical features

Many carcinoids are silent and are found incidentally — classically, a small appendiceal carcinoid discovered after appendicectomy. Intestinal tumors may cause obstruction, bleeding or ischaemia from the intense fibrotic (desmoplastic) reaction they provoke in the mesentery. When vasoactive substances secreted by the tumor reach the systemic circulation — usually after metastasis to the liver bypasses hepatic inactivation — the carcinoid syndrome results: episodic flushing, diarrhoea, bronchospasm and, over time, fibrotic damage to the right-sided heart valves (carcinoid heart disease).

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on histology with immunostaining for chromogranin and synaptophysin, measurement of urinary 5-HIAA (a serotonin metabolite), and imaging including somatostatin-receptor scans such as gallium-68 DOTATATE PET/CT. Localised tumors are treated surgically. For metastatic disease, somatostatin analogues (octreotide, lanreotide) control symptoms and slow growth; other options include peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (lutetium-177 dotatate), everolimus, liver-directed embolisation and, for high-grade disease, chemotherapy.

Prognosis

Well-differentiated carcinoids often grow slowly, and patients with metastatic midgut tumors may survive many years. Prognosis depends on site, grade, stage and the presence of carcinoid heart disease.
