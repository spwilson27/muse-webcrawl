Ernst Waldfried Josef Wenzel Mach (18 February 1838 – 19 February 1916) was an Austrian physicist and philosopher. He is remembered for his contributions to physics — including the study of shock waves and the Mach number, the ratio of an object's speed to the speed of sound — and for a philosophy of science that profoundly influenced the Vienna Circle and the development of logical positivism.

Physics

Mach worked in Vienna, Graz, Prague and again Vienna, holding the chair of experimental physics at Prague for 28 years. His experimental studies of projectiles and shock waves, using schlieren photography, established the behaviour of air flow at supersonic speeds. In optics and physiology he described the visual illusion now called Mach bands, an early demonstration of lateral inhibition in the retina.

Philosophy

In The Science of Mechanics (1883) Mach criticised Newton's concepts of absolute space and time, arguing that physical concepts should be defined by observable relations — a critique that influenced Albert Einstein's development of relativity. Einstein named "Mach's principle" — the idea that inertia arises from interaction with the mass of the universe — after him, though the two later diverged. Mach's insistence on observable "elements" of sensation led him to oppose atomic theory as metaphysical, placing him in famous disputes with Ludwig Boltzmann and Max Planck.
