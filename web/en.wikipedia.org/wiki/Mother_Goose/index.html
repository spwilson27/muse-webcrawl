Mother Goose

Mother Goose is the imaginary author of a collection of French fairy tales and later of English nursery rhymes. As a character, she appeared in tales written by Charles Perrault, whose Histoires ou contes du temps passé, avec des moralités (1697) bore the subtitle Les Contes de ma mère l'Oye ("Tales of My Mother the Goose"). In English, the name became attached to collections of nursery rhymes — songs and verses for children such as "Humpty Dumpty," "Jack and Jill," and "Hey Diddle Diddle" — and to the traditional figure of an old woman who tells them.

Origins

The image of a storytelling "Mother Goose" circulated in France before Perrault; Jean Loret's La Muse historique (1650) already compares a tale to "a story of Mother Goose." Perrault's frontispiece showed an old woman spinning and telling tales to children, with a placard reading Contes de ma mère l'Oye. In English, Robert Samber's translation of Perrault (1729) kept "Mother Goose's Tales," and John Newbery's Mother Goose's Melody (c. 1765) shifted the name to the nursery rhymes themselves. An American tradition identifies Mother Goose with Mary Goose of Boston, whose tombstone in the Granary Burying Ground is pointed out to tourists, though the connection is a later invention.

In culture

"Mother Goose rhymes" became synonymous with the canonical English nursery rhyme repertoire, studied by folklorists such as Iona and Peter Opie, who traced their origins to playground lore, popular ballads, riddles, and political satire. The character is a staple of British pantomime — Mother Goose is one of the classic pantomime subjects, with the dame riding a goose that lays golden eggs — and appears across children's books, illustrations by artists such as Kate Greenaway and Arthur Rackham, and Maurice Ravel's ballet suite Ma mère l'Oye (Mother Goose).
