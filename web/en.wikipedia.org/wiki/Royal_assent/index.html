Royal assent

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Royal assent is the method by which a monarch (or their representative, a governor-general) formally approves an act of a legislature, making it law. In constitutional monarchies and Commonwealth realms, assent is constitutionally required but conventionally automatic — the last refusal in Britain was by Queen Anne in 1708 for the Scottish Militia Bill — and is given by commission or signified by the sovereign personally in a few realms' ceremonies; in realms, governors-general give assent in the monarch's name (Australia's reserve-power controversy of 1975 concerned dismissal of a government, not refusal of assent).

Comparative equivalents

Republican analogues include presidential signature of bills (with veto possibilities in presidential systems; largely formal in parliamentary republics — Ireland's president may refer bills to the Supreme Court instead, and Germany's presidents have occasionally refused signature on constitutional grounds). Belgium solved King Baudouin's conscientious refusal in 1990 by declaring him temporarily unable to reign for one day while the government promulgated the law — the famous practical compromise. Debates about assent flare where monarchs retain political discretion (Liechtenstein's princely veto, upheld by referendum in 2012) or where governors' assent delays state legislation (a live issue in India's federalism).
