Applause

Applause (Latin applaudere, to strike upon, clap) is the expression of approval by audiences, made by the act of clapping the hands together to create a percussive sound. It is performed mainly to show appreciation for a performance, speech, or announcement, and in many cultures it is accompanied by cheering, whistling, foot-stamping, or a standing ovation.

History

Clapping as acclamation is ancient. In the Roman theatre, applause was semi-organised: audiences clapped, snapped fingers, or waved toga flaps, and professional claques were hired to lead acclaim — a practice that survived in the Paris opera of the 19th century, where the claque was an institution. Roman politicians also used organised applause, and the measured, rhythmic clapping of assemblies later became a feature of political meetings. In some settings slow handclaps have been used to signal impatience or disapproval rather than praise.

Customs and variations

Applause conventions vary. At classical concerts, audiences traditionally withhold applause between movements of a symphony or concerto. In some legislatures, applause is formally discouraged in favour of table-thumping or cries of "hear, hear". Deaf audiences express approval by raising and waving their hands ("visual applause"). Golf crowds use restrained clapping; jazz audiences may applaud individual solos mid-performance. A standing ovation, in which the audience rises to its feet, marks exceptional approval, while particularly long ovations in some state ceremonies have become symbols of political loyalty.

Acoustics

A single handclap produces a sharp broadband burst of noise; the sound of many clappers together depends on synchronisation. Research on applause dynamics has shown that audiences spontaneously shift between incoherent clapping and rhythmic, synchronised clapping — a widely cited example of self-organisation in social behaviour.
