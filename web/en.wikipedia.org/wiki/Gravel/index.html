Gravel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gravel is a loose aggregation of rock fragments. It is classified by particle size range, comprising granules (2–4 mm), pebbles (4–64 mm), and cobbles (64–256 mm) on the Udden–Wentworth scale. Gravel occurs naturally where rock is weathered and transported by water, ice, or gravity, and is also produced industrially by crushing stone. It is one of the most important commercial construction materials in the world, used principally as aggregate in concrete, road building, and drainage.

Formation

Natural gravel forms by the erosion and transport of bedrock. River gravel is typically rounded and smooth from abrasion during transport, and is deposited in bars, floodplains, and alluvial fans. Glacial gravel is deposited by meltwater streams (forming eskers and outwash plains) or directly by ice as poorly sorted till. Beach gravel accumulates where wave action winnows away finer material. Mechanically produced gravel, called crushed stone, is made by blasting and crushing quarry rock and has angular faces that interlock well in construction applications.

In engineering usage the Unified Soil Classification System defines gravel as material with particles between 4.75 mm and 75 mm in diameter.

Production and uses

The United States Geological Survey estimates that roughly a billion tonnes of sand and gravel are produced annually in the United States alone, the great majority for construction. Major uses include:

  • Concrete aggregate — gravel and crushed stone form the bulk of concrete by volume.
  • Road construction — base and sub-base courses, asphalt aggregate, and unpaved gravel roads.
  • Drainage and filtration — permeable beds around pipes, in French drains, and in water treatment.
  • Landscaping and surfacing — paths, driveways, mulches, and decorative stone.
  • Railway ballast — angular crushed stone supporting sleepers and providing drainage.

Environmental impact

Gravel extraction from riverbeds and floodplains can destabilise channels, lower water tables, and destroy aquatic habitat; many jurisdictions now restrict in-stream mining in favour of upland quarries and recycled aggregate. Gravel pits are commonly restored as wetlands or lakes after working.
