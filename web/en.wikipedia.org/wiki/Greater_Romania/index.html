Greater Romania

Greater Romania (Romanian: România Mare) refers both to the state of Romania between 1918 and 1940, when it reached its greatest territorial extent of some 295,649 km², and to the nationalist idea of uniting all Romanian-speaking peoples within a single state.

Formation

Following the First World War, Romania, which had joined the Allies in 1916, gained Transylvania, Banat, Crișana, and Maramureș from Hungary (Treaty of Trianon, 1920), Bukovina from Austria, Bessarabia from Russia, and Southern Dobruja (held since 1913). The unions were proclaimed by assemblies at Chișinău, Cernăuți, and Alba Iulia in 1918, and confirmed by the Paris peace treaties. The enlarged kingdom's population roughly doubled to about 18 million, though ethnic Romanians formed only about 72% of the population, with large Hungarian, German, Jewish, Ukrainian, and other minorities.

Interwar period and dissolution

Interwar Romania pursued land reform, universal male suffrage, and cultural Romanianization, while struggling with minority rights and political instability. In 1940, under Axis pressure and the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, Romania lost Bessarabia and Northern Bukovina to the Soviet Union, Northern Transylvania to Hungary (Second Vienna Award), and Southern Dobruja to Bulgaria (Treaty of Craiova). After the Second World War, Romania regained Northern Transylvania but permanently lost Bessarabia, Northern Bukovina, and Southern Dobruja, leaving it close to its present borders.

The idea today

The concept persists in Romanian politics and culture, notably in discussions of a possible unification with the Republic of Moldova, most of whose territory formed part of interwar Romania as Bessarabia.
