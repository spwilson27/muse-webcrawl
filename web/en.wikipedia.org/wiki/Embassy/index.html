Embassy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An embassy is the main diplomatic mission of one state in the capital of another, headed by an ambassador, representing the sending state's government and protecting its interests and nationals abroad. The term covers both the mission and often its chancery building. Embassies are distinct from consulates, which (in consulates-general and consulates in other cities) focus on services to citizens and visas; Commonwealth countries exchange high commissions instead of embassies.

Legal status

Under the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (1961), embassy premises are inviolable — host authorities may not enter without consent — archives and communications are protected, and staff enjoy diplomatic immunity. Popular belief that embassy grounds are the sending state's territory is a misconception: they remain host territory with special protections. The inviolability regime survived even grave crises (the 1979 Tehran embassy seizure was a flagrant breach, condemned by the ICJ), and asylum in embassies has sheltered famous dissidents and fugitives (from Cardinal Mindszenty's 15 years in the US legation in Budapest to Julian Assange's seven years in Ecuador's London embassy, 2012–19).

Functions

Embassies report on host-country politics, negotiate, promote trade and culture, issue statements, and manage crises and evacuations; large embassies (US missions in Baghdad and Kabul among the biggest ever built) are small cities of diplomats, intelligence officers, military attachés and aid officials.
