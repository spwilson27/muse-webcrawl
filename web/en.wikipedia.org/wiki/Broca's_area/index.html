Broca's area, or the Broca area, is a region in the frontal lobe of the dominant hemisphere, usually the left, with functions linked to speech production. It comprises parts of the inferior frontal gyrus known as the pars opercularis (Brodmann area 44) and pars triangularis (Brodmann area 45). Damage to the area causes Broca's aphasia (expressive aphasia), characterized by slow, effortful, telegraphic speech with relatively preserved comprehension.

Discovery

In 1861 the French physician Paul Broca examined the brain of his patient Louis Victor Leborgne, who could utter little more than the syllable "tan" but appeared to understand language. Post-mortem examination showed a lesion in the left inferior frontal cortex, and Broca presented the case as evidence that articulate language is localized in that region. The patient "Tan" became one of the most famous cases in neurology, and modern imaging of his preserved brain has shown the lesion extended deeper than Broca could see, into the insula and basal ganglia.

Connections and function

Broca's area is connected to Wernicke's area in the temporal lobe by the arcuate fasciculus and other dorsal and ventral pathways. Speech production requires coordination between the two regions and the motor cortex. Contemporary research assigns Broca's area broader roles than articulation alone, including aspects of syntactic processing, phonological working memory, action observation (it forms part of the putative mirror neuron system in the frontal operculum), and the processing of hierarchical sequences in music and sign language.

Broca's aphasia

Strokes affecting the left middle cerebral artery territory commonly damage Broca's area and surrounding tissue. Patients typically speak in short, agrammatic utterances, omit function words, and are aware of their errors, which may cause frustration. Pure lesions confined strictly to areas 44/45 often cause only transient mutism or mild deficits; persistent Broca's aphasia usually involves a wider network, including the insula and white-matter pathways — a point of ongoing scientific discussion about the area's precise role.
