Ancient Roman architecture
Periodc. 509 BC – 4th century AD
Key innovationsArch, vault, dome, concrete (opus caementicium)
LandmarksPantheon, Colosseum, aqueducts, basilicas

Ancient Roman architecture adopted the external language of Greek architecture — the classical orders — but transformed building through Roman engineering, above all the arch, the vault and the dome, and the use of a strong, versatile concrete (opus caementicium). It produced the monumental civic and utilitarian structures of an empire: amphitheatres, baths, basilicas, aqueducts, triumphal arches, insulae and villas.

Materials and techniques

Roman concrete, made from lime mortar, water and volcanic ash (pozzolana) mixed with aggregate, could be poured into formwork, set under water, and faced with brick or stone. It freed builders from the span limits of post-and-lintel construction. Barrel and groin vaults covered vast interiors; the dome of the Pantheon (c. AD 118–125), still the world's largest unreinforced concrete dome, shows the maturity of the technique, its aggregate graded lighter toward the oculus.

Building types

  • Temples: raised on high podiums with frontal steps, adapting Greek forms (e.g. Maison Carrée).
  • Basilicas: large roofed halls for law and commerce, later the model for Christian churches.
  • Amphitheatres and theatres: the Colosseum (AD 80) seated some 50,000 spectators on tiered vaults.
  • Thermae: imperial bath complexes combining bathing, exercise, libraries and gardens.
  • Aqueducts and roads: infrastructure binding the empire, from the Pont du Gard to the Appian Way.
  • Domestic: the domus with its atrium and peristyle, apartment blocks (insulae), and country villas.

Influence

Our main written source is Vitruvius' De architectura (1st century BC). Roman forms passed into Byzantine and Islamic architecture, were revived in the Renaissance, and shaped neoclassical and Beaux-Arts building worldwide.
