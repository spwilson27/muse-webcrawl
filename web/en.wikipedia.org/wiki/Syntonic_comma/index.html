Syntonic comma

The syntonic comma (also called the comma of Didymus) is a small musical interval, the difference between a series of four perfect fifths and a major third together with two octaves. Its frequency ratio is 81:80, and it is approximately 21.5 cents wide.

Four perfect fifths (each 3:2) span a ratio of 81:16. Reducing by two octaves gives 81:64, the Pythagorean major third. A just major third, by contrast, has the ratio 5:4, i.e. 80:64. The difference between the two thirds is therefore 81:80 — the syntonic comma. Because of this discrepancy, a tuning system built on pure fifths cannot simultaneously have pure thirds; the syntonic comma must be "tempered out" somewhere in the system.

Role in tuning systems

In meantone temperament, the comma is distributed among the fifths, each of which is narrowed slightly so that the major thirds become pure. In quarter-comma meantone, each fifth is flattened by one quarter of a syntonic comma. In just intonation, singers and players of unfretted instruments must be careful about comma drift: a sequence of purely tuned chords can gradually shift the overall pitch by a syntonic comma in either direction, an effect sometimes called comma pump.

History

The interval is named after Didymus the Musician, the 1st-century BC Greek music theorist who described it, though it was known earlier. It should not be confused with the Pythagorean comma (531441:524288, about 23.46 cents), the difference between twelve perfect fifths and seven octaves.
