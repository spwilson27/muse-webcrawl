Climate variability and change

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Global surface temperature anomalies show both short-term variability and a long-term warming trend

Climate variability is the term used to describe deviations in climate statistics (such as temperature, precipitation, and wind) over periods ranging from seasons to centuries, around a long-term average state. Climate change refers to a long-term shift in those average conditions. The two are linked: change in mean climate also alters variability, for example making heatwaves more frequent or intense.

Sources of variability

  • Internal variability: natural oscillations of the coupled ocean–atmosphere system, notably the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO), the Pacific Decadal Oscillation, and the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation.
  • Natural external forcing: volcanic eruptions (cooling for a few years), solar output variations.
  • Anthropogenic forcing: increasing greenhouse gases and aerosols, which drive the observed long-term warming trend.

Detection and attribution

Scientists use climate models and statistical methods to separate internal variability from forced change. Studies conclude that internal variability alone cannot explain the observed warming since the mid-20th century.

Regional impacts

Changes in variability affect water resources, agriculture, ecosystems, and extreme events. Many regions are experiencing shifts in precipitation patterns — wetter wet seasons, drier dry seasons — even where mean rainfall changes little.
