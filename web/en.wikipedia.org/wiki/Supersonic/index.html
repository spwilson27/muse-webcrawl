Supersonic speed

Supersonic speed is the speed of an object that exceeds the speed of sound (Mach 1). For objects travelling in dry air at a temperature of 20 °C (68 °F) at sea level, this speed is approximately 343.2 m/s (1,236 km/h; 768 mph). Speeds greater than five times the speed of sound are referred to as hypersonic; the regime just below and around Mach 1, roughly Mach 0.8–1.2, is called transonic.

Aerodynamics

At supersonic speeds the airflow around an object is dominated by shock waves, producing wave drag that adds sharply to total drag. Supersonic aircraft therefore use thin, swept or delta wings, area-ruled fuselages, and powerful engines with afterburners. An observer on the ground hears a sonic boom — the merged shock waves reaching the ear — continuously along the flight path, not only at the moment the sound barrier is "broken".

History

On 14 October 1947, Chuck Yeager piloted the rocket-powered Bell X-1 to Mach 1.06, generally credited as the first controlled level flight faster than sound. Many rifle bullets and the tip of a cracked whip also travel supersonically. Supersonic transport arrived with the Tu-144 and Concorde (Mach 2 airliners), but noise restrictions, fuel costs, and the 2000 Concorde crash ended scheduled supersonic passenger service in 2003; several companies are developing successors.
