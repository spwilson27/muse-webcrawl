The three ships of the first Columbian voyage.

The Niña, Pinta, and Santa María were the three ships used by Christopher Columbus on his first voyage across the Atlantic Ocean in 1492. The fleet departed Palos de la Frontera in Spain on 3 August 1492 and, after a stop in the Canary Islands, reached land in the Bahamas on 12 October 1492 — an encounter between the Old World and the Americas that reshaped world history.

Santa María

The Santa María, originally named La Gallega, was the largest of the three vessels and Columbus's flagship. She was a carrack (nau) of roughly 100 tons, owned by Juan de la Cosa, who sailed as her master. Slow and cumbersome compared with the caravels, she ran aground on a reef off Hispaniola on Christmas Day 1492 and was wrecked. Her timbers were used to build the settlement of La Navidad, the first European outpost in the Americas, garrisoned by 39 men — none of whom survived to Columbus's return.

Niña

The Niña, formally Santa Clara, was a small caravel of about 50–60 tons owned by Juan Niño of Moguer and captained on the outward voyage by Vicente Yáñez Pinzón. She became Columbus's favourite ship: he sailed her on the return voyage of 1493, purchased a half share in her, and used her again on later voyages. She survived a hurricane in 1495 and remained in service for years afterwards.

Pinta

The Pinta was a caravel captained by Martín Alonso Pinzón, with his brother Francisco as master. It was from her rigging that the lookout Rodrigo de Triana first sighted land on 12 October 1492. Pinzón separated from Columbus for a period during the exploration of the Caribbean, rejoining before the return voyage; he died shortly after reaching Spain in March 1493.

Replicas

Numerous full-scale replicas of the three ships have been built, notably for the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition and the 1992 quincentenary. Replicas of all three are moored at Palos de la Frontera in Spain, and a sailing replica of the Niña has toured widely.
