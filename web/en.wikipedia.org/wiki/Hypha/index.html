Hypha

A hypha (plural hyphae, from Greek hyphē, "web") is a long, branching, filamentous structure of a fungus, oomycete or actinobacterium. In most fungi, hyphae are the main mode of vegetative growth and are collectively called a mycelium.

Structure

A hypha consists of one or more cells surrounded by a tubular cell wall, typically containing chitin and glucans. In most fungi the hypha is divided into cells by internal cross-walls called septa; septate hyphae may have one nucleus per cell or several. In coenocytic (aseptate) fungi, such as many zygomycetes, the hypha is a continuous multinucleate tube. Growth occurs at the tip, where the Spitzenkörper organises the delivery of materials for wall extension.

Functions

Hyphae release enzymes into their surroundings and absorb the resulting nutrients, making them essential to decomposition and nutrient cycling. Specialised hyphae form haustoria that penetrate host cells in parasitic and mycorrhizal fungi, aerial hyphae bearing spores, and cord-like rhizomorphs. In medicine, the presence of hyphae in tissue is diagnostic of invasive fungal infections such as aspergillosis and mucormycosis.

Modifications

Aggregations of hyphae form fruiting bodies (such as mushrooms), sclerotia that survive unfavourable conditions, and the cords and mats by which some fungi spread. Some fungi can switch between yeast-like and hyphal growth — dimorphism — which is important in the virulence of pathogens such as Candida albicans.
