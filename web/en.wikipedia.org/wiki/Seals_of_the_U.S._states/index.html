Seals of the U.S. states

The great seals of the fifty U.S. states.

The seals of the U.S. states are the official insignia of the fifty states of the United States of America. Each state's great seal is used to authenticate official documents, and most also appear on state flags, buildings and vehicles. The designs are generally prescribed in state law or constitution, and many date from statehood or from the territorial period.

Number: 50 state seals (plus federal and territorial seals)

Keeper: Usually the secretary of state

Purpose: Authentication of official documents; state symbolism

Common motifs: Agriculture, industry, eagles, shields, Latin mottos, state landscapes

Design and symbolism

Most state seals follow the heraldic tradition of a circular device bearing a central scene — often a shield flanked by figures or symbols of farming, commerce, mining, shipping and nature — encircled by the words "The Great Seal of the State of …". Many include the year of statehood and a motto, frequently in Latin, such as Missouri's Salus populi suprema lex esto or Colorado's Nil sine numine. A few, like California's, feature classical figures (Minerva) alongside local emblems (the grizzly bear and a miner).

History

The original thirteen states adopted seals during or shortly after the American Revolution, often adapting colonial devices. Newer states typically adopted a seal at or before statehood. Several seals have been revised to correct historical inaccuracies or heraldic errors; Mississippi replaced its seal's Confederate-associated imagery in 2021, adopting a new design bearing magnolias and the words "In God We Trust".

Usage

The physical seal is usually kept by the secretary of state and impressed or affixed on commissions, pardons, proclamations and certified copies of laws. Unauthorised commercial use of a state seal is restricted in many jurisdictions.
