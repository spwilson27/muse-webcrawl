Tiffany glass

MakerTiffany Studios, New York
FounderLouis Comfort Tiffany
Activec. 1878–1930s
Known forFavrile glass, leaded lampshades, stained glass windows

Tiffany glass refers to the many varieties of glass developed and produced between about 1878 and the 1930s at the Tiffany Studios in New York, founded by Louis Comfort Tiffany. The term encompasses his patented Favrile glass, leaded glass lampshades, stained glass windows, mosaics, and blown vessels, and is closely associated with the Art Nouveau movement.

Favrile glass

Tiffany patented Favrile glass in 1894. Unlike earlier iridescent glass, in which a surface coating produced the sheen, Favrile glass has its iridescence ingrained in the glass itself, with metallic oxides mixed into the molten material. The name derives from an Old English word for "handmade." Favrile was used for vases, bowls, and the coloured glass pieces assembled into lamps and windows.

Lamps and shades

The studio's most famous products are its leaded glass lamps, whose shades were made by wrapping small pieces of coloured glass in copper foil and soldering them together — a technique Tiffany adapted for intricate naturalistic designs such as dragonflies, wisteria, and peonies. Many shades were designed by Clara Driscoll, head of the Women's Glass Cutting Department, whose contribution was recognised only long after the lamps were produced. Original Tiffany lamps are highly prized by collectors; major examples have sold at auction for over a million dollars.

Windows and mosaics

Tiffany Studios produced stained glass windows for churches, public buildings, and private homes, layering multiple sheets of opalescent glass to achieve painterly effects of depth and light without relying on painted detail. The studio also made glass mosaics for interiors, including work for the Mark Twain House and various churches. After Tiffany's companies went into decline during the Great Depression, the studios closed in the early 1930s.

Legacy

Tiffany glass is held in major collections including the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Morse Museum of American Art in Winter Park, Florida, and the New-York Historical Society, which holds a large collection of lamps and the records of the studios.
