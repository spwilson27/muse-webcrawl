Compositionality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In semantics, mathematical logic, and related disciplines, compositionality is the principle that the meaning of a complex expression is determined by the meanings of its constituent expressions and the rules or syntactic operations used to combine them. It is often called the principle of compositionality or Frege's principle, after the philosopher Gottlob Frege, although Frege never stated it in quite this form.

Statement and motivations

The principle is commonly offered as an explanation of two striking facts about natural language: speakers can understand sentences they have never encountered before (productivity), and a speaker who understands the parts of a novel combination can systematically understand related combinations (systematicity). If meanings of wholes were not computed from meanings of parts, the ability of finite speakers to grasp an unbounded range of sentences would be mysterious.

Formally, compositionality requires that the interpretation function be a homomorphism from the syntactic algebra to the semantic algebra: the meaning of a syntactic combination is a function of the meanings of the combined expressions and the mode of combination.

History

The idea is implicit in Frege's work on sense and reference and was developed by Rudolf Carnap and, in its modern form, by Richard Montague, whose Montague grammar treated fragments of English as interpreted formal languages with compositional semantics. Donald Davidson argued that a learnable theory of meaning for a language must be compositional, an argument that has been highly influential in the philosophy of language.

Criticisms and challenges

Critics point to apparent counterexamples: idioms such as "kick the bucket", whose meaning seems not derivable from the parts; quotations; belief reports, which resist substitution of co-referential terms; and expressions whose meaning depends on context in complex ways. Defenders reply that compositionality is compatible with context-sensitivity provided context-dependence is itself handled systematically, and that idioms can be treated as lexical items stored whole. Others argue the principle, suitably stated, is too weak to be falsifiable, or that natural language semantics may be only "mostly" compositional.

Applications

Beyond natural language, compositionality is a design goal in programming language semantics (denotational semantics is compositional by construction), in formal semantics, and in research on neural networks, where the ability of models to generalise compositionally is a major benchmark of their capacities.
