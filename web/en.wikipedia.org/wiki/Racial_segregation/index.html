Racial segregation

Racial segregation is the systematic separation of people into racial or ethnic groups in daily life. Segregation can apply to activities such as eating in a restaurant, drinking from a water fountain, using a public toilet, attending school, going to the movies, riding on a bus, or renting or purchasing a home. It may be enforced by law (de jure segregation) or exist through social norms and economic practice (de facto segregation), and it has historically served to enforce the political and economic dominance of one group over others.

United States

Following the abolition of slavery and the collapse of Reconstruction, Southern states enacted Jim Crow laws from the late 19th century that mandated segregation in schools, transport, and public accommodations. The Supreme Court upheld the system in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) under the doctrine of "separate but equal." Segregation in the North and West was more often de facto, maintained by restrictive covenants, redlining, and discriminatory lending that shaped residential patterns for African Americans and other minorities. The legal edifice was dismantled by Brown v. Board of Education (1954), which declared segregated schooling unconstitutional, and by the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965, won through the civil rights movement.

South Africa

In South Africa, segregation hardened into the system of apartheid ("apartness") after 1948, when the National Party legislated racial classification, group areas, pass laws, and separate political representation. Black South Africans were assigned to nominally independent "homelands" (Bantustans) and stripped of South African citizenship. Apartheid ended with the first universal-franchise elections of 1994, which brought Nelson Mandela and the African National Congress to power.

Other cases

Nazi Germany's Nuremberg Laws of 1935 segregated Jews from German society and forbade intermarriage, a stage on the road to the Holocaust. Colonial regimes across Africa and Asia maintained segregated cities, schools, and clubs. Australia's policies toward Aboriginal people included segregated missions and reserves, while many countries historically segregated religious or caste groups — forms that shade into related systems of social stratification.

Effects and legacy

Scholars document segregation's lasting effects on wealth, education, health, and life expectancy: segregated neighborhoods received inferior public investment for generations, and disparities produced by mortgage redlining persist in home-ownership and wealth gaps today. Residential segregation remains measurable in many countries even where laws no longer enforce it, sustained by income inequality, zoning, and private choice.
