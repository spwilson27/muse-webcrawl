People's Party (United States)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The People's Party, also known as the Populist Party, was a left-wing agrarian political party in the United States in the late 19th century. Founded in 1891 out of the Farmers' Alliances, it championed farmers and labourers against railroads, banks and the gold standard, and its Omaha Platform of 1892 demanded free silver, a graduated income tax, government ownership of railroads and telegraphs, the secret ballot, and the direct election of senators.

Its presidential candidate James B. Weaver won over a million votes and 22 electoral votes in 1892. In 1896 the party endorsed the Democratic nominee William Jennings Bryan — “fusion” — and after his defeat it rapidly declined, though many of its demands were later enacted: the income tax (16th Amendment), direct election of senators (17th Amendment), and stricter railroad regulation. The party gave American English the word “populism”, though historians debate how much modern populism resembles the original movement, which was programmatic and organisation-based rather than centred on a charismatic leader.

Legacy

The Populists' coalition of Southern and Western farmers broke partly on race: some Southern Populists sought biracial alliances, but the movement operated within, and its defeat was followed by, the Jim Crow disenfranchisement of Black voters. Historians from Richard Hofstadter (critical) to Lawrence Goodwyn (sympathetic) have contested its meaning for American democracy.
