A&M Records, Inc. v. Napster, Inc.

The case shut down the original Napster file-sharing service.

A&M Records, Inc. v. Napster, Inc.
CourtUnited States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit
Full case nameA&M Records, Inc. et al. v. Napster, Inc.
DecidedFebruary 12, 2001
Citation239 F.3d 1004
District court114 F. Supp. 2d 896 (N.D. Cal. 2000)

A&M Records, Inc. v. Napster, Inc., 239 F.3d 1004 (9th Cir. 2001), was a landmark intellectual property case in which the United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit affirmed a preliminary injunction against the Napster file-sharing service. The court held that Napster's users directly infringed the plaintiffs' copyrights and that Napster itself was liable for contributory and vicarious copyright infringement. The decision effectively ended the original Napster service and shaped the law of online intermediaries for a generation.

Background

Napster, founded in 1999 by Shawn Fanning and Sean Parker, operated a peer-to-peer network that allowed users to share MP3 music files. Napster maintained central servers holding an index of files available on users' computers; users searched the index and downloaded files directly from one another. By early 2001 the service had tens of millions of users.

In December 1999, A&M Records and seventeen other record companies sued Napster for contributory and vicarious copyright infringement. The heavy metal band Metallica and rapper Dr. Dre filed separate suits. In July 2000, Judge Marilyn Hall Patel of the Northern District of California granted a preliminary injunction ordering Napster to shut down; the Ninth Circuit stayed the order pending appeal and heard argument in October 2000.

Holding

Writing for the panel, Judge Mary M. Schroeder held that the record companies had demonstrated a likelihood of success on the merits. Napster's users engaged in direct infringement by uploading and downloading copyrighted songs without permission. Napster had actual knowledge of infringing material — the recording industry had given it notice of thousands of infringing files — and materially contributed to the infringement by providing the servers and software that enabled it, establishing contributory infringement. Napster also had the right and ability to supervise its users (it could block accounts) and a direct financial interest in the infringing activity, since a larger user base attracted investment, establishing vicarious infringement.

The court rejected Napster's defences. The Sony "staple article of commerce" doctrine from Sony Corp. v. Universal City Studios did not immunise a service with actual knowledge of specific infringements and the ability to block them. The users' sampling and space-shifting were not fair uses because the sharing was commercial in effect and displaced record sales. The court also held that the Audio Home Recording Act did not protect downloading by Napster users. The injunction was affirmed but remanded for modification so that the record companies bore the burden of identifying infringing recordings, after which Napster had to remove them from its index.

Aftermath

Napster attempted to comply by filtering copyrighted works, with limited success, and shut down its network in July 2001. The company filed for bankruptcy in 2002, and its brand was later sold and relaunched as a licensed music service. The case remains a leading precedent on secondary copyright liability on the internet and influenced later litigation over services such as Grokster, decided by the Supreme Court in MGM Studios v. Grokster (2005).
