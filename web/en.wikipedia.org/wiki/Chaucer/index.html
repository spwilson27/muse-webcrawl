Geoffrey Chaucer (c. 1343 – 25 October 1400) was an English poet, author, and civil servant best known for The Canterbury Tales. He has been called the "father of English literature" and was the first writer to be buried in what has since become Poets' Corner in Westminster Abbey. Chaucer was among the first to demonstrate the artistic legitimacy of the vernacular Middle English, at a time when French and Latin dominated English literary culture.

Life

Chaucer was born in London into a family of prosperous wine merchants. He served as a page in the household of Elizabeth de Burgh, and in 1359–60 campaigned in France during the Hundred Years' War, where he was captured and ransomed with a contribution from Edward III. He married Philippa Roet, a lady-in-waiting and sister of John of Gaunt's third wife Katherine Swynford, and enjoyed Gaunt's patronage throughout his career.

Chaucer's working life combined poetry with public service: he was a diplomat on missions to Italy (where he likely encountered the works of Dante, Petrarch, and Boccaccio), controller of customs for the port of London, a justice of the peace, a member of Parliament for Kent, and clerk of the king's works, supervising projects including Westminster Palace and the Tower of London. Late in life he was robbed more than once, fell into financial difficulty, and died in 1400, shortly after taking a lease on a house in the precincts of Westminster Abbey.

Works

Chaucer's early dream-vision poems — The Book of the Duchess (an elegy for Gaunt's first wife), The House of Fame, and The Parliament of Fowls — show French influence. His masterpiece of the middle period is Troilus and Criseyde, a long poem adapting Boccaccio's Il Filostrato into a profound meditation on love and fortune. His unfinished final work, The Canterbury Tales, frames a story-telling contest among some thirty pilgrims traveling to the shrine of Thomas Becket at Canterbury; its General Prologue is a celebrated portrait gallery of medieval society, and its tales range from the chivalric Knight's Tale to the fabliaux of the Miller and Reeve and the moral exemplum of the Pardoner. Chaucer also wrote prose, including Boece, a translation of Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy, and a Treatise on the Astrolabe.

Influence

Chaucer's dialect and prestige helped establish the London standard that became modern English; John Dryden called him "the father of English poetry". His works were among the first printed by William Caxton, and poets from Spenser to Shakespeare drew directly on his tales.
