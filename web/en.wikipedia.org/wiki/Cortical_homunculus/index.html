Cortical homunculus

A cortical homunculus (from Latin homunculus, "little man") is a distorted representation of the human body, based on a neurological "map" of the areas and proportions of the human brain dedicated to processing motor functions, or sensory functions, for different parts of the body. The concept was developed from the work of Canadian neurosurgeon Wilder Penfield and his collaborators, who mapped the cerebral cortex by electrically stimulating points on the exposed brains of conscious epilepsy patients during surgery in the 1930s–1950s.

Sensory and motor homunculi

The motor homunculus maps the primary motor cortex along the precentral gyrus, while the sensory homunculus maps the primary somatosensory cortex along the postcentral gyrus. In both maps, body parts are represented in an orderly sequence from the foot (medially) to the face and tongue (laterally), but the proportions are grossly distorted: regions with fine motor control or dense sensory innervation — the hands, fingers, lips, and tongue — occupy vastly more cortical territory than the trunk or legs. The familiar drawing of the homunculus as a grotesque little figure draped over a brain slice, with enormous hands and lips, was first published by Penfield and Theodore Rasmussen in 1950 and was drawn by Hortense Cantlie.

Modern refinements

Modern neuroimaging has refined the classical picture: the motor cortex representation is more interleaved and integrative than Penfield's strict point-to-point map, with regions coordinating whole-body actions interspersed among effector-specific zones. Nonetheless, the somatotopic layout and the disproportionate representation of the hands and face remain robust, and the homunculus remains a standard teaching device in neuroscience and neurology.
