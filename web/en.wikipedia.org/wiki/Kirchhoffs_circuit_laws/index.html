Kirchhoff's circuit laws

The sum of currents entering a node equals the sum leaving it.

Kirchhoff's circuit laws are two equalities that deal with the current and potential difference (voltage) in the lumped element model of electrical circuits. They were first described in 1845 by the German physicist Gustav Kirchhoff and are widely used in electrical engineering.

Kirchhoff's current law (KCL)

The algebraic sum of currents entering and leaving any node in a circuit is zero: the sum of currents entering a node equals the sum of the currents leaving it. This is a consequence of the conservation of electric charge.

Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL)

The directed sum of the electrical potential differences (voltages) around any closed loop in a circuit is zero: the sum of voltages gained around a loop equals the sum of voltage drops. This follows from the conservation of energy.

Limitations

The laws rest on the lumped-element approximation: they assume that changing magnetic fields do not link the circuit loops (otherwise KVL can fail, since a changing magnetic flux induces an EMF) and that no net charge accumulates at a node. At high frequencies, where transmission-line effects matter, the laws must be replaced by full electromagnetic analysis.
