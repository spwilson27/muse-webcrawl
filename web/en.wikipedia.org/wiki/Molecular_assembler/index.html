Molecular assembler

Molecular assembler
FieldMolecular nanotechnology
Proposed byK. Eric Drexler (1986)
StatusHypothetical; not demonstrated

A molecular assembler, as defined by K. Eric Drexler, is a "proposed device able to guide chemical reactions by positioning reactive molecules with atomic precision." Popularised in Drexler's 1986 book Engines of Creation, the assembler is the central device of molecular nanotechnology (MNT): a machine, typically imagined as a nanoscale robotic arm or conveyor system, that would build structures atom by atom or molecule by molecule, potentially including copies of itself.

Concept

Drexler argued that because chemistry is governed by molecular mechanics, sufficiently precise positional control — mechanosynthesis — could build almost any structure consistent with physical law, from diamondoid machinery to complex products, enabling a revolution in manufacturing. The ribosome, which assembles proteins from amino acids under program-like control from mRNA, is often cited as a natural existence proof for programmable molecular assembly in aqueous environments.

Debate

The feasibility of general-purpose assemblers was famously debated between Drexler and Nobel laureate Richard Smalley in Chemical & Engineering News (2001–2003). Smalley argued that "fat fingers" (manipulators too bulky to fit in a reaction space) and "sticky fingers" (atoms adhering to the tool) made Drexler-style mechanosynthesis unworkable. Drexler responded that his proposals used specific tooltip chemistries and machine-phase environments rather than free manipulation in solution. Mainstream nanoscience has focused instead on conventional nanotechnology, and research funding for assemblers has been minimal; the concept remains speculative.

In fiction and culture

The assembler underpins the "grey goo" scenario of uncontrolled self-replication, and appears widely in science fiction as "nanolathes" or replicator-like factories. Related real developments include DNA origami, molecular machines (recognised by the 2016 Nobel Prize in Chemistry), and positional assembly demonstrated with scanning probe microscopes.
