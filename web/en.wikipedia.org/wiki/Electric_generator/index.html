Electric generator

An electric generator is a device that converts motive power (mechanical energy) into electric power for use in an external circuit. Sources of mechanical energy include steam turbines, gas turbines, water turbines, internal combustion engines, wind turbines and even hand cranks. Generators work through electromagnetic induction, discovered by Michael Faraday in 1831–32.

Principle of operation

When a conductor moves through a magnetic field, or a magnetic field moves past a conductor, an electromotive force is induced. In most large generators the field magnets rotate (the rotor) inside stationary armature windings (the stator), producing alternating current whose frequency is fixed by the rotation speed and the number of poles.

History

Early machines included Faraday's disk (a homopolar generator) and the Pixii dynamo of 1832. The self-excited dynamo of the 1860s–70s made generators practical, and the polyphase alternators developed by Nikola Tesla and others underpinned the AC power systems demonstrated at the 1893 Chicago World's Fair and Niagara Falls.

Types and uses

Generators range from tiny bicycle dynamos to the 1,000 MW-class turbo-generators of power stations. Portable engine-driven generators provide backup power; alternators in vehicles charge batteries and run electrical systems; wind turbines and hydroelectric plants are essentially generators driven by natural flows.
