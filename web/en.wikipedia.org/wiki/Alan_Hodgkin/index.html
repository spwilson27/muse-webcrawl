Overview

Sir Alan Lloyd Hodgkin (1914–1998) was an English physiologist and biophysicist who, with Andrew Huxley, established the mathematical theory of how nerve impulses (action potentials) are generated and propagated. Working on the squid giant axon at Plymouth and Cambridge, first in 1939 and then after wartime radar work, they published their five classic papers in 1952.

The Hodgkin–Huxley model

Using the voltage clamp, Hodgkin and Huxley separated the ionic currents across the axon membrane and showed that the action potential results from voltage-dependent changes in sodium and potassium conductance. Their equations — four coupled differential equations — founded computational neuroscience and earned the 1963 Nobel Prize, shared with John Eccles. Hodgkin later served as President of the Royal Society (1970–75) and Master of Trinity College, Cambridge.
