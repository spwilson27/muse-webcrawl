Canadian nationality law

Canadian nationality law is the law of Canada governing the acquisition, transmission, and loss of Canadian citizenship. It is principally embodied in the Citizenship Act, first enacted in 1947 — making Canada the first Commonwealth country to create a citizenship distinct from British subject status — and replaced by the modern Act that came into force on February 15, 1977.

Acquisition

Citizenship is acquired automatically by birth in Canada (jus soli), with a narrow exception for children of foreign diplomats, and by descent (jus sanguinis) for children born abroad to a Canadian parent. Since amendments in 2009, descent is generally limited to the first generation born abroad. Naturalization is available to permanent residents who have been physically present in Canada for at least 1,095 days in the five years before applying, meet language and knowledge requirements, and take the oath of citizenship.

History

Before 1947, Canadians were British subjects under common law and imperial statute. The Canadian Citizenship Act of 1947, championed by Secretary of State Paul Martin Sr., created Canadian citizenship as a primary status while retaining British subject status as secondary. The 1977 Act removed distinctions between citizens by birth and naturalization, permitted dual citizenship, and made loss of citizenship more difficult.

Amendments in 2009 restored citizenship to many "Lost Canadians" who had lost status under older rules, while legislation in 2014 introduced revocation for certain dual nationals convicted of terrorism or treason; those revocation provisions were repealed in 2017.

Rights and proof

Citizens may vote, hold public office, and enter and remain in Canada unconditionally. Proof of citizenship is provided by a citizenship certificate or a Canadian passport issued by Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada.
