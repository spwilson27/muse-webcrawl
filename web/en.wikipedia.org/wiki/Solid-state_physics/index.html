Solid-state physics

Solid-state physics is the study of rigid matter, or solids, through methods such as quantum mechanics, crystallography, electromagnetism, and metallurgy. It is the largest branch of condensed matter physics. Solid-state physics studies how the large-scale properties of solid materials result from their atomic-scale properties, and it forms the theoretical basis of materials science and of the transistor and semiconductor technology at the heart of modern electronics.

The crystalline structure of solids — a periodic lattice of atoms — is the starting point of solid-state theory.

History

Although the physical properties of solids had been studied for centuries, the field took shape in the early 20th century. The discovery of X-ray diffraction by crystals (1912) by Max von Laue and its development by William Henry and William Lawrence Bragg revealed atomic structure directly. Quantum mechanics in the 1920s supplied the essential theory: Felix Bloch's theorem on electrons in periodic potentials (1928) underpins band theory. The field expanded rapidly after the invention of the transistor at Bell Labs in 1947 by John Bardeen, Walter Brattain, and William Shockley. The name "solid state" became widespread in the 1940s and 1950s; from the 1960s the broader label "condensed matter physics" increasingly encompassed liquids and soft matter as well.

Crystal structure and bonding

Solids may be crystalline, with atoms arranged in a repeating lattice, or amorphous, lacking long-range order. Crystal structures are described by Bravais lattices and space groups, and are characterised experimentally by diffraction. The cohesion of solids arises from chemical bonding — ionic, covalent, metallic, hydrogen, and van der Waals — whose nature strongly influences mechanical, thermal, and electrical behaviour. Real crystals contain defects (vacancies, dislocations, grain boundaries) that often dominate practical properties such as strength and conductivity.

Electronic properties

Band theory explains why materials are conductors, semiconductors, or insulators: electrons occupy allowed energy bands separated by forbidden gaps. In metals a band is partly filled; in insulators the gap is large; in semiconductors it is small enough for thermal excitation or doping to produce conduction. Key models include the free electron model, the nearly free electron model, and tight-binding theory. Collective phenomena studied in the field include superconductivity, magnetism (ferromagnetism, antiferromagnetism), ferroelectricity, and the quantum Hall effect.

Lattice dynamics and thermal properties

Quantised lattice vibrations, called phonons, govern heat capacity and thermal conductivity. The Debye and Einstein models describe the temperature dependence of the specific heat of solids, resolving failures of classical equipartition at low temperatures.

Applications

Solid-state physics underlies semiconductor devices (diodes, transistors, integrated circuits, LEDs, solar cells), magnetic storage, superconductors for magnets and power applications, and emerging technologies such as spintronics and topological materials.
