The oldest dated rocks on Earth are fragments of the planet's earliest crust, formed during the Hadean and early Archean eons. Because plate tectonics, erosion and metamorphism have destroyed or altered nearly all of the original crust, rocks older than about 4 billion years are extremely rare and are found only in a few ancient cratons.

Oldest rock formations

  • Acasta Gneiss Complex (Slave Craton, Northwest Territories, Canada): tonalitic gneisses dated by uranium–lead methods on zircon to about 4.03 billion years, with some components possibly as old as 4.2 billion years.
  • Nuvvuagittuq Greenstone Belt (Quebec, Canada): claimed ages range from about 3.8 to possibly 4.4 billion years based on samarium–neodymium dating; these oldest claims remain debated among geologists.
  • Isua Greenstone Belt (southwestern Greenland): well-dated rocks around 3.7–3.8 billion years old, containing some of the oldest evidence bearing on early life.
  • Pilbara and Yilgarn Cratons (Western Australia): extensive Archean rocks up to about 3.6–3.7 billion years old.

Oldest minerals

Individual mineral grains can be older than any surviving rock formation. Detrital zircon crystals from the Jack Hills of Western Australia, preserved within younger sedimentary rocks, have been dated to as much as 4.404 billion years old — only about 140 million years younger than the Earth itself. Their oxygen isotope ratios have been interpreted as evidence that liquid water existed at the surface remarkably early, suggesting a "cool early Earth".

Oldest extraterrestrial material

Material older than the Earth comes from meteorites. Calcium–aluminium-rich inclusions in carbonaceous chondrites are dated to about 4.567 billion years, marking the age of the Solar System, and the Murchison meteorite contains presolar silicon carbide grains estimated at up to about 7 billion years old — the oldest solid material ever dated.
