The Sierra Leone Civil War (1991–2002) was a civil war in Sierra Leone that began on 23 March 1991 when the Revolutionary United Front (RUF), with the support of Liberian warlord Charles Taylor's NPFL, invaded from Liberia in an attempt to overthrow the one-party government of President Joseph Momoh. The eleven-year conflict devastated the country, killed tens of thousands, displaced more than two million people, and became notorious for the RUF's atrocities, including systematic amputation of civilians' limbs and the widespread use of child soldiers.

Freetown was invaded by the RUF/AFRC in January 1999 in the brutal "Operation No Living Thing".

Course of the war

The war was driven in part by control of Sierra Leone's alluvial diamond fields — the original "blood diamonds" — which the RUF used to buy weapons. The government collapsed into military rule (the NPRC junta of Valentine Strasser, 1992) and, later, the AFRC junta of Johnny Paul Koroma, which in 1997 invited the RUF into power in Freetown. Nigerian-led ECOMOG forces restored the elected president, Ahmad Tejan Kabbah, in 1998, but the RUF retook much of the capital in January 1999 with extreme violence. The Lomé Peace Accord (1999) made RUF leader Foday Sankoh vice-president and head of the diamond commission, but fighting resumed in May 2000 when the RUF took hundreds of UN peacekeepers hostage. A decisive British intervention (Operation Palliser, 2000), combined with a strengthened UN mission (UNAMSIL), broke the RUF; Sankoh was captured, disarmament proceeded, and President Kabbah declared the war officially over on 18 January 2002.

Aftermath

The Special Court for Sierra Leone prosecuted leaders of all sides; Charles Taylor was convicted in 2012 of aiding and abetting RUF crimes, the first former head of state convicted by an international court since Nuremberg. A Truth and Reconciliation Commission documented the conflict, and the war's exposure of "conflict diamonds" led to the Kimberley Process certification scheme.
