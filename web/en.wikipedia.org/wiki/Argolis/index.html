Argolis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Lion Gate at Mycenae, the great Bronze Age citadel of Argolis.

Argolis or the Argolid (Greek: Αργολίδα, Argolída) is one of the regional units of Greece. It is part of the region of Peloponnese, situated in the eastern part of the Peloponnese peninsula and part of the tripoint area of Argolis, Arcadia and Corinthia. Much of the territory of this region is situated in the Argolic Peninsula. Its capital is Nafplio.

Geography

Argolis occupies the easternmost portion of the Peloponnese. It borders Arcadia to the west and Corinthia to the north, and it is washed by the Argolic Gulf to the south and the Myrtoan Sea to the east. The fertile Argive plain, watered by the Inachos River, has been continuously inhabited since prehistoric times.

History

Argolis was one of the heartlands of Mycenaean Greece. The Bronze Age citadels of Mycenae, Tiryns, Argos, Midea, and Asine dominated the plain, and in Greek epic Argos was the seat of kings such as Diomedes and, in the Homeric poems, a byword for the Greeks themselves ("Argives"). In the classical period the city of Argos was a major power and long-standing rival of Sparta. The region later passed under Macedonian, Roman, Byzantine, Frankish, Venetian, and Ottoman rule. Nafplio, Argolis's port city, was the first capital of the modern Greek state after the War of Independence (1828–1832), before the capital moved to Athens in 1834.

Main towns and sites

The principal towns are Nafplio, Argos, Kranidi, Ermioni, and Epidaurus. Archaeological sites of world importance include Mycenae and Tiryns (UNESCO World Heritage Sites) and the sanctuary of Asclepius at Epidaurus, famous for its exceptionally well-preserved ancient theatre, still used for performances during the Athens and Epidaurus Festival.

Economy

The Argive plain is a major agricultural area, noted especially for oranges and other citrus fruit, olives, and vegetables. Tourism centred on Nafplio and the archaeological sites is also important.
