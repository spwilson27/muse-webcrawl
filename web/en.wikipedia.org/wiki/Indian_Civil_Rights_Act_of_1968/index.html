Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968 (ICRA) is a United States federal law that extends many of the protections of the Bill of Rights to persons subject to the jurisdiction of tribal governments in the United States. Enacted as Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1968, it was signed into law by President Lyndon B. Johnson on 11 April 1968.

Background

Because federally recognized tribes are sovereign political entities, the United States Supreme Court had long held that the Bill of Rights did not apply of its own force to tribal governments (Talton v. Mayes, 1896). During the 1960s, a series of congressional hearings examined civil rights within Indian Country, and concern grew that tribal members lacked enforceable protections against their own governments in areas such as criminal procedure, free speech and due process.

Provisions

The Act imposes on tribes most of the core limitations found in the Bill of Rights and the Fourteenth Amendment, including freedom of speech and of the press, freedom of religion (though the establishment clause was adapted to tribal circumstances), protection against unreasonable search and seizure, protection against double jeopardy and self-incrimination, the right to a speedy and public trial, the right to counsel at one's own expense, and protection against cruel and unusual punishment and excessive bail. It also guarantees due process and equal protection of the laws.

As originally enacted, the Act limited tribal sentencing authority to a maximum of six months' imprisonment and a $500 fine; amendments in 1986 raised the limits, and the Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010 permits sentences of up to three years per offence under enhanced procedural safeguards.

Enforcement

The sole federal remedy expressly provided by the Act is a writ of habeas corpus under 25 U.S.C. § 1303. In Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez (1978), the Supreme Court held that the Act does not create an implied private right of action in federal court for other violations, reasoning that tribal forums are the appropriate venue for resolving such disputes and that broader federal review would undermine tribal sovereignty.

Legacy

The ICRA remains a central statute in federal Indian law, balancing individual civil rights against tribal self-governance. The Violence Against Women Reauthorization Act of 2013 amended the ICRA framework to recognize limited tribal criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians in domestic violence cases, subject to ICRA-based safeguards.
