The forearm is the region of the upper limb between the elbow joint and the wrist joint. Its skeleton consists of two bones, the radius and the ulna, joined by an interosseous membrane; this two-bone arrangement allows the forearm to rotate, turning the hand palm-up (supination) or palm-down (pronation).

Structure

The muscles of the forearm are arranged in anterior (flexor–pronator) and posterior (extensor–supinator) compartments, separated by fascia and the interosseous membrane. The anterior compartment, supplied mainly by the median and ulnar nerves, flexes the wrist and fingers and pronates the forearm; its superficial muscles include the flexor carpi radialis, palmaris longus, flexor carpi ulnaris and pronator teres. The posterior compartment, supplied by the radial nerve, extends the wrist and fingers and includes the brachioradialis, extensor carpi radialis longus and brevis, extensor digitorum and extensor carpi ulnaris.

Blood reaches the forearm mainly through the radial and ulnar arteries, terminal branches of the brachial artery, and the pulse is commonly palpated at the wrist where the radial artery runs superficially. The three major nerves of the upper limb — median, ulnar and radial — all traverse the forearm to supply the hand.

Clinical significance

Fractures of the radius and ulna are common, especially the distal radius (Colles') fracture after a fall on an outstretched hand. Rising pressure within the tight fascial compartments causes compartment syndrome, a surgical emergency. Tendons and nerves of the forearm are vulnerable in lacerations at the wrist, and the median nerve may be compressed in pronator syndrome.
