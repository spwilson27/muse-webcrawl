Qasida

The qaṣīda (Arabic: قصيدة, Persian: قصیده, also spelled qasidah or kasida) is an ancient Arabic poetic form — an ode of some length, typically 15 to over 100 lines, written in a single metre with a single end-rhyme throughout (qafia). It originated in pre-Islamic Arabia and spread throughout the Islamic world.

Structure

The classical qaṣīda is often tripartite: it opens with the nasīb, a nostalgic lament at the abandoned desert encampment of the beloved, followed by the raḥīl, a journey section describing the poet's mount and desert travel, and concludes with the main purpose — praise (madḥ) of a patron or tribe, boast (fakhr), or satire (hijā). Famous pre-Islamic examples form the Mu'allaqat, the "Hanging Odes".

Later development

The form was adapted into Persian, Turkish and Urdu poetry, often as religious praise. The Burda of al-Busiri, in praise of the Prophet Muhammad, is among the most famous qaṣīdas. In South Asia, the qasida is recited as devotional praise (na'at and manqabat).
