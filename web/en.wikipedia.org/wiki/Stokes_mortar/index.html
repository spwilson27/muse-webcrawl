Stokes mortar

The Stokes mortar was a British trench mortar invented by Wilfred Stokes and used in the First World War and afterwards. It is the direct ancestor of the modern infantry mortar: a simple smooth-bore tube on a bipod and baseplate, firing a fin-stabilised bomb that is dropped down the muzzle onto a fixed firing pin. The standard version was the 3-inch Stokes mortar.

Design

The weapon consisted of a smooth-bored metal tube about 3 inches (76 mm, in practice 81 mm bore) in calibre, mounted on a simple bipod with elevation and traverse adjustments, and a baseplate that absorbed recoil into the ground. The bomb, weighing about 10 pounds (4.5 kg), was a cylindrical canister with a shotgun-blank-like propellant cartridge in its tail. The loader simply dropped the bomb down the tube; the cartridge struck a fixed pin at the breech and fired, launching the bomb. This gave a practical rate of fire of 20–25 rounds per minute — extraordinarily high for the time — and skilled crews could have several bombs in the air simultaneously.

Service

Stokes, then chairman of an Ipswich agricultural machinery firm, developed the design in 1915 in response to trench warfare's need for a portable high-angle weapon to drop shells into enemy trenches a few hundred yards away. After initial official scepticism, the mortar was rushed into production and first used in quantity in 1916. By the Armistice the British Empire had thousands in service, crewed by infantry rather than artillery. The design was copied and developed by many nations; the French Brandt mle 27/31 and the standard 81 mm mortars of the Second World War descend from it, as does, in concept, essentially every modern muzzle-loading infantry mortar.

Legacy

Stokes was knighted for his invention. His insight — that a mortar could be reduced to a tube, a pin, and a self-firing bomb, with the bomb's own cartridge providing both ignition and propulsion — defined a whole class of weapons whose descendants remain standard infantry equipment worldwide more than a century later.
