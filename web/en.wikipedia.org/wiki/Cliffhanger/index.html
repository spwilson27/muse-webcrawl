Cliffhanger

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. For other uses, see the disambiguation page.

A cliffhanger is a plot device in fiction in which a main character is left in a precarious or dangerous situation, or confronted with a shocking revelation, at the end of an episode, chapter, or instalment. The unresolved ending is designed to create suspense and compel the audience to return for the next part to learn the outcome.

Serial films of the 1910s, such as The Perils of Pauline, popularised literal cliff-edge endings.

History

Serialised storytelling has used suspended endings for centuries: Scheherazade in One Thousand and One Nights survives by breaking off each tale at dawn mid-story. Nineteenth-century serial novels by authors such as Charles Dickens ended instalments on moments of suspense to retain readers. The term itself comes from early film serials and chapter plays, whose heroes were repeatedly left dangling from cliffs, tied to railway tracks, or trapped in burning buildings; Thomas Hardy's novel A Pair of Blue Eyes (1873) contains an early literal cliff-hanging scene sometimes cited as the origin of the expression.

In television and film

Cliffhangers became a staple of television season finales. Famous examples include "Who shot J.R.?" in Dallas (1980) — resolved by the episode "Who Done It", watched by a record audience — and the end of Star Trek: The Next Generation's "The Best of Both Worlds" (1990). Soap operas use them almost daily. In the streaming era, episode-ending cliffhangers encourage binge-watching, while unresolved series finales after cancellation are a persistent source of audience frustration.

Technique

A cliffhanger differs from simple suspense by deferring resolution beyond the end of the narrative unit. Variants include the danger cliffhanger, the revelation cliffhanger ("I am your father"), and the decision cliffhanger. Critics distinguish earned cliffhangers, which arise organically from the plot, from manipulative ones resolved by deus ex machina or retroactive retconning.
