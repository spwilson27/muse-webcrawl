Nineveh Plains

Nineveh Plains
Country: Iraq
Governorate: Nineveh
Largest towns: Al-Hamdaniya (Qaraqosh), Alqosh, Tel Keppe, Karamlesh, Bartella
Population: Historically Assyrian Christian majority

The Nineveh Plains (Syriac: Dastā d-Ninwē; Arabic: Sahl Naynawā; Kurdish: Destî Neynewa) is a region of northern Iraq, lying northeast, east, and southeast of the city of Mosul in the Nineveh Governorate. The plains form the historic heartland of the Assyrian people and have been home for centuries to a mosaic of communities: Assyrian Christians of the Chaldean Catholic, Syriac Orthodox, Syriac Catholic, and Assyrian Church of the East traditions, alongside Shabaks, Yazidis, Kaka'i, Arabs, Kurds, and Turkmen.

Geography

The region is a broad, fertile plain watered by seasonal rains, bounded by the Tigris to the west and the Great Zab and Khazir rivers to the east, rising toward the Zagros foothills. Its principal settlements include Al-Hamdaniya (Qaraqosh/Bakhdida), Bartella, Karamlesh, Tel Keppe, Batnaya, Alqosh, Telskuf, and Bahzani and Bashiqa. Ancient Assyrian sites such as Nineveh, Nimrud, and Khorsabad lie in or near the plain, and the 4th-century monastery of Mar Mattai overlooks it from Mount Alfaf.

History

The plain took its name from the ancient Assyrian capital of Nineveh, destroyed in 612 BC. Its Christian communities were established in the early centuries AD and maintained distinctive Aramaic (Sureth) dialects and church traditions through centuries of changing rule. The region suffered severely during the Ottoman-era massacres and again in the 20th century, but retained its plural character.

2014 invasion and displacement

In June 2014 the Islamic State captured Mosul, and in August 2014 its forces overran most of the Nineveh Plains, causing the flight of virtually the entire Christian, Yazidi, Shabak, and Kaka'i population — some 200,000 people — to the Kurdistan Region. Churches and monasteries were destroyed or desecrated, and mass graves were later found. Iraqi forces, Peshmerga, and allied units liberated the region between 2016 and 2017, after which returns began slowly amid destroyed infrastructure and disputes over administration between Baghdad and Erbil.
