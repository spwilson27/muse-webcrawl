Year 2038 problem

The Year 2038 problem (also called Y2038, Y2K38, or the Epochalypse) is a time-formatting bug in computer systems that store time as a signed 32-bit integer count of seconds since the Unix epoch (00:00:00 UTC on 1 January 1970). Such integers overflow at 03:14:07 UTC on 19 January 2038, after which the stored value wraps around to a negative number, interpreted as a date in December 1901.

Cause

The largest value representable in a signed 32-bit integer is 231 − 1 = 2,147,483,647 seconds, which corresponds to the overflow moment above. Systems using the traditional C time_t type on 32-bit platforms — long standard in Unix-like systems and embedded devices — are affected, as are file systems, databases, protocols, and data formats that embed 32-bit timestamps. Consequences of the wraparound can include incorrect dates, crashed schedulers, expired-certificate errors, and corrupted records in long-lived equipment such as industrial controllers, vehicles, and infrastructure expected to remain in service past 2038.

Mitigations

The general solution is to move to 64-bit time representations, whose overflow lies hundreds of billions of years in the future. Most 64-bit operating systems already use 64-bit time_t; Linux added support for 64-bit time on 32-bit architectures in kernel 5.6 (2020), and OpenBSD adopted 64-bit time_t in 2014. Remediation is nevertheless complicated for embedded and legacy systems that cannot easily be updated, and for stored data and on-disk formats. The problem is frequently compared with the Year 2000 problem, though its long lead time has allowed a quieter, more gradual fix.
