Hermit

Hermit
Also known asEremite, anchorite (enclosed), recluse, solitary
From Greekerēmitēs — "of the desert"
MotivationReligious devotion, prayer, contemplation; also philosophical or personal withdrawal

A hermit, also called an eremite, is a person who lives in seclusion, typically for religious reasons. The word derives from the Latin eremita and the Greek erēmitēs, meaning "of the desert", reflecting the desert origins of Christian eremitism in Egypt and Syria. Hermits withdraw wholly or partly from society to devote themselves to prayer, contemplation and austerity, though the term is also used loosely for anyone living a solitary, reclusive life.

Christian tradition

Christian hermit life is usually traced to St Paul of Thebes and above all to St Anthony the Great (c. 251–356), whose withdrawal into the Egyptian desert and whose biography by Athanasius inspired generations of imitators, the Desert Fathers and Mothers. From these solitaries developed both purely eremitic orders — such as the Carthusians and Camaldolese, who combine solitude with some community life — and anchorites, hermits permanently enclosed in a cell attached to a church, a form prominent in medieval England (Julian of Norwich is the best known). In Eastern Orthodoxy the solitary tradition continued in the monasteries of Mount Athos and in the Russian starets tradition. Roman Catholic canon law still recognises hermits consecrated by their diocesan bishop.

Other traditions

Withdrawal is equally central elsewhere: Hindu sannyasa and the forest-dwelling stage of life (vanaprastha), Buddhist forest monks in the Theravada tradition, Jain ascetics, Taoist mountain recluses in China, and Sufi solitaries all represent forms of the eremitic life.

Secular hermits

Not all hermits are religious. Henry David Thoreau's experiment at Walden Pond was temporary and philosophical; other solitaries withdraw from misanthropy, grief, artistic concentration or simple preference. In 18th- and 19th-century Britain, wealthy landowners sometimes employed "ornamental hermits" to inhabit picturesque hermitages on their estates.
