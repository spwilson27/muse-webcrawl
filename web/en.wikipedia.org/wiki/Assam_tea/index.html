Assam tea

Assam tea
TypeBlack tea
RegionAssam, India (Brahmaputra valley)
PlantCamellia sinensis var. assamica
CharacterFull-bodied, malty, brisk; deep red liquor

Assam tea is a black tea named after the region of its production, Assam in north-eastern India. It is made from the large-leaved variety Camellia sinensis var. assamica, native to the region, and is known for a strong, malty flavour and a bright, deep-red colour. Assam is one of the world's largest tea-growing regions, producing more than half of India's tea output, mostly processed by the CTC (crush, tear, curl) method for tea bags and blends.

History

The Singpho people of Assam had long consumed tea made from the local plant before the British "discovered" it in the 1820s — the Scottish adventurer Robert Bruce is credited with reporting the indigenous tea plants in 1823. The British East India Company established commercial cultivation in the 1830s to break China's tea monopoly, founding the Assam Company in 1839. Tea gardens spread through the Brahmaputra valley, worked largely by indentured labourers recruited from central India under harsh conditions that remain a sensitive part of the industry's history.

Production and character

Unlike high-grown Darjeeling, Assam tea comes from low-altitude gardens in a hot, rainy tropical climate, which yields a bold, full-bodied brew. Two main harvests ("flushes") are taken: a lighter first flush in spring and a prized second flush in early summer, whose tips give the characteristic malty sweetness. Assam is a staple of breakfast blends — including Irish breakfast tea — and is widely used in masala chai.
