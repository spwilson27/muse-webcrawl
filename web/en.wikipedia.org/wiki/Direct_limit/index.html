Direct limit

In mathematics, a direct limit is a way of constructing a (typically large) object from many (typically smaller) objects that are put together in a specific way. These objects may be groups, rings, vector spaces, topological spaces, or objects of any other category, together with structure-preserving maps between them. Direct limits are a special case of the categorical concept of colimit.

Definition

Let I be a directed set and let {Ai : i ∈ I} be a family of objects indexed by I, equipped with homomorphisms fij : Ai → Aj for all i ≤ j, such that fii is the identity and fik = fjk ∘ fij whenever i ≤ j ≤ k. Such a family is called a direct system.

The direct limit of the system, written lim→ Ai, is defined as the disjoint union of the Ai modulo an equivalence relation: elements xi ∈ Ai and xj ∈ Aj are equivalent if there exists some k ≥ i, j with fik(xi) = fjk(xj). Intuitively, two elements are identified if they eventually become equal somewhere further along the system.

Examples

  • The collection of subgroups of a group forms a direct system under inclusion, and the group is the direct limit of its subgroups; in particular, any group is the direct limit of its finitely generated subgroups.
  • The construction of the Prüfer group and of the group of all roots of unity uses direct limits of finite cyclic groups.
  • In topology, the weak topology on a CW complex can be characterized via direct limits of its skeletons.
  • Every vector space is the direct limit of its finite-dimensional subspaces.

Properties

Direct limits in categories of algebraic structures are "exact": taking direct limits over a directed set commutes with finite limits in many settings, a fact that fails for inverse limits. The dual notion is the inverse limit, a special case of a categorical limit.
