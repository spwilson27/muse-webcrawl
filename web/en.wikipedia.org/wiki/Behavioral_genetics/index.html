Behavioral genetics

Behavioral genetics, also written behavioural genetics, is a field of scientific research that uses genetic methods to investigate the nature and origins of individual differences in behaviour. The field seeks to understand both the genetic and environmental contributions to behaviour, and the interplay between them. It is closely associated with the long-running "nature versus nurture" debate.

Methods

Classical methods include twin studies, comparing identical (monozygotic) twins, who share nearly all their DNA, with fraternal (dizygotic) twins, who share on average half of their segregating genes; adoption studies, which separate genetic relatedness from shared environment; and family studies. Comparisons of these groups allow researchers to partition variation in a trait into components attributed to genetic influences (heritability), shared environment, and non-shared environment.

Since the completion of the Human Genome Project, molecular methods have become central. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) search for statistical associations between specific genetic variants — typically single-nucleotide polymorphisms — and behavioural traits such as educational attainment, schizophrenia risk, or personality dimensions. Because individual variants have tiny effects, very large samples are required, and polygenic scores aggregate thousands of variants into a single predictor.

Findings and debate

Twin studies consistently find moderate heritability for most behavioural traits, including intelligence, personality, and liability to psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. The field remains controversial: critics worry about genetic determinism, the interpretation of heritability (which describes populations, not individuals), and historical associations with eugenics. Researchers emphasise that genes and environments interact and correlate in complex ways, and that heritable does not mean immutable.
