Booker Taliaferro Washington (5 April 1856 – 14 November 1915) was an American educator, author and orator who was the dominant leader of the African American community in the United States from the 1890s until his death. Born enslaved in Virginia, he rose after emancipation through education at Hampton Institute, and in 1881 became the founding principal of the Tuskegee Normal and Industrial Institute in Alabama, which he built into a leading Black university.

Early life

Washington was born to Jane, an enslaved cook, on a tobacco farm near Hale's Ford, Virginia; his father was an unknown white man. Freed by the Emancipation Proclamation in 1865, the family moved to Malden, West Virginia, where Washington worked in salt furnaces and coal mines while teaching himself to read, encouraged by his mother and by Viola Ruffner, a mine-owner's wife. In 1872 he travelled to Hampton Normal and Agricultural Institute in Virginia, working as a janitor to pay his way, and came under the influence of its founder, General Samuel Chapman Armstrong, whose philosophy of industrial education and self-help shaped his life's work.

Tuskegee

Recommended by Armstrong, Washington was chosen to head a new school for Black students at Tuskegee, Alabama, opening in July 1881 in a shanty and church. Students literally built the campus — making bricks, erecting buildings, farming the land — while learning trades, agriculture and teaching. By his death Tuskegee had some 1,500 students, more than 100 buildings and an endowment of about $2 million; its faculty included the botanist George Washington Carver.

Atlanta Compromise

Washington's address to the Cotton States and International Exposition in Atlanta on 18 September 1895 made him nationally famous. He counselled Black southerners to "cast down your bucket where you are" — to seek economic advancement through trades and farming — and appeared to accept social segregation: "In all things that are purely social we can be as separate as the fingers, yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress." White audiences embraced the speech; later generations, led by W. E. B. Du Bois in The Souls of Black Folk (1903), criticised this "Atlanta Compromise" as surrendering political rights and higher education, though Washington secretly funded lawsuits against disfranchisement and Jim Crow.

National influence

Washington advised Presidents Theodore Roosevelt and William Howard Taft on appointments; his 1901 dinner at the White House with Roosevelt provoked a storm of Southern racist outrage. His autobiography Up from Slavery (1901) became a bestseller. Through the "Tuskegee Machine" — his network of newspapers, organisations such as the National Negro Business League (founded 1900), and control of philanthropic funding — he dominated Black public life, marginalising critics such as Du Bois and William Monroe Trotter. He died in 1915, exhausted by overwork; Tuskegee is now Tuskegee University, and his birthplace and home are national historic sites.
