The tradition and style of garden design represented by Persian gardens or Iranian gardens (Persian: باغ ایرانی) has influenced the design of gardens from Andalusia to India and beyond. The gardens of the Alhambra and the Taj Mahal show the influence of Persian garden philosophy and style. The concept of the Persian garden as an earthly paradise — the very word "paradise" derives from Old Persian pairidaēza, "enclosed park" — is central to the tradition.

Charbagh

The archetypal form is the charbagh (from Persian chahār bāgh, "four gardens"), a quadrilateral layout divided by walkways or flowing water into four smaller parts, representing the four elements or the four rivers of paradise. Water, delivered by qanat channels, is the organising element: pools, canals and fountains provide sound, reflection and cooling in the arid climate. Shade trees, fruit trees and fragrant flowers fill the quarters, and a pavilion typically commands the central axis.

History

The earliest known charbagh was the garden of Cyrus the Great at Pasargadae (c. 546 BC). Persian garden design spread with the expansion of Islam, shaping the gardens of Umayyad and Abbasid palaces, Mughal gardens such as Humayun's Tomb and the Taj Mahal, and the courtyard gardens of North Africa and Spain.

Elements

Characteristic elements include the enclosed wall, the geometric water channels, raised pavilions (kushk), plane and cypress trees, roses and pomegranate trees, and intricate tilework. Nine Persian gardens in Iran were inscribed collectively as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2011, including Fin Garden in Kashan and Eram Garden in Shiraz.
