Consumer Price Index (United Kingdom)

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) is the official measure of consumer price inflation in the United Kingdom, published monthly by the Office for National Statistics (ONS). It measures the change in the price of a representative basket of goods and services bought by households. First published in 1996 (with a modelled back series to 1988), the CPI uses the same methodology as the Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP) developed across the European Union to allow international comparisons.

Use in monetary policy

In December 2003 the CPI replaced the Retail Prices Index excluding mortgage interest payments (RPIX) as the target measure for the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee. The government's inflation target is 2% per year as measured by the CPI, with a tolerance of one percentage point on either side; if inflation moves more than one point away from target, the Governor must write an open letter to the Chancellor of the Exchequer explaining why and what the Bank intends to do.

Methodology

The basket contains over 700 representative items, weighted according to household expenditure patterns derived mainly from the Living Costs and Food Survey. Around 180,000 price quotes are collected each month from shops, markets and online retailers across the country, supplemented by centrally collected prices. Unlike the older Retail Prices Index (RPI), the CPI excludes most housing costs — mortgage interest payments, house prices, council tax and buildings insurance — though a separate measure, CPIH, includes owner-occupiers' housing costs using rental equivalence, and became the ONS's headline measure in 2017. The CPI also differs from the RPI in using the geometric mean (Jevons formula) rather than arithmetic averages at the lowest level of aggregation, which generally produces a lower measured inflation rate — the so-called "formula effect".

History and comparisons

UK CPI inflation spiked after the 2008 financial crisis to 5.2% in September 2011, fell close to zero in 2015, and rose above 11% in October 2022 — its highest rate since the early 1980s — during the cost-of-living crisis driven by energy and food prices. The long-running debate over the RPI's flaws led to the announcement that RPI methodology would be aligned with CPIH from 2030.
