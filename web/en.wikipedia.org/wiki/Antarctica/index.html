Antarctica

[Image: a research station's red buildings on stilts in a vast white Antarctic landscape, mountains on the horizon]

Antarctica is Earth's southernmost continent, containing the geographic South Pole and surrounded by the Southern Ocean. It is the coldest, driest, windiest and highest continent, almost entirely covered by an ice sheet averaging about two kilometres thick that holds roughly 60% of the world's fresh water. It has no permanent human population, though about 1,000–5,000 researchers and support staff live there temporarily at scientific stations.

The continent was first sighted in 1820 and first landed on in the following decades; the race to the South Pole ended with Roald Amundsen's party reaching it on 14 December 1911, a month before Robert Falcon Scott's ill-fated expedition.

Governance and science

The Antarctic Treaty (signed 1959, in force 1961) demilitarised the continent, set aside territorial disputes among the seven claimant states, and dedicated Antarctica to peaceful scientific research; its environmental protocol bans mining. Research focuses on climate records in ice cores (extending back some 800,000 years), ozone depletion (the 'ozone hole' was discovered from Antarctic data in 1985), astronomy, and the ecosystems of the Southern Ocean.

Antarctic ice loss is a major uncertainty in projections of sea-level rise: the West Antarctic Ice Sheet alone holds enough ice to raise global seas by several metres if it collapsed — a slow but consequential risk studied intensively by glaciologists.
