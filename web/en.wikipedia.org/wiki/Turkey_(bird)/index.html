The turkey is a large bird in the genus Meleagris, native to North America. There are two extant turkey species: the wild turkey (Meleagris gallopavo) of eastern and central North America and the ocellated turkey (Meleagris ocellata) of the Yucatán Peninsula in Mexico. Males of both species have a distinctive fleshy wattle and are known for their gobbling calls and elaborate courtship displays.

Taxonomy and evolution

Turkeys are classed in the family Phasianidae (pheasants, partridges, junglefowl, chickens, peacocks) within the order Galliformes. The genus Meleagris was described by Linnaeus in 1758. Turkeys evolved in the Americas, with fossil records extending back millions of years.

Domestication

The domestic turkey descends from the south Mexican subspecies of wild turkey, domesticated by Indigenous peoples of Mesoamerica. Spanish explorers brought turkeys to Europe in the early 16th century, from where they spread worldwide as a source of meat. Commercial production is now dominated by broad-breasted white breeds. In the United States, turkey is traditionally eaten at Thanksgiving, and in many countries at Christmas.

Behaviour

Wild turkeys are agile fliers and roost in trees at night. They forage on the ground for acorns, nuts, seeds, and insects. During the breeding season males (toms or gobblers) fan their tail feathers, inflate their bodies, and gobble to attract hens. Despite a reputation for dour temperament in domestic breeds, wild turkeys are wary and highly social.
