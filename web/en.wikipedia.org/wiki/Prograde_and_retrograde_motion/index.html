Prograde and retrograde motion

Prograde motion is motion in the same direction as the rotation of the central body, while retrograde motion is motion in the opposite direction. In astronomy, these terms describe both the orbital motion of a body around another and the rotation of a body on its axis, relative to the general direction of motion in the system.

In the Solar System

Most bodies in the Solar System move in a prograde sense: viewed from above the Sun's north pole, the planets orbit counterclockwise, in the same direction as the Sun's rotation, reflecting the rotation of the original solar nebula. Most planets also rotate prograde. Notable exceptions include Venus, which rotates retrograde (very slowly), and Uranus, whose rotation axis is tilted past 90 degrees so that its rotation is technically retrograde.

Retrograde orbits

A minority of moons orbit retrograde, most famously Neptune's moon Triton, which is thought to be a captured Kuiper belt object. Many irregular satellites of the giant planets also have retrograde orbits, likewise suggesting capture. A number of exoplanets, such as HAT-P-7b, are known to orbit retrograde relative to their star's rotation.

Apparent retrograde motion

Distinct from true retrograde motion is the apparent retrograde motion of the planets as seen from Earth: as Earth overtakes an outer planet, the planet appears to halt and move backward against the background stars for several weeks. This optical effect was a central puzzle of ancient astronomy, explained in the Ptolemaic system by epicycles and naturally resolved in the heliocentric model of Copernicus.
