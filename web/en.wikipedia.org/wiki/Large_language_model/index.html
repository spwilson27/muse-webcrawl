Large language model

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A diagram of stacked transformer layers with attention connections]

A large language model (LLM) is a language model trained with self-supervised learning on a vast amount of text, characterised by its large number of parameters (billions to trillions). LLMs are built on the transformer architecture, using self-attention to model relationships between tokens regardless of distance.

The lineage runs from statistical n-gram models and word embeddings (2013) through the transformer's introduction ('Attention Is All You Need', 2017), BERT (2018), and the GPT series (2018–), whose scaling — bigger models, more data, more compute — produced emergent-seeming abilities in translation, reasoning, coding, and dialogue. GPT-3 (2020, 175 billion parameters) demonstrated few-shot learning from prompts alone.

Training and alignment

Pre-training predicts the next token on web-scale corpora; fine-tuning follows — supervised instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) — to make models helpful and safer. Inference generates text autoregressively, sampling token by token; temperature and context windows (which grew from thousands to millions of tokens in leading models) characterise deployments.

Costs are immense: frontier training runs use tens of thousands of GPUs and budgets reported in the hundreds of millions of dollars, concentrating development in a few companies (OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Meta, Mistral, DeepSeek and others).

Assessment

LLMs show striking fluency and breadth but hallucinate facts, inherit data biases, and consume large amounts of energy; whether they 'understand' is contested. Regardless, they have become general-purpose interfaces to computation — summarising, translating, coding, tutoring — with regulation (e.g. the EU AI Act, 2024) scrambling to keep pace.
