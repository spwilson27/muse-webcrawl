Thought experiment

A thought experiment (German: Gedankenexperiment) is a hypothetical scenario in which a hypothesis, theory, or principle is laid out for the purpose of thinking through its consequences. Thought experiments pose questions that cannot (yet, or in principle) be answered by direct experiment or observation, and explore what would follow if certain assumptions were true. Johann Witt-Hansen established that Hans Christian Ørsted was the first to use the German term Gedankenexperiment around 1811–12; Ernst Mach later popularised the notion and the term.

Uses

Thought experiments are used in philosophy, physics, economics, history, mathematics and other fields. Their purposes include challenging or refuting a theory by showing that its consequences are absurd or contradictory (a form of reductio ad absurdum), clarifying the meaning of concepts, illustrating an idea, supporting or undermining a proposed explanation, and preparing the way for real experiments. Philosophers distinguish, for example, between thought experiments intended as counterexamples and those designed to elicit intuitions about hypothetical cases.

Famous examples

  • Galileo's falling bodies: imagining a heavy and a light ball tied together showed the Aristotelian view that heavier bodies fall faster to be self-contradictory.
  • Newton's cannonball: a cannon on a mountain firing faster and faster until the projectile orbits the Earth, illustrating universal gravitation.
  • Maxwell's demon: a tiny being sorting fast and slow molecules, probing the second law of thermodynamics.
  • Einstein's elevator and trains: scenarios that led to the equivalence principle and clarified simultaneity in special relativity.
  • Schrödinger's cat: a cat simultaneously alive and dead in a sealed box, dramatising problems in interpreting quantum mechanics.
  • The Chinese room (John Searle) and Mary's room (Frank Jackson): philosophical scenarios about machine understanding and qualia.
  • The trolley problem (Philippa Foot, Judith Jarvis Thomson): ethical dilemmas about killing one to save several.

Philosophical debate

Some philosophers, such as John Norton, argue that thought experiments are disguised arguments whose conclusions rest only on the premises they smuggle in; others, including James Robert Brown, defend them as a distinctive source of a priori knowledge. In science, thought experiments often serve heuristically in theory construction — Einstein credited his youthful fantasy of chasing a beam of light with setting him on the path to relativity — while their conclusions still require empirical confirmation.
