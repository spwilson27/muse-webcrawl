Dejima

Location: Nagasaki, Japan

Built: 1634–1636

Trading post of: Dutch East India Company (from 1641)

Dejima (出島, "exit island"; historically romanised as Deshima) is a small fan-shaped artificial island in the bay of Nagasaki, Japan. Built in the 1630s to segregate Portuguese traders, it became the residence of the Dutch East India Company factory from 1641 and, during Japan's period of restricted foreign contact (sakoku), served for over two centuries as Japan's principal — at times only — window of trade and knowledge exchange with Europe.

History

Dejima was constructed on the orders of the Tokugawa shogunate to confine Portuguese merchants and separate them from Japanese society and Christianity. After the Portuguese were expelled in 1639, the Dutch were transferred there from Hirado in 1641. A Dutch chief (opperhoofd) and a small staff of officials, physicians, and clerks lived on the island under close supervision, making an annual court journey to Edo to present gifts to the shōgun. Through Dutch reports and imported books, Japanese scholars practised rangaku ("Dutch learning"), absorbing Western medicine, astronomy, and technology.

Layout and fate

The island measured roughly 120 by 75 metres, connected to the city by a guarded bridge, and contained warehouses, residences, and gardens. After Japan opened to foreign trade in the 1850s, Dejima lost its role; the island was absorbed into the city by land reclamation around 1904. Modern Nagasaki has restored many of its Edo-period buildings, and the site is a national historic monument.
