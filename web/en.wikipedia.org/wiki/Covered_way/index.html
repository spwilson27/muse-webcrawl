Covered way


Profile of a fortification

In military architecture, a covered way (French: chemin couvert) is a path on the counterscarp of a fortification's ditch, protected by a parapet formed by the top edge of the glacis. It runs around the main works, on the far side of the ditch from the ramparts, allowing defenders to move and fire on attackers approaching across the glacis while remaining screened from direct fire.

Features

The covered way typically incorporated places of arms — widened areas at the salients and re-entrants where troops could assemble for sorties — and traverses that limited enfilade fire along its length. From the covered way, defenders could descend into the ditch by ramps or stairs, and sally ports in the counterscarp gave access from the body of the place. It formed the outermost line of infantry defence in the bastioned fortresses developed from the sixteenth to the nineteenth century, notably in the systems of Vauban.

Siege warfare

In a formal siege, the "crowning of the covered way" — seizing it by assault or by sapping across the glacis — was a decisive phase: once lodged there, besiegers could establish breaching batteries close to the scarp and prepare to storm the breach.
