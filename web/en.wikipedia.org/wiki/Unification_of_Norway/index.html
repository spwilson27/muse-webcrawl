Unification of Norway

The unification of Norway (Norwegian: Rikssamlingen) was the process by which the many petty kingdoms of Norway were gradually consolidated into a single kingdom during the Viking Age and early Middle Ages. Tradition credits Harald Fairhair (Harald Hårfagre), who became king after the Battle of Hafrsfjord around 872, as the first king of a united Norway.

Background

Before unification, Norway was divided among numerous small kingdoms and earldoms, including Vestfold, Hordaland, Agder, Rogaland and Trøndelag, ruled by local kings and chieftains. Control of the coastal sea route — the "North Way" that gave Norway its name — was strategically and economically important.

Harald Fairhair and his successors

According to the sagas, Harald vowed not to cut his hair until he ruled all Norway. His campaigns subdued many petty kings, driving some chieftains to emigrate to Iceland and the Atlantic islands. Consolidation continued under his successors, including Haakon the Good and Olaf Tryggvason, and was closely tied to the Christianisation of Norway. The process was not completed in a single reign; regional power struggles continued into the 11th and 12th centuries, and a stable, unified kingdom is generally dated to around 1020–1060.
