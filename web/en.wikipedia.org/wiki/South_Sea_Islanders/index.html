South Sea Islanders

Australian South Sea Islanders
RegionsQueensland and northern New South Wales, Australia
Populationc. 40,000 descendants (estimates vary)
OriginsMelanesian peoples of Vanuatu, Solomon Islands, New Caledonia and neighbouring islands
RecognitionRecognised as a distinct ethnic group by the Australian (1994) and Queensland (2000) governments

South Sea Islanders are the Australian descendants of Pacific Islanders brought to Australia, principally Queensland, between 1863 and 1904 to work as indentured labourers in the sugar cane, cotton and pastoral industries. More than 62,000 Islanders were recruited — many through the coercive practice known as blackbirding, in which people were deceived or kidnapped into service — from some eighty islands of Melanesia, chiefly the New Hebrides (now Vanuatu) and the Solomon Islands.

Recruitment and labour

The traffic began in 1863 when Robert Towns imported Islander labour for his Queensland cotton plantation. The Pacific Island Labourers Act 1868 and later legislation regulated the trade, but abuses persisted. Islanders worked under three-year contracts for low wages, clearing land and harvesting sugar cane in tropical conditions; mortality rates were high.

Deportation

Under the Pacific Island Labourers Act 1901, a cornerstone of the White Australia policy, most Islanders were deported between 1904 and 1908. Roughly 7,000 were allowed to remain or were exempted, forming the nucleus of today's community, concentrated in towns such as Mackay, Bundaberg, Rockhampton, Townsville, Cairns and Innisfail.

Recognition and community

The Australian Government formally recognised Australian South Sea Islanders as a distinct ethnic group in 1994, and Queensland followed in 2000. The community maintains strong church networks — many Islanders were converted to Christianity by the London Missionary Society and other missions — and continues to campaign for acknowledgement of the injustices of blackbirding and for recognition alongside Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, from whom they are legally and culturally distinct.
