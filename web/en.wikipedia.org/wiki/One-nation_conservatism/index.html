One-nation conservatism

One-nation conservatism, also known as one-nationism or Tory democracy, is a form of British conservatism associated with Benjamin Disraeli, who set it out in his novels Sybil (1845) and Coningsby (1844). Disraeli warned that Britain risked dividing into "two nations"—the rich and the poor—and argued that the privileged classes had obligations to improve the condition of the working class in order to preserve social unity.

Principles

One-nation conservatism combines support for free markets and private property with paternalistic social policy, organic social hierarchy, and gradual reform undertaken to pre-empt revolution and maintain national cohesion. It accepts a significant role for the state in welfare and public services, distinguishing it from laissez-faire liberalism and from the more market-fundamentalist strands of later conservatism.

History

Disraeli's governments passed social reforms in the 1870s. In the 20th century, one-nation ideas influenced Conservative leaders from Stanley Baldwin to Harold Macmillan, whose post-war governments accepted much of the welfare state. The phrase was revived by later Tory Reform Group figures and by leaders including David Cameron and Theresa May, who both invoked "one nation" politics.
