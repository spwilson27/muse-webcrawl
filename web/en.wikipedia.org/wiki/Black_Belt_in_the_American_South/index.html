Black Belt in the American South

RegionSouthern United States
StatesVirginia to Texas, principally Alabama, Mississippi, Georgia, Louisiana
Name originDark, fertile prairie soils
Population characteristicHigh proportion of African American residents

The Black Belt is a region of the Southern United States that arcs through the lowlands of the Deep South from Virginia and the Carolinas westward to Texas. The name originally referred to the region's dark, rich, chalky soils — an band of fertile prairie along the ancient shoreline of a Cretaceous sea — which made the area exceptionally suited to cotton cultivation in the nineteenth century.

History

Because the soil was ideal for cotton plantations worked by enslaved labour, the Black Belt developed some of the largest enslaved populations in the United States before the Civil War. After emancipation, the region retained a high proportion of African American residents, many of whom worked as tenant farmers and sharecroppers. Sociologist W. E. B. Du Bois described the Alabama Black Belt in The Souls of Black Folk (1903), and Booker T. Washington's Tuskegee Institute was established in the Alabama portion of the region.

Demographics and society

Today the term commonly refers both to the soil region and, in social and political usage, to a chain of largely rural counties with majority or near-majority African American populations. The region played an important role in the civil rights movement, including the Selma to Montgomery marches in Alabama. Many Black Belt counties face persistent poverty, population loss and health disparities, alongside ongoing debates about land ownership, heirs' property and voting rights.

Geology

The classic Black Belt prairie rests on Cretaceous chalk and marl formations (the Selma Group), which weather into the characteristic dark vertisol soils that gave the region its name.
