1033 program

The 1033 program is a United States Department of Defense initiative that transfers excess military equipment to civilian law enforcement agencies. Named after Section 1033 of the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 1997, the program is administered by the Defense Logistics Agency's Law Enforcement Support Office (LESO). Through it, police departments have received billions of dollars' worth of equipment, ranging from office supplies and clothing to armoured vehicles, aircraft and rifles.

History

The program grew out of the "war on drugs": the National Defense Authorization Act for 1990–91 (Section 1208) first allowed transfers of surplus equipment for counter-drug activities. Section 1033 of the FY1997 act made the authority permanent and broadened it to all law enforcement purposes, with preferences for counter-drug and counter-terrorism activities. After the September 11 attacks, transfers expanded sharply; by the 2010s the program had transferred equipment originally worth more than $5 billion.

Equipment

Most transferred property is mundane — furniture, generators, clothing, tools — but the program is best known for controlled military items: MRAP mine-resistant vehicles, Humvees, helicopters and fixed-wing aircraft, night-vision devices, and M16/M4 rifles. Agencies pay only for shipping and maintenance, and must use items within a year of receipt.

Controversy and reform

Images of heavily armed police during the 2014 Ferguson protests intensified debate over police militarisation. In 2015 President Obama's Executive Order 13688 prohibited transfer of certain items (tracked armoured vehicles, bayonets, grenade launchers, weaponised aircraft) and tightened controls on others; President Trump rescinded those restrictions in 2017, and subsequent administrations revisited them again. Audits have also criticised oversight: a 2017 Government Accountability Office sting obtained controlled equipment for a fictitious agency.
