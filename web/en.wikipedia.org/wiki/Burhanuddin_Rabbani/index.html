Burhanuddin Rabbani (20 September 1940 – 20 September 2011) was an Afghan politician and theologian who served as President of Afghanistan from 1992 to 1996, and remained the internationally recognised head of state of the Islamic State of Afghanistan until 2001, even after the Taliban drove his government from Kabul. An ethnic Tajik from Badakhshan, he led the Jamiat-e Islami party, one of the major mujahideen factions in the war against the Soviet-backed government, and later chaired Afghanistan's High Peace Council. He was assassinated by a suicide bomber in 2011.

Early life and mujahideen leadership

Rabbani studied Islamic theology at Kabul University and at Al-Azhar University in Cairo, where he was influenced by Islamist thought, and became a professor at Kabul. He founded Jamiat-e Islami in the early 1970s; after the communist coup of 1978 and the Soviet invasion he operated from Peshawar, Pakistan, as one of the "Peshawar Seven" mujahideen leaders, with Ahmad Shah Massoud as his most famous military commander in the field.

Presidency and civil war

Under the Peshawar Accords of April 1992, Rabbani became president of the Islamic State of Afghanistan after an interim period. His government was immediately challenged by Gulbuddin Hekmatyar and other factions, and Kabul was devastated by factional fighting during the Afghan Civil War. In September 1996 the Taliban captured Kabul and Rabbani fled north, leading the Northern Alliance with Massoud. After the US-led intervention of late 2001 toppled the Taliban, Rabbani briefly returned to Kabul before handing power to the interim administration of Hamid Karzai in December 2001, as agreed at the Bonn Conference.

Later life and assassination

Rabbani remained an influential elder figure, supporting Karzai while leading Jamiat-e Islami in parliament. In 2010 he was appointed chairman of the High Peace Council, charged with negotiating with the Taliban. On 20 September 2011 — his 71st birthday — a suicide bomber posing as a Taliban peace emissary, with explosives concealed in his turban, killed Rabbani at his Kabul home. His death was a major blow to reconciliation efforts; Kabul's airport road area and a university were later named in his memory, and his son Salahuddin Rabbani became a senior government minister.
