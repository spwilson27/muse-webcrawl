In electromagnetism, the electric susceptibility χe (Latin: susceptibilis, "receptive") is a dimensionless proportionality constant that indicates the degree of polarisation of a dielectric material in response to an applied electric field. The greater the electric susceptibility, the greater the capacity of the material to polarise and thereby reduce the total field inside itself.

Definition

For a linear, isotropic dielectric, the electric polarisation density P is proportional to the electric field E: P = ε0χeE, where ε0 is the permittivity of free space. The electric displacement field is then D = ε0E + P = εE, giving the simple relation between relative permittivity (dielectric constant) and susceptibility: εr = 1 + χe. In vacuum χe = 0; in most ordinary dielectrics it is a small positive number (for example, about 1.0006 for air, and up to tens or more for high-κ materials such as strontium titanate).

General and nonlinear cases

In anisotropic materials, susceptibility is a tensor χij, so polarisation need not be parallel to the field. In many materials χe depends on the frequency of the applied field, with an associated imaginary part describing dielectric loss; the real and imaginary parts are linked by the Kramers–Kronig relations. At very high field strengths, nonlinear terms appear: P acquires contributions proportional to χ(2)E², χ(3)E³ and so on, which underlie nonlinear optics effects such as frequency doubling and the Kerr effect.

Microscopic origin

Polarisation arises from several microscopic mechanisms acting at different timescales: displacement of electron clouds, shifting of ions in a lattice, and reorientation of permanent molecular dipoles. Models such as the Clausius–Mossotti relation connect the macroscopic susceptibility to molecular polarisability.
