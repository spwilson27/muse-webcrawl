Overpopulation in pets

Pet overpopulation is the condition in which the number of companion animals — chiefly dogs and cats — exceeds the number of homes available for them, resulting in large populations of stray and feral animals and in the euthanasia of healthy animals in shelters. It is driven by uncontrolled breeding of owned and free-roaming animals, abandonment, and surrender of pets by owners.

Scale

Estimates vary widely. In the United States, several million dogs and cats enter shelters each year; the number euthanised annually has fallen from well over ten million in the 1970s–80s to roughly a million or fewer in recent years, as spay/neuter programmes and adoption rates have grown. Globally, the World Health Organization has estimated the free-roaming dog population at around 200 million or more, with consequences including rabies transmission, which kills tens of thousands of people annually.

Control measures

Standard responses include subsidised spaying and neutering, trap–neuter–return (TNR) programmes for feral cats, licensing and microchipping, adoption promotion, restrictions on commercial breeding and pet-shop sales, and public education on responsible ownership. No-kill sheltering aims to save at least 90% of incoming animals. Critics debate the effectiveness of TNR for reducing feral cat numbers and its impact on wildlife, since free-roaming cats are significant predators of birds and small mammals.
