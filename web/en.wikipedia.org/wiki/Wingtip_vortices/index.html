Wingtip vortices

Wingtip vortices are circular patterns of rotating air left behind a wing as it generates lift. As an airfoil generates lift, air near the top of the wing is at lower pressure than the air underneath the wing. Near the wingtips, the higher-pressure air beneath flows around the tips into the lower-pressure region above, producing a swirling trailing vortex from each tip.

Wingtip vortices visible behind an agricultural aircraft

Formation

The vortices arise from the pressure differential between the upper and lower wing surfaces. Because the wing is of finite span, air spills around the tip, and the boundary layers leaving the upper and lower surfaces roll up into two concentrated line vortices trailing downstream. The strength of the vortices is proportional to the lift and weight of the aircraft and inversely proportional to its speed and wing span: heavy, slow aircraft with short spans produce the strongest vortices.

Effects and hazards

Wingtip vortices are the principal cause of wake turbulence, which can be hazardous to following aircraft, particularly smaller aircraft encountering the wake of a large one. Air traffic control therefore enforces separation minima based on aircraft weight categories. The vortices also contribute to induced drag, since energy is continuously shed into the trailing wake. Devices such as winglets reduce vortex strength and induced drag by diffusing the flow at the tip.

Behavior

Behind the aircraft, the vortex pair descends and travels with the wind, persisting for up to several minutes in calm air before dissipating. Near the ground, the vortices spread outward and may rebound, an effect known as ground effect on wake behavior that controllers take into account for departures.
