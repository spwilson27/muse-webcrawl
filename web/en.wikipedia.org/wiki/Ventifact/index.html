Ventifact

In geology, a ventifact (also wind-faceted stone) is a rock that has been abraded, pitted, etched, grooved, or polished by wind-driven sand or ice crystals. These geomorphic features are most typically found in arid environments where there is little vegetation to interfere with aeolian particle transport, where there are strong winds, and where there is a steady but not overwhelming supply of sand.

Formation

Windblown grains strike exposed rock surfaces, slowly wearing them away. Because most abrasive action happens within half a metre of the ground, stones develop flat facets facing the prevailing wind, meeting at sharp edges. A stone with one facet is sometimes called a dreikanter when three facets meet in pyramidal form. Classic forms include faceted pebbles from desert pavements, grooved bedrock in polar regions (such as Antarctica's Dry Valleys), and the wind-scoured rocks photographed on Mars by rover missions, which record the planet's prevailing wind directions.

Uses in science

Ventifacts serve as palaeo-wind indicators: the orientation of their facets and grooves reveals past wind regimes after the landscape has changed. On Mars, their study helps reconstruct atmospheric conditions and surface processes. Small ventifacts should not be confused with yardangs, which are much larger, streamlined bedrock landforms carved by the same abrasive processes.
