Superacid

A superacid is an acid with an acidity greater than that of 100% pure sulfuric acid. By the classical definition, its Hammett acidity function (H0) is lower than −12, the value for pure sulfuric acid. Superacids are powerful protonating and dehydrating media and are used to generate and stabilize unusual carbocations and other reactive species in chemistry.

History

The term was introduced in 1927 by James Bryant Conant for acids stronger than conventional mineral acids. In the 1960s George Olah developed "magic acid" — an equimolar mixture of fluorosulfuric acid (HSO3F) and antimony pentafluoride (SbF5), so named after it dissolved a paraffin candle — and used superacid media at low temperatures to observe long-lived carbocations directly by NMR, work that contributed to his 1994 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Fluoroantimonic acid (HF/SbF5) is among the strongest known superacids, with H0 values reaching about −28 to −31, some 1016 times stronger than concentrated sulfuric acid.

Types and uses

Brønsted superacids include fluorosulfuric acid, triflic acid (H0 ≈ −14.9), and perchloric acid; Lewis superacids include antimony pentafluoride and boron trifluoride systems. In 2004, Christopher Reed's group reported carborane acids such as H(CHB11Cl11), among the strongest isolable acids yet remarkably non-corrosive to glassware because their anions are extremely weakly coordinating. Industrially, superacid chemistry underlies hydrocarbon isomerization, alkylation, and cracking catalysts, while in research it enables protonation of extremely weak bases — even alkanes and noble gases.
