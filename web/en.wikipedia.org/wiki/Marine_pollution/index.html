Marine pollution

Marine pollution occurs when substances used or spread by humans, such as industrial, agricultural and residential waste, particles, noise, excess carbon dioxide or invasive organisms, enter the ocean and cause harmful effects there. The majority of marine pollution comes from land-based sources; air pollution also contributes by carrying pesticides and other chemicals into the sea. Pollution of the oceans is associated with the broader problem of water pollution.

Sources and pathways

Marine pollution reaches the sea through several pathways. Direct discharge includes untreated sewage and industrial effluent released into rivers and coastal waters. Runoff carries fertilisers, pesticides, oil and sediment from land into waterways: nutrient runoff in particular drives eutrophication and coastal "dead zones" where oxygen depletion kills marine life. Other pathways include deliberate dumping of waste at sea, spills from ships and offshore installations, atmospheric deposition, and deep-sea mining.

Types of pollution

Oil pollution

Oil enters the sea from tanker accidents, operational discharges, offshore drilling and natural seeps. Major spills such as the Exxon Valdez (1989) and Deepwater Horizon (2010) disasters cause acute damage to wildlife and coastlines, and smaller chronic inputs collectively exceed spill volumes.

Plastic pollution

An estimated several million tonnes of plastic enter the oceans each year. Large items entangle and are ingested by wildlife, while weathered fragments become microplastics that permeate food webs. Ocean currents concentrate floating debris in gyres such as the Great Pacific garbage patch.

Chemical and nutrient pollution

Persistent organic pollutants, heavy metals such as mercury, and excess nitrogen and phosphorus accumulate in marine ecosystems and in seafood. Ocean acidification, caused by absorption of anthropogenic carbon dioxide, is sometimes treated as a form of pollution because it alters ocean chemistry.

Effects

Marine pollution damages habitats including coral reefs, seagrass beds and mangroves; causes algal blooms and hypoxia; introduces invasive species via ballast water; and affects human health through contaminated seafood and lost livelihoods in fisheries and tourism.

Regulation

International instruments include the MARPOL convention on pollution from ships, the London Convention on dumping at sea, and regional seas agreements. The United Nations Environment Programme coordinates much of the scientific monitoring of ocean pollution.
