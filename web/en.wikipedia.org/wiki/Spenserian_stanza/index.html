Spenserian stanza

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Edmund Spenser devised the stanza for The Faerie Queene.

The Spenserian stanza is a fixed verse form invented by Edmund Spenser for his epic poem The Faerie Queene (1590–96). Each stanza consists of nine lines: the first eight are iambic pentameter and the ninth is an iambic hexameter, called an alexandrine. The rhyme scheme is interlocking — ababbcbcc — so that the middle rhyme links the two quatrains and propels the verse forward. The form combines elements of the Italian ottava rima and the French-derived rhyme royal of Chaucer.

Example

A Gentle Knight was pricking on the plaine,
Y cladd in mightie armes and siluer shielde,
Wherein old dints of deepe wounds did remaine,
The cruell markes of many' a bloody fielde;
Yet armes till that time did he neuer wield:
His angry steede did chide his foming bitt,
As much disdayning to the curbe to yield:
Full iolly knight he seemd, and faire did sitt,
As one for knightly giusts and fierce encounters fitt.
— The Faerie Queene, Book I, Canto I, stanza 1

Later use

After long neglect, the stanza was revived by the Romantic poets. Notable later examples include Robert Burns's "The Cotter's Saturday Night", Lord Byron's Childe Harold's Pilgrimage, Percy Bysshe Shelley's Adonais and The Revolt of Islam, John Keats's "The Eve of St. Agnes", Walter Scott's The Lady of the Lake (in part), William Wordsworth's early work, and Alfred Tennyson's "The Lotos-Eaters". The closing alexandrine gives each stanza a sense of expansion and closure, making the form well suited to narrative and descriptive poetry, though its difficulty and interlocking rhymes demand considerable technical skill.
