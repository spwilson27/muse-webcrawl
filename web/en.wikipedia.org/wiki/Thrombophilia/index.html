Thrombophilia

Thrombophilia
SpecialtyHaematology
ComplicationsDeep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, recurrent miscarriage
CausesGenetic (factor V Leiden, antithrombin deficiency) or acquired (antiphospholipid syndrome, cancer, pregnancy)
TreatmentAnticoagulation when indicated

Thrombophilia (sometimes called hypercoagulability or a prothrombotic state) is an abnormality of blood coagulation that increases the risk of thrombosis — blood clots in blood vessels. Such abnormalities can be identified in about 50% of people who have an episode of thrombosis that was not provoked by other causes. A significant proportion of the population has a detectable thrombophilic abnormality, but most of these never develop thrombosis.

Causes

Inherited

The best-known inherited thrombophilia is factor V Leiden, a mutation that makes factor V resistant to inactivation by activated protein C; heterozygous carriers have a modestly increased risk, homozygotes a much higher one. Other inherited conditions include the prothrombin G20210A mutation, and deficiencies of the natural anticoagulants antithrombin, protein C and protein S — rarer but more strongly thrombogenic.

Acquired

Acquired causes include the antiphospholipid syndrome (associated with lupus anticoagulant and anticardiolipin antibodies), malignancy, pregnancy and the puerperium, oestrogen-containing contraceptives and hormone therapy, immobility, surgery, nephrotic syndrome and myeloproliferative neoplasms. Raised levels of clotting factors VIII, IX and XI, and hyperhomocysteinaemia, also increase risk.

Clinical features

Thrombophilia presents through its complications: deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, thrombosis at unusual sites (cerebral, hepatic or mesenteric veins), and obstetric complications such as recurrent miscarriage, particularly with antiphospholipid syndrome. Arterial events are less characteristic except in antiphospholipid syndrome and hyperhomocysteinaemia.

Investigation and treatment

Testing is generally reserved for younger patients, those with unprovoked or recurrent events, thrombosis at unusual sites, or a strong family history, because results seldom change management. Treatment of thrombosis is with anticoagulants — heparin, warfarin or direct oral anticoagulants — with duration guided by whether the event was provoked and by recurrence risk. Asymptomatic carriers are not routinely anticoagulated.
