Ostrich

Ostriches are large flightless birds of the genus Struthio, native to Africa. Two living species are now usually recognised: the common ostrich (Struthio camelus) and the Somali ostrich (Struthio molybdophanes). The common ostrich is the world's largest living bird: males stand up to about 2.8 m (9 ft 2 in) tall and weigh over 150 kg, and the species lays the largest eggs of any living bird.

Biology and behaviour

Ostriches are built for running, with long bare legs, two-toed feet and speeds of up to about 70 km/h, making them the fastest birds on land. Their wings, useless for flight, are used in courtship displays and for shading chicks. Ostriches live in nomadic groups in savanna and desert, feeding mainly on seeds, shrubs, fruit and occasional insects. A dominant male mates with several hens, which lay their eggs in a single communal nest scraped in the ground; the male incubates at night and the dominant hen by day. Contrary to the popular myth, ostriches do not bury their heads in sand, though they may lie flat to avoid detection.

Humans and ostriches

Ostriches are farmed worldwide for feathers, leather, meat and eggs; the feather trade drove near-extinction of wild populations in the nineteenth century before farming became established. The birds also have a long cultural history, featuring in ancient Egyptian art and heraldry.
