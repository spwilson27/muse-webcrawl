Subdivisions of Scotland

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

For local government purposes, Scotland is divided into 32 areas designated as "council areas" (comhairlean in Scottish Gaelic), which are all governed by single-tier authorities designated as "councils". They came into being on 1 April 1996 under the Local Government etc. (Scotland) Act 1994, replacing the previous two-tier system of regions and districts.

History

Historically, Scotland was subdivided into a shifting patchwork of jurisdictions: sheriffdoms, counties (or shires) governed in part by commissioners of supply, burghs with their own town councils, and parishes. The counties — 33 in number by the nineteenth century — were standardised for local government by the Local Government (Scotland) Act 1889. In 1975 these were replaced by nine mainland regions and three island areas, themselves divided into districts. The 1996 reorganisation created the present unitary council areas, many of which differ considerably in population and extent: Glasgow City is the most populous, while Highland is the largest by area and the largest local government area in the United Kingdom.

Council areas

The 32 council areas include the cities of Aberdeen, Dundee, Edinburgh, Glasgow, Inverness, Perth, and Stirling, county-like areas such as Aberdeenshire, Argyll and Bute, Dumfries and Galloway, Fife, Highland, North Lanarkshire, South Lanarkshire, and the island authorities of Orkney, Shetland, and Na h-Eileanan Siar (the Western Isles).

Other subdivisions

Beyond local government, Scotland has a variety of other geographies: registration counties, lieutenancy areas used for ceremonial purposes, historic counties retained in some contexts, community council areas, civil parishes (no longer administrative but still used for statistics), and subdivisions used by bodies such as Police Scotland, NHS health boards, and the Scottish Parliament's electoral regions.
