Eutectic system

A eutectic system (from the Greek εὔτηκτος eútēktos, "easily melted") is a homogeneous mixture of substances that melts or solidifies at a single temperature lower than the melting point of any of the individual constituents. The temperature at which this occurs is the eutectic temperature, and the composition at which it occurs is the eutectic composition. On a phase diagram, the eutectic temperature and composition together define the eutectic point.

Behaviour on cooling

When a liquid of exactly the eutectic composition is cooled, it solidifies at a constant temperature into an intimate mixture of two (or more) solid phases simultaneously. The resulting eutectic structure is often lamellar — alternating layers of the two solids — because the two phases must grow cooperatively, each rejecting the other's component into the remaining liquid ahead of the solidification front. Compositions near, but not at, the eutectic point first precipitate a primary phase and then undergo the eutectic reaction with the remaining liquid.

Types

Eutectic systems occur in alloys, salts, minerals and organic mixtures. Related invariant reactions in phase diagrams include the peritectic (where a liquid and a solid react to form a different solid), the eutectoid (the solid-state analogue, in which one solid decomposes into two others), and the monotectic reaction.

Examples and applications

  • Solder: Traditional tin–lead solder of 63% tin and 37% lead is a eutectic alloy melting at 183 °C, prized in electronics because it passes directly from liquid to solid without a pasty range.
  • Cast iron: The iron–carbon eutectic at about 4.3% carbon and 1,147 °C governs the solidification of cast irons.
  • Salt and ice: The sodium chloride–water eutectic at −21.1 °C explains why salt is used to de-ice roads and to make freezing mixtures for ice cream.
  • Local anaesthetics: EMLA cream is a eutectic mixture of lidocaine and prilocaine that is liquid at room temperature.
  • Metallic glasses and brazing alloys exploit deep eutectics where the melting point is strongly depressed.

Thermodynamics

At the eutectic point, three phases (liquid and two solids) coexist in equilibrium. By the Gibbs phase rule, a two-component system at constant pressure has zero degrees of freedom at this invariant point, which is why the temperature and composition are fixed. The depression of the liquidus curves arises because mixing lowers the chemical potential of the liquid phase relative to the pure solids.
