Euler–Maclaurin formula

The Euler–Maclaurin formula, sometimes also called Euler's summation formula, is a formula in mathematics that relates a finite sum of values of a function to the integral of the same function, with correction terms involving the derivatives of the function at the endpoints and the Bernoulli numbers. It was discovered independently by Leonhard Euler and Colin Maclaurin around 1735.

Statement

For integers m ≤ n and a sufficiently smooth function f,

Σk=mn f(k) = ∫mn f(x) dx + (f(m)+f(n))/2 + Σk=1p B2k/(2k)! · ( f(2k−1)(n) − f(2k−1)(m) ) + Rp,

where B2 = 1/6, B4 = −1/30, B6 = 1/42, … are the Bernoulli numbers and Rp is a remainder term expressible as an integral involving periodic Bernoulli polynomials. The series in the correction terms is generally asymptotic rather than convergent: truncating at the right point gives very accurate approximations.

Applications

The formula is used to evaluate sums that vary slowly, to derive Stirling's approximation to the factorial, and to analyse sums such as the harmonic numbers, for which it yields the Euler–Mascheroni constant as the difference between Hn and ln n. It underlies the trapezoidal rule's error expansion in numerical integration and the technique of summation acceleration in computational mathematics.
