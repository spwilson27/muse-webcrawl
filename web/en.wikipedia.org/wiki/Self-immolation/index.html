Self-immolation

Self-immolation is the act of setting oneself on fire, usually as a form of political protest, religious devotion, or extreme self-sacrifice. The term derives from the Latin immolare, meaning "to sacrifice." Because of its dramatic and irreversible nature, self-immolation has long been used to draw public attention to a cause and to express the depth of a protester's grievance.

History

Self-immolation has roots in several religious traditions. In some strands of Mahayana Buddhism and Hinduism, offering one's own body was regarded as an act of profound devotion. In early modern India, the related practice of sati — a widow immolating herself on her husband's funeral pyre — was eventually outlawed under British colonial rule.

In the modern era, self-immolation became prominent as a protest tactic during the 1960s. The Vietnamese Buddhist monk Thích Quảng Đức burned himself to death in Saigon in 1963 to protest the persecution of Buddhists by the South Vietnamese government; the photograph of the event became one of the most famous images of the twentieth century. In 1969, the Czech student Jan Palach set himself on fire in Prague to protest the Warsaw Pact invasion of Czechoslovakia.

In December 2010, the Tunisian street vendor Mohamed Bouazizi set himself alight after officials confiscated his goods; his death is widely credited with igniting the Tunisian revolution and the wider Arab Spring. Self-immolations by Tibetans protesting Chinese rule also drew international attention during the 2010s.

Motivations and effects

Scholars note that self-immolation is generally intended as a communicative act: the protester seeks to shock an audience and compel a response where other channels have failed. Its effectiveness is debated; while it reliably attracts media coverage, it does not always produce the political change sought, and governments sometimes suppress reporting of such acts to prevent imitation.
