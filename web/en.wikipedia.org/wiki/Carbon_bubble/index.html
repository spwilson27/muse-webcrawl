Carbon bubble

Image placeholder: Fossil fuel infrastructure and stranded-asset risk.

The carbon bubble is a hypothesised economic bubble in the valuation of fossil-fuel companies, based on the assumption that companies' market capitalisations reflect the full exploitation of their oil, gas, and coal reserves. Because burning all known reserves would far exceed the global carbon budget compatible with limiting climate change, a portion of those reserves — and the infrastructure built around them — may become "stranded assets", implying current valuations are inflated.

Origin of the concept

The concept was popularised by the Carbon Tracker Initiative's 2011 report Unburnable Carbon, which estimated that only about a fifth of listed fossil fuel reserves could be burned under a 2 °C carbon budget, and by subsequent analysis of the "stranded assets" problem at the University of Oxford's Smith School. Former Bank of England governor Mark Carney brought the issue to wide attention in his 2015 "Tragedy of the Horizon" speech, warning of financial-stability risks if the transition to a low-carbon economy were abrupt.

Responses

The carbon bubble thesis underpins the fossil fuel divestment movement and demands for climate-related financial disclosure, institutionalised in the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD). Critics argue that markets gradually price in transition risk and that reserve valuations already assume declining long-term demand growth.
