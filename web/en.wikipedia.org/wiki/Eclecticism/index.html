Eclecticism

Eclecticism is a conceptual approach that does not hold rigidly to a single paradigm or set of assumptions, but instead draws upon multiple theories, styles or ideas to gain complementary insights into a subject, or applies different theories in particular cases. The word derives from the Greek eklektikos, "choosing the best."

Philosophy

The Eclectics were a class of ancient Greek and Roman philosophers who combined doctrines from different schools — Platonic, Stoic, Aristotelian and others — rather than adhering to one system. In the 19th century the French philosopher Victor Cousin promoted "eclecticism" as an official philosophy in France, selecting what he took to be true from each historical system.

Art and architecture

In the arts, eclecticism describes the borrowing and combining of elements from various historical styles. Nineteenth-century architecture was notably eclectic, producing buildings in Gothic Revival, Classical Revival, Romanesque and other revival styles, sometimes mixed in a single design. The term can be used pejoratively, implying a lack of originality or coherence, or approvingly, for imaginative synthesis.

Other fields

Eclectic approaches are common in psychology and psychotherapy, where practitioners combine techniques from different schools; in medicine, historically, where the 19th-century "Eclectic medicine" movement used botanical remedies; and in religion, where eclectic paganism draws from multiple traditions.
