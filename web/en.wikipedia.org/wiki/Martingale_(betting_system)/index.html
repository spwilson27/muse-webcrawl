Martingale (betting system)

A martingale is a class of betting strategies that originated in 18th-century France. The simplest and best-known of these strategies was designed for a game in which the gambler wins their stake if a coin comes up heads and loses it if the coin comes up tails. The strategy consists of the gambler doubling their bet after every loss, so that the first win would recover all previous losses plus win a profit equal to the original stake.

How it works

The gambler begins with a base bet, say $10, on an even-money proposition. If the bet loses, the next bet is doubled to $20; if that loses, $40, and so on. When a bet finally wins, the cumulative winnings cover all previous losses plus $10 profit; the gambler then resets to the base bet. Because a win is (in theory) inevitable eventually, the system appears to guarantee a steady profit of one base unit per sequence.

Why it fails

The strategy's fatal flaw is that bet sizes grow exponentially. A losing streak of only ten bets turns a $10 base bet into a required wager of $10,240, with $10,230 already lost. In practice, gamblers have finite bankrolls and casinos impose table limits, so a sufficiently long losing streak — which becomes increasingly likely the longer one plays — exhausts the gambler's funds or hits the betting cap, producing a catastrophic loss. Analyses show the expected value remains negative (in a casino game with a house edge) or zero (in a fair game); the system trades many small wins for a small probability of ruin. This intuition is formalised in probability theory by the concept of a martingale and the optional stopping theorem: no stopping rule or staking plan can change the expected outcome of a fair game.

Variants

  • Grand martingale: after each loss, double the bet and add one extra unit, aiming to win more than the base stake.
  • Anti-martingale (reverse martingale): double after wins and halve after losses, attempting to exploit winning streaks.

Applications beyond gambling

"Martingale" strategies appear in trading, where traders double down on losing positions (also called averaging down). The same mathematics applies: the approach converts frequent small gains into a risk of total ruin, a pattern implicated in various trading blow-ups.
