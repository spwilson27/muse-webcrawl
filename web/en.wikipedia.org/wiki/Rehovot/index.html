Rehovot (Hebrew: רְחוֹבוֹת, Reḥovot, lit. "broad places" or "streets") is a city in the Central District of Israel, about 20 kilometres south of Tel Aviv. Founded in 1890 as a moshava during the First Aliyah, Rehovot grew from a citrus-growing agricultural settlement into a major centre of scientific research and higher education, home to the Weizmann Institute of Science and the Faculty of Agriculture of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem.

History

Rehovot was founded in 1890 by Polish Jewish immigrants of the Menuha VeNahala society on land purchased from local owners, near the site of Khirbat Deiran. Its name comes from Genesis 26:22, where Isaac names a well Rehoboth. The colony planted vineyards and later citrus groves, and was notable for early cooperation — and periodic friction — between Jewish settlers and neighbouring Arab villages. Chaim Weizmann chose the town for his scientific institute in the 1930s; the Daniel Sieff Research Institute, founded in 1934, was expanded and renamed the Weizmann Institute of Science in 1949, with Weizmann, Israel's first president, buried on its grounds. Rehovot received city status in 1950.

Science and economy

The Weizmann Institute, the Robert H. Smith Faculty of Agriculture, Food and Environment of the Hebrew University, and the Kaplan Medical Center anchor the city's knowledge economy. The Tamar science park hosts biotechnology and high-tech firms, and Rehovot brands itself as Israel's "city of science and culture". Citrus research remains historically significant: the Volcani Institute's origins are tied to the town's agricultural experiment station.

Demographics and culture

Rehovot's roughly 150,000 residents include large communities of immigrants from the former Soviet Union, Ethiopia and Yemen — Yemenite heritage is celebrated in the Sha'arayim neighbourhood — alongside a substantial religious and secular mix. The city has a railway station on the Lod–Ashkelon line and is crossed by Highway 40.
