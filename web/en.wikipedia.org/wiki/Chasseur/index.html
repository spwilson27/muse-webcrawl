Chasseur (French pronunciation: [ʃa.sœʁ], "hunter") is a designation given to certain regiments of French and Belgian light infantry (chasseurs à pied) or light cavalry (chasseurs à cheval), trained for rapid action and skirmishing. The term and the role were modeled on the German Jäger troops of the 18th century.

History

France first raised chasseur units during the War of the Austrian Succession in the 1740s, recruiting huntsmen, gamekeepers, and frontier woodsmen as marksmen and scouts. Under Napoleon, the chasseurs expanded greatly: regiments of chasseurs à cheval served as light cavalry for reconnaissance and pursuit, and the Imperial Guard maintained elite chasseur regiments whose bearskin headdress and green uniforms became iconic. The Chasseurs à Cheval of the Guard were famously charged with escorting the Emperor.

Chasseurs à pied and alpins

In 1838 the Duke of Orléans created battalions of chasseurs à pied as elite light infantry, distinguished by fast marching (the pas de chasseur), marksmanship, and dark blue uniforms; their bugle calls and rapid step remain traditions in the modern French Army. From 1888, battalions stationed in the Alps were converted into chasseurs alpins, mountain infantry wearing the distinctive wide blue beret (tarte). Chasseurs alpins fought in both world wars, notably at the Vosges and in Norway in 1940, and form today's 27th Mountain Infantry Brigade.

Other countries

Belgium raised regiments of chasseurs à pied and à cheval in the 19th century, and the name was borrowed widely — including by French colonial units and, as a courtesy, by units in other francophone armies. In the modern French Army the title survives in units such as the 16th Battalion of Chasseurs à Pied and the 4th Chasseurs (armored cavalry) Regiment.
