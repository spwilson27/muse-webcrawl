Possession (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In law, possession is the control a person intentionally exercises toward a thing, whether or not they own it. The classical analysis (Salmond) requires corpus (physical control) and animus possidendi (intention to possess). Possession is protected in its own right: a possessor has a title good against everyone except someone with a better right, so that even a thief may sue a later wrongdoer, and finders may prevail against all but the true owner (the principle illustrated by Armory v Delamirie, 1722, the chimney-sweep's jewel case).

Possession has constitutive effects: it is evidence of ownership; long possession of land can mature into title by adverse possession or prescription; delivery transfers possession in sales of goods; and criminal law protects possession (of drugs, weapons, stolen goods) as an offence in itself. In civil-law systems possession is likewise a protected fact distinct from ownership, with possessory actions (interdicts) restoring it without deciding title.
