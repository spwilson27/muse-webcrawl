Truth and Reconciliation Commission (South Africa)

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) was a court-like restorative justice body assembled in South Africa in 1995 after the end of apartheid. Authorized by the Promotion of National Unity and Reconciliation Act, it invited victims of gross human rights violations to testify about their experiences and allowed perpetrators to seek amnesty in exchange for full disclosure of politically motivated crimes.

Organisation and work

President Nelson Mandela appointed Archbishop Desmond Tutu as chair. The commission operated through three committees: the Human Rights Violations Committee, which heard testimony from some 21,000 victims; the Amnesty Committee, which considered over 7,000 applications and granted amnesty in roughly 850 cases; and the Reparation and Rehabilitation Committee. Public hearings, many televised, ran from 1996, and the commission's five-volume report was presented to Mandela in October 1998, with two further volumes in 2003.

Findings

The TRC found the apartheid state responsible for the preponderance of gross violations between 1960 and 1994, including torture, killings and chemical and biological warfare programmes, while also finding liberation movements, including the African National Congress, responsible for violations such as abuses in exile camps and attacks harming civilians. Political parties across the spectrum criticized aspects of the findings.

Legacy

The commission became the most influential model for truth commissions worldwide, though its reparations programme was criticized as inadequate — victims received once-off payments of about R30,000 — and prosecutions of those denied or not seeking amnesty were slow to follow. Debates continue about how far truth-telling advanced reconciliation in the face of persistent economic inequality.
