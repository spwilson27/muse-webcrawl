Robert Blum (10 November 1807 – 9 November 1848) was a German democratic politician, publicist, poet, and revolutionary. He was one of the most popular leaders of the German revolutions of 1848–49, a deputy in the Frankfurt Parliament, and was executed by imperial troops after the suppression of the Vienna uprising, becoming a martyr of the German democratic movement.

Early life

Born into poverty in Cologne, Blum worked as a craftsman's assistant and theatre secretary, eventually settling in Leipzig, where he became a bookseller, writer, and organizer of the German Catholic dissenting movement. His political almanacs and speeches made him a leading voice of the Saxon liberal and democratic opposition in the 1840s.

Revolution of 1848

When revolution broke out in March 1848, Blum was elected vice-president of the Preliminary Parliament (Vorparlament) and then a deputy to the Frankfurt National Assembly, where he led the democratic left. In October 1848 he travelled to Vienna as part of a parliamentary delegation of sympathy for the city's revolutionaries. When the uprising was crushed by Prince Windischgrätz's imperial army, Blum — despite his parliamentary immunity — was arrested, court-martialled, and shot on 9 November 1848 in the Brigittenau.

Legacy

Blum's execution caused outrage across Germany and made him a symbol of the failed revolution. A popular revolutionary song, the Robert-Blum-Lied, commemorated his death. Streets and institutions in Germany bear his name, and he is remembered as a pioneer of German democracy.
