Amylase

Amylase
EC number3.2.1.1 (alpha-amylase)
FunctionHydrolysis of starch into sugars
Found inSaliva, pancreas, plants, fungi, bacteria
TypesAlpha (α), beta (β), gamma (γ)
Industrial usesBrewing, baking, detergents, high-fructose corn syrup

An amylase is an enzyme that catalyses the hydrolysis of starch (from Latin amylum) into sugars such as maltose and dextrins. Amylase is present in the saliva of humans and some other mammals, where it begins the chemical process of digestion; foods containing much starch but little sugar, such as rice and potatoes, take on a slightly sweet taste as amylase breaks the starch down. The pancreas secretes a second amylase into the small intestine to complete carbohydrate digestion.

Classification

Alpha-amylases (EC 3.2.1.1) cleave internal alpha-1,4 glycosidic bonds at random and are found in animals, plants, fungi, and bacteria; human saliva and pancreatic juice contain them, requiring chloride ions and calcium for activity. Beta-amylases act from the non-reducing end, releasing maltose, and are important in ripening fruit and malting grain. Gamma-amylases (glucoamylases) cleave the final 1,4 bonds to release glucose. Serum amylase measurement is a classic diagnostic test for acute pancreatitis.

History and uses

Amylase was the first enzyme to be discovered and isolated: in 1833 Anselme Payen extracted it from malt and named it "diastase." Industrial amylases are produced by fermentation (e.g., from Bacillus species and Aspergillus fungi) and used in brewing and baking, laundry detergents, paper making, and the production of glucose and high-fructose syrups.
