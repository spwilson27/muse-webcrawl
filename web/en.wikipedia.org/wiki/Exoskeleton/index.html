Exoskeleton

An exoskeleton is a skeleton that is on the exterior of an animal, serving both as armor and as a body covering, in sharp contrast with the internal endoskeleton (e.g. that of a human) which is enclosed underneath other soft tissues. Some large, hard exoskeletons are known as shells.

FunctionsProtection, support, muscle attachment, water conservation

Roles

Exoskeletons contain rigid components powered by muscles and joints, which, together with other components of the exoskeleton, provide protection of internal tissues, support for organs, excretion of waste products (at moulting), and sensing of the environment. They also act as a barrier against desiccation in terrestrial arthropods.

Composition and growth

Arthropod exoskeletons are built largely of chitin, a nitrogenous polysaccharide, often hardened with proteins (sclerotisation) or minerals. Mollusc shells are chiefly calcium carbonate secreted by the mantle. Because a rigid exoskeleton cannot expand, arthropods grow by moulting (ecdysis): the old cuticle is shed and a new, larger one hardens in its place, leaving the animal briefly soft and vulnerable.

Diversity

Exoskeletons range from the microshells of diatoms and radiolarians to the massive shells of giant clams. They are the ancestral body plan of the most species-rich animal phylum, the arthropods — insects, crustaceans, arachnids and myriapods.

Artificial exoskeletons

The term is also applied to wearable robotic devices — powered exoskeletons — that augment human strength or restore mobility to people with limb paralysis, and to the external frames of some buildings and vehicles.
