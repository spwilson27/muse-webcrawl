Countercurrent exchange

Countercurrent exchange
TypeTransfer mechanism (heat, gases, solutes)
Found inFish gills, kidneys, limbs of birds and mammals, industrial heat exchangers
RelatedCountercurrent multiplication

Countercurrent exchange is a mechanism in which two fluids flow in opposite directions past one another, exchanging heat, gases or dissolved substances along their length. Because the flows run in opposite directions, a gradient is maintained along the whole exchange surface, allowing transfer to approach completeness — far more efficiently than if the streams flowed in the same (concurrent) direction, in which case they would simply approach a common intermediate value.

In biology

Fish gills: blood in the gill lamellae flows opposite to the water passing over them, so blood always meets water with a higher oxygen content; fish thereby extract up to 80–90% of dissolved oxygen, compared with roughly 25% for human lungs. Heat conservation: in the flippers of dolphins, legs of wading birds and limbs of arctic mammals, arteries and veins lie side by side in a rete mirabile; warm arterial blood heats returning venous blood, keeping extremities cold and minimising heat loss. Some tunas and sharks use the same principle to keep swimming muscles warm. Kidneys: the loop of Henle uses countercurrent multiplication to build the medullary osmotic gradient that concentrates urine, while the vasa recta act as countercurrent exchangers preserving it.

In engineering

Industrial heat exchangers, distillation columns and chemical absorbers are arranged countercurrently for the same reason: counterflow maximises the average driving gradient and the effectiveness of transfer, a principle analysed with the log mean temperature difference method.
