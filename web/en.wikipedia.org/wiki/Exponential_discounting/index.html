Exponential discounting

The exponential discount function declines at a constant proportional rate.

Exponential discounting is a specific form of the discount function used in economics and finance, in which the value of a future reward declines by a constant percentage per unit of time. If δ is the per-period discount factor (0 < δ < 1), the value today of a reward received t periods in the future is δt times its nominal value. Equivalently, in continuous time the discount function takes the form e−rt, where r is the discount rate.

Properties

The distinguishing feature of exponential discounting is time consistency: the relative preference between two future rewards depends only on the time between them, not on when the comparison is made. An agent who discounts exponentially never exhibits preference reversals merely because time has passed. Exponential discounting is the standard assumption in classical economics and underlies the discounted utility model introduced by Paul Samuelson in 1937.

Contrast with hyperbolic discounting

Empirical research in behavioural economics frequently finds that people discount more steeply in the near term than exponential discounting predicts, a pattern better described by hyperbolic discounting. Hyperbolic discounters show dynamic inconsistency — for example, planning to save tomorrow but failing to do so when tomorrow arrives. Exponential discounting remains the benchmark in normative models and in much of applied finance, where compound interest grows (and hence present value shrinks) exponentially.
