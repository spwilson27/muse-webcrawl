Narcissus is a genus of predominantly spring-flowering perennial plants of the amaryllis family, Amaryllidaceae. Various common names including daffodil, narcissus, and jonquil are used to describe all or some members of the genus. Narcissus has conspicuous flowers with six petal-like tepals surmounted by a cup- or trumpet-shaped corona.

The genus is native to meadows and woods in southern Europe and North Africa, with a centre of diversity in the Iberian Peninsula. Species numbers are disputed because of extensive hybridisation; estimates range from about 36 to over 70 species. Thousands of cultivars are grown as ornamentals, and the daffodil is the national flower of Wales.

Description

Plants grow from bulbs and bear strap-shaped leaves and a leafless hollow stem (scape) carrying one or more flowers. The corona — the defining feature of the genus — ranges from a shallow cup to a long trumpet, in colours from white and cream through yellow to orange and pink. The fruit is a dry capsule.

Etymology and culture

The name derives from the Greek myth of Narcissus, the youth who fell in love with his own reflection. In the story, retold by Ovid, the flower sprang from the place of his death. In medicine, the alkaloid galantamine, obtained from Narcissus and related genera, is used in the treatment of Alzheimer's disease; bulbs contain toxic lycorine and other alkaloids and are poisonous if eaten.

Cultivation

Daffodils are among the most popular spring bulbs worldwide. Cultivars are classified by the Royal Horticultural Society into 13 divisions based on flower form. Bulbs are planted in autumn and naturalise readily in grass, borders and woodland.
