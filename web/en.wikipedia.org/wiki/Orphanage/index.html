Orphanage

An orphanage is a residential institution, total institution or group home devoted to the care of orphans and children who, for various reasons, cannot be cared for by their biological families. Reasons include the death or disappearance of parents, abuse, parental substance abuse, mental illness in a parent, or poverty.

A 19th-century orphanage building

History

Institutions for orphans existed in ancient Rome and in the Byzantine Empire, and religious foundations — monasteries, Islamic schools and charitable hospitals — cared for abandoned children through the Middle Ages. Large-scale orphanages expanded in the 18th and 19th centuries in Europe and North America, including foundling hospitals such as Thomas Coram's Foundling Hospital in London (founded 1739). Many institutions were tied to workhouses, and conditions were often harsh, with high infant mortality.

Modern developments

From the mid-20th century, most developed countries moved away from large orphanages toward foster care, adoption and small family-style group homes, influenced by research on attachment and by studies such as the Bucharest Early Intervention Project, which documented developmental harm from institutional care in Romanian orphanages after 1989. The UN Guidelines for the Alternative Care of Children (2009) promote family-based care and restrict institutional placement. Orphanages remain common in parts of the developing world, and "orphanage tourism" and volunteering have drawn criticism for incentivising family separation, since most children in institutions worldwide have at least one living parent.
