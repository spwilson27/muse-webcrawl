Scientific paper

A scientific paper is a written report describing original research results, published in a scientific journal. Since the 17th century, scientific papers have been the principal means by which scientists communicate their findings to one another and build cumulatively upon previous work. A scientific paper is generally expected to be written in a formal, structured style, to document methods sufficiently that other researchers can evaluate or reproduce the work, and to survive the scrutiny of peer review before publication.

History

The first scientific journals — the Journal des sçavans and the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society, both founded in 1665 — created the venue for short, signed reports of experiments and observations. Over the 18th and 19th centuries, as science professionalised, the journal article displaced the scientific book and the letter as the standard unit of publication. The 20th century brought standardisation of citation styles, the abstract, and eventually the dominance of English as the language of scientific publishing, alongside steep growth in the number of journals and papers.

Structure

Most empirical papers follow the IMRAD format:

  • Title and abstract — a summary of the problem, methods, key results and conclusions.
  • Introduction — background, relevant prior literature, and the research question or hypothesis.
  • Methods — materials, procedures, and analysis in reproducible detail.
  • Results — the findings, often with tables and figures, without extensive interpretation.
  • Discussion — interpretation, comparison with prior work, limitations, and implications.
  • References — citations to earlier literature relied upon.

Other article types include review articles synthesizing a field, meta-analyses, case reports, theoretical papers, and short communications.

Peer review and publication

Submitted manuscripts are typically screened by an editor and sent to independent referees, who recommend acceptance, revision, or rejection, usually anonymously. Published papers are identified by persistent identifiers such as the DOI. Criticisms of the paper-based system include publication bias toward positive results, slow turnaround, paywalls restricting access (addressed by the open-access movement), and the replication crisis raised by failures to reproduce published findings.
