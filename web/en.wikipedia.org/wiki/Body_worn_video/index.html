Body worn video

Body worn video (BWV), also called body worn cameras (BWCs), is the use of wearable cameras, typically by police and other law enforcement personnel, to record their interactions with the public or to gather video evidence at crime scenes. Body worn video has been shown to often result in benefits such as increased transparency and legitimacy of policing, faster resolution of complaints, and corroborating evidence for arrest and prosecution. It is a prominent implementation of the broader body camera concept.

An officer wearing a body worn video camera

History

The first recorded uses of body worn video in policing were small trials in the United Kingdom in the mid-2000s, including a 2005 pilot in Plymouth. Following a period of evaluation by the Home Office, UK police forces adopted BWV more broadly, and the technology spread to the United States, Australia, Canada, and many other countries from the early 2010s onward. Demand in the US surged after the 2014 shooting of Michael Brown in Ferguson, Missouri, and the federal government funded body camera programs for state and local departments.

Equipment and operation

BWV cameras are typically worn on the chest, shoulder, or head/eyewear, have wide-angle lenses and microphones, and record to internal storage (often with "pre-event" buffering that retains video from before the record button is pressed). At shift end, cameras dock for charging, and footage is uploaded to evidence-management systems with audit trails. Leading vendors include Axon, Motorola Solutions, Reveal Media, and Getac.

Benefits and evidence

Supporters cite deterrence of misconduct, de-escalation of confrontations, protection against unfounded complaints, and high-quality evidence. The widely cited Rialto, California randomized experiment (2012) found a 59% reduction in use-of-force incidents and an 88% reduction in complaints among camera-wearing officers. However, subsequent larger studies and systematic reviews have produced mixed findings, with complaint reductions being the most consistent effect and results on use of force varying; effects appear sensitive to compliance with activation policies.

Issues

Debates concern officer discretion to activate or deactivate cameras, notification of persons being recorded, public disclosure and open-records laws, data retention costs, privacy of victims and bystanders, and whether the camera's perspective can bias viewers (the "camera perspective bias" — footage shows what the officer faced, but not the officer's own actions). BWV policies commonly require activation during all enforcement and investigative contacts, with exceptions for sensitive situations.

Use beyond policing

Body worn video is also used by firefighters, paramedics, prison and border officers, railway staff, retail security, and healthcare workers, and by civilians as action cameras. In medicine, clinicians have trialled BWV to improve safety in emergency departments.
