The International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) is an alphabetic system of phonetic notation based primarily on the Latin alphabet. It was devised by the International Phonetic Association, founded in Paris in 1886, as a standardised representation of the sounds of spoken language: ideally, one symbol for each distinctive sound (phoneme or phone), and each symbol for one sound only. Linguists, lexicographers, language teachers, speech therapists and singers use it worldwide; pronunciations in dictionaries and in Wikipedia articles are given in IPA.

Design

The IPA comprises consonants arranged on a chart by place and manner of articulation (with voiced–voiceless pairs side by side), a trapezoid vowel chart plotting tongue height and backness, and a large set of diacritics that modify symbols for finer detail — aspiration [ʰ], nasalisation [◌̃], length [ː] and so on. Suprasegmentals mark stress [ˈ], tone, and syllable boundaries. Broad transcription between slashes / / records phonemes; narrow transcription between brackets [ ] records actual speech sounds.

Examples

Standard southern British English phonetics is /fəˈnetɪks/; General American water may be transcribed [ˈwɔɾɚ], showing the flapped /t/. Symbols go far beyond English: [ŋ] (the ng of sing), [ʃ] (sh), [θ] and [ð] (the two th sounds), [x] (Scottish loch), clicks such as [ǃ], and ejectives such as [pʼ].

History

Growing from Otto Jespersen's and Paul Passy's proposals, the first IPA chart was published in 1888. It has been revised repeatedly (major revisions 1900, 1932, 1989, 1993, 2005), most recently adding a symbol for the labiodental flap in 2005. An Extensions set (extIPA) covers disordered speech, and the alphabet is fully encoded in Unicode.
