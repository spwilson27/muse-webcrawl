Understeer and oversteer

Understeer and oversteer are terms used in vehicle dynamics to describe the sensitivity of a vehicle to steering inputs. Understeer occurs when the front tyres generate less cornering force than the driver demands, so the car turns less than intended and tends to run wide. Oversteer occurs when the rear tyres lose grip relative to the front, so the car turns more than intended, with the rear stepping out and potentially leading to a spin.

Definitions

In formal terms, a vehicle understeers if the slip angle of the front axle exceeds that of the rear axle during cornering; it oversteers if the rear slip angle is greater. At the limit of adhesion, an understeering car ploughs straight on in a widening arc, while an oversteering car's rear swings outward, requiring opposite lock (counter-steering) to correct. A neutral-steering car balances the two.

Causes

The tendency is designed into a chassis by weight distribution, suspension geometry, tyre sizes and alignment, and it varies with speed, load transfer, and whether the driver is accelerating or braking. Front-heavy, front-wheel-drive family cars are usually tuned for stable understeer, which is considered safer for average drivers because the instinctive response — lifting off the throttle and steering more — generally restores grip at the front first. Rear- and mid-engined performance cars can exhibit power-on oversteer when torque overwhelms the rear tyres.

Control and safety systems

Drivers correct mild understeer by reducing speed or adding steering lock, and oversteer by steering into the slide and modulating the throttle. Modern electronic stability control systems detect both conditions by comparing the driver's steering input with the vehicle's actual yaw rate, braking individual wheels to restore the intended path. Stability control has dramatically reduced loss-of-control crashes and is mandatory on new cars in many jurisdictions.
