Hiroshi Yoshida

Hiroshi Yoshida (吉田 博, September 19, 1876 – April 5, 1950) was a Japanese painter and woodblock printmaker, regarded as one of the greatest artists of the shin-hanga ("new prints") movement. He is especially known for luminous landscape prints of Japan and of the many countries he traveled through.

Life and travels

Born in Kurume, Fukuoka, Yoshida moved to Kyoto and Tokyo to study Western-style painting (yōga). From 1899 he traveled repeatedly to the United States and Europe, exhibiting watercolors and oils with success; his American earnings and contacts later underpinned his print career. He was also an avid mountaineer, and mountain landscapes — the Japanese Alps, the Himalayas, the Grand Canyon — became signature subjects.

Printmaking

After the 1923 Great Kantō earthquake destroyed the blocks of publisher Watanabe Shōzaburō with whom he had begun printing, Yoshida established his own studio in 1925, personally supervising carving and printing. His prints combine traditional Japanese woodblock technique with Western perspective and atmospheric light; he sometimes printed the same blocks in different color schemes to render dawn, dusk, or night. Famous series include the United States series (1925), the India and Southeast Asia series (1932), and many views of Mount Fuji and Japanese shrines and castles.

Legacy

Yoshida's sons Tōshi and Hodaka continued as print artists. His works are held by major museums worldwide, and he is credited with bringing shin-hanga to an international audience.
