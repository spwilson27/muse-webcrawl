Jewish prayer (Hebrew: תְּפִלָּה, tefillah) is the prayer recitation that forms part of the observance of Rabbinic Judaism. These prayers, often with instructions and commentary, are found in the siddur, the traditional Jewish prayer book. Observant Jews pray three times a day—morning (Shacharit), afternoon (Mincha) and evening (Ma'ariv)—with additional services on the Sabbath and festivals.

History

According to tradition, fixed prayer was instituted in part to correspond to the daily sacrifices of the Temple in Jerusalem, which ceased after its destruction in 70 CE. The rabbis of the Mishnah and Talmud standardised the core liturgy, centring on the Shema and the Amidah ("standing prayer"), while leaving room for personal supplication.

Structure and elements

The weekday morning service includes preliminary blessings, verses of song (Pesukei dezimra), the Shema with its blessings, the Amidah, and concluding prayers such as Aleinu. Torah reading takes place on Mondays, Thursdays, Sabbaths and festivals. Many worshippers wear a tallit (prayer shawl) and, on weekdays, tefillin (phylacteries). Communal prayer ideally requires a minyan, traditionally ten adult Jews.

Variations

Rites differ between Ashkenazi, Sephardi and other traditions, and the branches of Judaism differ over issues such as egalitarian language and the role of music; Reform and Conservative prayer books include substantial vernacular translation and some revised texts.
