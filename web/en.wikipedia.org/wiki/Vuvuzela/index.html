The vuvuzela (/vuːvuːˈzɛlə/) is a horn, about 65 centimetres (2 feet) long, traditionally made from plastic and based on the kudu horn, used by football fans in South Africa. Blown with a buzzing of the lips, it produces a loud, raucous, monotone note — typically B♭ — likened to an elephant's trumpet or the drone of a swarm of bees. The vuvuzela achieved worldwide fame during the 2009 FIFA Confederations Cup and the 2010 FIFA World Cup in South Africa.

Origins

The name is of Zulu origin, commonly explained as derived from vuvu, an onomatopoeic word for the sound of the instrument, with -zela meaning "to make noise". Plastic vuvuzelas were popularised at South African football matches from the 1990s, succeeding earlier horns made from kudu antelope horns and bicycle tubes used by supporters of clubs such as Kaizer Chiefs and Orlando Pirates.

2010 World Cup controversy

At the 2010 World Cup, the continuous drone of tens of thousands of vuvuzelas dominated stadium sound and television broadcasts. Players and coaches complained that the noise impeded communication on the pitch, and broadcasters complained it drowned out commentary. FIFA president Sepp Blatter defended the instrument as part of South African football culture, and FIFA declined to ban it. Measurements showed sound levels near a vuvuzela's bell could reach about 127 dB, loud enough to pose a risk of hearing damage with prolonged exposure; earplugs were widely sold at matches.

Following the tournament, UEFA banned vuvuzelas from its competitions, and many clubs and leagues worldwide followed suit, citing safety and the disruption to the atmosphere. In South Africa itself, the instrument remained a fixture of domestic football.

Legacy

The vuvuzela became a global internet meme, spawned smartphone apps and audio filters used to remove its frequency from broadcasts, and briefly entered wider popular culture as a symbol of the 2010 tournament.
