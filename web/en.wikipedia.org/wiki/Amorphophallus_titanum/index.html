Amorphophallus titanum

Amorphophallus titanum, the titan arum, is a flowering plant in the arum family (Araceae) endemic to the rainforests of western Sumatra. It produces the largest unbranched inflorescence in the world and is widely known as the corpse flower or corpse plant for the powerful odour of rotting flesh it emits during its brief flowering, in Indonesian bunga bangkai. (The name "corpse flower" is also applied to the unrelated parasitic Rafflesia arnoldii, which has the largest single flower.)

Description and life cycle

The plant grows from a massive underground corm that can weigh 50–75 kg. For most of its life it produces a single tree-like leaf up to 6 m tall, which photosynthesises for a year or more before dying back. After a dormant period the corm either produces another leaf or, roughly every few years when it has stored enough energy, an inflorescence: a tall yellow spadix wrapped in a frilled, deep-red spathe, reaching over 3 m. During the first evening of the roughly 24–48 hour bloom, the spadix heats up to near human body temperature (thermogenesis), volatilising sulphurous compounds that smell of carrion, garlic and decaying fish, attracting carrion beetles and flesh flies that pollinate the unisexual flowers hidden at the spadix's base.

Discovery and cultivation

The Italian botanist Odoardo Beccari encountered the species in Sumatra in 1878 and sent seeds to Kew Gardens, where it first flowered in cultivation in 1889. Its first flowering in the United States, at the New York Botanical Garden in 1937, was a media sensation. Blooms in botanical gardens remain rare public events, often livestreamed and given nicknames; celebrated specimens include "Morticia", "Putrella" and the Chicago Botanic Garden's plants. In the wild the species is threatened by deforestation for palm oil plantations and is listed as Endangered.
