The Einstein–Szilárd letter was a letter written by physicist Leó Szilárd and signed by Albert Einstein on August 2, 1939, and sent to United States President Franklin D. Roosevelt. Written in consultation with fellow Hungarian physicists Edward Teller and Eugene Wigner, the letter warned that Nazi Germany might develop atomic bombs and suggested that the United States should initiate its own nuclear program. It prompted action by Roosevelt, which eventually resulted in the Manhattan Project.

Background

Following the discovery of nuclear fission by Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann in December 1938, and its theoretical explanation by Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch, émigré physicists in the United States grew concerned that German scientists would exploit the discovery to build a weapon. Szilárd, who had conceived the nuclear chain reaction in 1933, learned that German uranium research was advancing and that Germany had halted uranium exports from occupied Czechoslovakia. He concluded that Einstein's fame made him the ideal signatory for a warning to the President.

Drafting and delivery

Szilárd drove to Einstein's summer home at Peconic, Long Island, in July 1939, where Einstein reportedly responded to the news of a possible chain reaction with "Daran habe ich gar nicht gedacht" ("I hadn't thought of that at all"). A first version was drafted; a second, revised and shorter pair of letters was signed on August 2. The letter was delivered to Roosevelt on October 11, 1939, by economist Alexander Sachs, a friend of the President, who read it aloud along with supporting documents.

Contents

The letter explained that uranium could be used to construct "extremely powerful bombs of a new type," that a single such bomb carried by boat into a port might destroy the entire port, and that Germany might already be working along these lines. It recommended that the United States secure supplies of uranium ore, particularly from the Belgian Congo, and accelerate experimental work.

Consequences

Roosevelt responded by establishing the Advisory Committee on Uranium, chaired by Lyman Briggs, which first met on October 21, 1939. Although progress was initially slow, the committee's work fed into the National Defense Research Committee and, by 1942, the full-scale Manhattan Project. Einstein himself, a pacifist, took no part in the project and was denied a security clearance; he later described signing the letter as the one great mistake of his life, while maintaining that the danger of a German bomb justified it.
