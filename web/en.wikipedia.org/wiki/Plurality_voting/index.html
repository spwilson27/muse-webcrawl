Plurality voting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Plurality voting is an electoral system in which the candidate with the most votes in a district wins, whether or not they achieve a majority. In single-member districts it is first-past-the-post (FPTP), used for the UK House of Commons, the US House of Representatives, Canada and India, among others. Multi-member plurality (block voting) elects several candidates at once.

Plurality is simple to administer and understand, tends to produce single-party majority governments from two-party competition (Duverger's law), and gives voters a single identifiable local representative. Its defects are equally standard in the literature: winners with well under half the vote (UK governments have been elected on about 35–43 per cent), large numbers of wasted votes, gross disproportionality between votes and seats, incentives to vote tactically, and the premium it places on the drawing of district boundaries (Redistricting and gerrymandering).
