Political socialization

Political socialization is the process by which individuals acquire political attitudes, values, beliefs and behaviours, and by which political culture is transmitted from one generation to the next. The concept became a central topic of political science in the mid-twentieth century through the work of scholars such as Herbert Hyman, Gabriel Almond and Sidney Verba.

Agents of socialization

  • Family — usually the earliest and most influential agent; party identification in particular is often inherited from parents.
  • Schools — civics education, rituals such as pledges of allegiance, and the "hidden curriculum" of authority and hierarchy.
  • Peer groups — friends and colleagues who reinforce or challenge inherited views, especially in adolescence and early adulthood.
  • Mass and social media — news coverage and online networks that frame political events and supply information.
  • Religion, workplace and government — institutions that shape values and direct experiences with the state.

Life-cycle and generational effects

Research distinguishes life-cycle effects (attitudes changing as people age), period effects (events affecting everyone at once, such as wars or depressions) and generational effects, in which the formative events of youth — the "impressionable years" — leave a lasting imprint on a cohort's outlook. Political socialization continues throughout life, but early learning tends to structure how later information is interpreted. See also political culture and socialization.
