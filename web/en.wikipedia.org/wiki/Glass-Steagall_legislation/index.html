Glass-Steagall legislation refers collectively to the banking reforms of the early 1930s sponsored by Senator Carter Glass and Representative Henry B. Steagall, principally the Banking Act of 1932 and the Banking Act of 1933, enacted in response to the wave of bank failures following the Wall Street Crash of 1929.

Background

Between 1930 and 1933 roughly nine thousand American banks failed. The Pecora Commission hearings of 1932–1934 exposed conflicts of interest in which commercial banks had used depositors' funds in speculative securities affiliates, fuelling demands for structural reform.

The acts

The Banking Act of 1932 expanded the Federal Reserve's lending powers and allowed the Treasury to support the gold supply. The Banking Act of 1933 contained the provisions most often called "Glass-Steagall": the separation of commercial and investment banking (sections 16, 20, 21 and 32) and the creation of federal deposit insurance through the FDIC. The Bank Holding Company Act of 1956 extended aspects of the separation to bank holding companies.

Erosion and repeal

From the 1960s onward, banks and regulators chipped away at the separation through reinterpretations, culminating in the Gramm–Leach–Bliley Act of 1999, which repealed the affiliation restrictions. The Volcker Rule in the Dodd–Frank Act of 2010 re-imposed narrower restrictions on proprietary trading, sometimes called "Glass-Steagall lite".
