Istishhad (Arabic: استشهاد) is an Arabic term meaning the act of seeking martyrdom (shahada). In contemporary usage it has become associated with Islamist militant organisations, which use it to describe suicide attacks framed as martyrdom operations (ʿamaliyyāt istišhādiyya), in contrast to the condemnation of suicide (intihar) in Islamic tradition.

Background

The concept of the shahid (martyr) is ancient in Islam, traditionally applied to those killed while fighting in defence of the faith, who are promised paradise in the Qur'an and hadith. Deliberate self-killing, however, is explicitly prohibited: Qur'an 4:29 warns "do not kill yourselves", and hadith literature assigns punishment to those who take their own lives.

Modern debate

From the 1980s onward — with Hezbollah's attacks in Lebanon, Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad bombings during the Second Intifada, and al-Qaeda's and later Islamic State's campaigns — militant ideologues argued that a combatant who dies while attacking an enemy is a martyr rather than a suicide, especially where the weapon cannot be delivered otherwise. Scholars including Yusuf al-Qaradawi endorsed "martyrdom operations" in the Palestinian context while condemning attacks elsewhere. Most mainstream Muslim authorities reject the framing: statements from bodies such as al-Azhar, the 2004 Amman Message signatories, and the 2010 Mardin fatwa conference hold that suicide attacks, and in particular the killing of civilians, are forbidden. Academic analysts note the term is contested precisely because it is a legitimating label rather than a neutral description.
