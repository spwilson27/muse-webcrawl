Flatboat

A flatboat is a rectangular flat-bottomed boat with a simple box-like hull, used mainly for transporting freight down inland waterways. Flatboats were the workhorses of early American river commerce on the Ohio, Mississippi, and other rivers in the late 18th and early 19th centuries.

Use

Built cheaply from planks, a flatboat was loaded with farm produce, livestock, or goods, floated downstream with the current, steered with long oars ("sweeps"), and at its destination was typically sold for lumber, since it could not travel back upstream. Abraham Lincoln famously made flatboat trips from Illinois to New Orleans in 1828 and 1831. Keelboats, which could be poled or towed upstream, complemented flatboats until steamboats displaced both.

Construction

Flatboats ranged from small craft to "broadhorns" over 100 feet long. A small cabin or shelter often stood at the stern. Their simplicity and disposability made them economic for one-way trade.
