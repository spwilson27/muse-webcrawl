Juneteenth (officially Juneteenth National Independence Day) is a federal holiday in the United States commemorating the end of slavery. It is celebrated annually on June 19. The name is a portmanteau of "June" and "nineteenth".

Originating in Galveston, Texas, Juneteenth marks the day in 1865 when Union troops arrived in Galveston Bay and announced that the more than 250,000 enslaved black people in the state were free by executive decree. This day came to be known as "Juneteenth" by the newly freed people in Texas.

History

President Abraham Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation of 1 January 1863 declared enslaved people in the Confederate states legally free, but enforcement depended on the advance of Union troops, and Texas, the most remote slave state, saw little enforcement. On 19 June 1865, Union Major General Gordon Granger arrived in Galveston and issued General Order No. 3, announcing that "all slaves are free" in accordance with the Proclamation.

The freed people of Texas began celebrating the anniversary the following year, with church gatherings, readings of the Emancipation Proclamation, food, music, and later rodeos, parades, and pageants. Celebrations spread with the Great Migration to other parts of the country. Texas made Juneteenth a state holiday in 1980, the first state to do so.

Federal recognition

After decades of advocacy — notably by activist Opal Lee, the "Grandmother of Juneteenth" — Congress passed the Juneteenth National Independence Day Act, and President Joe Biden signed it into law on 17 June 2021, making Juneteenth the first new federal holiday since Martin Luther King Jr. Day in 1983.

Observances

Modern observances include parades, festivals, cookouts featuring red foods and drinks (such as red velvet cake and strawberry soda, symbolising resilience), historical re-enactments, and educational events. The Juneteenth flag, designed by Ben Haith in 1997, features a bursting star and an arc representing a new horizon.
