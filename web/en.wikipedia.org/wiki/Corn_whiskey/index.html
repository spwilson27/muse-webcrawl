Corn whiskey

Corn whiskey is an American liquor made from a mash of at least 80 percent corn. It is distilled to no more than 160 U.S. proof (80% alcohol by volume) and, unlike bourbon, is not required to be aged in new charred oak barrels; if aged, it must be in uncharred or used barrels, which gives it a lighter colour and a cleaner, sweeter corn flavour.

Legal definition

Under United States federal regulations, corn whiskey is a type of American whiskey distilled from a fermented mash of at least 80% corn, distilled at less than 160 proof, and stored — if at all — in uncharred new oak containers or used cooperage, and bottled at not less than 80 proof. A "straight" corn whiskey must be aged at least two years in such containers.

History

Corn whiskey descends from the earliest American frontier distilling in Kentucky, Pennsylvania and the southern Appalachians, where corn was the abundant grain. Much illicit moonshine was essentially unaged corn whiskey. Commercial production declined in the 20th century as bourbon and blended whiskey dominated, but craft distillers have revived the style, including "white" (unaged) versions sold in jars evoking moonshine traditions.

Character

Because it may be unaged, corn whiskey typically tastes of sweet corn, grain and light vanilla, with less wood influence than bourbon. It is consumed neat in the southern United States and used in cocktails.
