120 is a roll film format for still photography introduced by Kodak in 1901 for its No. 2 Brownie camera. It is the most common of the medium format film sizes and, unusually for a film format of its age, remains in production and widespread use in the twenty-first century, long after most other roll film sizes were discontinued. The film is approximately 61.5 mm wide, wound on a plastic spool together with a protective backing paper printed with frame numbers for the several frame sizes the format supports.

Characteristics

Unlike 35 mm film, 120 has no perforations and no cassette; the film and its backing paper are rolled onto an open spool, loaded in daylight, and wound onto a take-up spool through the camera. Frame advance is typically controlled by numbers viewed through a red window in the camera back on older cameras, or by mechanical counters on later ones. Frame length is determined by the camera, not the film, which allows one film stock to serve many formats:

History

Kodak introduced the 120 size as the twentieth in its sequence of roll film designations, for a camera producing 2¼ × 3¼ inch negatives. It became the standard amateur and later professional medium format. A longer double-length version, 220, was introduced in 1965 with thinner backing arrangements but was discontinued in the 2010s; the similar 620 format (1932), using a slimmer spool, died out by the 1990s. Classic cameras using 120 include the Rolleiflex and Hasselblad 500 series, the Pentax 67, Mamiya RZ67 and countless folding and box cameras.

Survival in the digital era

Although digital photography displaced film in most professional work after the 2000s, 120 film survived thanks to continued production by Kodak Alaris, Fujifilm (until it trimmed its range) and Ilford, and to demand from fine-art, portrait and enthusiast photographers who value the large negative, shallow depth of field and the deliberate pace of medium format cameras. New and revived 120 cameras and backs continue to be marketed, and 120 remains the only roll film size with a full range of colour negative, slide and black-and-white emulsions in regular production.
