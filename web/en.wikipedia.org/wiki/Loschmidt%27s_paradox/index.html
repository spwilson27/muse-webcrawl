Loschmidt's paradox

Loschmidt's paradox, also known as the reversibility paradox, is an objection raised by Johann Loschmidt in 1876 against the use of statistical mechanics to explain the irreversibility of thermodynamic processes. Loschmidt argued that the microscopic equations of motion are time-reversal invariant, so for every trajectory in which entropy increases there exists a reversed trajectory, equally valid, in which entropy decreases.

The objection

Ludwig Boltzmann's H-theorem purported to derive a monotonic decrease of the quantity H (corresponding to increasing entropy) from molecular dynamics. Loschmidt pointed out that if all molecular velocities in an equilibrating gas were exactly reversed, the system would evolve back to its low-entropy initial state, apparently contradicting the theorem and the second law of thermodynamics.

Resolution

Boltzmann's reply — "Go ahead, reverse them!" — foreshadows the modern understanding: the reversed states exist but are extraordinarily special and unstable, requiring correlations of fantastic precision, and they occupy a vanishingly small fraction of phase space. Entropy increase is thus overwhelmingly probable rather than absolutely necessary, a view embedded in modern statistical mechanics and in discussions of the arrow of time and the low-entropy initial condition of the universe.
