Two-sided market

A two-sided market, also called a two-sided network, is an economic platform having two distinct user groups that provide each other with network benefits. The organisation that creates value primarily by enabling direct interactions between two (or more) distinct types of affiliated customers is called a multi-sided platform. This concept of two-sided markets has mainly been developed in the literature on platform economics pioneered by Jean-Charles Rochet and Jean Tirole, and by Geoffrey Parker and Marshall Van Alstyne.

Examples

Classic examples include payment cards (cardholders and merchants), dating services, video game consoles (players and game developers), operating systems (users and application developers), ride-hailing apps (riders and drivers), marketplaces such as eBay and Amazon Marketplace (buyers and sellers), search engines funded by advertising (users and advertisers), and newspapers (readers and advertisers).

Key features

Two-sided markets are characterised by cross-side network effects: the value of the platform to members of one group increases with the number of participants on the other side. This creates a "chicken-and-egg" problem at launch, which platforms often solve by subsidising one side — charging it little or nothing, or even paying it — while earning revenue from the other side, whose willingness to pay depends on access to the subsidised group. Pricing therefore cannot be analysed side by side; what matters is the overall price structure. Rochet and Tirole's influential 2003 formalisation defined a market as two-sided when the platform can affect transaction volume not just by the total price charged to the two sides combined, but by its allocation between them.

Competition and regulation

Strong network effects can produce winner-take-all dynamics and natural concentration, raising antitrust questions that have featured in cases concerning payment cards (such as Ohio v. American Express, decided by the US Supreme Court in 2018) and digital platform markets generally. Multi-homing (using several competing platforms), congestion, and platform governance rules all influence outcomes.
