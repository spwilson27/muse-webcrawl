Computer-aided manufacturing

Computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) is the use of software to control machine tools in the manufacturing of work pieces. This is not the only definition for CAM, but it is the most common. CAM may also refer to the use of a computer to assist in all operations of a manufacturing plant, including planning, management, transportation and storage. Its primary purpose is to create a faster production process and components and tooling with more precise dimensions and material consistency, which in some cases, uses only the required amount of raw material (thus minimizing waste), while simultaneously reducing energy consumption.

CAM is a subsequent computer-aided process after computer-aided design (CAD) and sometimes computer-aided engineering (CAE), as the model generated in CAD and verified in CAE can be input into CAM software, which then controls the machine tool. CAM is used in many schools and is a dominant factor used in manufacturing everything from cars to planes to small machined components.

Overview

Traditionally, CAM has been a numerical control (NC) programming tool, wherein two-dimensional (2-D) or three-dimensional (3-D) models of components generated in CAD are used to generate G-code instructions to drive computer numerically controlled (CNC) machine tools. The CAM workflow involves defining machining strategies (toolpaths), selecting cutting tools, setting speeds and feeds, simulating the machining to detect collisions and errors, and post-processing the toolpaths into the dialect of NC code required by a specific machine controller.

History

Early commercial applications of CAM were in large companies in the automotive and aerospace industries; for example, Pierre Béziers's work at Renault developing the UNISURF CAD/CAM system in the 1960s–70s, and numerical control originated with John T. Parsons's and MIT's work on computer-controlled milling in the 1950s, which produced the APT (Automatically Programmed Tool) language. CAD/CAM integration deepened through the 1980s and 1990s as solid modelling kernels (such as Parasolid and ACIS) became standard in both design and manufacturing software.

Modern practice

Contemporary CAM systems (such as Mastercam, Fusion 360's manufacture workspace, NX CAM, and hyperMILL) support multi-axis machining (3+2 and simultaneous 5-axis), high-speed machining toolpaths, turning and mill-turn operations, wire EDM, additive manufacturing toolpaths, and in-process probing. Integration with product lifecycle management and manufacturing execution systems links CAM programs to revision-controlled CAD models — the CAD/CAM chain is a core element of the "digital thread" in Industry 4.0.
