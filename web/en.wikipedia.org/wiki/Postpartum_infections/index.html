Postpartum infections

Nineteenth-century maternity wards were scourged by puerperal fever.

Postpartum infections, also known as childbed fever and puerperal fever, are bacterial infections of the female reproductive tract following childbirth or miscarriage. They most often take the form of endometritis, infection of the lining of the uterus, but may also involve caesarean or perineal wounds, the urinary tract or the breasts (mastitis). Signs usually include fever above 38 °C, pelvic pain, foul-smelling discharge and an increased heart rate in the days after delivery.

Causes and risk factors

The infections are typically polymicrobial, caused by organisms from the normal vaginal flora — including streptococci, staphylococci, Escherichia coli and anaerobes — ascending into the uterus, which after delivery provides a large raw surface at the placental site. The greatest risk factor is caesarean delivery, especially after labour and rupture of the membranes; other risks include prolonged labour, multiple vaginal examinations, retained products of conception and poor hygiene. Group A streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes) is a less common but particularly dangerous cause, capable of causing rapidly fatal sepsis.

History

Childbed fever was a leading cause of maternal death in the lying-in hospitals of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, with mortality in some institutions exceeding one in five deliveries. In 1847 the Hungarian physician Ignaz Semmelweis showed that hand disinfection with chlorinated lime in a Vienna clinic cut mortality dramatically, concluding that doctors carried "cadaverous particles" from autopsies to patients; his findings were resisted for decades. With germ theory, antisepsis and later antibiotics, deaths fell sharply in the developed world.

Treatment and outcomes

Treatment is with broad-spectrum antibiotics, drainage of abscesses and removal of retained tissue where needed. Globally, postpartum sepsis remains a significant cause of maternal mortality, accounting for roughly a tenth of maternal deaths, with the burden concentrated in low-income countries where access to clean delivery care and antibiotics is limited.
