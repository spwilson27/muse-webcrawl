Pierre Maurice Marie Duhem (10 June 1861 – 14 September 1916) was a French theoretical physicist who worked on thermodynamics, hydrodynamics, and elasticity. He was also a historian of science and a philosopher of science, remembered for the Duhem–Quine thesis and for his monumental history of medieval science.

Life

Duhem was born in Paris and educated at the Collège Stanislas and the École Normale Supérieure. His doctoral thesis on thermodynamic potentials was rejected, partly because of opposition from Marcellin Berthelot, and he instead earned his doctorate with a thesis on magnetization. He taught at Lille, Rennes, and from 1894 at the University of Bordeaux, where he remained for the rest of his career.

Scientific work

Duhem made contributions to thermodynamics and chemical physics, including work associated with the Gibbs–Duhem equation. He sought to found all of physics on thermodynamics and was sceptical of atomism and of mechanical models of physical phenomena.

Philosophy of science

In The Aim and Structure of Physical Theory (1906), Duhem argued that physical experiments never test an isolated hypothesis but always a whole theoretical group — an idea later developed by W. V. O. Quine and known as the Duhem–Quine thesis. He viewed physical theory as a symbolic classification of experimental laws rather than an explanation of underlying realities.

History of science

Duhem's ten-volume Le système du monde traced the history of astronomy from antiquity to the Copernican era and drew attention to the achievements of medieval scholars such as Jean Buridan and Nicole Oresme, arguing for continuity between medieval and modern science.
