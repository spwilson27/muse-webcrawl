Hilbre Island

Hilbre Island is the largest of three tidal islands at the mouth of the estuary of the River Dee, in north-west England, lying about 1.6 kilometres (1 mile) off the coast of the Wirral Peninsula, near West Kirby. Cut off from the mainland at high tide, the islands can be reached on foot at low water by a traditional crossing route. Hilbre is a local nature reserve and a site of international importance for birds.

Geography

The Hilbre islands are outcrops of red Triassic sandstone. Hilbre Island itself is about 800 metres long; the smaller Middle Eye and Little Eye lie along the same ridge toward the mainland. At high tide the islands are surrounded by the sea, and visitors who walk out must follow the safe route — from the slipway at West Kirby, around Little Eye and between Middle Eye and Hilbre — and allow several hours either side of low water, since the tide floods the sands quickly.

History

The islands were occupied from the Stone Age onward. In the medieval period Hilbre was the site of a small cell of Benedictine monks attached to St Werburgh's Abbey in Chester, and a place of pilgrimage to St Hildeburgh, from whom the island takes its name. Later it served as a station for a lifeboat, a telegraph station relaying shipping signals to Liverpool, and a lookout during both world wars. The island was bought by Hoylake Urban District Council in the 1940s and passed to Wirral Council. A small number of houses stood on the island for many years, though the last permanent residents left in the early twenty-first century.

Wildlife

Hilbre is one of the most important bird observatory sites in north-west England: the Hilbre Island Bird Observatory, founded in 1957, records and rings migrating birds, and the surrounding Dee Estuary hosts internationally important wintering populations of waders and wildfowl, including knot, oystercatcher, and pintail. Grey seals haul out on the sandbanks west of the island, and the rock pools are studied for their marine life.
