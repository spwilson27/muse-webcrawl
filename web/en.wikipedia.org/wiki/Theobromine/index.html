Theobromine

Theobromine
IUPAC name: 3,7-dimethylxanthine
Formula: C7H8N4O2
Molar mass: 180.17 g/mol
CAS number: 83-67-0
Appearance: white crystalline powder
Solubility: slightly soluble in water, more soluble in hot water and acids
Class: methylxanthine alkaloid

Theobromine, formerly known as xantheose, is a bitter alkaloid of the cacao plant. It is found in chocolate, as well as in a number of other foods, including the leaves of the tea plant and the kola nut. It is classified as a methylxanthine, a class of chemical compounds that also includes caffeine and theophylline.

Despite its name, theobromine contains no bromine. The name derives from Theobroma, the genus name of the cacao tree, itself formed from the Greek roots theo ("god") and broma ("food"), meaning "food of the gods", plus the suffix -ine used for alkaloids.

Chemistry

Theobromine is a purine alkaloid derived from xanthine. It is a white or colourless crystalline powder with a bitter taste. It is only slightly soluble in water but dissolves more readily in hot water, in acids, and in solutions of fixed alkalis. The compound is structurally similar to caffeine, differing by the absence of one methyl group on the nitrogen of the imidazole ring.

Sources

The primary natural source is the cacao bean (Theobroma cacao), in which theobromine occurs at concentrations of roughly 1 to 2.5 percent by dry weight; cocoa products consequently contain significant amounts. Smaller quantities are found in tea, yerba mate, guarana, and the kola nut. Dark chocolate contains substantially more theobromine than milk chocolate because of its higher cocoa content.

Pharmacology

In humans, theobromine is a mild stimulant and a smooth-muscle relaxant. It acts principally as an antagonist of adenosine receptors and, to a lesser degree, as a phosphodiesterase inhibitor. Its effects include vasodilation, diuresis, and mild cardiac stimulation. Compared with caffeine, theobromine has a weaker effect on the central nervous system but a longer half-life in the body, typically around six to eight hours.

Historically, theobromine and its synthetic derivatives were used medically as diuretics and in the treatment of angina and other circulatory conditions.

Toxicity in animals

Many animals, most notably dogs, metabolise theobromine much more slowly than humans and are therefore susceptible to theobromine poisoning from chocolate. Symptoms include restlessness, vomiting, increased heart rate, tremors, and, in severe cases, seizures and death. Cats are similarly sensitive, though they lack sweet taste receptors and rarely eat chocolate. Birds and rodents are also affected.

History

Theobromine was first isolated from cacao beans in 1841 by the Russian chemist Alexander Voskresensky. It was first synthesised from xanthine by Hermann Emil Fischer in the early 1880s, work that contributed to the elucidation of purine chemistry.
