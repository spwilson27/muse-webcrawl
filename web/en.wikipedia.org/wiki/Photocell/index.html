Photocell

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A cadmium-sulfide photoresistor, a common type of photocell.

A photocell is a resistor whose resistance varies with incident light intensity. It is also called a photoresistor, light-dependent resistor (LDR), or photoconductor. The term is also used more broadly for any light sensor, including photodiodes and photovoltaic cells.

Photoresistors work on the principle of photoconductivity: photons striking a semiconductor material (commonly cadmium sulfide, CdS) excite electrons into the conduction band, lowering the resistance. Resistance may fall from over a megohm in darkness to a few hundred ohms in bright light. The response is slow — tens of milliseconds — and shows a memory effect, but the devices are cheap and simple, requiring no amplification for many uses.

Applications

Photocells are used in street lights and outdoor lighting that switch on at dusk, in camera light meters, alarm systems, solar garden lights, and in street-lighting photocontrols mounted on utility fixtures. In audio equipment, photoresistors paired with LEDs ("vactrols") are used in optical compressors and tremolo circuits. Because cadmium is toxic, cadmium-based cells are restricted under regulations such as the EU RoHS directive, and silicon photodiodes are preferred in many new designs.
