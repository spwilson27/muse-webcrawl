360-degree feedback (also known as multi-rater feedback, multi-source feedback or multi-source assessment) is a performance appraisal and development process in which an employee receives confidential, anonymous feedback from the people who work around them: typically their manager, peers and direct reports ("subordinates"), together with a self-assessment. Some processes also include feedback from external sources such as customers and suppliers. The name refers to the full circle of perspectives surrounding the employee.

History

Multi-source assessment has antecedents in military personnel evaluation, including practices of the German Reichswehr and the United States armed forces during the Second World War. It entered corporate practice through assessment centres in the post-war decades and spread widely in large organisations in the 1990s, when falling survey costs and books by practitioners popularised it as a leadership development tool. By the early 2000s a majority of large United States corporations reported using some form of 360-degree feedback.

Process

Raters complete a questionnaire — commonly rating the subject on competencies such as leadership, communication, teamwork and decision-making, often on Likert scales with space for written comments. A third-party administrator or software platform aggregates the responses into a report that contrasts the subject's self-perception with each rater group's view. Best practice keeps individual responses anonymous (except the manager's), requires a minimum number of raters per group, and is usually facilitated by a coach or manager conversation that produces a development plan.

Uses and criticism

Organisations use 360-degree feedback mainly for development — raising self-awareness and targeting training — and sometimes, controversially, for appraisal decisions such as pay and promotion. Research generally finds modest improvements in behaviour when feedback is followed by goal-setting and follow-up, but effects are inconsistent. Critics warn of rater politics, inflated or vindictive ratings, survey fatigue, and the risk that linking results to pay corrupts honesty. Scholars also note that feedback interventions can backfire when they direct attention to the self rather than the task. Despite these cautions, 360-degree feedback remains one of the most widely used tools in leadership development.
