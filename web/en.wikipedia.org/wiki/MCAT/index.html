The Medical College Admission Test (MCAT) is a computer-based standardized examination for prospective medical students (both allopathic and osteopathic) in the United States, Australia, Canada, and the Caribbean Islands. It is designed to assess problem solving, critical thinking, written analysis, and knowledge of scientific concepts and principles. Before 2015 the test also included a writing sample section; the current version, introduced in April 2015, added sections on psychology, sociology, and biochemistry.

Nearly all U.S. medical schools and many Canadian schools require applicants to submit MCAT scores. The test is administered by the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) at Pearson VUE test centers.

Structure

The exam consists of four sections, presented in a fixed order:

  • Chemical and Physical Foundations of Biological Systems — general chemistry, organic chemistry, physics, biology and biochemistry concepts applied to living systems.
  • Critical Analysis and Reasoning Skills (CARS) — passage-based reading comprehension drawn from the humanities and social sciences; no outside science knowledge required.
  • Biological and Biochemical Foundations of Living Systems — biology, biochemistry, and introductory chemistry.
  • Psychological, Social, and Biological Foundations of Behavior — psychology, sociology, and biology of behaviour and perception.

Each section contains 59 questions (53 for CARS) and is timed at 95 minutes (90 for CARS), for a total of 230 questions over about six hours of test time. All questions are multiple choice; most are based on short passages.

Scoring

Each of the four sections is scored from 118 to 132, with a midpoint of 125, giving a total score range of 472 to 528 with a midpoint of 500. Scores are scaled so that raw-score differences are equated across test forms and dates. Results are typically released about 30–35 days after the test date, and scores are generally valid for two to three years depending on the school.

History

The test traces its origin to the Moss Test of 1928, developed after high attrition rates in medical schools prompted educators to seek a standardized aptitude measure. The AAMC adopted a standardized examination in 1946, and it became the MCAT in 1962. Major revisions occurred in 1977, 1991 (which introduced the writing sample and the familiar 1–15 section scales), 2007 (conversion to computer-based testing and a shortened exam), and 2015 (the current format, the first comprehensive revision in 25 years, reflecting changes in medical education including greater emphasis on the social determinants of health).

Preparation and criticism

Applicants commonly prepare for three to six months using AAMC practice materials and commercial courses. The examination and its cost have been criticized for contributing to socioeconomic barriers in medical admissions, and several schools have experimented with alternative or flexible pathways, though the MCAT remains a near-universal requirement in the United States.
