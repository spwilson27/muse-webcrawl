Diatessaron

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Folio of the Arabic Diatessaron]

The Diatessaron (Greek διά, "through", and τεσσάρων, "four"; Syriac ܐܘܢܓܠܝܘܢ ܕܡܚܠܛܐ, "Gospel of the Mixed") is the most prominent early gospel harmony, a single continuous narrative woven from the four canonical Gospels. It was compiled in Syriac by the Christian apologist Tatian around AD 160–175.

Composition

Tatian, a pupil of Justin Martyr in Rome who later returned to the East, combined Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John into one account of Jesus's life, resolving discrepancies by selection and rearrangement, opening with the prologue of John and largely following John's chronology. He omitted the genealogies of Jesus and passages that would place him in Davidic descent — omissions connected by ancient critics to his Encratite asceticism — and harmonised parallel episodes into single composite scenes.

Use and suppression

For two centuries the Diatessaron was the standard gospel text of the Syriac-speaking church, read in worship where other churches read the separate Gospels; Ephrem the Syrian wrote a commentary on it. In the fifth century bishops such as Theodoret of Cyrrhus suppressed it — Theodoret reports collecting some two hundred copies — and replaced it with the four separate Gospels of the Peshitta. No complete Syriac copy survives; the text is reconstructed from Ephrem's commentary (in Syriac and Armenian), an Arabic translation, and harmonies in Latin, Persian, and European vernaculars that descend from it.

Significance

For textual criticism the Diatessaron is a valuable early witness to the text of the Gospels around AD 170, and its readings sometimes attest Greek variants otherwise lost. Its long dominance in Syria also shows how fluid the boundary between the fourfold Gospel and harmonised retellings remained in the early church.
