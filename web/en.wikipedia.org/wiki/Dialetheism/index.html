Dialetheism

Dialetheism

Dialetheism is the view that there are statements that are both true and false. More precisely, it is the view that there are dialetheias — true contradictions — statements of the form "A and not-A" that are true. Dialetheism is contrary to Aristotle's law of non-contradiction, which holds that a statement and its negation cannot both be true.

Motivation

The most common motivation for dialetheism comes from the semantic paradoxes, such as the liar paradox: the sentence "This sentence is false" appears to be true if it is false, and false if it is true. Dialetheists argue that the most natural treatment is to accept that the sentence is both true and false. Other motivations include paradoxes of motion, the boundary of vague predicates, inconsistent bodies of law, and certain features of Hegelian and Buddhist philosophy, which some interpreters read as endorsing true contradictions.

Paraconsistent logic

Accepting true contradictions requires abandoning the classical principle of explosion (ex contradictione quodlibet), according to which anything follows from a contradiction. Dialetheists therefore employ paraconsistent logics, such as Graham Priest's Logic of Paradox (LP), in which a statement may be assigned both truth values without trivialising the theory.

Criticism

Critics object that dialetheism undermines the very notion of falsity and disagreement: if a statement can be both true and false, then asserting its negation may not amount to denying it. Others argue that paradoxes can be resolved by revising logic or semantics without accepting true contradictions, and regard dialetheism as a last resort.
