Acute stress disorder (ASD) is a mental disorder that can occur in the first month following exposure to a traumatic event. It was introduced in DSM-IV in 1994 partly to identify people at risk of developing post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD); symptoms lasting beyond one month are assessed as PTSD.

[Image: Counselling session after trauma]

Signs and symptoms

Under DSM-5, the diagnosis requires exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence, plus nine or more symptoms from categories of intrusion (flashbacks, distressing dreams), negative mood, dissociation (numbing, derealisation, amnesia), avoidance, and arousal (hypervigilance, sleep disturbance, irritability). The disturbance must cause significant distress or impairment and last from three days to one month after the trauma.

Risk and course

ASD occurs in a substantial minority of trauma survivors, with rates varying by trauma type — higher after interpersonal violence. Roughly half of those who meet ASD criteria go on to develop PTSD, though many cases of PTSD are not preceded by full ASD.

Treatment

Trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy is the best-supported treatment and may prevent progression to PTSD. Single-session psychological debriefing is not recommended, as evidence suggests it may be unhelpful or harmful.
