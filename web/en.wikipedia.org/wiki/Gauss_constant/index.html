In mathematics, Gauss's constant, denoted by G, is defined as the reciprocal of the arithmetic-geometric mean of 1 and the square root of 2:

G = 1 / agm(1, √2) = 0.8346268416740731862814297327990468...

The constant is named after Carl Friedrich Gauss, who in 1799 recorded in his diary the identity connecting G with the arc length of the lemniscate.

Relation to the lemniscate

Gauss showed that

G = (2/π) ∫01 dx / √(1 − x4)

The integral equals half the arc length of the lemniscate of Bernoulli in one quadrant, so G is directly connected with the lemniscate constant ϖ = 2.622057554292119...: since ϖ = 2 ∫01 dx / √(1 − x4), it follows that G = ϖ / π, which is equivalent to Gauss's identity agm(1, √2) = π / ϖ.

Other formulae

Gauss's constant can also be expressed using the gamma function:

G = Γ(1/4)2 / (2√2 π3/2)

It can also be written through beta functions and elliptic integrals, and as an infinite product involving terms of the form 21/2^k. Like π, G has been conjectured to be transcendental, although this remains unproven; it is not even known whether G is irrational, though numerical evidence strongly suggests it behaves like a "generic" irrational number.

History

Gauss computed the arithmetic-geometric mean in May 1799 and noted with astonishment that the result agreed, to many digits, with the lemniscate integral — an entry in his mathematical diary often called one of the most famous in mathematics. The discovery opened the theory of elliptic functions, which Gauss and later Abel and Jacobi developed.
