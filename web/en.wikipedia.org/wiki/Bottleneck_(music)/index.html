Bottleneck, in music, refers to a method of playing guitar — and the glass or metal tube used to do it — in which the player slides a hard object along the strings to change pitch continuously rather than stopping the strings at frets with the fingertips. The technique, most widely known as slide guitar, takes its name from the original implement: the neck of a glass bottle, cut off, smoothed, and worn on the ring or little finger. The unbroken glissando and singing sustain it produces became a defining sound of Delta blues and later of blues rock, country, and Hawaiian music.

Technique

The slide is placed lightly on the strings directly above the fret for the desired pitch — intonation is by ear, since vibrato and microtonal inflection are central to the style — while the fingers behind the slide damp unwanted string noise. Slide playing is usually done in open tunings (such as open G or open D), so that a single position across the strings yields a full chord. Players may hold the guitar conventionally or lay it flat across the lap, as in Hawaiian and bluegrass dobro styles; lap players typically use a heavier bar of solid steel held in the hand rather than a finger slide.

History

The slide technique has several converging roots. African one-string instruments were played with a moving stop; in Hawaii, Joseph Kekuku is credited with developing steel-guitar playing around the 1890s after discovering the effect of sliding a steel bar along the strings, and Hawaiian music's popularity in the mainland United States spread the style widely. In the American South, blues musicians adapted the technique using knives, bottle necks, and polished bones. W. C. Handy famously recalled hearing a guitarist at a Mississippi railway station around 1903 playing with a knife on the strings — "the weirdest music I had ever heard."

Early recording masters included Sylvester Weaver, the first to record slide guitar (1923), and Tampa Red, whose polished National steel-guitar playing earned him the nickname "The Guitar Wizard." In the Delta, Charley Patton, Son House, and Robert Johnson made bottleneck playing central to country blues; Johnson's slide work on pieces such as "Come On in My Kitchen" remains a touchstone. After the Second World War, Elmore James electrified the Delta style — his signature slide riff on "Dust My Broom" became a blues standard — while Muddy Waters brought a raw bottleneck sound to Chicago blues.

Slides and guitars

Slides are now manufactured in glass (including replicas of the classic Coricidin medicine bottle favoured by Duane Allman), brass, steel, chrome, and ceramic, each with a distinct tonal character: glass is warmer and lighter, metal brighter and heavier, with greater sustain. Resonator guitars — such as the National and Dobro instruments with spun metal cones in place of a wooden soundboard — were developed in the 1920s partly to give slide players more volume. Modern slide players also commonly use standard electric guitars with raised action to prevent the slide from striking the frets.

Notable players

  • Robert Johnson, Son House, Charley Patton — Delta blues originators.
  • Elmore James — "King of the Slide Guitar."
  • Muddy Waters, Hound Dog Taylor, J. B. Hutto — Chicago electric slide.
  • Duane Allman — elevated slide to melodic lead playing in The Allman Brothers Band.
  • Ry Cooder, Bonnie Raitt, Derek Trucks — later masters across rock, folk, and jam-band idioms.
  • Blind Willie Johnson — revered gospel slide player whose "Dark Was the Night, Cold Was the Ground" was included on the Voyager Golden Record.
