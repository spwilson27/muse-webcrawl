Megafauna

Megafauna are large animals of any particular region, habitat, or geological period. The most common threshold used is a body mass of 45 kilograms (99 lb) or more, though some definitions use 1 tonne (1,000 kg). Living examples include elephants, rhinoceroses, hippopotamuses, giraffes and large bovids. The term is most often used in connection with the Pleistocene megafauna — the giant mammals of the last ice age, many of which became extinct in the Quaternary extinction event.

Definitions

Thresholds vary by discipline: terrestrial mammal studies often use 45 kg (the size at which fauna become "large" relative to most mammals), while some marine or invertebrate contexts use lower limits. Categories below megafauna include mesofauna and microfauna, terms most used in soil ecology.

Pleistocene megafauna

During the Pleistocene, every continent supported giants such as the woolly mammoth, mastodons, giant ground sloths, Glyptodon, the woolly rhinoceros, the Irish elk, saber-toothed cats (Smilodon), the giant short-faced bear, and in Australia Diprotodon and Genyornis. Most of these disappeared between roughly 50,000 and 10,000 years ago.

Extinction debate

The causes of the late Quaternary megafauna extinctions remain debated. The overkill hypothesis, associated with Paul S. Martin, attributes the losses mainly to hunting by newly arrived humans, noting that extinctions closely track human arrival on each landmass. Climate-change hypotheses emphasize rapid warming at the end of the last glacial period and habitat disruption. Many researchers now favor a combination of human pressure and climate stress; the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis remains a minority view.

Modern megafauna and conservation

Africa retains the richest surviving assemblage of terrestrial megafauna. Large herbivores are ecosystem engineers: elephants disperse seeds and maintain savanna structure, and their loss can transform landscapes. Many surviving megafauna species are threatened by poaching, habitat loss and human–wildlife conflict, and "rewilding" proposals — such as Pleistocene rewilding — suggest restoring large herbivores and predators to ecosystems.
