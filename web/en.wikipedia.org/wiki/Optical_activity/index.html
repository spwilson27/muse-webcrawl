Optical activity

Optical activity is the ability of certain substances to rotate the plane of polarization of plane-polarized light passing through them. Such substances are called optically active. The effect is a form of birefringence and is associated with chiral molecules — molecules that are not superimposable on their mirror images.

History

Optical rotation was first observed in quartz crystals by François Arago in 1811, and Jean-Baptiste Biot demonstrated the effect in liquids such as turpentine and sugar solutions. In 1848 Louis Pasteur showed that the effect in tartaric acid arose from molecular asymmetry, separating left- and right-handed crystals by hand and founding the field of stereochemistry.

Dextrorotation and levorotation

A compound that rotates the plane of polarized light clockwise is dextrorotatory (designated (+) or d), while one that rotates it counterclockwise is levorotatory (designated (−) or l). The angle of rotation is measured with a polarimeter and reported as the specific rotation, normalized for path length and concentration.

Cause

Optical activity results from the interaction of light with a chiral environment. Circularly polarized components of the light travel at slightly different speeds through the medium (circular birefringence), so their recombination produces a rotated plane of polarization. Enantiomers rotate light by equal amounts in opposite directions; a 50:50 mixture (a racemic mixture) shows no net rotation.
