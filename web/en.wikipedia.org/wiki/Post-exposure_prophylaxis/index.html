Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP)

Field: Infectious disease medicine
Used for: HIV, rabies, hepatitis B, tetanus
Window: As soon as possible; HIV PEP within 72 hours

Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is any preventive medical treatment started after exposure to a pathogen — a virus, bacterium or toxin — in order to prevent infection from taking hold. PEP is used after possible exposure to HIV, rabies, hepatitis B, anthrax and other infections.

HIV PEP

Occupational PEP is offered to healthcare workers after needlestick injuries or other exposures to HIV-infected blood; non-occupational PEP (nPEP) may follow sexual exposure or shared injecting equipment. Treatment is a 28-day course of combination antiretroviral drugs — typically tenofovir/emtricitabine plus an integrase inhibitor such as dolutegravir — and should begin as soon as possible, ideally within hours and no later than 72 hours after exposure. A widely cited case-control study estimated that zidovudine PEP reduced the risk of HIV transmission after needlestick injury by about 81%.

Rabies PEP

After a bite or scratch from a potentially rabid animal, PEP combines thorough wound washing, a course of rabies vaccine, and — for previously unvaccinated patients — rabies immunoglobulin infiltrated around the wound. Properly administered, rabies PEP is essentially 100% effective at preventing the disease, which is otherwise almost invariably fatal once symptoms appear.

Other uses

PEP also includes hepatitis B immunoglobulin and vaccine after exposure, tetanus boosters and immunoglobulin after wounds, and antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin or doxycycline after exposure to anthrax spores.
