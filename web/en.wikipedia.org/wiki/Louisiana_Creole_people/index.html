Louisiana Creoles (French: Créoles de Louisiane; Louisiana Creole: Moun Kréyòl Lalwizyàn) are people inhabiting the U.S. state of Louisiana who descend from the original French (and later Spanish) colonial settlers of French Louisiana, generally including people of European, African, Native American and mixed heritage shaped by the colonial culture of the region. The word Creole derives from the Spanish criollo and Portuguese crioulo, meaning "born in the colony".

History

Creole culture formed during Louisiana's French (1699–1762) and Spanish (1762–1800) colonial periods in settlements such as New Orleans, Natchitoches and Mobile. Free people of colour (gens de couleur libres) formed a distinct, often prosperous class within colonial society under the Code Noir and Spanish law. After the Louisiana Purchase (1803), Creoles of all backgrounds negotiated an increasingly Anglo-American and racially binary order, a tension dramatised by the Plessy v. Ferguson case (1896), brought by the Creole shoemaker Homer Plessy. Twentieth-century suppression of French in schools pushed many Creoles toward English, though French and Creole language revival efforts continue.

Culture

Creole identity is expressed through distinctive cuisine (gumbo, jambalaya, étouffée), architecture (Creole cottages and townhouses), Catholic feast-day traditions, and music — Creoles of color were central to the creation of jazz in New Orleans and of zydeco in south-west Louisiana. Louisiana Creoles are related to, but historically distinct from, the Cajuns, descendants of Acadian exiles who settled the bayou country after 1764.
