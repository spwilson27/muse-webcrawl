Roman roads in Britain


Built: AD 43 – c. 410
Network length: about 2,000 miles of major surfaced roads
Major routes: Watling Street, Ermine Street, Fosse Way, Dere Street, Stane Street

Roman roads in Britain formed the principal transport network of the Roman province of Britannia, built and maintained by the Roman army over nearly four centuries of occupation. At their greatest extent the major metalled roads ran for roughly 2,000 miles (3,200 km), connecting Londinium (London) with legionary fortresses at York, Chester, and Caerleon, the forts of Hadrian's Wall, and the towns and ports of the province. Many alignments are still followed by modern A-roads, and others survive as bridleways, parish boundaries, or visible earthworks.

Construction

Roman roads were engineered for directness, surveyed with the groma to run in long straight alignments between sighting points, changing direction at high points. A typical road had a raised cambered foundation (agger) of local material, layers of stone and gravel, and a hard-wearing surface of gravel or stone slabs, flanked by drainage ditches. Widths varied from about 5 to 8 metres for major routes. Milestones recorded distances, and posting stations (mansiones) provided lodging and fresh horses for official travellers.

Principal routes

  • Watling Street — Dover and London to Wroxeter and Chester, later extended to York and Carlisle in effect via linking routes.
  • Ermine Street — London to Lincoln and York.
  • Fosse Way — Exeter to Lincoln, marking the early frontier of the province.
  • Dere Street — York north through Hadrian's Wall to the Antonine Wall in Scotland.
  • Stane Street — London to Chichester.
  • Akeman Street, Ermin Way, Peddars Way, and the Stanegate along Hadrian's Wall.

After Rome

Following the end of Roman government around 410, maintenance ceased; surfaces were robbed for building stone and some routes fell out of use, but the main lines remained the best roads in Britain for over a thousand years and underpinned the Anglo-Saxon and medieval road network. The principal ancient itinerary, the Antonine Itinerary, remains the key written source for the network in Britain.
