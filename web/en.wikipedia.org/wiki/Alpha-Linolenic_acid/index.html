α-Linolenic acid (ALA) is an n−3 (omega-3) fatty acid with the chemical formula C18H30O2. It is a carboxylic acid with an 18-carbon chain and three cis double bonds, the first located at the third carbon from the methyl end of the chain. ALA is an essential fatty acid for humans: it cannot be synthesized in the body and must be obtained from the diet.

Sources

ALA is concentrated in certain plant oils and seeds. Rich sources include flaxseed (linseed) oil, in which it constitutes roughly half the fatty acids, chia seeds, hempseed oil, canola (rapeseed) oil, soybean oil, perilla oil, and walnuts. Green leafy vegetables contain smaller amounts in their chloroplast membranes, where ALA is a major component of thylakoid lipids in plants generally.

Role in nutrition

In the body, ALA can be converted to the longer-chain omega-3 fatty acids eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), though this conversion is limited in humans — typically well under 10% for EPA and lower still for DHA. Dietary ALA has been studied for possible cardiovascular benefits. Because the double bonds are susceptible to oxidation, ALA-rich oils are prone to rancidity; linseed oil's reactivity with oxygen is also the basis of its traditional use as a drying oil in paints and varnishes.

History

Linolenic acid was first isolated from linseed oil in the nineteenth century and its structure elucidated in the 20th century. The designation "alpha" distinguishes it from gamma-linolenic acid, an omega-6 isomer with double bonds in different positions.
