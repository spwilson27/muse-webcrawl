Buses in London

Buses in London are a major part of the public transport network of the British capital, operated under the control of Transport for London (TfL). The distinctive red double-decker bus is one of the most recognisable symbols of the city. The network comprises around 675 routes served by approximately 8,000 buses, carrying over 1.7 billion passenger journeys per year in the mid-2010s, making it one of the largest bus networks in the world.

History

Bus services in London began on 4 July 1829, when George Shillibeer started a horse-drawn omnibus service from Paddington to the Bank of England. Motor buses were introduced at the turn of the 20th century, and by the 1910s the London General Omnibus Company dominated services. The iconic AEC Routemaster, introduced in 1956, became synonymous with London buses and remained in regular service until 2005.

Since the 1980s, services have been tendered to private operating companies while TfL sets routes, fares, and service levels. London's buses became fully cashless in 2014, with payment by contactless bank card or Oyster card.

Fleet

The fleet consists predominantly of double-decker buses, with single-deckers and articulated buses on some routes. The New Routemaster, introduced in 2012, revived the open rear platform design in modified form. TfL has progressively introduced hybrid, electric, and hydrogen fuel-cell vehicles, with a goal of a zero-emission bus fleet.

Fares and ticketing

A flat fare applies across the network, and the "Hopper" fare allows unlimited bus journeys within one hour for a single fare. Buses do not accept cash; passengers use contactless payment or Oyster cards.
