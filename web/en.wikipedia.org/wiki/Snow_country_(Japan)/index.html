Snow country (Japan)

Snow country (雪国, yukiguni) refers to the areas of Japan characterised by heavy, long-lasting snowfalls. Typically it denotes the regions along the Sea of Japan coast of Honshu, from Yamagata and Niigata through Toyama, Ishikawa and Fukui prefectures, where winter monsoon winds pick up moisture over the sea and deposit it as snow on the mountains.

These areas record some of the deepest snowfalls on Earth for inhabited regions: seasonal accumulations of 8 metres or more are common, and snow walls along roads such as the Tateyama Kurobe Alpine Route can reach 20 metres. The heavy snow shapes local architecture (steep roofs, covered arcades called gangi), agriculture, the famous snow country sake and hot springs, and has required extensive government support for snow removal.

In literature

The term was popularised by Yasunari Kawabata's novel Snow Country (1935–1947), set in a hot spring town in Niigata—often identified with Yuzawa—which opens with one of the most famous lines in Japanese literature, describing the train emerging from the long tunnel into the snow country.

Climate

The heavy snowfall results from cold Siberian air masses crossing the relatively warm Sea of Japan, forming convective snow bands that stall against the mountainous spine of Honshu. Climate change has measurably reduced snowfall in recent decades.
