William Tell (opera)

ComposerGioachino Rossini
LibrettistsVictor-Joseph Étienne de Jouy and Hippolyte Bis
LanguageFrench
Based onWilhelm Tell by Friedrich Schiller
Premiere3 August 1829, Salle Le Peletier, Paris

William Tell (French: Guillaume Tell) is an opera in four acts by Gioachino Rossini to a French libretto by Victor-Joseph Étienne de Jouy and Hippolyte Bis, based on Friedrich Schiller's play Wilhelm Tell (1804), which drew on the legend of the Swiss folk hero William Tell. First performed at the Salle Le Peletier in Paris on 3 August 1829, it was Rossini's last opera, although he lived for almost forty more years. Its overture — in particular the final section, the "March of the Swiss Soldiers" — is among the most famous pieces of classical music.

Background

Rossini composed Guillaume Tell for the Paris Opéra, which expected a grand opera on a large scale. The work runs to well over four hours in its complete form and was frequently cut in early performances; Rossini himself prepared an abridged version, and the opera was often given in Italian as Guglielmo Tell.

Synopsis

In thirteenth-century Switzerland, under harsh Austrian rule, the herdsman and marksman William Tell becomes the unwilling leader of the Swiss resistance. The tyrannical governor Gessler forces Tell to shoot an apple off his own son's head; Tell succeeds, but reveals a second bolt intended for Gessler and is condemned. He escapes during a storm on Lake Lucerne, kills Gessler, and the Swiss cantons rise in revolt, ending the opera with a hymn to liberty as the sun rises over the mountains.

The overture

The overture is written in four linked sections depicting dawn, a storm, a pastoral scene with the ranz des vaches (the call to the cows), and the famous galloping finale. The finale was used as the theme of The Lone Ranger in American radio, television and film, and the pastoral section features a celebrated cor anglais solo.

Performance history

The opera's length and the extremely high tenor writing for Arnold — with its many high Cs — long kept it a rarity, but complete performances and recordings became more common in the later twentieth century. Notable interpreters include the tenors Nicolai Gedda and Juan Diego Flórez in the role of Arnold.
