Baboon

Baboons are primates comprising the genus Papio, in the family of Old World monkeys. There are six widely recognized species: the hamadryas, Guinea, olive, yellow, Kinda, and chacma baboons. Native to Africa and the Arabian Peninsula, they are among the largest non-hominoid primates and have existed for at least two million years.

Characteristics

Baboons have long, dog-like muzzles, powerful jaws with sharp canine teeth, close-set eyes, thick fur, and short tails. Males of the larger species weigh 30–40 kg; hamadryas males sport a silvery mane. All species show pronounced sexual dimorphism. They are largely terrestrial, spending most of the day foraging on the ground in savanna, steppe, and rocky habitats, and sleep in trees or on cliffs at night.

Behaviour and diet

Baboons live in hierarchical troops of 5 to 250 animals, with rank, grooming alliances, and matrilineal kinship structuring social life. Hamadryas baboons differ, organizing into one-male harems. They are omnivores eating grasses, seeds, fruit, roots, insects, small mammals, and occasionally livestock, which brings them into conflict with farmers. Predators include leopards, lions, and hyenas.

Humans and conservation

In ancient Egypt the hamadryas baboon was sacred, associated with the god Thoth. Most species are listed as Least Concern by the IUCN, though habitat loss and hunting pressure some populations. Baboons are widely used in neuroscience and biomedical research. See also mandrill and primate.
