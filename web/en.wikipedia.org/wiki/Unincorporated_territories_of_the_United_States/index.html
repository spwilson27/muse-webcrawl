Unincorporated territories of the United States

An unincorporated territory is a region controlled by the United States federal government that is not part of any US state and to which Congress has not applied the full Constitution. The concept was developed in the Supreme Court's Insular Cases (1901 and after), which held that territories acquired after the Spanish–American War were "appurtenant" to but not part of the United States, and that only "fundamental" constitutional rights extend there automatically.

Current territories

The inhabited unincorporated territories are Puerto Rico, Guam, the US Virgin Islands, the Northern Mariana Islands, and American Samoa. Puerto Rico, Guam, the Virgin Islands, and the Northern Mariana Islands are additionally organized territories, having received an organic act from Congress establishing local government; American Samoa is unorganized in this technical sense, though it has its own constitution and elected government. Citizens of most of these territories are US citizens at birth, but American Samoans are US nationals unless they have a citizen parent. Residents of territories cannot vote in presidential elections and send only non-voting delegates to the House of Representatives.

History

The doctrine distinguished earlier territories — which under the Northwest Ordinance model were "incorporated" as prospective states (all eventually admitted) — from the island possessions acquired from 1898 onward. The Philippines and Cuba were also governed as unincorporated territories before their independence. A number of uninhabited island possessions, such as Wake Island, Johnston Atoll, Midway Atoll, and Palmyra Atoll (the only incorporated, unorganized territory), round out US territorial holdings. The legal status of the territories remains politically and judicially debated.
