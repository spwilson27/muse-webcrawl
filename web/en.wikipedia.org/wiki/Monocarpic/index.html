Monocarpic

Monocarpic plants are those that flower, set seeds and then die. The term, from Greek mono (single) and karpos (fruit), designates a reproductive strategy — also called semelparity — in which an organism reproduces only once in its lifetime. The opposite strategy, flowering repeatedly over several seasons, is called polycarpy.

Life cycles

Monocarpic plants include all annuals, which complete their cycle in one growing season, and biennials, which flower in their second year. More strikingly, some long-lived perennials are monocarpic: agaves ("century plants") may grow for decades before sending up a single enormous flower stalk and dying; many bamboos flower gregariously at intervals of decades, with whole populations producing seed and dying simultaneously; and talipot palms and certain Strobilanthes species behave similarly.

Mechanism and evolution

Death after flowering results from the diversion of the plant's resources into reproduction and from hormonal changes triggered by seed production; in some species, removing the flowers can prolong the plant's life. The strategy is advantageous where a single massive reproductive effort yields more surviving offspring than repeated smaller efforts, or where adult survival prospects are poor.

Examples

  • Annual cereals and vegetables such as wheat, maize and lettuce.
  • Biennials such as foxglove and carrot (when allowed to bolt).
  • Long-lived monocarps: many Agave species, talipot palm (Corypha umbraculifera), and mass-flowering bamboos such as Melocanna baccifera.
