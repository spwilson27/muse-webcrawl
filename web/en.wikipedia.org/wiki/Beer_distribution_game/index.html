The beer distribution game (also simply the beer game) is an educational simulation game created by a group of professors at the MIT Sloan School of Management in the early 1960s to demonstrate a number of key principles of supply chain management. The game is played by teams of four players, often in heated competition, who must manage a supply chain of a beer brand under conditions of incomplete information.

Gameplay

Four participants occupy the positions of retailer, wholesaler, distributor and factory (brewery). Each week, customers order beer from the retailer; orders and shipments flow up and down the chain with delays of several weeks. Each player sees only their own inventory, incoming orders and shipments, and places orders with their immediate supplier. The goal is to minimise total team cost: holding inventory costs money, and unfilled orders (backlogs) cost twice as much.

The bullwhip effect

Although customer demand in the standard game changes only once (doubling early on and then staying constant), players characteristically over-react: inventories swing into huge backlogs and then into excess stock, with oscillations that amplify at each step upstream. This is the bullwhip effect. The game shows that such instability arises from the structure of the system — delays, batching and locally rational decisions — rather than from individual incompetence, a central lesson of system dynamics as developed by Jay Forrester and popularised in Peter Senge's The Fifth Discipline and John Sterman's work.

Use and variants

The game is played in management courses and companies worldwide, in board-game, spreadsheet and online versions. Variants include computerised play against algorithmic teammates, which has been used in research on human and machine decision-making in supply chains.
