Retreat of glaciers since 1850

The retreat of glaciers since 1850 is well documented and is one of the most obvious effects of climate change. The retreat of mountain glaciers provides evidence for the rise in global temperatures since the late 19th century, coinciding with the end of the Little Ice Age and accelerating markedly in recent decades. Mid-latitude mountain ranges such as the Himalayas, Rockies, Alps, Cascades, and the southern Andes, as well as isolated tropical summits such as Mount Kilimanjaro in Africa, are showing some of the largest proportionate glacial losses.

Causes

Glacier mass balance — the difference between accumulation and ablation — responds sensitively to temperature and precipitation. Although the initial retreat from Little Ice Age maxima partly reflected natural changes, scientific assessments including those of the IPCC attribute the great majority of the observed global glacier mass loss since the mid-20th century, and its acceleration since the 1990s, to human-caused warming.

Impacts

Mountain glaciers are important sources of water for rivers in many regions, feeding irrigation, hydroelectric power, and drinking water supplies for hundreds of millions of people, especially in South and Central Asia and the Andes. Their loss contributes to sea level rise — glaciers are estimated to have contributed roughly a quarter of observed sea-level rise in recent decades — and reduces dry-season water security, while initially increasing meltwater flows and risks from glacial lake outburst floods.

Examples

  • Kilimanjaro's ice fields have shrunk by more than 80% since 1912 and may disappear within decades.
  • Glacier National Park (USA) has lost most of its roughly 150 glaciers present in 1850; only about 25 active glaciers remained in recent counts.
  • Alpine glaciers have lost roughly half their volume since 1900.
  • The Chacaltaya glacier in Bolivia, once the world's highest ski area, vanished in 2009.
