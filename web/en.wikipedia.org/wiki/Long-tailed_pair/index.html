Long-tailed pair

Circuit diagram of a long-tailed pair differential amplifier

A long-tailed pair, also called a differential pair, is a fundamental building block of analogue electronics: a pair of transistors (or vacuum tubes) whose emitters (or cathodes) are joined to a shared, high-impedance "tail" connection, classically a large resistor to a negative supply rail, or increasingly an active current source.

Operation

The two input signals are applied to the bases (or grids) of the pair. Because the tail holds the sum of the two device currents approximately constant, an increase in one device's current is matched by a decrease in the other's. The circuit therefore responds to the difference between the two inputs while rejecting signals common to both, giving a high common-mode rejection ratio. Outputs may be taken from one collector (single-ended) or from both (differential).

Uses

The long-tailed pair is the input stage of most operational amplifiers and comparators, and it appears throughout discrete and integrated analogue design, in emitter-coupled logic (ECL) gates, and in the classic phase-splitter stages of earlier valve amplifiers. The name derives from the circuit diagram, in which the shared emitter resistor drops down like a long tail below the pair.

The configuration was developed in the 1930s in the context of DC amplifiers for physiological and instrumentation work; Alan Blumlein is among those credited with related differential circuits from that era, and the topology became ubiquitous with the arrival of integrated circuits, where matched transistor pairs are easy to fabricate on one die.
