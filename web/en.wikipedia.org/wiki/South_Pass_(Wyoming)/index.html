South Pass (Wyoming)

South Pass is a route across the Continental Divide of the Americas in the Rocky Mountains in southwestern Wyoming. It is a broad, low saddle, about 2,300 metres (7,550 feet) above sea level, lying between the Wind River Range to the north and the Great Divide Basin and the Oregon Buttes to the south. Unlike the steep, high crossings elsewhere in the Rockies, South Pass offers a gradual slope that permitted loaded wagons to cross the divide, making it the key gateway for westward emigration in the nineteenth century.

History

Indigenous peoples used the pass for centuries. The first recorded crossing by people of European descent was by Robert Stuart and the returning Astorians in 1812, who were guided in part by information from the Shoshone. Fur traders spread word of the easy crossing in the 1820s, and in 1830 William Sublette took the first wagons over the pass. In 1832 Captain Benjamin Bonneville brought a wagon train through, proving its practicality for emigrants.

From the 1840s through the 1860s, hundreds of thousands of emigrants on the Oregon, California, and Mormon Pioneer trails crossed at South Pass, following the Sweetwater River westward and descending to the Green River. The Pony Express and the first transcontinental telegraph also used the route. Once over the pass, travellers had crossed from Atlantic to Pacific watershed — waters west of the pass flow toward the Pacific via the Green and Colorado rivers.

The completion of the transcontinental railroad in 1869, which crossed Wyoming farther south, ended the pass's era as a mass migration corridor, though the area saw a gold rush at nearby Atlantic City and South Pass City beginning in 1867. Today Wyoming Highway 28 crosses the pass, and the site is a National Historic Landmark; ruts of the emigrant trails remain visible in the sagebrush landscape.
