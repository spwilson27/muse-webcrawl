Ton (society)

PeriodBritish Regency, c. 1811–1820 (roots in the Georgian era)
MeaningHigh society; the fashionable elite
FromFrench ton, "tone"

The ton was Britain's high society during the late Georgian and Regency eras. The word derives from the French ton, meaning tone or style. To be "in the ton" was to belong to the upper stratum of wealthy, fashionable London society — the aristocracy and landed gentry who gathered for the London Season.

Society and customs

The ton was governed by elaborate rules of etiquette policed by arbiters of fashion such as the dandy Beau Brummell and the patronesses of Almack's assembly rooms, whose vouchers were fiercely coveted and whose dress codes were strict — arriving after eleven o'clock or, famously, wearing trousers instead of knee breeches could mean exclusion. The season revolved around balls, routs, morning calls, promenades in Hyde Park, and presentation at court. Reputation was everything: a breach of propriety could ruin a family socially, a theme central to the novels of Jane Austen and, in modern popular culture, to Regency romance fiction such as the Bridgerton series, which uses "the Ton" as the name for its society world.

Usage

The phrase "that's not the ton" meant a thing was unfashionable or improper. Though the social structure dissolved with the Victorian era, "the ton" survives in historical writing and romance fiction as shorthand for Regency high society.
