Moral relativism

Moral relativism
FieldMeta-ethics
ClaimMoral judgements are true or false only relative to a standpoint

Moral relativism, or ethical relativism, is the family of views holding that moral judgements are true or false, and moral norms justified or unjustified, only relative to some particular standpoint — for example a culture, a historical period, or an individual's commitments — and that no standpoint is uniquely privileged over all others. It differs from moral scepticism and nihilism, which deny that moral judgements can be true at all.

Forms of relativism

  • Descriptive relativism — the empirical observation that moral codes in fact differ between societies. This alone does not entail any normative conclusion.
  • Meta-ethical relativism — the claim that the truth of moral judgements is relative to the moral framework of those making them.
  • Normative relativism — the claim that we ought to tolerate the moral practices of other cultures because there is no standpoint from which to condemn them.

Arguments and history

Diversity of moral belief — noted already by Herodotus and the Greek Sophists, including Protagoras ("man is the measure of all things") — is the classic motivation. In the 20th century, anthropologists such as Ruth Benedict documented wide variation in customs, and philosophers including Gilbert Harman and David B. Wong developed sophisticated relativist or pluralistic theories. Wong's "pluralistic relativism" holds that morality is constrained by human nature and the function of morality, but that more than one moral system can satisfy those constraints.

Criticisms

Critics argue that relativism is self-refuting (if asserted as universally true), that it cannot condemn practices such as slavery or genocide carried out with cultural approval, that it makes moral reformers wrong by definition, and that it exaggerates disagreement — cultures often share underlying values while differing in factual beliefs or circumstances. Defenders reply that relativism need not forbid criticism from within one's own standpoint.
