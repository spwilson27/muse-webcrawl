The White Rose (German: Weiße Rose) was a non-violent, intellectual resistance group in Nazi Germany, formed by students at the University of Munich and their professor, Kurt Huber. Active from June 1942 to February 1943, the group wrote, printed and distributed a series of leaflets calling on Germans to resist the Nazi regime, at a time when dissent was punishable by death.

Members and origins

The core group formed around Hans Scholl, a medical student and former soldier, and Alexander Schmorell. They were joined by Hans's younger sister Sophie Scholl, Christoph Probst, Willi Graf, and the philosophy and musicology professor Kurt Huber. Several members had served as medics on the Eastern Front, where they had witnessed atrocities against Jews and the suffering of occupied peoples, experiences that deepened their opposition. The group drew inspiration from German classical literature, philosophy and Christian faith, and named itself after a white rose, a symbol of purity and innocence; the precise origin of the name remains debated.

The leaflets

Between June 1942 and February 1943 the group produced six leaflets, typed, duplicated in quantities of several thousand, and mailed or carried to cities across Germany and Austria. The leaflets quoted Aristotle, Laozi, the Bible and German poets, denounced the mass murder of Jews and Poles, and urged passive resistance, sabotage and the overthrow of the regime. In January and February 1943 members also painted anti-Nazi slogans — "Down with Hitler" and "Freedom" — on buildings in Munich after news of the German defeat at Stalingrad.

Arrest and execution

On 18 February 1943, Hans and Sophie Scholl scattered copies of the sixth leaflet from a balcony into the atrium of the university's main building. They were seen by a janitor and arrested by the Gestapo. After interrogation, they and Christoph Probst were tried before Roland Freisler's People's Court on 22 February 1943, sentenced to death, and executed by guillotine the same day at Stadelheim Prison in Munich. Schmorell, Graf and Huber were executed later in 1943 after a second trial; others received prison sentences.

Legacy

The text of the sixth leaflet was smuggled out of Germany and dropped over the country by Allied aircraft in July 1943 as "The Manifesto of the Students of Munich". After the war the White Rose became one of the most widely honoured symbols of German resistance to Nazism. Streets, schools and prizes bear the Scholls' names; Geschwister-Scholl-Platz outside the University of Munich commemorates the siblings, and a memorial with copies of the leaflets is set into the pavement. The story has been retold in books and films, notably Sophie Scholl — The Final Days (2005).
