The Southern Annular Mode (SAM), also known as the Antarctic Oscillation, is the dominant pattern of climate variability in the extratropical Southern Hemisphere. It describes the north–south movement of the westerly wind belt that circles Antarctica, expressed as a seesaw in atmospheric pressure between the mid-latitudes (around 45°S) and the Antarctic cap (around 65°S).

Phases and impacts

In the SAM's positive phase, the westerlies contract poleward and strengthen over the Southern Ocean: pressure falls over Antarctica and rises in the mid-latitudes. This tends to bring drier conditions to southern Australia, New Zealand's east, southern South America and South Africa, and wetter, stormier weather to the Antarctic Peninsula region, while the Antarctic interior cools. In the negative phase the westerlies expand equatorward, bringing more rain-bearing systems to the populated mid-latitudes.

Trends and causes

Since the mid-twentieth century the SAM has trended positive, especially in austral summer. Research attributes this trend partly to stratospheric ozone depletion over Antarctica — the "ozone hole" — and partly to increasing greenhouse gases; the Montreal Protocol's phase-out of ozone-depleting substances is expected to slow or partly reverse the trend. SAM variations influence Southern Ocean carbon uptake, sea ice, and rainfall across the southern continents, making it a key index in climate monitoring, usually calculated from station or reanalysis pressure data.
