Thin-film optics

Thin-film optics is the branch of optics that deals with very thin structured layers of different materials. In order to exhibit thin-film optics, the thickness of the layer(s) of material must be on the order of the wavelengths of visible light (about 500 nm). Layers at this scale can have remarkable reflective properties due to light wave interference and the difference in refractive index between the layers, the air, and the substrate. These effects alter the way the optic reflects and transmits light. This phenomenon can be observed in everyday life in the rainbow colours seen in soap bubbles and oil on water.

Interference

When light strikes the surface of a thin film, it is partly reflected from the top surface and partly transmitted; the transmitted portion reflects off the bottom surface and re-emerges. The two reflected waves interfere constructively or destructively depending on their phase difference, which depends on the film thickness, the refractive indices involved, and the angle of incidence. Because different wavelengths interfere constructively at different thicknesses, thin films display vivid colours that change with viewing angle — an effect known as iridescence.

Applications

Optical coatings built from thin films are used throughout technology:

  • Anti-reflective coatings on camera lenses, eyeglasses, and solar cells, which use destructive interference to reduce reflection.
  • Dielectric mirrors and dichroic filters, built from alternating high- and low-index layers.
  • Optical filters for photography, astronomy, and telecommunications (fabry–Pérot etalons and thin-film filter multiplexers in fibre-optic networks).
  • Decorative and protective coatings, including the coloured finishes on some architectural glass.

Natural examples

Structural colouration produced by thin-film interference occurs in butterfly and beetle wings, peacock feathers, nacre (mother of pearl), and the skins of some fish and cephalopods. Biological multilayers can achieve reflectivities comparable to synthetic coatings.
