Prosecution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The prosecution is the legal party responsible for presenting the case in a criminal trial against an individual accused of breaking the law. Typically, the prosecutor represents the state or government (styled the People, the Crown, the State or the Republic, depending on jurisdiction) in the case against the accused.

Role and discretion

Prosecutors decide whether to charge, what charges to bring, whether to offer plea bargains (which resolve the large majority of cases in systems like the United States), and how to present evidence at trial. Their formal duty in most traditions is to justice rather than conviction: disclosing exculpatory evidence to the defence (the Brady obligation in US law) is obligatory, and suppression of it is a leading cause of overturned convictions. Institutional forms differ: elected district attorneys (US), crown prosecution services (England and Wales since 1985), career prosecutors under ministries of justice (much of continental Europe), and investigating judges directing the pre-trial phase in inquisitorial systems.

Debates

Prosecutorial discretion is among the least reviewed powers in criminal justice; critics tie it to mass incarceration, racial disparities and coercive plea pressure, while defenders credit focused prosecution with crime reduction. Reforms discussed include charging standards, diversion programmes, conviction-integrity units and data transparency.
