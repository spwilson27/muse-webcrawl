Arba'in pilgrimage

The Arba'in pilgrimage is the world's largest annual public gathering, held at the end of the 40-day mourning period following Ashura, the day Muslims — especially Shia Muslims — commemorate the martyrdom of Husayn ibn Ali, grandson of the Prophet Muhammad, at the Battle of Karbala in 680 AD. Arbaʿīn (Arabic: الأربعين) means "forty" in Arabic.

The pilgrimage

Each year millions of pilgrims converge on the Iraqi city of Karbala, many walking from Najaf — about 80 kilometres — or from Baghdad and other cities over several days. Along the routes, volunteers staff free rest stations (mawakib) offering food, drink, medical care, and lodging to walkers. Estimates of attendance in recent years commonly range from 15 to over 20 million people, including large numbers of Iranian and other international pilgrims, making the gathering larger than the Hajj in raw participation, though the Hajj is a single prescribed rite.

History and significance

Tradition holds that Jabir ibn Abd Allah visited Husayn's grave on the first Arba'in after Karbala, and that the surviving captives of Husayn's family returned then to Karbala. The pilgrimage was periodically suppressed — notably under Saddam Hussein — and revived on a mass scale after 2003. For participants it is an expression of loyalty, sacrifice, and solidarity with Husayn's stand against tyranny; the walk itself, and the hospitality offered along it, are central devotional acts.
