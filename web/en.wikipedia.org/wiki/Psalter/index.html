A psalter is a volume containing the Book of Psalms, often with other devotional material such as a liturgical calendar, the Litany of the Saints and canticles. In the Middle Ages the psalter was the most widely owned and richly illuminated devotional book: before the rise of the book of hours it was the standard prayer book of the laity, the primer from which children learned to read, and a prestige object commissioned by kings and nobles. The word derives from the Latin psalterium, originally the stringed instrument accompanying the psalms.

Manuscript psalters

Surviving medieval psalters range from plain monastic copies to masterpieces of illumination. The Carolingian Utrecht Psalter (c. 820–835), with its 166 agitated pen drawings illustrating every psalm, was among the most influential artworks of the early Middle Ages. The St. Albans Psalter (c. 1120–1145), associated with Christina of Markyate, and the exuberantly marginal Luttrell Psalter (c. 1320–1340), famous for its scenes of everyday rural life, exemplify the English Gothic psalter. Deluxe copies mark the text with historiated initials, most conventionally a great "B" opening Psalm 1 (Beatus vir) and divisions at Psalms 26, 38, 52, 68, 80, 97 and 109.

Printed and liturgical psalters

The Psalms were among the earliest texts printed — the Mainz Psalter of 1457 was the second major book produced with movable type and the first to print its own date and coloured initials. In the English Reformation, complete metrical psalters such as Sternhold and Hopkins (1562) put singable verse psalms in every parish; the Bay Psalm Book (1640) was the first book printed in British North America. Modern liturgical books — the Roman Psalter, the psalter of the Book of Common Prayer in Coverdale's translation — perpetuate the psalter as a discrete volume.
