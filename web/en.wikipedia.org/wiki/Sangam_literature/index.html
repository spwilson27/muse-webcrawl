Sangam literature

Sangam literature is the earliest known literature of South India, composed in Old Tamil between roughly 300 BCE and 300 CE (with some scholarly estimates extending the period somewhat earlier or later). The corpus is named after the legendary sangams — academies of poets said to have been held under royal patronage at Madurai. It was largely lost for centuries and recovered in the 19th century from temple and monastery manuscripts by scholars such as U. V. Swaminatha Iyer.

Palm-leaf manuscripts preserved the Sangam corpus, the oldest literature of South India.

Corpus

The surviving corpus comprises 2,381 poems by some 473 poets (including about 102 anonymous works), ranging from short lyrics of a few lines to poems of several hundred lines. The works are classified into the Eṭṭuttokai ("Eight Anthologies") and the Pattuppāṭṭu ("Ten Idylls"). Poems are divided by theme into akam (interior: love and domestic life) and puram (exterior: war, kingship and public life), organized around symbolic landscapes (tinai) such as mountain, pasture, coast, farmland and wasteland.

Historical value

The poems give a vivid picture of early Tamil society — its three crowned kings (Chera, Chola and Pandya), chieftains, ports trading with Rome and Southeast Asia, agriculture, warfare and religion. Together with the grammatical treatise Tolkāppiyam, the corpus defines classical Tamil and remains a touchstone of Tamil cultural identity.

Recovery and legacy

After centuries of neglect, the texts were printed in the late 19th and early 20th centuries and came to be celebrated as evidence of an ancient, independent Tamil civilization. Sangam poetry continues to influence modern Tamil literature and scholarship.
