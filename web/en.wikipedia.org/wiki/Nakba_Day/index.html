Nakba Day (Arabic: يوم النكبة, Yawm an-Nakba, "Day of the Catastrophe") is the day of commemoration observed by Palestinians on 15 May, the day after the Gregorian date of Israeli Independence Day, to remember the displacement that accompanied the creation of Israel and the 1948 Arab–Israeli War. During the 1947–1949 war, more than 700,000 Palestinian Arabs — over half the Arab population of Mandatory Palestine — fled or were expelled from their homes, and over 400 Palestinian villages were depopulated or destroyed.

Commemoration

The day was formally inaugurated by Palestinian leader Yasser Arafat in 1998, on the fiftieth anniversary. Commemorations include marches and rallies in the West Bank, Gaza Strip, Israel and the Palestinian diaspora; the "March of Return" processions to destroyed villages inside Israel; sirens and minutes of silence; and the display of symbols such as the key of return and the map of Palestine. Nakba Day events have sometimes turned into clashes with Israeli security forces, with casualties in years such as 2011 and 2021.

Context

The term Nakba was applied to the events of 1948 by the Syrian historian Constantin Zureiq in his 1948 pamphlet Ma'na an-Nakba ("The Meaning of the Disaster"). Debates over the causes and character of the Palestinian exodus remain central to the Israeli–Palestinian conflict and to arguments about the Palestinian right of return.
