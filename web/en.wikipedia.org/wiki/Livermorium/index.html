Livermorium is a synthetic superheavy chemical element with the symbol Lv and atomic number 116. It is named after the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in California, which collaborated with the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia, in its discovery. The name was adopted by IUPAC on 30 May 2012.

Discovery

Element 116 was first reported in 2000, when researchers at Dubna bombarded a curium-248 target with calcium-48 ions and detected a single atom of 292Lv, which decayed within tens of milliseconds. Further experiments at Dubna and Livermore in 2004–2006 produced a handful more atoms of 292Lv and 293Lv, confirming the synthesis. Only a few dozen atoms of livermorium have ever been made.

Properties

Livermorium sits in group 16, below polonium in the periodic table, and would be expected to behave as a heavy chalcogen — a poor metal or metalloid with a preference for the +2 oxidation state because of relativistic stabilisation of its 7s and 7p1/2 electrons. In practice, all known isotopes are extremely radioactive, with half-lives measured in milliseconds, so no bulk chemistry has been observed; its physical properties (predicted melting point around 364–507 °C, high density) come from theory.

Isotopes

Four isotopes are known, 290Lv through 293Lv, all produced either directly or as decay products of the still heavier element oganesson (element 118). The longest-lived, 293Lv, has a half-life of roughly 60 milliseconds. Livermorium lies on the approach to the hypothesised "island of stability" around neutron number 184, which its isotopes, at N = 174–177, do not reach.
