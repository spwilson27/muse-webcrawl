Identity (philosophy)

Identity
BranchMetaphysics, logic
Key relationNumerical identity (sameness)
Key principlesLaw of identity; indiscernibility of identicals; identity of indiscernibles

In philosophy, identity is the relation each thing bears only to itself. The concept concerns sameness: two objects are numerically identical if they are one and the same thing, as opposed to qualitatively identical, meaning merely alike in all their features. Identity is among the most fundamental notions in logic and metaphysics, and questions about it arise in the philosophy of mind (personal identity), the metaphysics of material objects, and the philosophy of language.

Principles of identity

The law of identity states that each thing is identical with itself: for all x, x = x. Leibniz formulated two further principles. The indiscernibility of identicals says that if x and y are identical, then x and y share every property — a widely accepted consequence of the definition of identity, used through substitution salva veritate. The converse, the identity of indiscernibles, says that if x and y share every property then they are identical; this principle is controversial, since it is unclear whether two distinct but perfectly similar objects (such as Max Black's two identical spheres) are possible.

Identity over time

Many puzzles concern persistence: how an object can remain the same while changing. The ship of Theseus, whose planks are gradually replaced, asks at which point (if ever) it ceases to be the same ship. Endurantists hold that objects are wholly present at each moment of their existence; perdurantists hold that objects are extended in time and have temporal parts. Personal identity raises the sharpest version of the question: what makes a person at one time the same person as at another — bodily continuity, psychological continuity, or something else? Derek Parfit argued that identity is "not what matters" in survival, defending instead the importance of psychological connectedness.

Contingent and relative identity

Saul Kripke and others argued that identity, when it holds between rigid designators, is necessary: if Hesperus is Phosphorus, it could not have failed to be so. Others, such as Peter Geach, defended relative identity — the view that x can be the same F as y without being the same G — though most logicians reject it in favour of absolute identity.
