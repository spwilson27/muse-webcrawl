Cross country equestrianism

Cross country is the second phase of the equestrian sport of eventing, and is also contested as a standalone discipline. It consists of jumping a course of solid, natural-looking obstacles — such as logs, ditches, banks, water complexes and stone walls — set across open country, within an optimum time. The phase tests the horse's speed, endurance, jumping ability and bravery, and the rider's judgement of pace and terrain.

The course

Courses range from about 1.5 km at the lowest levels to over 6 km at championship level, with 20–40 jumping efforts. Obstacles are fixed and solid, unlike show-jumping poles, because they imitate natural hunting-country fences; modern design increasingly uses frangible pins and deformable devices that allow a fence to collapse when struck hard, reducing the risk of rotational falls. Combinations, narrow "skinny" fences, corners and fences in or after water test accuracy as well as boldness.

Scoring

Faults are incurred for refusals or run-outs (20 penalties for the first, 40 for the second at the same fence, elimination for a third), for falls of horse or rider (elimination), and for exceeding the optimum time (0.4 penalties per second). Finishing far under the time may also attract penalties at some levels. Cross-country penalties are added to the dressage score before the final show-jumping phase decides the eventing result.

Safety

Because falls at fixed fences can be catastrophic, cross country has been the focus of sustained safety research by the FEI and national federations: frangible technology, fence-design rules, rider qualifications, protective body protectors and inflatable air jackets, and course-walking and veterinary checks are all mandatory features of the modern sport.
