Corporation tax in the Republic of Ireland has been a cornerstone of the country's industrial policy since the late 1950s, evolving from export-profits relief into the well-known low headline rate of 12.5% on trading income. The regime made Ireland one of Europe's most attractive locations for foreign direct investment, particularly from United States multinationals in technology and pharmaceuticals, but also made it a focus of international debate about tax avoidance and "tax haven" practices.

History

Ireland introduced export profits tax relief in 1956 and a 10% rate for manufacturing in 1980. Following pressure from the European Union over selective rates, Ireland legislated in the Finance Act 1999 for a single uniform 12.5% rate on trading income, fully in force by 2003. Passive (non-trading) income is taxed at 25%, and capital gains are generally taxed at 33%. Despite international criticism, successive Irish governments treated the 12.5% rate as a "red line" issue, successfully defending it during the eurozone crisis bailouts of 2010.

Base erosion controversies

Irish structures such as the "Double Irish" arrangement allowed multinationals to shift profits to tax havens; it was closed to new entrants in 2015 and fully phased out by 2020. In 2016 the European Commission ordered Apple to pay Ireland €13 billion in alleged unpaid taxes; the ruling was annulled by the EU General Court in 2020, but the European Court of Justice reinstated the Commission's decision in September 2024. Ireland also became notable for tax-driven corporate inversions and for the distortion of its GDP statistics, which reached a 26.3% growth rate in 2015 through "leprechaun economics".

OECD reform

In October 2021 Ireland joined the OECD Inclusive Framework agreement on a global minimum tax. From 1 January 2024, companies with global revenues above €750 million are subject to a 15% effective rate in Ireland under the EU Minimum Tax Directive, while smaller companies continue to pay 12.5%. Corporation tax receipts nonetheless grew dramatically, exceeding €22 billion in 2023 and contributing to large budget surpluses, though the Department of Finance has warned of the concentration risk from a small number of large payers.
