Communalism

Communalism is a term with several distinct meanings. In its best-known political sense, developed by the American social theorist Murray Bookchin, communalism is a theory of government and a social philosophy in which autonomous, self-governing communities confederate with one another through assemblies of recallable delegates, replacing the nation-state. In South Asian usage, by contrast, "communalism" refers to allegiance to one's own religious or ethnic community in preference to wider society, and is associated with inter-religious tension and violence.

Bookchin's communalism

Bookchin developed communalism out of his earlier philosophy of social ecology and libertarian municipalism. Its core institutions are popular assemblies in towns and city neighbourhoods that make policy decisions directly, administering them through mandated, recallable councils. Municipalities would own the means of production in common and link together in confederations to coordinate on larger scales, gradually creating a dual power capable of replacing capitalism and the state. In his later writings Bookchin adopted the term "communalism" to distinguish this mature system from anarchism generally.

Elements of communalist thought later influenced democratic confederalism in northern Syria, where Bookchin's works were read by Kurdish movement thinkers.

South Asian usage

In India and neighbouring countries, communalism denotes sectarian loyalty to a religious community — most often Hindu or Muslim — treated as the fundamental unit of political life. Historians link its modern form to colonial census categories and separate electorates, which hardened religious identities into political blocs. Communal politics has been blamed for episodes of large-scale violence, including the Partition of India in 1947 and later riots. Indian secularism is commonly framed as the counter-ideology to communalism in this sense.

Other uses

The word is also used loosely for communal living arrangements, communal ownership of property, and religious communal movements such as those practised historically by the Hutterites and in the kibbutz movement.
