Filial piety

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Filial piety (Chinese: 孝, xiào) is the virtue of respect, obedience, and care owed by children to their parents and ancestors. It is a central concept in Confucian ethics, and also important in Chinese Buddhism and Daoism, and it has deeply influenced cultures across East and Southeast Asia, including Korea (where it is hyo), Japan (kō), and Vietnam (hiếu).

In Confucian thought

The Classic of Filial Piety (Xiaojing), framed as a dialogue between Confucius and his disciple Zengzi, teaches that filial piety is "the root of all virtue" and the foundation of social order: the same reverence given to parents extends outward to rulers and society, anchoring the Confucian ideal that proper family relations model proper governance. Filial duty includes supporting parents materially, obeying them, bringing honor to the family, mourning them properly (traditionally for three years), and continuing ancestor veneration. Confucius stressed, however, that true filial piety involves respectful remonstrance when parents err, not blind obedience, and that the attitude of reverence matters more than mere material support — "even dogs and horses are fed."

Practices and influence

Traditional practices include living with and caring for elderly parents, ancestral rites at household altars and graves, and elaborate mourning customs. The popular Twenty-four Filial Exemplars illustrated extreme acts of devotion and were widely taught to children. In modern East Asian societies, filial piety remains influential — expressed in multigenerational households, financial support of parents, and, in Singapore, even legislation (the Maintenance of Parents Act) allowing parents to claim support from their children. Scholars and reformers have debated the tension between filial obedience and individual autonomy, gender equality, and democratic values.
