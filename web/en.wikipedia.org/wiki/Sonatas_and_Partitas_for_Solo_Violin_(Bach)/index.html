The Sonatas and Partitas for Solo Violin (BWV 1001–1006) are a set of six works by Johann Sebastian Bach: three sonatas, each in four movements following the sonata da chiesa pattern, and three partitas composed of dance movements. Together with the Cello Suites, they are the foundation of the solo string repertoire and a touchstone of violin technique and musicianship.

Composition and manuscript

The works were completed by 1720, during Bach's service as Kapellmeister at Köthen, a period that also produced the Brandenburg Concertos and the first book of The Well-Tempered Clavier. Bach's fair-copy autograph survives and is celebrated for its calligraphic beauty; its title page reads Sei Solo a Violino senza Basso accompagnato ("Six Solos for Violin without accompanying bass"). The first printed edition appeared in 1802 from Simrock in Bonn, arranged by Nikolaus Simrock from a copy of the autograph.

The works

  • Sonata No. 1 in G minor, BWV 1001 — Adagio, Fuga (Allegro), Siciliana, Presto.
  • Partita No. 1 in B minor, BWV 1002 — Allemanda and three further movements, each followed by a Double variation.
  • Sonata No. 2 in A minor, BWV 1003 — Grave, Fuga, Andante, Allegro.
  • Partita No. 2 in D minor, BWV 1004 — Allemanda, Corrente, Sarabanda, Giga, and the famous Ciaccona.
  • Sonata No. 3 in C major, BWV 1005 — Adagio, Fuga (its theme derived from the chorale Komm, heiliger Geist), Largo, Allegro assai.
  • Partita No. 3 in E major, BWV 1006 — Preludio, Loure, Gavotte en Rondeau, Menuets I and II, Bourrée, Gigue.

The Chaconne

The Ciaccona (Chaconne) that closes the D minor Partita is the most celebrated single movement: a set of some 64 variations over a repeated bass pattern lasting roughly a quarter of an hour — longer than the other four movements combined. Brahms wrote that on one stave, for a small instrument, Bach "writes a whole world of the deepest thoughts and most powerful feelings." The movement has been transcribed for piano, guitar, orchestra, and many other instruments, notably by Busoni and Stokowski.

Performance practice

Rediscovered and championed in the 19th century by Joseph Joachim, who performed the Chaconne publicly from 1848, the works became central to the repertoire through recordings by violinists including Yehudi Menuhin, Jascha Heifetz, and Nathan Milstein. Since the late 20th century, historically informed performances on Baroque violin with a convex bow have become common, addressing the works' demanding chords and implied polyphony.
