Work function

In solid-state physics, the work function (sometimes spelled workfunction) is the minimum thermodynamic work (i.e., energy) needed to remove an electron from a solid to a point in the vacuum immediately outside the solid surface. Typical values range from about 2 to 6 electronvolts depending on the material and its surface orientation; caesium has one of the lowest values (~2.1 eV) and platinum among the highest (~5.6 eV).

Photoelectric effect

The work function is central to the photoelectric effect: a photon of energy hν can eject an electron only if hν exceeds the work function φ, the excess appearing as kinetic energy, Ek = hν − φ. Einstein's 1905 explanation of this threshold behaviour, building on the experiments of Heinrich Hertz and the measurements of Robert Andrews Millikan, was a founding result of quantum mechanics and is a standard test of the Photoelectric effect.

Measurement and applications

Work functions are measured by photoelectron spectroscopy and Kelvin-probe techniques, and differences in work function between joined materials produce contact potentials. The quantity governs thermionic emission (Richardson's law), electron sources in vacuum tubes and microscopes, and the design of photocathodes and solar-cell contacts.
