Black dog (folklore)

A black dog is a supernatural, spectral or demonic hellhound that occurs in the folklore of the British Isles. It is usually described as larger than a normal dog, sometimes the size of a calf, with large, glowing eyes, and is often associated with the Devil, death omens, thunderstorms, crossroads, churches and ancient paths.

Characteristics

Reports typically describe a shaggy black dog of unnatural size whose appearance may portend death — sometimes of the witness, sometimes of a relative — though in some traditions the animal is a harmless or even helpful guide that escorts travellers safely home. Black dogs are frequently said to vanish suddenly, to leave no footprints, or to be immune to blows.

Regional forms

Named black dogs include Black Shuck of East Anglia, said to have burst into St Mary's Church, Bungay, and Holy Trinity Church, Blythburgh, during a storm on 4 August 1577, leaving scorch marks still shown on the Blythburgh door; the Barghest and Gytrash of northern England; the Gwyllgi of Wales; the Moddey Dhoo of the Isle of Man; and the Church Grim of Yorkshire and Scandinavian tradition, guardian of churchyards.

Origins and interpretation

Folklorists have linked the motif to older European beliefs about the Wild Hunt, to Odin's wolves, to guardian spirits of burial grounds, and to memories of real large dogs guarding churches and travellers. The figure entered popular literature through works such as Sir Arthur Conan Doyle's The Hound of the Baskervilles (1902), which drew on Dartmoor legends of spectral hounds, and it continues to appear in modern fiction and cryptozoological writing.
