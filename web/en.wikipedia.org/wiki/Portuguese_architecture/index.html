Portuguese architecture

Region: Portugal
Key styles: Romanesque, Manueline, Pombaline
Noted architects: Álvaro Siza, Eduardo Souto de Moura

Portuguese architecture refers to the architecture practised in the territory of present-day Portugal and in the former Portuguese Empire. It developed from Roman and Moorish precedents through Romanesque and Gothic forms, and is distinguished by two original styles: the exuberant late-Gothic Manueline of the Age of Discoveries, and the rational Pombaline style of post-earthquake Lisbon.

Manueline style

Named for King Manuel I (r. 1495–1521), the Manueline style (c. 1490s–1520s) celebrates Portugal's maritime expansion with carved ropes, armillary spheres, crosses of the Order of Christ, coral, shells and exotic plants. Masterpieces include the Jerónimos Monastery and the Tower of Belém in Lisbon, and the chapter house window of the Convent of Christ in Tomar, carved by Diogo de Arruda.

Pombaline style

After the 1755 Lisbon earthquake, the Marquis of Pombal's engineers — Manuel da Maia, Eugénio dos Santos and Carlos Mardel — rebuilt downtown Lisbon (the Baixa) on a grid plan with uniform neoclassical facades and the gaiola pombalina, a wooden lattice structure designed to resist earthquakes, one of the earliest seismic construction systems.

Other features

Distinctive Portuguese elements include azulejos (glazed ceramic tiles) covering facades and interiors, whitewashed vernacular houses of the Alentejo and Algarve, baroque sanctuaries such as Bom Jesus do Monte in Braga, and the "Portuguese House" movement of Raul Lino. In the 20th and 21st centuries the Porto School produced Álvaro Siza Vieira and Eduardo Souto de Moura, both winners of the Pritzker Architecture Prize.
