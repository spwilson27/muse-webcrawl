Naser al-Din Shah Qajar (Persian: ناصرالدین شاه قاجار; 17 July 1831 – 1 May 1896) was the fourth Shah of Qajar Iran, reigning from 1848 until his assassination in 1896. His nearly half-century rule — the longest of the Qajar dynasty — spanned Iran's first sustained encounters with European modernity: he introduced the telegraph, modern coinage, a new army, educational institutions and photography, while his government repeatedly granted economic concessions to Britain and Russia that provoked deep domestic resentment.

Accession and early reforms

Naser al-Din succeeded his father Mohammad Shah while still in his teens. Power initially lay with his reforming prime minister Amir Kabir, who curbed court expenditure, reorganised finances and the army, suppressed the Babi movement's armed revolts, and in 1851 founded the Dar ul-Funun, Iran's first modern polytechnic school. Court intrigue turned the shah against his minister, and Amir Kabir was dismissed and executed in 1852 — a decision the shah reportedly regretted for the rest of his life.

Foreign policy and concessions

Iran in this period was caught between the expanding Russian Empire to the north and British India to the east. The Anglo-Persian War of 1856–57, fought over Herat, ended with the Treaty of Paris and Iran's renunciation of claims in Afghanistan. In 1872 the shah granted Baron Julius de Reuter a sweeping concession over railways, mines, irrigation and industry, which was cancelled under domestic and Russian pressure; a later tobacco concession (1890) triggered the nationwide Tobacco Protest of 1891–92, a clerically backed boycott that forced its cancellation and is often seen as a prelude to the Constitutional Revolution. The shah made three celebrated journeys to Europe (1873, 1878 and 1889), keeping diaries that recorded his fascination with European capitals, technology and ceremony.

Culture and modernisation

Under Naser al-Din, Tehran was rebuilt with new walls, avenues and the Golestan Palace complex; the telegraph network spread across the country; newspapers multiplied; and the court embraced photography, with the shah himself an enthusiastic amateur photographer. The court painter Kamal-ol-Molk rose to prominence under his patronage.

Assassination

On 1 May 1896, the eve of the fiftieth anniversary of his accession, Naser al-Din was shot at the shrine of Shah Abdol-Azim in Rayy by Mirza Reza Kermani, a follower of the pan-Islamic activist Jamal al-Din al-Afghani. He was succeeded by his son Mozaffar al-Din Shah.
