Margarine is a food spread made principally from refined vegetable oils and water, designed as a substitute for butter. It is used for spreading, baking, and cooking. Modern margarines are emulsions of oil in water stabilised with emulsifiers, coloured and flavoured to resemble butter.

History

Margarine was invented in 1869 by the French chemist Hippolyte Mège-Mouriès in response to a prize offered by Emperor Napoleon III for a cheap butter substitute for the armed forces and the poor. His original product, "oleomargarine", was made from beef tallow; the name derives from the Greek margarites, "pearl", for its pearly sheen. Dairy interests in many countries pushed through restrictive laws — some required margarine to be dyed pink or banned yellow colouring — that survived well into the 20th century.

Health and reformulation

Classic margarines were made by partial hydrogenation, which created trans fats linked to cardiovascular disease. From the 1990s manufacturers reformulated using interesterified oils and fuller hydrogenation; many jurisdictions, including the United States (2018) and the European Union, have effectively banned industrial trans fats. Soft tub margarines with added plant sterols are marketed for cholesterol reduction.
