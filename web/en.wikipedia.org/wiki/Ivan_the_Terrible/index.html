Ivan IV Vasilyevich (1530–1584), commonly known in English as Ivan the Terrible — from the Russian Ivan Grozny, closer to "Ivan the Formidable" — was Grand Prince of Moscow from 1533 and the first Russian monarch crowned as Tsar of all Russia (1547). His reign transformed Russia into an expanding empire through the conquest of the Kazan, Astrakhan and Siberian khanates, but is equally remembered for the Oprichnina, a state policy of mass repression and terror against the boyar aristocracy.

Early reign and reform

Ivan succeeded his father Vasili III at the age of three and was crowned tsar at sixteen, in 1547, asserting imperial status equal to the Byzantine emperors. With advisers of the "Chosen Council", notably Alexei Adashev and the priest Sylvester, the young tsar enacted significant reforms: the Sudebnik law code (1550), church reform at the Stoglav council, military reorganisation including the streltsy musketeers, and the creation of the Zemsky Sobor assembly. In foreign policy he conquered Kazan (1552) and Astrakhan (1556), opening the Volga, and began the colonisation of Siberia with the Stroganovs' expedition under Yermak (1581–82). St Basil's Cathedral on Red Square was built to commemorate the fall of Kazan.

Livonian War and the Oprichnina

The Livonian War (1558–83) to win a Baltic outlet ended in failure against Poland-Lithuania and Sweden. Amid war strain and suspicion of treason after the defection of Prince Kurbsky, Ivan in 1565 established the Oprichnina: a separate territory under his personal rule, policed by the black-clad oprichniki, who carried out confiscations, executions and massacres — most notoriously the sack of Novgorod in 1570. The policy devastated the economy and nobility without ending the war; it was abolished in 1572 after the oprichniki failed to defend Moscow from a Crimean Tatar raid that burned the city in 1571.

Personal life and death

Ivan married at least six times; his first wife, Anastasia Romanovna (died 1560), was widely mourned, and her death is often linked to the darkening of his reign. In a famous episode of 1581 he struck and killed his heir, Tsarevich Ivan, in a fit of rage — the subject of Ilya Repin's celebrated painting. He died in 1584, reportedly while setting up a chess game, and was succeeded by his feeble-minded son Feodor I, the last Rurikid tsar.
