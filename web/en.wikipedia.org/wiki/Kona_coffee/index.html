Kona coffee is the market name for coffee (Coffea arabica) cultivated on the slopes of Hualālai and Mauna Loa in the North and South Kona districts of the island of Hawaii (the "Big Island"). Only coffee grown in this defined "Kona coffee belt" may legally be labelled Kona. It is one of the most expensive and sought-after coffees in the world.

Growing conditions

The Kona belt, roughly 3 km wide and 50 km long, combines rich volcanic soil with a distinctive microclimate: sunny mornings, afternoon cloud cover and rain, and mild, frost-free nights. Coffee was first planted in Kona in 1828 by the missionary Samuel Reverend Ruggles using plants brought from Brazil; the Guatemalan Typica variety later became dominant.

Production

Some 700 mostly small farms, many of them established by Japanese immigrant families in the early 20th century, produce Kona coffee. Cherries are hand-picked in several passes between August and January, pulped, fermented, and sun-dried on flat platforms (hoshidana). Hawaiian law grades beans (Extra Fancy, Fancy, No. 1, Prime, and peaberry) and regulates blends: a product labelled "Kona blend" historically needed only 10% Kona coffee, a rule long contested by Kona farmers who have pursued labelling reform and lawsuits against sellers of counterfeit Kona.
