Palinopsia

Palinopsia (from Greek palin, "again", and opsia, "seeing") is the persistent recurrence of a visual image after the stimulus has been removed. It is a broad term describing a heterogeneous group of symptoms in which abnormally prolonged or repeated visual images occur. Palinopsia may be physiological, occurring briefly in healthy people, or pathological, associated with disease of the visual pathways, the occipital cortex, or certain drugs and metabolic conditions.

Classification

Palinopsia is commonly divided into two categories. Illusory palinopsia consists of ill-defined, low-resolution afterimages influenced by ambient light and motion, and includes visual trailing, light streaking, and prolonged indistinct afterimages. Hallucinatory palinopsia consists of high-resolution, well-formed images or scenes that persist for minutes to days and are unaffected by environmental conditions; it is more often associated with structural lesions of the posterior cortex.

Causes

Pathological palinopsia has been reported with occipital and parietal lobe lesions, including stroke, tumour, arteriovenous malformation, and seizure activity, as well as with migraine, head trauma, and hallucinogen persisting perception disorder (HPPD). Prescription drugs implicated include trazodone, nefazodone, clomiphene, and topiramate.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the underlying cause. Medication trials reported in the literature include acetazolamide, clonidine, and various anticonvulsants, though evidence is limited to case reports and small series.
