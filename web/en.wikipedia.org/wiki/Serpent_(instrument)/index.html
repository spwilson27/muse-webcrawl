Serpent (instrument)

ClassificationBrass (lip-vibrated aerophone)

The serpent takes its name from its undulating, snake-like shape.

The serpent is a low-pitched early wind instrument, a bass member of the cornett family, in use from the late sixteenth century into the nineteenth. Despite being made mostly of wood, it is classified with the brass instruments because the player's lips vibrate against a cup-shaped mouthpiece. Its conical bore is bent into a distinctive serpentine shape about 2.4 metres (8 feet) long when extended, with six finger holes (later supplemented by keys).

History

Tradition credits the serpent's invention around 1590 to Edmé Guillaume, a canon of Auxerre Cathedral in France, who sought an instrument strong enough to support plainchant in church choirs. For two centuries the serpent served in church music across France and England, doubling the men's voices in chant and psalmody. In the eighteenth century it entered military bands and orchestras: Handel, Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven scored for it, and it appears in works such as Mendelssohn's Reformation Symphony and Wagner's Rienzi.

The serpent is notoriously difficult to play in tune because its finger holes are placed for the hands rather than for acoustics, yet skilled players valued its vocal, expressive tone—Berlioz both praised its colour and mocked its use in church. In the nineteenth century it was progressively replaced by the ophicleide and then by valved brass instruments such as the tuba, though it survived in some French church bands into the early 1900s.

Modern revival

Since the late twentieth century the serpent has been revived by the historically informed performance movement. Makers such as Christopher Monk's workshop reintroduced it, and players including Michel Godard have used it in jazz and contemporary music as well as early repertoire.
