Resource curse

Resource curse
Also known asParadox of plenty
FieldDevelopment economics, political economy
Term coined/popularisedRichard Auty (1993); popularised by Jeffrey Sachs and Andrew Warner (1995)

The resource curse, also known as the paradox of plenty or the poverty paradox, is the hypothesis that countries with an abundance of natural resources — especially non-renewable resources such as oil and minerals — tend to have less economic growth, less democracy, and worse development outcomes than countries with fewer natural resources. Empirical work by Sachs and Warner (1995) found a negative correlation between resource abundance and growth across many countries over the period 1970–1990.

Proposed mechanisms

Dutch disease

Resource exports raise the real exchange rate, making other traded sectors, especially manufacturing, uncompetitive — the phenomenon named after the effects of natural gas discoveries in the Netherlands.

Volatility

Commodity prices are highly volatile, making government revenue and investment unstable and complicating long-term planning.

Institutional effects

Rulers who can finance themselves from resource rents need not tax citizens, weakening accountability ("no taxation without representation" in reverse). Rents can fuel corruption, patronage, rent-seeking and civil conflict, including struggles to control resource-rich regions.

Crowding out

Easy resource income may reduce incentives to invest in education, diversification and human capital.

Evidence and exceptions

The curse is not universal. Norway is frequently cited as having escaped it through strong pre-existing institutions and a sovereign wealth fund (the Government Pension Fund Global) that sterilises oil revenue. Botswana managed diamond wealth comparatively well. Researchers debate whether the effect depends on the type of resource (point-source resources such as oil and hard-rock minerals being more "curse-prone" than diffuse agricultural resources), the quality of institutions at the time of discovery, and whether "abundance" or "dependence" is measured.

Policy responses

Common prescriptions include sovereign wealth funds, transparency initiatives such as the Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative (EITI), fiscal rules that smooth spending over the commodity cycle, and economic diversification.
