Peasants' Revolt

The Peasants' Revolt, also named Wat Tyler's Rebellion or the Great Rising, was a major uprising in England in 1381, triggered by taxes, serfdom and labour laws imposed after the Black Death.

DateMay–June 1381
LocationEngland, especially Kent and Essex
LeadersWat Tyler, John Ball

Causes

The poll tax of 1380 — the third in four years — and the Statute of Labourers' cap on wages provoked widespread resentment. Rebels from Kent and Essex marched on London in June 1381, destroying tax records, burning the Savoy Palace and executing the Archbishop of Canterbury, Simon Sudbury, and the treasurer Robert Hales.

Suppression

The 14-year-old King Richard II met the rebels at Smithfield on 15 June, where their leader Wat Tyler was killed by the Lord Mayor's party. Richard's promises of reform were revoked, and the revolt was suppressed across the country, though poll taxes were never again levied in medieval England.
