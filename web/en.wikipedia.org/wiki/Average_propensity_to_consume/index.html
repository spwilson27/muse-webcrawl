Average propensity to consume

In economics, the average propensity to consume (APC) is the fraction of household income spent on consumption. It is defined as total consumption divided by total disposable income:

APC = C / Yd

The concept was introduced by John Maynard Keynes in The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936) as part of his consumption function.

Properties

Keynes proposed the "fundamental psychological law" that consumption rises with income, but by less than income. In the simple Keynesian consumption function C = a + bYd (with a > 0 and 0 < b < 1), the APC equals a/Yd + b, so it decreases as income rises and approaches the marginal propensity to consume (MPC, equal to b). The complement of the APC is the average propensity to save (APS), with APC + APS = 1.

Empirical findings

Cross-section data show richer households consume a smaller share of income, consistent with a falling APC, but long-run time-series data (notably work by Simon Kuznets) suggested a roughly constant APC over the long run, a puzzle addressed by the relative income hypothesis (Duesenberry), the permanent income hypothesis (Friedman) and the life-cycle hypothesis (Modigliani).
