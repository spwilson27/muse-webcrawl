Sephardi Jews (Hebrew: סְפָרַדִּים, romanized Sefaraddim), also known as Sephardic Jews or Sephardim, are a Jewish ethnic division originating from traditionally established communities on the Iberian Peninsula. The name derives from Sepharad, a Biblical location identified with Spain. In a broader modern usage, especially in Israel, "Sephardi" is sometimes applied to Jews of Middle Eastern and North African origin who follow Sephardic liturgy and law, though historically those communities are also called Mizrahi Jews.

History

Jewish communities existed on the Iberian Peninsula from antiquity and flourished under both Christian and Muslim rule. Under Muslim al-Andalus, Jews participated in a cultural flowering sometimes called the Golden Age of Jewish culture in Spain, producing figures such as the poet Judah Halevi and the philosopher and physician Maimonides, who was born in Córdoba (though he wrote most of his work in Egypt).

The position of Jews deteriorated under the later Christian kingdoms: the massacres and forced conversions of 1391 created a large class of conversos (New Christians), many of whom were later targeted by the Spanish Inquisition, established in 1478. In 1492 the Catholic Monarchs Ferdinand and Isabella issued the Alhambra Decree, expelling practising Jews from Spain; Portugal followed with forced conversion and expulsion in 1496–1497.

Diaspora

The exiles — numbering in the tens of thousands or more — dispersed around the Mediterranean and beyond. The Ottoman Empire welcomed Sephardic refugees: Sultan Bayezid II is said to have mocked Ferdinand for impoverishing his own kingdom while enriching the Sultan's. Major Sephardic centres arose in Constantinople, Salonica (Thessaloniki), Smyrna, Safed, and Jerusalem, as well as in North Africa, Italy, and later Amsterdam, London, Hamburg, and the Americas. Salonica became a predominantly Jewish, Ladino-speaking city until the Holocaust destroyed its community in 1943.

Sephardic scholars in Safed produced foundational works of Jewish law and mysticism, including Joseph Karo's Shulchan Aruch, which became the standard code of Jewish law, and the Kabbalah of Isaac Luria.

Language

The traditional language of the Sephardim is Ladino, also called Judeo-Spanish or Judezmo: a Romance language derived from Old Spanish, written historically in Hebrew characters (Rashi script), and preserving features lost in modern Castilian. In Morocco, the dialect Haketia developed. Ladino is now endangered, though it is still spoken by communities in Israel, Turkey, and elsewhere, and has seen a modest revival of interest.

Customs and liturgy

Sephardic religious practice follows the legal rulings of Maimonides and Joseph Karo and differs from Ashkenazi custom in liturgy, pronunciation of Hebrew, synagogue music, and details of observance — for example, the permissibility of rice and legumes (kitniyot) during Passover, and distinctive foods such as borekas and huevos haminados. Sephardic cuisine reflects Iberian, Mediterranean, and Ottoman influences.

Modern communities

Today large Sephardic and Sephardic-rite communities live in Israel, France, the United States, Canada, Turkey, and Latin America. In Israel the Shas party was founded to represent Sephardic and Mizrahi religious interests. The Spanish and Portuguese governments in 2015 enacted laws offering citizenship to descendants of expelled Sephardim, acknowledging the historical expulsion.
