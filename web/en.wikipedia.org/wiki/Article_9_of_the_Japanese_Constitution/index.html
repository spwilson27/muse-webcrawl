ChapterII — Renunciation of War
SubjectPacifism; prohibition of war potential

Article 9 of the Constitution of Japan is the clause in the national constitution of Japan that outlaws war as a means to settle international disputes involving the state. The constitution came into effect on 3 May 1947, following the Second World War. In its text, the state formally renounces the sovereign right of belligerency and aims at an international peace based on justice and order. The article also states that, to accomplish these aims, armed forces with war potential will not be maintained.

Nevertheless, Japan maintains the Japan Self-Defense Forces, which successive governments have interpreted as defensive forces permitted under a right of self-defence inherent in international law. The interpretation of Article 9 has been one of the central questions of Japanese politics since the 1950s.

Text of the article

Aspiring sincerely to an international peace based on justice and order, the Japanese people forever renounce war as a sovereign right of the nation and the threat or use of force as means of settling international disputes.

In order to accomplish the aim of the preceding paragraph, land, sea, and air forces, as well as other war potential, will never be maintained. The right of belligerency of the state will not be recognized.

Background and drafting

The clause was included during the Allied occupation of Japan after the Second World War. Its origins are commonly attributed to Prime Minister Kijūrō Shidehara, who is said to have suggested the renunciation of war to General Douglas MacArthur, Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, in January 1946; MacArthur's headquarters then drafted the constitution. The provision drew on earlier international instruments such as the Kellogg–Briand Pact of 1928, by which signatory states renounced war as an instrument of national policy.

Interpretation

Shortly after the constitution's adoption, the outbreak of the Korean War in 1950 led the United States to press Japan to rearm. The National Police Reserve was created in 1950, followed by the National Safety Force and, in 1954, the Self-Defense Forces (JSDF). The government maintained that Article 9 does not deny Japan's inherent right of self-defence and that forces maintained solely for that purpose, limited to the "minimum necessary level," do not constitute prohibited "war potential."

The Supreme Court of Japan has largely avoided ruling on the constitutionality of the JSDF. In the 1959 Sunakawa case, the Court held that the U.S.–Japan Security Treaty did not obviously violate Article 9 and invoked a form of the political question doctrine. In the 1973 Naganuma Nike missile case, a district court ruled the JSDF unconstitutional, but the judgment was overturned on appeal on procedural grounds.

Reinterpretation and debate

In July 2014, the cabinet of Prime Minister Shinzo Abe adopted a reinterpretation permitting Japan to exercise collective self-defence — defending an ally under attack — under limited conditions. Implementing security legislation was enacted in September 2015 amid large public protests. Abe also campaigned for formal amendment of Article 9 to explicitly recognize the JSDF, but no amendment was put to a referendum. Polls have consistently shown substantial public attachment to the clause, which is often credited in Japan with the country's postwar peace; supporters of revision argue it is outdated and constrains normal military policy.
