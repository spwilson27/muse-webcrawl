Navel orange

The navel orange is a variety of sweet orange (Citrus × sinensis) characterised by the growth of a second, small and underdeveloped fruit at the apex of the primary fruit, opposite the stem end. Viewed from outside, this rudimentary twin fruit resembles a human navel, giving the variety its name. Navel oranges are seedless, easy to peel, and are grown primarily for fresh consumption rather than for juice production.

History

The navel orange originated as a spontaneous mutation, probably in a monastery orchard in Bahia, Brazil, in the early 1800s. The mutation was propagated by grafting, since the fruit is seedless and the tree is sterile. In 1870, cuttings were sent to the United States Department of Agriculture in Washington, D.C., and from there propagated trees were distributed. Two trees sent to Eliza Tibbets in Riverside, California, in 1873 thrived in the dry California climate, and their fruit proved exceptionally sweet and of high quality. These trees became the foundation of the California navel orange industry; virtually all Washington navel orange trees in the world are descended from the Riverside parent trees, one of which still survives.

Characteristics

Navel oranges are produced by a single mutation that causes the fruit to develop a second fruitlet embedded at the blossom end. Because the flowers lack viable pollen and ovules, the fruit develops without fertilisation (parthenocarpy) and contains no seeds. The fruit is large, round to slightly oval, with a thick, deep-orange rind that peels easily. The flesh is sweet, low in acid compared with some other varieties, and separates readily into segments.

Common navel cultivars include the Washington (or Bahia) navel, the Lane Late, the Navelate, and the Cara Cara, a pink-fleshed mutation discovered in Venezuela in 1976.

Cultivation

Navel oranges are grown in Mediterranean and subtropical climates worldwide, including California and Florida in the United States, Spain, Australia, South Africa, Morocco, Egypt, and Brazil. The fruit matures in winter, typically from November to spring in the Northern Hemisphere, making navel oranges a classic winter citrus. Because the trees are sterile, all propagation is vegetative, by grafting buds onto rootstocks.
