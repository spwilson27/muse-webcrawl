African diaspora religions are a set of related religious traditions that developed in the Americas among descendants of enslaved Africans. Forced to practise under colonial regimes that suppressed African worship, communities preserved and recombined the religions of the Yoruba, Fon, Kongo, Igbo and other peoples, often in syncretism with Roman Catholicism, whose saints came to be associated with African deities.

Principal traditions

Santería (Regla de Ocha) in Cuba centres on the orishas, matched with Catholic saints and venerated through divination, sacrifice and initiation. Candomblé in Brazil maintains Yoruba-, Fon- and Kongo-derived "nations" centred on terreiros (temples). Haitian Vodou serves the lwa, drawing chiefly on Fon and Yoruba sources. Other traditions include Jamaican Obeah and Myal, Trinidad's Shango Baptists and Orisha faith, Palo and the Abakuá society in Cuba, and Brazil's Umbanda, which blends African, Catholic and Kardecist spiritist elements.

Common features

Shared elements include veneration of ancestors, possession by deities during ceremonies, drumming and dance, ritual languages preserving African vocabulary, divination systems such as Ifá, and healing practices. Many traditions were persecuted under slavery and afterwards, surviving through secrecy and adaptation; several are now legally recognised and openly practised, with large followings in the Americas and growing communities in Europe and Africa.
