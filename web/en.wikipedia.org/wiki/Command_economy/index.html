Command economy

[Image: a government planning office with officials studying production charts and maps]

A command economy is an economic system in which a central authority — the state — makes the major economic decisions: what to produce, how to produce it, and who receives it, with prices administered rather than market-formed. It is essentially synonymous with a centrally planned economy, the term emphasising direct state command over enterprises and resources.

Scope and contrasts

Pure command economies existed in the Soviet Union, Maoist China and their allies; today North Korea is the closest example, and wartime economies (Britain and the US in the Second World War) adopted extensive command features temporarily. Most real economies are mixed economies on a spectrum between command and free-market poles: even command systems contained tolerated markets (collective-farm plots, second economies), and market systems retain command islands (public services, the military, conscription historically). Debates over planning versus markets — from the calculation debate to modern industrial policy — are debates over where on this spectrum complex societies function best.
