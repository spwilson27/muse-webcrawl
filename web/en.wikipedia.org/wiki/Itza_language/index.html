Itza (also Itza Maya) is a nearly extinct Yucatecan Mayan language spoken by the Itza people around Lake Petén Itzá in the Petén Department of northern Guatemala. It was the language of the last independent Maya polity, the Itza kingdom centred on Nojpetén (modern Flores), which resisted Spanish conquest until 1697. Today only a small number of elderly fluent speakers remain, alongside a larger group of semi-speakers and revival learners.

History

The Itza migrated into Petén from Yucatán and their language belongs to the Yucatecan branch of Mayan, closest to Mopan. After the fall of Nojpetén in 1697, Spanish colonial policy suppressed indigenous language use in Petén, and through the 20th century fluency collapsed as parents stopped transmitting Itza to children in favour of Spanish. By the early 2000s, linguistic surveys found only a few dozen fully fluent speakers, most of advanced age.

Revitalisation

Since the 1990s, community organisations in San José and the Academia de Lenguas Mayas de Guatemala have documented the language and run classes to teach it as a second language, and Itza cultural identity remains strong even among Spanish-dominant community members. Charles A. Hofling's documentary work, including an Itza dictionary and texts, forms the principal scholarly record.

Linguistic features

Like other Mayan languages, Itza is ergative–absolutive, verb-initial by default, and uses a vigesimal (base-20) counting system with numeral classifiers. It has largely lost the split-ergative tense-aspect system found in Yucatec and has borrowed considerable vocabulary from Spanish.
