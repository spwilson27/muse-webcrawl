The Huangdi Neijing (simplified Chinese: 黄帝内经; traditional Chinese: 黃帝內經; pinyin: Huángdì Nèijīng), literally the Inner Canon of the Yellow Emperor or Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon, is the ancient Chinese medical text that serves as the fundamental doctrinal source for Chinese medicine for more than two millennia. It is comparable in importance to the Hippocratic Corpus in Greek medicine and is regarded as one of the four great classics of Chinese medicine.

The work, compiled by multiple authors between the Warring States period (475–221 BCE) and the Han dynasty, is presented as dialogues between the mythical Yellow Emperor (Huangdi) and his ministers, most notably Qibo.

Structure

The received text comprises two main parts, each of 81 chapters:

  • The Suwen (素問, "Basic Questions" or "Essential Questions"), covering the theoretical foundation of Chinese medicine and its diagnostic methods.
  • The Lingshu (靈樞, "Spiritual Pivot", also known as the Zhenjing, "Canon of Acupuncture"), focusing in detail on acupuncture therapy.

Two further texts, the Taisu (太素, "Grand Basis") and the partially extant Mingtang (明堂, "Hall of Light"), are sometimes counted as additional parts of the Neijing corpus.

Content and doctrines

The dialogues articulate the core theoretical framework of Chinese medicine: the doctrines of yin and yang and the five phases (wuxing); the circulation of qi through the meridians; the zangfu organ systems; pathogenic factors such as wind, cold, dampness and heat; and diagnostic methods including pulse examination. A distinctive emphasis runs through the text: the prevention of disease and the maintenance of health through harmony with natural cycles, diet, rest and emotional balance — summarized in the ideal of the sage who treats illness before it arises.

Composition and transmission

Although attributed to the legendary Yellow Emperor, the text is a layered compilation reflecting medical thought from the late Warring States and Han periods. Scholars note that it contradicts the earlier "demonological" model of disease — the excavated Mawangdui medical manuscripts, for example, rely heavily on incantations — in favour of naturalistic explanations grounded in yin-yang and five-phase theory. Important commentaries and recensions include that of Wang Bing in the Tang dynasty (762 CE), who reorganized the Suwen and whose edition became standard, and Yang Shangshan's Huangdi Neijing Taisu.

Influence

Nearly all later schools of Chinese medicine cite the Neijing as their canonical authority, and its terminology and diagnostic categories remain embedded in modern traditional Chinese medicine practice and curricula.
