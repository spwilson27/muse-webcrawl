The Pentium 4 is a line of single-core and, in its last versions, dual-core central processing units (CPUs) introduced by Intel on November 20, 2000, and shipped through 2008. It was the first CPU to employ Intel's NetBurst microarchitecture, which was designed to reach very high clock speeds by using an unusually deep instruction pipeline — initially 20 stages, later 31 stages.

Overview

Unlike the Pentium II, Pentium III, and various Celeron processors, which were based on the P6 microarchitecture, the Pentium 4 used the NetBurst design. Intel expected NetBurst eventually to scale to 10 GHz; in practice, power consumption and heat dissipation limited commercial parts to about 3.8 GHz. The deep pipeline meant the processor did less work per clock cycle than its predecessors, but Intel's marketing emphasized clock speed, fitting the era's "megahertz myth".

The first Pentium 4 chips, codenamed Willamette, ran at 1.4 and 1.5 GHz and were manufactured on a 180 nm process. They used the Socket 423 package and RDRAM memory, which was expensive; performance was widely judged disappointing relative to the Pentium III and AMD's Athlon. Later versions corrected many shortcomings.

Variants included the Pentium 4 Extreme Edition, with a third-level cache or faster front-side bus, the mobile Pentium 4-M and Mobile Pentium 4, and the dual-core Pentium D, which combined two Prescott or Cedar Mill cores in one package. Hyper-Threading — Intel's simultaneous multithreading implementation — debuted on the 3.06 GHz Northwood model in 2002.

Architecture

NetBurst featured a "Rapid Execution Engine" in which the arithmetic logic units ran at twice the core clock, a trace cache that stored decoded micro-operations instead of instructions, and a quad-pumped front-side bus delivering effective speeds of 400–1066 MT/s. The long pipeline carried a large branch misprediction penalty, and replay of stalled instructions could waste power, contributing to the design's inefficiency.

End of the line

In 2004 Intel cancelled the forthcoming 4 GHz Pentium 4 and the Tejas and Jayhawk cores, signalling the end of the clock-speed race. Intel refocused on efficiency with the Core microarchitecture, derived from the mobile Pentium M, and the Pentium 4 brand was succeeded in desktops by the Core 2 Duo in 2006, though Pentium 4 processors continued to ship until August 8, 2008.
