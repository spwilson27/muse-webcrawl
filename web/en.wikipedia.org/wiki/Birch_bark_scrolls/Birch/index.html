Birch as the material of birch bark scrolls

Paper birch (Betula papyrifera) provides the finest scroll bark.

Within the subject of birch bark scrolls, the birch itself is the enabling technology: not every tree's bark can serve as a writing surface, and the scroll traditions of North America and Eurasia arose precisely where birch species with separable, durable bark were abundant.

Preferred species

The paper birch (Betula papyrifera) of North America is the classic scroll tree: its bark peels in large horizontal sheets, is smooth on its inner surface, and resists rot thanks to its betulin content — the same compound that gives the bark its white colour. In Eurasia, silver birch (Betula pendula) and the Himalayan birch (Betula utilis, whose Sanskrit name bhūrja gives the bark itself its name) supplied Gandharan, Kashmiri and Novgorodian scribes. Scroll-grade bark is harvested in spring, when rising sap loosens the layers, and a well-chosen sheet can be flattened, dried and stored for years before use.

Why birch and not other trees

Most barks are fibrous, deeply furrowed, or quick to decay. Birch bark is unusual in combining a fine, even writing surface with natural waterproofing: incised characters on the inner surface remain legible for centuries, as demonstrated by Novgorod letters recovered from anaerobic soil layers and by Ojibwe Midewiwin scrolls preserved since at least the eighteenth century. The same properties made birch bark the default material for canoes, containers and roofing across the boreal forest — the scroll is one member of a whole material culture built on this single tree.
