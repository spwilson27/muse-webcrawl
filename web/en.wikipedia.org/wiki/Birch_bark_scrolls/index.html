Birch bark scrolls

Birch bark scrolls
MaterialBirch bark (wiigwaas)
CulturesOjibwe, Algonquian peoples; also used historically in Europe, Siberia, and India
PurposeCeremonial records, maps, songs, migration histories

Birch bark scrolls are documents written on pieces of birch bark. Among the Ojibwe (Anishinaabe) people of the Great Lakes region, such scrolls—wiigwaasabak—were used by the Midewiwin society to record songs, rituals, maps, and migration histories using pictographic writing. Similar birch bark documents were produced in many northern cultures where the material was abundant and durable.

Ojibwe scrolls

Ojibwe scrolls were inscribed with sharp tools such as bone or metal points, and the incised lines were sometimes darkened with charcoal or red ochre. They served as mnemonic aids for the elaborate degrees of the Midewiwin (Grand Medicine Society): a trained keeper could "read" long sequences of ceremony, myth, and instruction from the figures. The most famous examples record the migration of the Anishinaabe from the Atlantic coast to the Great Lakes. Scrolls were treasured, copied when they wore out, and stored in cylindrical birch bark containers, sometimes for centuries.

Birch bark writing elsewhere

In medieval Novgorod and elsewhere in Russia, hundreds of birch bark letters (berestyanye gramoty)—everyday messages, debt records, and school exercises—have been excavated from waterlogged soil, giving a unique view of medieval literacy. In India, birch bark (bhurja-patra) from the Himalayan birch was a prestigious writing surface; some of the oldest surviving Sanskrit and Buddhist manuscripts, including the Bakhshali manuscript on mathematics and first-century Gandharan Buddhist scrolls, are written on it.

Properties

Birch bark is rich in betulin and suberin, which resist decay and insects, allowing scrolls to survive far longer than other organic writing materials when kept dry or buried in anaerobic soil.
