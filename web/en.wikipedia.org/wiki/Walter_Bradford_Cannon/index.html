Walter Bradford Cannon (October 19, 1871 – October 1, 1945) was an American physiologist, professor and chairman of the Department of Physiology at Harvard Medical School. He coined the term fight or flight to describe an animal's response to threat, and popularised the concept of homeostasis — the maintenance of stable internal conditions — in his book The Wisdom of the Body (1932).

Research

Cannon began his career as a Harvard undergraduate working with Henry Pickering Bowditch. With Arthur Washburn he pioneered the use of X-ray contrast studies (the bismuth meal) to observe digestion, work that led to The Mechanical Factors of Digestion (1911). During the First World War he studied traumatic shock in wounded soldiers, research summarised in Traumatic Shock (1923) that influenced fluid-resuscitation practice.

His most influential work concerned the sympathetic nervous system and the adrenal medulla. In Bodily Changes in Pain, Hunger, Fear and Rage (1915) he described how adrenaline release prepares the body for emergency — increased heart rate, blood flow to muscles, and mobilisation of glucose — the fight-or-flight response. He proposed that the amygdala and hypothalamus organise emotional responses, and with Philip Bard formulated the Cannon–Bard theory of emotion, which held that emotional experience and physiological arousal occur simultaneously rather than one causing the other.

Homeostasis

Building on Claude Bernard's idea of the milieu intérieur, Cannon coined "homeostasis" (from Greek homoios, similar, and stasis, standing) for the coordinated physiological processes that keep conditions such as temperature, blood sugar and pH within narrow limits. The idea became a founding principle of modern physiology and a model for later work in cybernetics and systems biology.

Later life

Cannon was a prominent public figure: he chaired medical relief committees for Spain and China, campaigned on the ethics of animal experimentation, and served as president of the American Physiological Society. He died in 1945, having been nominated several times for the Nobel Prize without receiving it.
