The Great Stand on the Ugra River (Russian: Стояние на реке Угре, Stoyanie na reke Ugre) was a military standoff in 1480 between the forces of Akhmat Khan of the Great Horde and Grand Prince Ivan III of Moscow, on opposite banks of the Ugra River. After weeks of manoeuvring and skirmishing, the Horde withdrew without a pitched battle. The event is traditionally regarded as the end of the "Mongol-Tatar yoke" — the roughly 240 years during which the Rus' principalities were subordinate to the successors of the Mongol Empire.

Background

Muscovy had effectively stopped paying tribute to the Horde in 1476, and in 1480 Akhmat marched to enforce submission, allied with Casimir IV of Poland-Lithuania. Ivan mobilised his armies but hesitated, sending envoys and gifts even as his troops faced the Tatars across the Ugra, a tributary of the Oka on Moscow's western approaches. Attempts by the Horde to force a crossing in early October were repelled by Russian firearms and arrows.

The stand and withdrawal

As neither side would commit, the armies stood facing each other as winter approached. Ivan redeployed toward Borovsk to protect his flank; Akhmat, short of supplies, still waiting for Lithuanian help that never came (Casimir was distracted by a Crimean attack — Moscow's allies), and facing the onset of winter with news that Ivan's allies had raided toward his capital Sarai, withdrew on 11 November 1480. Akhmat was killed the following year by rival Tatars, and the Great Horde itself collapsed in 1502.

Significance

Though historians note that Mongol suzerainty had been decaying for decades and that Russia continued paying occasional "gifts" to the Crimean Khanate long afterwards, the Great Stand became a foundational episode in Russian national historiography: the bloodless moment Rus' formally regained its independence.
