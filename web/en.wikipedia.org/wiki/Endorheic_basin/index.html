Endorheic basin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An endorheic basin is a drainage basin that normally retains water and allows no outflow to other bodies of water — its rivers end in lakes, marshes or dry sinks within the basin, and water leaves only by evaporation and seepage. Endorheic regions cover roughly a fifth of Earth's land surface (excluding Antarctica), mostly in arid interiors far from oceans or cut off by mountains, and their terminal lakes are often saline, because evaporation concentrates the salts that rivers bring in.

Major examples

The largest endorheic basin is that of the Caspian Sea — technically the world's largest lake precisely because it has no outlet — fed chiefly by the Volga. Others include: the Aral Sea basin of Central Asia (the Amu and Syr Darya), whose Soviet-era diversion for cotton irrigation shrank the Aral from the world's fourth-largest lake to a fraction of its size; the Okavango Delta in Botswana, a "delta" that famously ends in the Kalahari sands; the Lake Chad basin; Australia's Lake Eyre basin, covering about a sixth of the continent; the Great Basin of the western United States (Great Salt Lake); and the Tarim Basin of Xinjiang. Because their water levels balance inflow against evaporation, endorheic lakes are sensitive recorders of climate change and of human water use — the shrinking Aral, Salton Sea and Great Salt Lake are cautionary examples of the fresh water politics of closed basins.
