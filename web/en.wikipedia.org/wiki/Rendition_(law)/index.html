Rendition (law)

[Image: A Gulfstream business jet of the type identified in investigations of CIA rendition flights after 2001]

In law, rendition is the practice of sending a person from one jurisdiction to another, typically for trial or imprisonment. The term covers lawful interstate rendition within federations (such as the transfer of fugitives between US states under the Extradition Clause of the US Constitution) and, in modern usage, the informal transfer of suspects between countries outside formal extradition procedures. Its most controversial form, extraordinary rendition, denotes the transfer of terrorism suspects to countries where they faced interrogation practices, including torture, that would be unlawful in the sending state.

Interstate rendition in the United States

Article IV of the US Constitution and the Uniform Criminal Extradition Act provide for the return of fugitives between states on executive demand. Although expressed as a duty of state governors, it was long unenforceable in federal court (Kentucky v. Dennison, 1861) until the Supreme Court held in Puerto Rico v. Branstad (1987) that federal courts could compel compliance.

Rendition to justice

Before the modern controversy, "rendition to justice" described programmes, used by the United States from the 1980s, to seize indicted suspects abroad — including drug traffickers and the Achille Lauro hijacker Fawaz Yunis — and bring them before US courts without the host state's extradition process. Courts generally held that the manner of capture did not defeat jurisdiction, in line with the Ker–Frisbie doctrine in the US and similar reasoning elsewhere, though such abductions drew diplomatic protests.

Extraordinary rendition after 2001

After the September 11 attacks, the CIA's rendition programme expanded greatly: suspects were flown, often via chartered jets and through European airspace and airports, to detention in countries including Egypt, Syria, Jordan and Morocco, or to secret CIA prisons ("black sites"). The Council of Europe (the Marty report, 2006), the European Parliament and later the US Senate Select Committee on Intelligence (2014 report) documented the system. European Court of Human Rights judgments, including El-Masri v. Macedonia (2012) and cases against Poland, Romania and Lithuania, found states responsible for complicity in torture and secret detention. Italy prosecuted CIA officers in absentia over the 2003 abduction of Hassan Mustafa Osama Nasr (Abu Omar) in Milan. US administrations defended the programme as lawful transfer with diplomatic assurances; human rights organisations characterised it as enforced disappearance and torture by proxy, in violation of the Convention Against Torture's bar on refoulement. President Obama's 2009 executive orders closed the black sites and restricted interrogation while rendition with assurances continued in some form.
