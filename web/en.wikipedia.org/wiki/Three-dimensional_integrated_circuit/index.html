Three-dimensional integrated circuit

A three-dimensional integrated circuit (3D IC) is a MOS (metal-oxide-semiconductor) integrated circuit (IC) manufactured by stacking silicon wafers or dies and interconnecting them vertically using, for instance, through-silicon vias (TSVs) or Cu–Cu connections, so that they behave as a single device. By building upward rather than only shrinking transistors, 3D ICs achieve higher device density, shorter interconnects, lower power consumption, and higher bandwidth than conventional planar chips.

Advantages

Vertical stacking shortens the average wire length dramatically, reducing delay and energy per bit. It also enables heterogeneous integration — combining dies made in different process technologies, such as logic, DRAM, flash memory, and analogue or radio-frequency circuits, in one package. Prominent commercial examples include High Bandwidth Memory (HBM), in which DRAM dies are stacked on a logic base die and connected to a processor through a silicon interposer, and 3D NAND flash memory, in which memory cells are stacked in tall vertical strings.

Manufacturing approaches

3D integration is classified by granularity: package-level stacking, and true monolithic or die-level 3D ICs. Key processes include wafer thinning, alignment, bonding (face-to-face or face-to-back), and formation of through-silicon vias — vertical electrical connections etched completely through the silicon substrate. Hybrid bonding with very fine-pitch copper pads has enabled cache stacking on CPUs (for example, AMD's 3D V-Cache).

Challenges

Important obstacles include heat dissipation from interior dies, mechanical stress, known-good-die testing, yield multiplication across stacked dies, limited design tools, and cost. Thermal management is often the limiting factor for high-performance logic stacks.
