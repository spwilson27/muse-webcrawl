Beverly Clock

The Beverly Clock, driven by daily variations in temperature and air pressure.

The Beverly Clock is a clock built in 1864 by Arthur Beverly (1822–1907) of Dunedin, New Zealand, which is still running despite never having been manually wound. It is driven solely by variations in atmospheric pressure and daily temperature fluctuations in its environment.

Mechanism

The clock's energy comes from an airtight box containing about a cubic foot of air. As the temperature rises, the air expands and presses on a diaphragm; as it falls, the air contracts. A daily temperature swing of around 6 °C is sufficient to lift a one-pound weight by one inch, and this small store of potential energy drives the clock's escapement. Variations in barometric pressure also contribute. The mechanism is displayed in a glass case in the lift well of the Physics Department at the University of Otago.

Interruptions

The clock has stopped on a handful of occasions — for cleaning, mechanical failure, and when the Physics Department moved — but on each occasion it was restarted, and it has otherwise run continuously for well over a century and a half. It is often cited in discussions of perpetual motion, although it is not one: it extracts energy from its surroundings.
