Leigh Woods

Leigh Woods is a 2-square-kilometre (0.77 sq mi) area of woodland on the south-west bank of the Avon Gorge, opposite the city of Bristol in North Somerset, England. Part of the woods is a National Nature Reserve and a Site of Special Scientific Interest, and most of the area is managed by the National Trust and Forestry England. The Clifton Suspension Bridge crosses the gorge at its northern end, and the village of Leigh Woods lies at the woodland's edge.

Description

The woods occupy a plateau and steep slopes above the River Avon, on Carboniferous limestone. They include areas of ancient semi-natural woodland with oak, ash, and small-leaved lime, alongside extensive plantations laid out in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The woodland is notable for rare whitebeam species endemic to the Avon Gorge, including the Bristol whitebeam and Leigh Woods whitebeam, and supports a rich flora of spring flowers and ferns. The gorge slopes are also home to peregrine falcons and other wildlife.

History

Within the woods stand the remains of Stokeleigh Camp, an Iron Age hill fort overlooking the gorge. Parts of Leigh Woods were historically managed as coppice, and in the nineteenth century the land was landscaped with specimen trees and walks, including the Nightingale Valley path descending to the river. Quarrying of limestone and the construction of the Portishead Railway along the river narrowed the gorge-side woods. In the twentieth century public access was secured when the land was given to the National Trust and designated for conservation; it remains a popular walking area for Bristol residents, traversed by waymarked trails.

Access

The woods are crossed by public footpaths and by National Cycle Route 41, and connect via the suspension bridge and towpaths to Ashton Court and central Bristol.
