Mollweide projection

The Mollweide projection is an equal-area, pseudocylindrical map projection generally used for maps of the world or celestial sphere. It is also known as the Babinet projection, homalographic projection, homolographic projection, and elliptical projection. The projection trades accuracy of angle and shape for accuracy of proportions in area, which is what it was designed for.

The world on the Mollweide projection, bounded by a 2:1 ellipse.

History

The projection was first published by the German mathematician and astronomer Karl (Carl) Brandan Mollweide (1774–1825) of Leipzig in 1805. It was reinvented and popularized in 1857 by the French mathematician Jacques Babinet, who gave it the name homalographic projection. The projection was widely adopted for world maps in atlases in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

Formulation

The projection maps the globe onto a 2:1 ellipse. Given a latitude φ and longitude λ relative to the central meridian λ0, coordinates on the map are computed using an auxiliary angle θ found by solving 2θ + sin 2θ = π sin φ, typically by Newton–Raphson iteration:

x = (2√2/π) R λ cos θ,    y = √2 R sin θ,

where R is the radius of the globe being projected. Parallels are unequally spaced straight horizontal lines, and meridians are elliptical arcs; the central meridian is a straight line, and the meridians at ±90° form a circle.

Properties and uses

As an equal-area projection, areas are represented in correct proportion, making the Mollweide well suited for thematic maps showing distributions such as population density, climate zones, and geological features. However, shapes and angles are distorted, especially near the outer meridians and corners. Interrupted versions of the projection (such as Goode's homolosine, which combines the Mollweide with the sinusoidal projection) reduce distortion by cutting the oceans or continents.

In astronomy, the projection is used for all-sky maps, for instance of the cosmic microwave background, where preserving equal areas is important for statistical analysis.
