Vicuña

KingdomAnimalia
FamilyCamelidae
GenusVicugna
SpeciesV. vicugna
Conservation statusLeast Concern (IUCN)

The vicuña (Spanish: [biˈkuɲa]; Vicugna vicugna) is one of the two wild South American camelids, along with the guanaco, which live in the high-altitude alpine areas of the Andes. It is a relative of the llama, and is now believed to be the wild ancestor of the domesticated alpaca, which is raised for its fleece.

Vicuñas produce small amounts of extremely fine wool, which is very expensive because the animal can be shorn only every three years and has to be caught from the wild. Under the Inca, vicuña garments were reserved for royalty, and the animals were protected by law. Poaching reduced numbers to roughly 6,000 by the 1960s, but sustained conservation efforts under the Vicuña Convention raised the population to several hundred thousand, and the species is now classified as Least Concern.

Characteristics

The vicuña is the smallest and most graceful of the camelids, standing about 0.85 m (2.8 ft) at the shoulder and weighing 35–50 kg (77–110 lb). It is adapted to life at 3,200–4,800 m (10,500–15,700 ft), with an enlarged heart and uniquely efficient haemoglobin for oxygen-poor air. Vicuñas live in family groups led by a territorial male, grazing on tough puna grasses and drinking daily — unlike their hardier relatives.

Wool

Vicuña fibre, about 12 micrometres in diameter, is among the finest and warmest natural fibres; a single vicuña coat can sell for many thousands of dollars. Animals are rounded up in a communal drive called a chaccu, shorn, and released, reviving an Inca practice.
