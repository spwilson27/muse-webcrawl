Gavrilo Princip

Gavrilo Princip (25 July 1894 – 28 April 1918) was a Bosnian Serb student who assassinated Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria and his wife Sophie, Duchess of Hohenberg, in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914. The assassination set off the July Crisis, a chain of diplomatic and military escalations that led to the outbreak of the First World War within weeks. Princip was a member of Young Bosnia, a movement of students seeking the union of Bosnia and Herzegovina with Serbia.

Early life

Princip was born in the village of Obljaj near Bosansko Grahovo, the son of a poor farming family; six of his nine siblings died in childhood. He attended school in Sarajevo and Tuzla, and in 1912 moved to Belgrade, Serbia, where he joined circles of South Slav nationalists. In Serbia he trained with the Black Hand-linked conspirators and received weapons from Serbian military intelligence officer Vojislav Tankosić.

The assassination

On 28 June 1914, Franz Ferdinand visited Sarajevo to inspect military manoeuvres. Six conspirators lined the motorcade route; after one threw a bomb that missed the Archduke's car, Princip assumed the attempt had failed. Later that morning, the Archduke's driver took a wrong turn onto Franz Josef Street and stopped in front of the café where Princip happened to be standing. Princip fired two shots from an FN Model 1910 pistol, fatally wounding both Franz Ferdinand and Sophie.

Trial and death

Because he was 19 at the time of the shooting — under 20, the legal threshold — Princip could not receive the death penalty under Austro-Hungarian law. He was sentenced to 20 years in prison, the maximum. Held in harsh conditions in the Theresienstadt fortress, his health collapsed; tuberculosis spread to his bones and one arm was amputated. He died on 28 April 1918, weighing about 40 kilograms.

Legacy

Princip remains a divisive figure: condemned as a terrorist by many and honoured as a national hero by many Serbs and Yugoslavs. Monuments and assessments of him have shifted with the politics of Bosnia and Herzegovina, from Yugoslav-era commemoration to renewed controversy at the centenary of the assassination in 2014.
