Grief

Grief is the response to loss, particularly to the loss of someone or some living thing that has died, to which a bond or affection was formed. Although conventionally focused on the emotional response to loss, grief also has physical, cognitive, behavioural, social, cultural, spiritual, and philosophical dimensions. Bereavement refers to the state of loss, while grief is the reaction to that loss.

Responses and stages

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross proposed in On Death and Dying (1969) that dying patients pass through denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance; the model was later applied to bereavement, though research has found grief rarely follows a fixed sequence. The dual-process model of Stroebe and Schut describes oscillation between loss-oriented and restoration-oriented coping. William Worden described "tasks" of mourning. Prolonged, disabling grief is recognized as prolonged grief disorder in the ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR.

Forms of grief

Anticipatory grief occurs before an impending loss; disenfranchised grief is loss that is not socially acknowledged, such as the death of a pet or a former partner; complicated grief involves persistent yearning and difficulty accepting the death. Grief also follows non-death losses such as divorce, job loss, or loss of health.

Biological aspects

Bereavement is associated with increased mortality, especially in the early months and among widowed spouses. Stress hormones, inflammation, and immune changes have been documented, and intense grief can precipitate takotsubo ("broken heart") cardiomyopathy.

Cultural aspects

Mourning rituals — funerals, wakes, periods of formal mourning dress, memorial days such as Día de los Muertos and Obon — structure communal responses to death across cultures, providing social support and marking the transition of both the deceased and the bereaved.
