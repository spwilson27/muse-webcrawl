Weierstrass approximation theorem

In mathematical analysis, the Weierstrass approximation theorem states that every continuous function defined on a closed interval [a, b] can be uniformly approximated as closely as desired by a polynomial function. Because polynomials are among the simplest functions, and because computers can directly evaluate polynomials, this theorem has both theoretical and practical relevance, especially in polynomial interpolation.

Statement

Suppose f is a continuous real-valued function on [a, b]. For every ε > 0 there exists a polynomial p such that for all x in [a, b], |f(x) − p(x)| < ε; equivalently, polynomials are dense in the space C[a, b] of continuous functions under the uniform norm. Karl Weierstrass proved the theorem in 1885. A constructive proof using Bernstein polynomials was given by Sergei Bernstein in 1912, showing explicit approximating polynomials built from binomial probabilities.

Generalizations

The Stone–Weierstrass theorem (Marshall Stone, 1937) generalizes the result dramatically: any subalgebra of continuous functions on a compact Hausdorff space that separates points and contains a nonzero constant function is dense. Corollaries include approximation by trigonometric polynomials on the circle and by polynomials in several variables on compact subsets of Rn. The theorem does not extend to uniform approximation on unbounded intervals, where polynomials cannot uniformly track functions such as ex on the whole real line.
