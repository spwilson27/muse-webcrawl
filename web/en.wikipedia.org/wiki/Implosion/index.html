Implosion

Implosion is the process in which objects are destroyed by collapsing (or being squeezed in) on themselves. The opposite of explosion—which expands a volume—implosion reduces the volume occupied and concentrates matter and energy. True implosion usually involves a difference between internal (lower) and external (higher) pressure, or inward and outward forces, that is so large that the structure collapses inward into itself.

Examples

Controlled demolition

In building demolition, "implosion" is the strategic placing of explosive charges so that a structure collapses inward under its own weight, minimising damage to surrounding buildings. The term is something of a misnomer: the collapse is driven chiefly by gravity after key supports are severed. Demolition crews sequence charges with delays of milliseconds so the structure falls into its own footprint, a technique used for stadiums, cooling towers, and tower blocks.

Implosion-type nuclear weapons

An implosion-type nuclear weapon uses a precisely arranged sphere of conventional explosives whose detonation waves converge to compress a subcritical sphere (the "pit") of plutonium-239 to supercritical density, initiating a runaway fission chain reaction. This design was used in the Trinity test and the "Fat Man" bomb dropped on Nagasaki in 1945, and required solving formidable problems in explosive lens design and high-speed diagnostics.

Pressure-hull failure

Submersibles taken below their rated depth suffer catastrophic implosion when the pressure hull fails: the surrounding water rushes in and the vessel collapses essentially instantaneously. The loss of the Titan submersible in June 2023, which imploded while descending to the wreck of the Titanic, brought the phenomenon wide public attention.

Bubbles and stars

Collapsing cavitation bubbles in liquids generate inward shocks that can erode propellers and, in the extreme case argued for sonoluminescence, concentrate energy enough to emit light. In astrophysics, the core of a massive star implodes during a core-collapse supernova before rebounding in an explosion that disperses the star's outer layers.
