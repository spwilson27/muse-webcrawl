Samatha (Pali; Sanskrit: śamatha), "calm abiding" or "tranquillity," is a Buddhist practice of calming the mind through sustained, single-pointed concentration on a meditation object. Together with vipassanā (insight), it forms one of the two principal categories of Buddhist meditation (bhāvanā). Samatha is cultivated by fixing attention on a single object until the mind becomes unified and the five hindrances are suppressed, culminating in the states of meditative absorption known as the jhānas.

Practice

Classical manuals such as Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga list forty meditation subjects suitable for samatha, including the ten kasiṇas (coloured discs and elements), mindfulness of breathing (ānāpānasati), recollection of the Buddha, and the four divine abidings (brahmavihāras): loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy and equanimity. The practitioner progresses through preliminary signs of concentration to "access concentration" (upacāra-samādhi) and then "full absorption" (appanā-samādhi). Breath meditation is the most widely taught samatha method today, recommended in the Ānāpānasati Sutta.

Relationship to insight

Schools differ on the sequence: some teach samatha first as a foundation, holding that a concentrated mind investigates reality more effectively; others practise "dry insight," developing vipassanā directly with only momentary concentration. In the earliest discourses the two are frequently presented as qualities to be developed in tandem, and mastery of the jhānas is described as stabilising insight rather than an end in itself, since tranquillity alone does not uproot the defilements permanently.

In other traditions

In Tibetan Buddhism śamatha (Tibetan: zhi gnas) is a prerequisite for vipaśyanā and is often taught with the nine stages of mental abiding. Zen and Chan traditions emphasise "silent illumination" and shikantaza, which blend calm and insight, while the term has also passed into modern secular mindfulness teaching, where concentration practice is used for stress reduction.
