Mu'izz ad-Din Muhammad ibn Sam (c. 1149–1206), commonly known as Muhammad Ghori (or Muhammad of Ghor), was a ruler of the Ghurid dynasty from the mountainous region of Ghor in present-day Afghanistan. Ruling jointly with his elder brother Ghiyath al-Din Muhammad, who held the west, Muhammad Ghori directed the Ghurid expansion eastward and is regarded as the founder of Muslim political power in northern India: his victories opened the Gangetic plain to conquest and prepared the way for the Delhi Sultanate.

Campaigns in India

From 1175 Ghori campaigned through the Indus valley, taking Multan, Uch and Peshawar and, in 1186, Lahore, extinguishing the last Ghaznavid ruler. His first invasion of the Gangetic plain ended in defeat at the First Battle of Tarain (1191) at the hands of the Chauhan king Prithviraj III, and Ghori himself was wounded. He returned the next year and at the Second Battle of Tarain (1192) destroyed Prithviraj's army — a battle conventionally treated as one of the turning points of Indian history. Further victories followed at Chandawar (1194) over the Gahadavala king Jayachandra of Kannauj, while his general Bakhtiyar Khalji carried Ghurid arms into Bihar and Bengal.

Lacking a son, Ghori relied on his Turkish slave-generals (mamluks), foremost among them Qutb al-Din Aibak, whom he left in charge of the Indian conquests. Ghori was assassinated on 15 March 1206 at Dhamiak on the Indus while returning from a campaign against the Khokhar rebellion in Punjab. Within months Aibak had founded the Delhi Sultanate, the first of the Muslim empires of northern India.
