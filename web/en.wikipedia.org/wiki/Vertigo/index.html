Vertigo

The vestibular system of the inner ear, dysfunction of which causes most vertigo.

Vertigo is a condition in which a person has the sensation of movement or of surrounding objects moving when they are not. Often it feels like a spinning or swaying movement. It is a symptom, rather than a condition itself, and is frequently associated with nausea and vomiting, and a balance disorder can make standing and walking difficult.

Causes

Vertigo is classified as either peripheral or central, depending on the location of the dysfunction of the vestibular pathway. The most common peripheral causes are benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV), Ménière's disease, and vestibular neuritis (including labyrinthitis). Central causes include stroke, migraine, multiple sclerosis, and tumours of the cerebellopontine angle.

Pathophysiology

The vestibular system detects angular and linear acceleration through the semicircular canals and otolith organs of the inner ear. A mismatch between vestibular signals and the visual and proprioceptive inputs to the brain produces the illusion of motion, often accompanied by nystagmus — involuntary rhythmic eye movements.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis relies on history and examination, including the Dix–Hallpike manoeuvre for BPPV and the HINTS examination to distinguish central from peripheral causes. Treatment depends on the cause: canalith repositioning manoeuvres such as the Epley manoeuvre for BPPV, vestibular rehabilitation exercises, and short courses of anti-vertiginous medication. Vertigo should be distinguished from presyncope, disequilibrium, and non-specific dizziness.
