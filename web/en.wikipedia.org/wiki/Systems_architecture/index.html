Systems architecture

A systems architecture is the conceptual model that defines the structure, behaviour, and views of a system. An architecture description is a formal description and representation of a system, organised in a way that supports reasoning about the structures and behaviours of the system. A systems architecture can comprise the system components and the sub-systems developed, that will work together to implement the overall system.

Overview

Systems architecture is closely related to systems engineering and enterprise architecture. It addresses the fundamental organisation of a system: its components, their relationships to each other and to the environment, and the principles governing its design and evolution. Because a system's architecture determines qualities such as performance, security, maintainability and evolvability, architectural decisions are among the earliest and most consequential in a system's life cycle.

Standard ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010 defines the concepts of architecture description, stakeholders, concerns, and architecture viewpoints — standardised perspectives (such as functional, information, concurrency, deployment and operational views) through which the architecture is documented. Multiple views are typically needed because no single description serves all stakeholders.

Architecture frameworks

  • DoDAF and MODAF — used by the US and UK defence ministries.
  • TOGAF — The Open Group Architecture Framework, for enterprise architecture.
  • Zachman Framework — a schema for classifying architecture artefacts.
  • Federal Enterprise Architecture — used by the US federal government.

Types

One may distinguish hardware architecture, software architecture, enterprise architecture, collaborative systems architectures (such as the Internet, intelligent transportation systems, and distributed defence systems), and manufacturing systems architectures. In practice the term "systems architecture" is often applied to complex engineered systems that combine hardware, software, people and procedures, where no single disciplinary view suffices.
