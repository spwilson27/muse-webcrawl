Sir John Fielding (1721 – 4 September 1780) was a notable English magistrate and police reformer. Despite being blinded in a naval accident at the age of 19, he succeeded his half-brother, the novelist Henry Fielding, as chief magistrate at Bow Street in 1754 and there organised the Bow Street Runners, often regarded as London's first professional police force.

Career at Bow Street

Fielding presided at the Bow Street court for over twenty-five years, recognising accused persons by their voices — he was said to know more than 3,000 London thieves by voice alone — which earned him the nickname the "Blind Beak of Bow Street". He systematised his brother's experiments: keeping records of crimes and criminals, publishing a "Hue and Cry" gazette of stolen property and wanted offenders, and dispatching his Runners anywhere in the country to pursue suspects.

Reforms and writings

Fielding campaigned against the causes of crime, writing on the employment of the poor, the regulation of pawnbrokers and receivers, and the treatment of juvenile offenders; he helped found the Marine Society (1756) and supported the Asylum for Orphan Girls. He was knighted in 1761.

Death and legacy

Fielding died in Brompton in 1780. His Bow Street office endured until 1839, a decade after the Metropolitan Police superseded the Runners, and his record-keeping and detective methods are foundational to modern British policing.
