Kleenes theorem

Finite automata and regular expressions describe the same class of languages

In theoretical computer science, Kleene's theorem — sometimes written without the apostrophe as Kleenes theorem — is a fundamental result in the theory of finite automata, proved by Stephen Cole Kleene in 1956. It states that the class of languages recognised by finite automata is exactly the class of languages that can be described by regular expressions.

Statement

The theorem has two directions. First, every language described by a regular expression is regular: it can be recognised by a (possibly nondeterministic) finite automaton, which may be built inductively from the structure of the expression using constructions for union, concatenation and the Kleene star. Second, every language recognised by a finite automaton can be described by a regular expression, obtainable for example by Kleene's algorithm, which computes for each pair of states the expression for strings taking the automaton from one state to the other using only intermediate states from a bounded set, or by solving systems of language equations using Arden's lemma.

Significance

Together with the equivalence of deterministic and nondeterministic automata and the closure properties of regular languages, Kleene's theorem completes the classical picture of the lowest level of the Chomsky hierarchy. The constructions in both directions are implemented in practice in pattern-matching engines, lexical analysers and compiler tools.
