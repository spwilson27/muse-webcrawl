Umm El Qa'ab

Umm El Qa'ab (Arabic: أم القعاب, "Mother of Pots") is a necropolis of the early kings of ancient Egypt, located about 1.5 kilometres into the desert west of Abydos in Upper Egypt. It takes its modern Arabic name from the millions of pottery sherds left there by centuries of pilgrims who brought offering vessels to the site, long venerated as the tomb of the god Osiris.

Contents

The cemetery contains burials from the late Predynastic period through the First and Second Dynasties (c. 3300–2700 BC). It is divided into areas including Cemetery U, with hundreds of graves of the Naqada culture, and Cemetery B, where the kings of Dynasty 0 and the First Dynasty were buried in increasingly elaborate mudbrick tombs. The royal tombs were accompanied by subsidiary burials of courtiers and servants, stelae bearing the king's name in a serekh, and grave goods including vessels inscribed with some of the earliest known Egyptian writing — bone and ivory labels from the tomb of King Scorpion I are among the oldest examples of the script.

Excavation

The site was first excavated by Émile Amélineau in the 1890s and then, more carefully, by Flinders Petrie in 1899–1901, who recovered much that the earlier work had missed. Since 1977 the German Archaeological Institute Cairo, under Werner Kaiser and later Günter Dreyer, has re-excavated the necropolis, clarifying the sequence of tombs and the development of kingship at the dawn of the Egyptian state. One tomb, that of Djer, was reinterpreted in the Middle Kingdom as the tomb of Osiris, complete with a famous "Osiris bed", which drew pilgrims for over a thousand years.

Burials

Kings buried at Umm El Qa'ab include Narmer, Hor-Aha, Djer, Djet, Den, Anedjib, Semerkhet and Qa'a of the First Dynasty, and Peribsen and Khasekhemwy of the Second Dynasty — after which royal burials moved to Saqqara. The related funerary enclosures of the kings stand nearer the cultivation at Abydos North.
