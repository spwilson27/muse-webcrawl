Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds

Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds is an early study of crowd psychology by the Scottish journalist Charles Mackay, first published in 1841 under the title Memoirs of Extraordinary Popular Delusions and expanded, under the now-famous title, in 1852. It chronicles a wide range of historical manias, frauds and follies, and remains a classic text on speculative bubbles and mass behaviour.

Mackay's subjects ranged from financial manias to witch trials and fortune-telling.

Contents

The best-known chapters concern three financial manias: the Mississippi Scheme of John Law in France (1719–20), the South Sea Bubble in England (1720), and the Dutch tulip mania of the 1630s. Mackay concluded of these episodes that "men, it has been well said, think in herds; it will be seen that they go mad in herds, while they only recover their senses slowly, and one by one." Other chapters treat alchemists and the search for the philosopher's stone, prophecies and fortune-telling, witch mania and witch trials, the Crusades, haunted houses, duelling, relic worship, and popular fads in language and dress.

Influence

The book is a founding work of crowd psychology, anticipating Gustave Le Bon's The Crowd (1895), and is frequently cited in financial literature — the financier Bernard Baruch wrote a foreword to a 20th-century edition saying it had helped him on Wall Street. Its account of tulip mania became the standard popular version, although modern historians such as Anne Goldgar have argued that Mackay exaggerated the scale and social damage of the tulip episode, and economists debate how far the other episodes fit the modern definition of a bubble.
