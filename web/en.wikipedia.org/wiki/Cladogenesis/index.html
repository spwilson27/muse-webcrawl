Cladogenesis

Cladogenesis is an evolutionary splitting event in which a parent species splits into two distinct species, forming a clade. The term derives from the Greek klados (branch) and genesis (origin). Cladogenesis is the principal way in which biological diversity increases over time, because each splitting event increases the number of lineages. It is contrasted with anagenesis, in which a single lineage changes gradually over time without splitting, and with phyletic extinction (pseudoextinction), in which a lineage is considered to have become a new species through accumulated change.

Mechanisms

Cladogenetic events are usually associated with speciation — the evolution of reproductive isolation between populations. In allopatric speciation, geographic barriers divide a population, and the separated populations diverge genetically until they can no longer interbreed. Peripatric speciation involves a small peripheral population becoming isolated at the edge of a species' range, while parapatric and sympatric speciation involve divergence along environmental gradients or within a single area, respectively. Adaptive radiations, such as the diversification of Darwin's finches on the Galápagos Islands or of cichlid fishes in the African Great Lakes, are rapid sequences of cladogenetic events in which lineages diversify to fill ecological niches.

In the fossil record and phylogenetics

In phylogenetic systematics (cladistics), cladogenesis is represented as a branching node on a cladogram, where an ancestral lineage gives rise to two sister lineages. Palaeontologists debate how often cladogenesis occurs through "budding", in which a new species branches off while the parent species persists, versus bifurcation, in which the parent species is replaced by two descendants. Theories of punctuated equilibrium, proposed by Niles Eldredge and Stephen Jay Gould in 1972, hold that most morphological change is concentrated in relatively brief cladogenetic events, with long intervals of stasis between them.
