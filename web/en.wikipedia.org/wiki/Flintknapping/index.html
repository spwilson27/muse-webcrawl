Flintknapping

PracticeShaping stone by controlled fracture
MaterialsFlint, chert, obsidian, other cryptocrystalline stone
ProductsHandaxes, blades, arrowheads, gunflints, scrapers

Flintknapping (or simply knapping) is the shaping of stones such as flint, chert and obsidian through the process of lithic reduction — striking or pressing controlled flakes from a core to produce sharp-edged tools. It is one of humanity's oldest technologies, practised for over three million years from the earliest Oldowan stone tools through the gunflint industry of the nineteenth century.

Techniques

Knapping exploits the conchoidal fracture of fine-grained, homogeneous stone: a blow or pressure applied near an edge propagates a cone of force that detaches a flake with a characteristic bulb of percussion. Principal techniques include hard-hammer percussion with a stone hammerstone; soft-hammer percussion with antler, bone or wood billets for finer thinning; and pressure flaking, in which a pointed tool presses off small flakes to sharpen or shape an edge. Prepared-core methods such as the Levallois technique, used by Neanderthals and early modern humans, shape the core in advance so that a single strike removes a flake of predetermined form.

Historical and modern practice

Knapped flint provided the cutting edges of the Stone Age: handaxes, spear and arrow points, knives, sickles and scrapers. Long after metal arrived, knapping persisted in gunflints — Brandon in Suffolk remained a centre of production into the twentieth century — and in threshing implements in parts of the Mediterranean. Today, experimental archaeologists knap to understand prehistoric technology, and the craft survives among hobbyists and in the replication of traditional tools. Knapping debris (debitage) is a major source of archaeological evidence about past behaviour.
