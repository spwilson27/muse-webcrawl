Jean Buridan

Image placeholder: Jean Buridan

Jean Buridan (French: [byʁidɑ̃]; Latin: Johannes Buridanus; c. 1301 – c. 1359/62) was an influential French philosopher. He taught at the University of Paris and is best known for his contributions to logic, metaphysics, ethics, and natural philosophy, including the theory of impetus, a precursor of the modern concept of inertia.

Life

Buridan was born probably at Béthune in Picardy. He studied at the University of Paris under the nominalist philosopher William of Ockham, became a master of arts, and served twice as rector of the university (in 1328 and 1340). Unusually for the time, he remained a secular cleric in the faculty of arts rather than proceeding to theology, and taught in Paris for his entire career.

Work

In natural philosophy, Buridan developed the theory of impetus: a moving body acquires an internal motive force proportional to its speed and quantity of matter, which continues its motion unless diminished by external resistance. He applied the idea to projectiles and to the celestial spheres, suggesting God could have imparted impetus to the heavens at creation, removing the need for angels to move them. His pupil Albert of Saxony and later Nicole Oresme transmitted these ideas.

Buridan's name is popularly attached to the paradox of "Buridan's ass," a hungry donkey placed equidistant between two identical piles of hay that starves for want of a reason to choose — an illustration of his discussion of the will and choice, although the example does not appear in exactly this form in his writings.
