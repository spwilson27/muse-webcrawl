Fallacy of division

A fallacy of division is the error in logic that occurs when one reasons that something true of a whole must also be true of all or some of its parts. It is a type of informal fallacy, and is the converse of the fallacy of composition, which reasons that what is true of a part must be true of the whole.

Examples

An everyday example: "This machine is heavy, therefore every part of it must be heavy" — a part may be light even though the whole is heavy. In statistics, the related ecological fallacy infers characteristics of individuals from aggregate data about the group to which they belong. Another example is assuming that because a company's employees are on average highly paid, each employee is highly paid.

Some apparent instances are not fallacies: if a property is genuinely distributive — for example, being made of atoms — then its application to the parts holds. The fallacy arises when the property depends on the arrangement or totality of the parts, as with weight in the machine example or "being numerous".

The fallacy of division was discussed by Aristotle in his Sophistical Refutations, and in mereology it relates to the distinction between distributive and collective predication.
