Gas laws

Gas laws
FieldThermodynamics, physical chemistry

The gas laws are a set of empirical laws describing how gases behave under varying conditions of pressure, volume, temperature, and amount. Developed between the 17th and 19th centuries, they were later combined into the ideal gas law and eventually explained by the kinetic theory of gases.

Principal laws

Boyle's law

Formulated by Robert Boyle in 1662: at constant temperature, the pressure of a fixed amount of gas is inversely proportional to its volume (P ∝ 1/V).

Charles's law

Named for Jacques Charles and published by Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac in 1802: at constant pressure, the volume of a gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature (V ∝ T).

Gay-Lussac's law

At constant volume, the pressure of a gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature (P ∝ T).

Avogadro's law

Proposed by Amedeo Avogadro in 1811: equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules, so volume is proportional to the amount of substance (V ∝ n).

Combined and ideal gas laws

Combining Boyle's, Charles's, and Gay-Lussac's laws gives the combined gas law. Adding Avogadro's law yields the ideal gas law, PV = nRT, where R is the universal gas constant (8.314 J·mol−1·K−1). Real gases deviate from ideal behavior at high pressures and low temperatures, corrections for which are given by equations of state such as the van der Waals equation.
