The Securities Exchange Act of 1934 (also called the Exchange Act, '34 Act, or 1934 Act) is a law governing the secondary trading of securities (stocks, bonds, and debentures) in the United States of America. A landmark piece of wide-ranging legislation, the Act of '34 and related statutes form the basis of regulation of the financial markets and their participants in the United States.

The 1934 Act also established the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), the agency primarily responsible for enforcement. Companies raise billions of dollars by issuing securities in what is known as the primary market. The 1934 Act regulates secondary trading between individuals and companies which are often unrelated to the original issuers.

History

The Act followed the stock market crash of 1929 and the Great Depression. Together with the Securities Act of 1933, which regulates primary offerings, it was part of the New Deal reforms intended to restore confidence, require disclosure, and prevent fraud and market manipulation.

Provisions

Key provisions require registration and periodic reporting by public companies (Forms 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K), regulate proxy solicitations, tender offers, insider trading (Section 16 and Section 10(b)), and exchanges, brokers and dealers. Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5 are the principal antifraud provisions, used in cases of securities fraud.

Amendments

Major amendments include the Williams Act (1968) on tender offers, the Private Securities Litigation Reform Act (1995), the Sarbanes-Oxley Act (2002) and the Dodd-Frank Act (2010).
