Mortise and tenon

Mortise and tenon joint
ClassificationWoodworking joint; also used in stone and metal
ComponentsMortise (hole), tenon (tongue)
Known sinceNeolithic era; among the oldest joinery methods
UsesTimber framing, furniture, doors, boats, stone construction

A mortise and tenon (occasionally mortice and tenon) joint is a classic method of joining two pieces of material, most often wood, at or near a right angle. A mortise is a rectangular or rounded cavity cut into one member to receive a tenon, a projecting tongue formed on the end of the other member, shaped to fit snugly into the mortise. When glued, pinned, or wedged, the joint is among the strongest in woodworking, and it has been used by woodworkers around the world for thousands of years.

Construction

Typically the tenon is about one-third the thickness of the stock and is cut with shoulders that seat against the face of the mortised piece. The mortise is usually chopped with a mortise chisel or bored with a mortiser or router. Variations include the through tenon, which passes entirely through the mortised member and may be wedged from the far side; the stub (blind) tenon, which stops within the member; the haunched tenon, used at the top of door frames; and loose-tenon joints, in which a separate piece of wood is fitted into mortises in both members — a technique used by furniture makers and popularized in modern form by floating-tenon systems.

History

The mortise and tenon is one of humanity's oldest joints. Examples survive from Neolithic wells in Europe, ancient Egyptian furniture and coffins, Chinese timber architecture of great antiquity, and Stonehenge, whose great sarsen lintels were fixed to their uprights with mortise-and-tenon joints carved in stone. The Khufu ship of ancient Egypt, timber-framed barns and houses across Europe, and traditional Japanese joinery all depend on the technique.

Applications

The joint is the foundation of timber frame construction, where posts and beams are joined with large pegged mortises and tenons, and is used throughout fine furniture — in chair legs, table aprons, and framed doors — wherever strong right-angle connections are needed. In stonemasonry and metalworking, analogous tongue-and-socket joints serve similar purposes.
