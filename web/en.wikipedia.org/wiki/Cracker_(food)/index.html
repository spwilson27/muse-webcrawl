Cracker (food)

A cracker is a flat, dry baked biscuit typically made with flour. Crackers are eaten on their own as a snack or served with toppings such as cheese, dips, or spreads. The name is generally said to come from the crackling sound made during baking, or from the medieval term for hard, dry breads. In the United Kingdom, savoury biscuits of this type are distinguished from sweet biscuits, and "cracker" may also refer to the Christmas cracker novelty.

History

Precursors include ship's biscuit (hardtack), a twice-baked staple of sailors. In 1792 John Pearson of Newburyport, Massachusetts, began producing "pilot bread," and in 1801 Josiah Bent of Milton, Massachusetts, baked thin, crisp biscuits that cracked in the oven — an early commercial cracker. The invention of soda crackers (saltines) in the 19th century, using baking soda to leaven the dough, gave crackers their characteristic lightness. Industrial production by firms such as Nabisco (maker of Ritz, introduced 1934, and Premium saltines) made crackers a mass-market snack in the twentieth century.

Types

  • Saltines (soda crackers) — square, perforated, salted on top.
  • Water crackers — plain flour-and-water crackers served with cheese.
  • Graham crackers — sweetened whole-wheat crackers, originally promoted by Sylvester Graham.
  • Oyster crackers — small round crackers served with chowder and soup.
  • Rice crackers — including Japanese senbei and arare.
  • Cream crackers — popular in Britain and Ireland.

Characteristics

Crackers are typically leavened with baking soda or yeast, docked with holes to prevent blistering, and baked at low moisture for crispness and long shelf life. Flavourings such as salt, herbs, seeds, and cheese are commonly added to the dough or surface.
