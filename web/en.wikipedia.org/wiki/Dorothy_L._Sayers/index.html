Dorothy Leigh Sayers (June 13, 1893 – December 17, 1957) was an English crime novelist, playwright, translator, and lay theologian. She is best known for her mysteries featuring the amateur sleuth Lord Peter Wimsey, and is regarded as one of the "Queens of Crime" of the Golden Age of Detective Fiction alongside Agatha Christie, Margery Allingham, and Ngaio Marsh.

Life

Sayers was born in Oxford, the daughter of a clergyman and school headmaster. She won a scholarship to Somerville College, Oxford, and in 1915 was among the first women to graduate from the university when degrees were opened to women in 1920. She worked as an advertising copywriter at S. H. Benson's in London — an experience used in Murder Must Advertise — and is credited with the famous Guinness "toucan" advertisements.

Works

Her first Wimsey novel, Whose Body?, appeared in 1923. Later novels such as Strong Poison (1930) introduced the detective novelist Harriet Vane, Wimsey's eventual wife, and Gaudy Night (1935), set at an Oxford women's college, is often considered her masterpiece. From the late 1930s she largely abandoned detective fiction for religious drama, notably the radio cycle The Man Born to Be King (1941–42), essays such as The Mind of the Maker (1941), and a celebrated translation of Dante's Divine Comedy, left unfinished at her death.
