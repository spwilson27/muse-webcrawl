Hohle Fels (German for "hollow rock") is a cave in the Swabian Jura of southwestern Germany, near Schelklingen, famous for discoveries of early Upper Paleolithic art and music. Excavations since the 19th century, and intensively in recent decades, have yielded some of the oldest known figurative artworks.

The Venus of Hohle Fels

In 2008 excavators led by Nicholas Conard recovered the Venus of Hohle Fels, a small mammoth-ivory figurine dated to at least 35,000 years ago, among the earliest undisputed depictions of a human being. Its exaggerated features are interpreted in relation to fertility symbolism.

Musical instruments

The cave also yielded flutes made from bird bone and mammoth ivory, dated to roughly 35,000–43,000 years ago—among the oldest musical instruments known, demonstrating that music was established in the culture of early modern humans in Europe.

Significance

Together with nearby caves such as Geissenklösterle and Vogelherd, Hohle Fels documents the Aurignacian culture's remarkable efflorescence of art, ornament, and music. In 2017 UNESCO inscribed the caves as a World Heritage Site.
