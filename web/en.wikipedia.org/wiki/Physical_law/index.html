Physical law

A physical law, scientific law, or law of nature is a statement, inferred from repeated empirical observations, that describes or predicts a range of natural phenomena. Scientific laws summarise the results of experiments and observations, usually within a limited range of application where their accuracy has been empirically established.

Nature of physical laws

Laws differ from scientific theories in that they describe what happens under given conditions rather than explaining the underlying mechanism. A law is typically expressed as a mathematical equation — for example Newton's law of universal gravitation, Ohm's law, the laws of thermodynamics, and the conservation laws for energy, momentum, and electric charge.

Laws are developed from data and can be further developed through mathematics. They are generally true under stated conditions, but new data may reveal a need for refinement or limit their domain, as when Newtonian mechanics was superseded by relativity and quantum mechanics at extreme scales.

Examples

  • Newton's laws of motion
  • Laws of thermodynamics
  • Maxwell's equations
  • Conservation of energy and momentum
  • Hooke's law, Boyle's law, and other empirical regularities

Philosophers of science debate whether laws are discovered features of nature or human descriptions, and whether they hold universally or only approximately.
