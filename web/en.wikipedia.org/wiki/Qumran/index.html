Qumran (Hebrew: קומראן; Arabic: خربة قمران, Khirbet Qumran) is an archaeological site on a dry marl terrace about 1.5 kilometres from the north-western shore of the Dead Sea, in the West Bank. It is best known as the settlement nearest to the Qumran Caves, where the Dead Sea Scrolls were discovered between 1946 and 1956.

The settlement

Excavations directed by Roland de Vaux uncovered a small communal complex occupied mainly from the Hasmonean period (c. 100 BCE) until Roman forces destroyed it in 68 CE during the First Jewish–Roman War. The ruins include a tower, a large assembly or dining room with an adjacent pantry stacked with pottery, an elaborate water system fed by an aqueduct from Wadi Qumran, several stepped ritual baths (mikva'ot), kilns, and a cemetery of over a thousand graves. A room with fallen mudbrick benches and inkwells was interpreted by de Vaux as a scriptorium where scrolls were copied.

The scrolls and the Essene hypothesis

Eleven caves near the settlement yielded remains of some 900 manuscripts, dating from the third century BCE to the first century CE: biblical books, sectarian rule books such as the Community Rule, commentaries, prayers, and the great Isaiah Scroll. De Vaux identified the inhabitants with the Essenes, the ascetic Jewish sect described by Josephus, Pliny the Elder, and Philo. That identification remains the scholarly consensus, though alternative theories — a villa, a fortress, a pottery factory, or a Sadducean community — continue to be debated.
