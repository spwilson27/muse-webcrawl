Constant elasticity of substitution

Constant elasticity of substitution (CES) is a property of some production functions and utility functions in which the elasticity of substitution between two inputs is constant. The CES production function was introduced by Kenneth Arrow, Hollis Chenery, Bagicha Minhas and Robert Solow in 1961 and has become a standard tool in macroeconomics, trade and growth theory.

CES production function
FormQ = A [αK−ρ + (1−α)L−ρ]−1/ρ
Elasticity of substitutionσ = 1 / (1 + ρ)
Introduced1961 (Arrow, Chenery, Minhas, Solow)
Special casesCobb–Douglas (σ→1), Leontief (σ→0), linear (σ→∞)

Formulation

In its two-input form, output Q is produced from capital K and labour L: Q = A [αK−ρ + (1−α)L−ρ]−1/ρ, where A is a total factor productivity parameter, α is the share parameter, and ρ determines the elasticity of substitution, σ = 1/(1+ρ). Unlike the Cobb–Douglas function, which forces σ = 1, CES allows the ease of substituting capital for labour to be estimated from data.

Special cases

As σ → 1 the CES function converges to the Cobb–Douglas function; as σ → 0 it becomes the Leontief (fixed-proportions) function; as σ → ∞ it approaches a linear function with perfect substitutes.

Applications

CES functions are used in computable general equilibrium models, in growth models with capital-augmenting technical change, and as CES utility or aggregator functions in international trade (the Armington assumption) and monopolistic competition models of the Dixit–Stiglitz type.
