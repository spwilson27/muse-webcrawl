A seizure is a period of abnormal, excessive, or synchronous neuronal activity in the brain, producing signs and symptoms that range from subtle staring spells to violent convulsions and loss of consciousness. Seizures are symptoms of underlying brain dysfunction rather than a disease in themselves. A person has epilepsy when they have recurrent unprovoked seizures. Around 8–10 percent of people will experience a seizure at some point in life, though far fewer have epilepsy.

Classification

The International League Against Epilepsy classifies seizures by onset. Focal seizures begin in one part of the brain; awareness may be retained (focal aware) or impaired, and symptoms depend on the region involved — jerking of one limb, déjà vu, fear, or automatisms such as lip-smacking. Generalized seizures engage both hemispheres from the start and include tonic-clonic (formerly "grand mal") seizures, with stiffening then rhythmic jerking; absence seizures (brief staring and unresponsiveness, typical in children); myoclonic (brief jerks); atonic (sudden loss of muscle tone); and tonic seizures. Focal seizures may evolve into bilateral tonic-clonic seizures.

Causes and triggers

Seizures may be provoked by acute insults: high fever in young children (febrile seizures), stroke, head trauma, brain infection such as meningitis, tumours, low blood sodium or glucose, alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, and drug toxicity. In epilepsy, seizures arise from a persistent predisposition — genetic, structural, metabolic, or unknown. Common triggers in people with epilepsy include missed medication, sleep deprivation, illness, flashing lights (in photosensitive epilepsy), and alcohol.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on the clinical history and eyewitness accounts, supported by EEG and MRI. A prolonged seizure lasting more than five minutes, or repeated seizures without recovery, is status epilepticus, a medical emergency. First-aid consists of protecting the person from injury, timing the seizure, and placing them in the recovery position; nothing should be put in the mouth. Long-term management uses antiseizure medications (such as levetiracetam, lamotrigine, valproate, or carbamazepine chosen by seizure type), and for drug-resistant focal epilepsy may include surgery, vagus nerve stimulation, or ketogenic diet. Most seizures stop on their own within one to two minutes, followed by a period of confusion or fatigue called the postictal state.
