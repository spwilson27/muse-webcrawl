Favism

TriggerFava beans (or their pollen)
Underlying causeGlucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency
Main effectAcute haemolytic anaemia

Favism is an acute haemolytic reaction that occurs in people with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency after eating fava beans (Vicia faba), and occasionally after inhaling the plant's pollen. It is the most dramatic clinical presentation of G6PD deficiency.

Mechanism

Fava beans contain the glucosides vicine and convicine, whose metabolites are powerful oxidants. In healthy red blood cells, G6PD supplies the reducing power (NADPH and glutathione) needed to neutralize oxidants. In deficient individuals, oxidative damage denatures haemoglobin (forming Heinz bodies) and red cells are destroyed, producing sudden anaemia, jaundice, dark urine, abdominal pain, and fatigue, typically within 24 hours of eating the beans.

Epidemiology and history

G6PD deficiency is one of the commonest human enzyme defects, affecting hundreds of millions of people, with the highest frequencies in the Mediterranean, Africa, and Asia — regions where fava beans are a traditional staple, and where the trait may have been maintained by its protective effect against malaria. The connection between beans and illness was known to the ancients; a famous tradition holds that Pythagoras forbade his followers to eat beans. Most attacks resolve spontaneously, but severe cases in children may require blood transfusion. Prevention is simply avoidance of fava beans by deficient individuals.
