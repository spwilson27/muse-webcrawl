West Timor (Indonesian: Timor Barat) is the western half of the island of Timor in the Lesser Sunda Islands, forming part of Indonesia's East Nusa Tenggara province. The eastern half of the island is the independent state of Timor-Leste (East Timor), which also holds the exclave of Oecusse surrounded by West Timorese territory. West Timor's capital and largest city is Kupang, on the island's southwestern coast.

History

Timor was long a source of sandalwood traded to China and later to European merchants. The Portuguese established settlements in the east in the 16th century, while the Dutch East India Company seized Kupang in 1653 and gradually consolidated control of the west. The island's formal division was settled by treaties between the Netherlands and Portugal in 1859 and 1914. During the Second World War, Timor was a battleground between Allied and Japanese forces. After 1949 West Timor became part of the Republic of Indonesia, and from 1975 to 1999 Indonesia also occupied East Timor, a period marked by conflict and by refugee movements into West Timor after the 1999 independence referendum.

Geography and people

The terrain is mountainous, with Mount Mutis (2,427 m) the highest point, and a pronounced dry season produces savanna landscapes dotted with lontar palms. The population of roughly two million is ethnically diverse — Atoni (Dawan), Tetum, Rotenese, and others — and majority Christian (Catholic and Protestant), unlike most of Indonesia. Languages include Uab Meto (Dawan), Tetum, and Indonesian as the lingua franca. The economy rests on agriculture (maize, rice, coffee), livestock, and sandalwood.

Administration

West Timor comprises the city of Kupang and the regencies of Kupang, South Central Timor, North Central Timor, Belu, and Malaka. The border region with Timor-Leste, including the Oecusse exclave, remains an area of cross-border trade and occasional negotiation over boundary segments.
