Ocean deoxygenation

Global ocean oxygen content has fallen by roughly 2% since 1960, and low-oxygen "dead zones" have expanded.

Ocean deoxygenation is the expansion of oxygen minimum zones and the overall decline of dissolved oxygen in the world's oceans, driven primarily by climate change and nutrient pollution. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change reports that the global ocean lost an estimated 0.5–3.3% of its oxygen inventory between 1970 and 2010, with further losses projected through the 21st century.

Causes

Warming reduces the solubility of oxygen in seawater and, more importantly, increases stratification, slowing the ventilation of deeper waters by oxygen-rich surface water. In coastal regions, nutrient runoff from agriculture and sewage fuels algal blooms whose decomposition consumes oxygen, producing hypoxic "dead zones" such as those in the Gulf of Mexico and the Baltic Sea.

Effects

Declining oxygen compresses habitat for tuna, marlin, sharks, and other active predators, stresses benthic communities, alters nutrient cycling (promoting release of the greenhouse gas nitrous oxide), and can reduce fish growth and body size. Oxygen minimum zones have expanded by an area roughly the size of the European Union since 1960. Deoxygenation is one of the three interlinked stressors — with warming and ocean acidification — sometimes called the "deadly trio" for marine ecosystems.

Outlook

Climate models project continued oxygen loss under all emissions scenarios, and full recovery, even if warming stops, would take centuries to millennia because of the slow circulation of the deep ocean.
