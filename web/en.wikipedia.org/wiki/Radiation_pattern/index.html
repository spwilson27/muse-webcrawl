Radiation pattern

In the field of antenna design, the term radiation pattern (or antenna pattern or far-field pattern) refers to the directional (angular) dependence of the strength of the radio waves emitted by the antenna, or equivalently the angular sensitivity of the antenna when receiving. It is typically a three-dimensional graph of the radiated power or field strength as a function of direction, measured in the antenna's far field.

Overview

A radiation pattern is usually plotted on polar or rectangular coordinates, often in decibels relative to the maximum (dB). The pattern consists of a main lobe, which contains the direction of maximum radiation, and smaller side lobes and back lobes, separated by nulls — directions of minimal radiation. The angular width of the main lobe, measured between the half-power (−3 dB) points, is the beamwidth.

Reciprocity

By the reciprocity theorem, the radiation pattern of an antenna used for transmitting is identical to its receiving pattern: an antenna radiates most strongly in the directions from which it receives most sensitively. This is why directional antennas such as Yagi–Uda antennas and parabolic dishes must be pointed at the station they communicate with.

Common patterns

An omnidirectional pattern, such as that of a vertical dipole or monopole, radiates equally in all azimuthal directions, producing a doughnut-shaped three-dimensional pattern. A directional pattern concentrates energy into a beam; highly directive patterns are produced by aperture antennas and phased arrays, whose patterns can be steered electronically by adjusting element phases.

Related quantities

The pattern is directly connected to directivity and gain: the narrower the main lobe, the higher the directivity. The front-to-back ratio compares the main lobe with the back lobe, and the side-lobe level is an important specification in radar and satellite antennas.

Measurement

Radiation patterns are measured on antenna ranges or in anechoic chambers, rotating the antenna under test while recording received power. Near-field scanning techniques can also be transformed mathematically to obtain the far-field pattern.
