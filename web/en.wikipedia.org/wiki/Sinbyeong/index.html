Sinbyeong

Sinbyeong (Korean: 신병; also Romanised shinbyeong or sinpyŏng), literally "divine illness" or "spirit sickness", is a phenomenon characteristic of Korean shamanism (musok), in which a person — typically the future shaman, or mudang — is believed to be chosen by spirits or gods and experiences an initiatory sickness that can only be resolved by accepting the calling and undergoing an initiation ritual.

Symptoms and course

The condition is described in the ethnographic literature as beginning with symptoms such as loss of appetite, insomnia, vivid dreams and visions, auditory or visual hallucinations, weakness, and pains or illnesses that resist medical treatment. The afflicted person may wander, speak in altered voices, or display uncanny knowledge attributed to spirit possession. Family members often consult a mudang, who diagnoses the illness as a sign that the spirits have selected the sufferer as their vessel.

Resolution: the naerim-gut

The accepted remedy is the naerim-gut, an initiation ceremony in which the novice (under the guidance of an experienced "spirit mother" or mentor shaman) ritually receives the spirits, dons their costumes, and demonstrates possession by dancing, divining, and performing feats such as standing on blades. After initiation, the symptoms are said to abate, and the new mudang sets up a shrine and begins practice. Refusing the calling is believed to prolong the illness, bring misfortune, or pass the affliction to descendants.

Interpretation

Anthropologists and psychiatrists have compared sinbyeong to initiatory illnesses reported in shamanic cultures elsewhere, such as among Siberian peoples, and have debated its relationship to psychopathology; it is generally treated as a culture-bound syndrome whose meaning and cure are embedded in the ritual system itself. It remains a recognised pathway into professional shamanic practice in contemporary South Korea.
