Inverse Compton scattering

Inverse Compton scattering occurs when a high-energy charged particle, usually a relativistic electron, transfers part of its energy to a low-energy photon, increasing the photon's energy. It is the reverse of ordinary Compton scattering, in which a photon loses energy to an electron. The process is an important energy-loss and radiation mechanism in astrophysics.

Mechanism

When an electron with Lorentz factor γ collides with a photon of energy ε, the scattered photon can gain energy of order γ²ε. A bath of low-energy photons — for example the cosmic microwave background or starlight — passing through a population of relativistic electrons is thereby up-scattered to X-ray or gamma-ray energies, while the electrons cool.

Astrophysical applications

Inverse Compton scattering explains part of the emission from supernova remnants, pulsar wind nebulae, and the jets of active galactic nuclei, where it complements synchrotron radiation. It is also responsible for the Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect, in which microwave background photons are up-scattered by hot electrons in galaxy clusters, and it limits the maximum brightness temperature of compact radio sources (the inverse Compton catastrophe).
