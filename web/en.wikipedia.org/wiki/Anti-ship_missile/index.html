Anti-ship missile

Anti-ship missile
Type: Guided missile
Targets: Surface ships
Launch platforms: Ships, aircraft, submarines, coastal batteries

An anti-ship missile (AShM) is a guided missile designed for use against ships and large boats. Anti-ship missiles can be launched from a variety of platforms, including surface ships, submarines, aircraft, and land-based coastal batteries. Most fly at low altitude over the sea — "sea-skimming" — to delay detection by radar, while others use high-altitude or ballistic trajectories.

History

Guided anti-ship weapons appeared in the Second World War with German radio-controlled glide bombs such as the Fritz X, which sank the Italian battleship Roma in 1943. The missile age arrived in 1967, when Egyptian missile boats sank the Israeli destroyer Eilat with Soviet-built Styx missiles — the first combat sinking by naval guided missiles. The 1982 Falklands War, in which Argentine Exocet missiles struck British warships, demonstrated their impact on modern naval warfare.

Guidance and design

Typical missiles cruise using inertial navigation or satellite updates, then use an active radar seeker for terminal homing. Warheads are usually high-explosive, semi-armour-piercing designs intended to detonate inside the hull. Examples include the Harpoon, Exocet, and the Russian P-270 Moskit, as well as modern long-range and hypersonic designs.

Defences

Ships defend against anti-ship missiles with surface-to-air missiles, close-in weapon systems such as Phalanx CIWS, electronic warfare, and decoys such as chaff.
