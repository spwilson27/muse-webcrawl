Obturator foramen

Part of: Pelvis

Bones: Pubis and ischium of the hip bone

Latin: foramen obturatum

The obturator foramen is the large opening in the pelvis created by the ischium and pubis bones of the hip bone. It is the largest foramen in the human skeleton. In life it is almost completely closed by the obturator membrane, leaving only a small gap superolaterally — the obturator canal — through which the obturator nerve and vessels pass from the pelvic cavity into the medial compartment of the thigh.

Structure

The foramen is bounded above by the superior pubic ramus, medially and below by the inferior pubic ramus and the ramus of the ischium, and laterally by the ischium. It is oval in males and more triangular in females, one of many pelvic features used in forensic sex estimation. The obturator membrane attaches around its margins; the obturator internus muscle arises from the pelvic surface of the membrane and surrounding bone, and the obturator externus arises from its outer surface.

Contents of the obturator canal

The obturator canal, at the upper part of the foramen, transmits the obturator nerve, obturator artery, and obturator vein. The nerve (from spinal roots L2–L4) supplies the adductor muscles of the thigh and skin over the medial thigh, and its entrapment or injury can cause pain and weakness in that distribution.

Clinical significance

An obturator hernia — protrusion of abdominal contents through the obturator canal — is rare but occurs mainly in elderly, thin women and can present with bowel obstruction and pain radiating down the medial thigh. The foramen is also a landmark in pelvic radiography and surgery.
