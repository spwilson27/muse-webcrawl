The winter solstice, also called the hibernal solstice, occurs when either of Earth's poles reaches its maximum tilt away from the Sun. This happens twice yearly, once in each hemisphere (Northern and Southern). For that hemisphere, the winter solstice is the day with the shortest period of daylight and longest night of the year, and when the Sun is at its lowest daily maximum elevation in the sky.

Each polar region experiences continuous darkness or twilight around its winter solstice. The opposite event is the summer solstice. The winter solstice occurs during winter of each hemisphere. In the Northern Hemisphere, this is the December solstice (December 20, 21, 22 or 23) and in the Southern Hemisphere, this is the June solstice (June 20 or 21). Although the winter solstice itself lasts only a moment, the term also refers to the day on which it occurs.

Astronomy

The solstices result from Earth's axis of rotation being tilted about 23.4 degrees relative to the plane of its orbit around the Sun. At the December solstice, the North Pole is tilted furthest away from the Sun, and the Sun shines directly over the Tropic of Capricorn at local noon. At the June solstice, the situation is reversed, with the Sun over the Tropic of Cancer.

On the day of the winter solstice, the Sun follows its lowest and shortest arc across the sky. Locations within the polar circles experience polar night, during which the Sun never rises. The length of daylight increases after the solstice, which is why the event has historically been seen as marking the rebirth of the Sun and the turning point of winter.

History and cultural significance

The winter solstice has been a significant time of year in many cultures since the Neolithic period. Evidence of its importance is seen in the layouts of late Neolithic and Bronze Age archaeological sites, such as Stonehenge in England and Newgrange in Ireland, whose primary axes appear to have been carefully aligned on a sight-line pointing to the winter solstice sunset and sunrise respectively.

Fewer hazards and the availability of fresh food made midwinter a traditional time for feasting. Festivals associated with the winter solstice include Yule in Germanic and modern pagan traditions, Saturnalia in ancient Rome, Dongzhi in East Asian cultures, and Yalda Night in Iran. The Roman festival of Sol Invictus, celebrating the unconquered Sun, was held on December 25 in the later Roman Empire and is often discussed in connection with the date chosen for Christmas.

Observances

Many modern observances gather at dawn or dusk on the solstice. At Stonehenge, crowds gather to watch the sunrise over the stones. In Ireland, a lottery is held for entry to the chamber of Newgrange at dawn on the days around the solstice, when a beam of sunlight can illuminate its inner chamber. Dongzhi, the East Asian winter solstice festival, is a time for family gatherings and the eating of tangyuan (glutinous rice balls) in southern China or dumplings in the north.
