Local government in England

Local government in England operates through a patchwork of structures built up over more than a century of reform. Responsibility for public services is divided between single-tier and two-tier authorities: county councils and district councils in two-tier areas, and unitary authorities, metropolitan boroughs and London boroughs elsewhere. There is no single unified system, and England (unlike Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland) has no devolved parliament of its own.

London

Greater London is administered strategically by the Greater London Authority, consisting of the directly elected Mayor of London and the London Assembly, while the 32 London boroughs and the City of London deliver most day-to-day services.

Combined authorities and mayors

Since 2011, groups of councils have formed combined authorities under devolution deals, many headed by directly elected "metro mayors" — including Greater Manchester, the West Midlands, the Liverpool City Region and the Tees Valley — with powers over transport, skills and economic development.

History

The modern system originates with the Municipal Corporations Act 1835, the Local Government Act 1888 (which created county councils) and the London Government Act 1899. The Local Government Act 1972 imposed the two-tier county-and-district pattern across most of England from 1974; the metropolitan county councils and the Greater London Council were abolished in 1986. From the 1990s onward, successive rounds of unitarisation — including major changes in 2009 and 2019–2023 — converted many two-tier areas to single-tier unitary councils.

Finance

Local authorities are funded by council tax, retained business rates, fees and charges, and central government grants. Council tax has been levied since 1993 and is based on property bands set in 1991.
