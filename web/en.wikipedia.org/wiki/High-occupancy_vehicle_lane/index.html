High-occupancy vehicle lane

A high-occupancy vehicle lane (HOV lane) is a restricted traffic lane reserved for vehicles carrying a minimum number of occupants — often also open to motorcycles, buses and emergency vehicles. Also known as carpool lanes or diamond lanes (after the diamond symbol painted on the roadway in the United States), HOV lanes are intended to maximise the people-moving capacity of highways by encouraging carpooling and public transit use rather than simply moving more vehicles.

Design and rules

Lanes are usually designated HOV 2+ or HOV 3+, requiring at least two or three people per vehicle including the driver. They may operate at all times or only during peak periods, and may be separated from general traffic by painted buffers, concrete barriers, or implemented as reversible lanes in the freeway median. Enforcement is typically by police observation; violations carry fines.

HOT lanes and variants

High-occupancy toll (HOT) lanes allow single-occupant vehicles to buy access with a toll, often dynamically priced, while carpools travel free. In some jurisdictions, low-emission and electric vehicles have been granted temporary HOV access privileges to promote adoption. The first major HOV facility in the United States opened in 1969 on the Shirley Highway (I-395) in northern Virginia as a bus-only lane, later extended to carpools.

Criticism

Critics note that many HOV lanes appear under-used ("empty lane syndrome") while adjacent lanes remain congested, and debate their measurable effect on carpooling rates; supporters respond that the lanes move more people per lane-hour than general-purpose lanes and give buses and carpools a reliable travel-time advantage.
