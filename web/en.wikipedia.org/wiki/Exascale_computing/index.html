Exascale computing

Exascale computing refers to computing systems capable of performing at least one exaFLOPS, or 1018 floating-point operations per second. Such systems represent a thousandfold increase over the petascale supercomputers of the late 2000s and are a major milestone in high-performance computing.

History

The first machine widely recognised as breaking the exascale barrier was Frontier at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in the United States, which topped the TOP500 list in June 2022 at about 1.1 exaFLOPS. Aurora at Argonne National Laboratory and El Capitan at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory followed. Chinese systems are also believed to have reached exascale performance, though they have not been submitted to the TOP500.

Challenges

Exascale systems must manage enormous power consumption (Frontier uses roughly 22 megawatts), resilience against frequent component failures, data movement costs, and software capable of exploiting massive parallelism across millions of cores and accelerators. Research programmes such as the US Exascale Computing Project (2016–2023) coordinated hardware, software, and applications development.

Applications

Exascale machines are used for nuclear stockpile stewardship, climate and weather modelling, materials science, genomics, fusion energy research, and large-scale artificial-intelligence workloads.
