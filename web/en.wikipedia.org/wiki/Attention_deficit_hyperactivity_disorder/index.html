Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterised by persistent patterns of inattention and/or hyperactivity–impulsivity that interfere with functioning or development. It is one of the most commonly diagnosed conditions of childhood — affecting roughly 5–7% of children and about 2.5–4% of adults worldwide — and frequently persists into adult life.

Signs and symptoms

Inattentive symptoms include difficulty sustaining attention, careless mistakes, forgetfulness, losing things and being easily distracted. Hyperactive–impulsive symptoms include fidgeting, restlessness, excessive talking, interrupting and difficulty waiting. Under the DSM-5, presentations are classified as predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive–impulsive, or combined. Symptoms must begin before age 12, occur in more than one setting, and not be better explained by another condition. People with ADHD often also experience executive dysfunction, emotional dysregulation and sleep problems, and the condition commonly co-occurs with learning disorders, anxiety, depression and autism spectrum disorder.

Causes and pathophysiology

ADHD is highly heritable (twin studies estimate heritability around 70–80%), with many common genetic variants each contributing a small effect. Neuroimaging studies find group-level differences in prefrontal and striatal circuits and in dopamine and noradrenaline signalling, consistent with the effectiveness of stimulant medications. Prematurity, low birth weight and prenatal exposures are modest risk factors; claims blaming sugar, food additives or parenting style are not supported as primary causes.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical, based on history (including from parents and teachers), rating scales and exclusion of mimics; there is no laboratory test. Management combines psychoeducation, behavioural and organisational strategies, school or workplace accommodations, and medication. Stimulants such as methylphenidate and amphetamine salts are the most effective pharmacotherapy; non-stimulants include atomoxetine and extended-release guanfacine. Debate continues over rising diagnosis rates, possible over- and under-diagnosis, and long-term outcomes.
