Six degrees of freedom

Six degrees of freedom (6DOF) refers to the six independent ways a rigid body can move in three-dimensional space: three translational movements and three rotational movements. The concept is central to engineering, robotics, naval architecture, aerospace, and computer graphics and virtual reality.

The six movements

The translational degrees of freedom are movement along the three perpendicular axes, conventionally called, for a vessel or aircraft:

  • Surge — forward and backward along the longitudinal axis;
  • Sway — left and right along the lateral axis;
  • Heave — up and down along the vertical axis.

The rotational degrees of freedom are rotations about those axes:

  • Roll — rotation about the longitudinal axis;
  • Pitch — rotation about the lateral axis;
  • Yaw — rotation about the vertical axis.

Together these six parameters completely describe the position and orientation (the pose) of a rigid body. A body with fewer degrees of freedom is constrained — for example, a train on a track has one, and a car (to a first approximation) has three.

Applications

In robotics, a manipulator arm generally needs six joints to reach an arbitrary pose. In virtual and augmented reality, tracking systems are classed as 3DOF (rotation only) or 6DOF (rotation plus position), with 6DOF enabling users to physically move within a virtual space. Motion platforms for flight simulators and ride systems use six actuators (typically a Stewart platform) to reproduce 6DOF motion, and inertial measurement units combine accelerometers and gyroscopes to estimate all six degrees for navigation.
