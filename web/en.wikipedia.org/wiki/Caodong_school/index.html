The Caodong school (Chinese: 曹洞宗; pinyin: Cáodòng zōng; Wade–Giles: Ts'ao-tung-tsung) is a Chinese Chan Buddhist branch and one of the Five Houses of Chan. It was founded by the Tang-dynasty master Dongshan Liangjie (807–869) and his student Caoshan Benji (840–901). The school's name derives from the first syllables of "Dongshan" (through the association with Mount Dong) and his student Caoshan — conventionally read as combining "Cao" from Caoshan with "Dong" from Dongshan. In Japan, the school was transmitted as Sōtō Zen by the monk Dōgen in the 13th century.

History

Dongshan Liangjie studied under several teachers, including Nanquan Puyuan and Yunyan Tansheng, before settling at Mount Dong (Dongshan) in present-day Jiangxi. There he taught a distinctive style of Chan influenced by the teachings associated with Shitou Xiqian's lineage. Dongshan is credited with the "Five Ranks" (五位), a scheme describing the interplay of absolute and relative truth, preserved in verses attributed to him. His disciple Caoshan Benji systematised the Five Ranks in extensive commentaries at Mount Cao (Caoshan) in modern Jiangxi.

After a period of decline in the late Tang and Five Dynasties period, the school was revived in the Song dynasty by masters such as Furong Daokai and, most famously, Hongzhi Zhengjue (1091–1157), abbot of Tiantong Monastery, who is associated with the practice of "silent illumination" (默照禪, mozhao chan) — a form of meditation emphasising quiet, objectless awareness. Hongzhi's contemporary Zhenxie Qingliao also played a key role in the revival.

Teachings and practice

Caodong teaching emphasises the inherent presence of Buddha-nature and the practice of "silent illumination", in contrast to the koan introspection stressed by the rival Linji school. The Song-dynasty polemic between Hongzhi's silent illumination and Dahui Zonggao's "huatou" practice defined much of later Chan debate. Caodong texts include the Precious Mirror Samadhi (寶鏡三昧), attributed to Dongshan, which expounds the integration of phenomena and principle.

Transmission to Japan and Vietnam

In 1227, the Japanese monk Dōgen returned from China, where he had studied under the Caodong master Tiantong Rujing, and went on to found the Sōtō school in Japan, centred at Eihei-ji. The school was also transmitted to Vietnam, where it merged with local Thiền traditions. Today, Caodong/Sōtō is one of the largest Zen schools in the world.
