Interstellar scintillation

Interstellar scintillation
FieldRadio astronomy, interstellar medium physics
CauseTurbulent ionised interstellar gas refracting radio waves
Affected sourcesPulsars, compact quasars and other microarcsecond radio sources
TypesDiffractive (DISS), refractive (RISS), weak scintillation

Interstellar scintillation is the fluctuation in observed intensity of radio waves from a compact astronomical source caused by irregularities in the ionised interstellar medium. It is analogous to the twinkling of stars produced by Earth's atmosphere, but results from density fluctuations in interstellar plasma along the line of sight. Only sources of extremely small angular size — pulsars, masers and the compact cores of quasars, typically microarcseconds across — scintillate appreciably, because larger sources average over many patches of the interference pattern.

Phenomena

Diffractive interstellar scintillation (DISS) produces rapid, deep variations (minutes to hours) over narrow bandwidths, arising from interference between rays scattered through small angles; in pulsar dynamic spectra it creates characteristic scintillation arcs that encode the geometry and velocity of the scattering material. Refractive interstellar scintillation (RISS) involves larger-scale plasma lenses and produces slower (days to months), broadband modulations. Extreme scattering events — months-long dips and spikes in quasar light curves, seen in sources such as PKS 1939-315 — are attributed to discrete plasma structures crossing the line of sight.

Uses

Scintillation is both a nuisance and a probe. It measures the turbulence spectrum of interstellar plasma (often Kolmogorov-like), locates scattering screens, constrains the sizes and brightness temperatures of quasar cores, and can reveal microarcsecond structure unresolvable by interferometry. Interplanetary scintillation, the corresponding effect in the solar wind, was used by Antony Hewish's group in the 1960s to survey compact radio sources — the work that led Jocelyn Bell Burnell to discover pulsars in 1967 — and is still used to track coronal mass ejections.
