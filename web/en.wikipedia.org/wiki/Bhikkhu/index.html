A bhikkhu (Pali: भिक्खु, Sanskrit: भिक्षु, bhikṣu) is an ordained male monastic in Buddhism. The word literally means "beggar" or "one who lives by alms". A fully ordained bhikkhu lives according to the Vinaya, the monastic code: in the Theravāda tradition, the Pātimokkha contains 227 rules for bhikkhus, governing everything from celibacy and non-violence to the handling of money and the times at which food may be eaten.

Life and ordination

A man typically first ordains as a novice (sāmaṇera), observing ten precepts, and may receive full ordination (upasampadā) at the age of twenty or older before a quorum of monks. Bhikkhus depend on the lay community for their material support — food, robes, shelter, and medicine — and in return provide teaching and a field of merit. The daily alms round (piṇḍapāta) remains a visible feature of life in Theravāda countries such as Thailand, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, Cambodia, and Laos.

Female monastics

The female counterpart is the bhikkhunī (Sanskrit: bhikṣuṇī). The bhikkhunī lineage, founded in the Buddha's lifetime with his stepmother Mahāpajāpatī, died out in the Theravāda world in the late first millennium but has been revived in recent decades, a development that remains debated.
