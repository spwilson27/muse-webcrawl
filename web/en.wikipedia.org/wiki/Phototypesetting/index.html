Phototypesetting

Phototypesetting is a method of setting type that uses photography to make columns of type on photographic paper or film. It replaced hot-metal typesetting (such as the Linotype machine) from the 1960s onward and was itself displaced by desktop publishing in the 1980s and 1990s.

A phototypesetting machine projecting characters onto photographic paper.

Operation

In a phototypesetting machine, characters are stored as images on a film strip, disc, grid or drum. A keyboard operator enters the text, and the machine projects light through the character images, one at a time and at the required size, onto photographic paper or film, which is then developed and used to make printing plates by offset lithography. Because size is set optically by a lens, one master could produce many point sizes, a flexibility hot metal could not match.

Generations

First-generation machines were mechanical adaptations of hot-metal systems; the Photon, developed from work by Louis Moyroud and René Higonnet, was an early success. Second-generation machines were purpose-built and faster. Third-generation machines used cathode-ray tubes to image characters from digital data, storing fonts as bitmaps or outlines, and led naturally into fully digital typesetting and imagesetters.

Impact

Phototypesetting was much faster and cheaper than casting metal type, allowed tighter letterspacing and easy mixing of sizes, and coincided with the rise of offset printing in newspapers and book production. Its output was typically pasted up by hand ("cold type") before page make-up. The arrival of the Macintosh, PostScript and desktop publishing software ended the dedicated phototypesetter era within a decade.
