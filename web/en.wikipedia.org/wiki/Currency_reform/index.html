Currency reform

Currency reform is the process of replacing or fundamentally restructuring a country's currency. It may involve introducing a new unit of account, redenominating existing notes (for example, removing zeros), replacing a discredited currency after hyperinflation or war, or joining a currency union. Currency reform is usually accompanied by fiscal and monetary measures intended to make the new currency credible.

Reasons for reform

  • To end hyperinflation and restore confidence in money.
  • To simplify accounting after inflation has made denominations unwieldy.
  • To sever links with a previous regime or occupying power.
  • To adopt a shared currency, as in the creation of the euro.
  • To combat counterfeiting or modernise note designs and security features.

Notable examples

The German currency reform of 1948 replaced the Reichsmark with the Deutsche Mark in the western occupation zones, exchanging old money at a sharply reduced rate and helping to launch the West German economic recovery. Brazil's Plano Real (1994) used a transitional unit of account, the URV, to break inflationary expectations before introducing the real. Turkey in 2009 completed a redenomination that removed six zeros from the lira. France's 1960 reform introduced the "new franc" worth 100 old francs.

Redenomination

The simplest form of currency reform is redenomination: a new unit is exchanged for a round number of old units without changing purchasing power. Although purely cosmetic in economic terms, it can signal a break with an inflationary past and make prices and accounting easier to handle.
