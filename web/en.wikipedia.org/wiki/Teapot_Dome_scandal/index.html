Teapot Dome scandal

[Image: Teapot Rock, Wyoming, near the reserve that gave the scandal its name]

The Teapot Dome scandal was a bribery scandal involving the administration of US President Warren G. Harding in the early 1920s. Secretary of the Interior Albert Bacon Fall had leased Navy petroleum reserves at Teapot Dome in Wyoming and two other fields in California to private oil companies at low rates without competitive bidding, after accepting bribes from oilmen Harry F. Sinclair and Edward L. Doheny.

Exposure and trials

The leases, negotiated in secret in 1921–1922, came to light through reporting by the Wall Street Journal and Denver Post and a Senate investigation led by Senator Thomas J. Walsh of Montana. The Supreme Court voided the leases in 1927 as obtained by fraud. In 1929 Fall was convicted of accepting bribes — becoming the first US cabinet official to go to prison for crimes committed in office — while Sinclair and Doheny were acquitted of giving them; Sinclair served time for contempt and jury tampering.

Significance

Teapot Dome became synonymous with government corruption and remains a landmark in the law of congressional investigations: the Supreme Court's decision in McGrain v. Daugherty (1927) affirmed Congress's power to compel testimony. The scandal shadowed Harding's reputation, though he died in 1923 before its full extent emerged.
