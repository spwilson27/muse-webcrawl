Vote of no confidence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A vote of no confidence, also known as a motion of no confidence or censure, is a parliamentary vote showing that a government (or officeholder) no longer commands the confidence of the legislature and must therefore resign or call an election. It is the definitive accountability mechanism of parliamentary systems, distinguishing them from presidential ones where the executive survives for a fixed term.

Variants

Systems calibrate the weapon's strength: Germany's Basic Law requires a constructive vote — the Bundestag must elect a successor by absolute majority in the same vote (used successfully in 1982 against Schmidt, installing Kohl; failed in 1972). Spain follows the same model. Others allow simple-majority dismissal (Italy, Israel in its Knesset form), contributing historically to cabinet instability. Governments may also designate votes as confidence questions, staking their survival on a bill (a favourite executive lever in France's Fifth Republic, Article 49.3, which passes bills unless censure succeeds — its use for pension reform in 2023 provoked crisis debates). Famous UK instances: the 1979 defeat of Callaghan's Labour government by one vote, triggering an election the Conservatives won; and repeated survival votes during the Brexit parliament.
