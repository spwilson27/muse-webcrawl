In statistical classification and machine learning, a generative model is a model of the joint probability distribution P(X, Y) over observations X and labels Y — or, for unsupervised learning, of P(X) alone. Because it captures how the data are generated, a generative model can be used to generate new synthetic examples by sampling, as well as to classify: the class-conditional densities P(X|Y) can be combined with class priors via Bayes' rule to obtain the posterior P(Y|X).

Generative versus discriminative

A discriminative model instead learns the conditional distribution P(Y|X) (or a direct decision boundary) directly, without modelling how X is distributed. Classical generative classifiers include naive Bayes and linear discriminant analysis; logistic regression and support vector machines are discriminative counterparts. With limited training data a generative classifier can approach its (higher) asymptotic error faster, while a discriminative model typically achieves lower error with abundant data — a trade-off analysed in the well-known comparison of naive Bayes and logistic regression by Ng and Jordan.

Deep generative models

Since the 2010s, deep neural networks have produced powerful generative models of images, text, audio and other data: variational autoencoders (VAEs), generative adversarial networks (GANs), autoregressive models such as PixelRNN and the transformer-based large language models, normalizing flows, and diffusion models. These systems underpin modern generative artificial intelligence applications including text-to-image generation and chatbots.

Other uses of the term

In Bayesian statistics "generative model" also denotes a full probabilistic specification — priors plus likelihood — from which synthetic data can be simulated, used in techniques such as posterior predictive checking and approximate Bayesian computation.
