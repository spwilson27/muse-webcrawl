Erythema infectiosum

Other names: Fifth disease, slapped cheek syndrome
Symptoms: Low-grade fever, bright red cheeks, lacy rash on body and limbs
Cause: Parvovirus B19
Usual onset: 4–14 days after exposure
Duration: 3–10 days, rash may recur for weeks
Treatment: Supportive only

Erythema infectiosum, commonly known as fifth disease or slapped cheek syndrome, is a mild childhood illness caused by infection with parvovirus B19. It is called "fifth disease" because it was historically the fifth in a classification of common childhood skin rashes (exanthems), after measles, scarlet fever, rubella, and Dukes' disease.

Signs and symptoms

The illness usually begins with a low-grade fever, headache, and mild cold-like symptoms. A few days later a distinctive bright red rash appears on the cheeks — the "slapped cheek" appearance — followed by a lacy, reticular rash on the trunk and limbs that may come and go for several weeks. In adults, joint pain and swelling (especially in the hands, wrists, knees, and ankles) are often more prominent than the rash.

Cause and transmission

The disease is caused by human parvovirus B19, a small single-stranded DNA virus discovered in 1975. It spreads mainly through respiratory secretions and from mother to fetus. A person is most contagious before the rash appears; once the rash develops, they are generally no longer infectious and need not be excluded from school or work.

Complications

In most healthy children the disease is mild and self-limiting. However, parvovirus B19 temporarily halts red blood cell production, which can cause a severe aplastic crisis in people with chronic haemolytic anaemias such as sickle cell disease. In pregnant women, infection can occasionally lead to fetal anaemia and hydrops fetalis. People with weakened immune systems may develop chronic anaemia.

Treatment

There is no specific antiviral treatment and no vaccine. Management is supportive: rest, fluids, and analgesics for fever or joint pain. Intravenous immunoglobulin may be used for chronic infection in immunocompromised patients.
