Powell v. McCormack

Powell v. McCormack

Court: Supreme Court of the United States

Decided: June 16, 1969

Citation: 395 U.S. 486

Chief Justice: Earl Warren

Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), is a landmark United States Supreme Court case in which the Court held that the U.S. House of Representatives could not exclude a duly elected member who met the qualifications for office specified in the United States Constitution.

Background

Adam Clayton Powell Jr., a congressman from Harlem, New York, was re-elected in 1966 amid allegations of misconduct, including misuse of committee funds and contempt of New York courts. In March 1967, the House voted to exclude him from the 90th Congress rather than seat him. Powell sued John William McCormack, the Speaker of the House, and other House officers, seeking a declaration that the exclusion was unconstitutional, along with back pay and an injunction.

Decision

In an 8–1 decision written by Chief Justice Earl Warren, the Court held that the House's power under the Qualifications Clause of Article I, Section 5 — to be the judge of the "Elections, Returns and Qualifications of its own Members" — is limited to the standing qualifications set out in Article I, Section 2: age, citizenship, and inhabitancy. Because Powell met those qualifications, the House could not exclude him; it could, however, expel a seated member by a two-thirds vote. The Court rejected the argument that the case presented a nonjusticiable political question.

Aftermath

Powell was seated in the 91st Congress in January 1969 after the House fined him and stripped his seniority, and he received back pay in light of the Court's ruling. The case remains a leading authority on the limits of Congress's power over its own membership.
