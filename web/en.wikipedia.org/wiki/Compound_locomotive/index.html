Compound locomotive

A compound locomotive is a steam locomotive in which the steam is expanded more than once: first in a high-pressure cylinder, and then, after giving up part of its energy, in one or more larger low-pressure cylinders before being exhausted. Compounding increases thermal efficiency and reduces fuel and water consumption compared with a simple-expansion locomotive of similar size, though at the cost of greater mechanical complexity.

History

The idea of compounding was applied to stationary and marine steam engines early in the nineteenth century. For locomotives, the Swiss engineer Anatole Mallet patented a compound system in the 1870s, in which the engine could be started in simple mode. Other notable systems were developed by von Borries, de Glehn, Vauclain, and Webb. Compound locomotives were widely adopted in continental Europe, especially France and Germany, and were also used in the United States and elsewhere, though British practice largely favoured simple expansion.

Configurations

Compound locomotives may have two, three, or four cylinders. In the Mallet type, the locomotive is articulated with two sets of driving wheels, the rear set driven by high-pressure cylinders and the front set by low-pressure cylinders. Three-cylinder and four-cylinder compounds with a single set of drivers were common in Europe. A receiver between the high- and low-pressure cylinders stores the exhaust steam from the high-pressure side.

Advantages and disadvantages

Compounding reduces the temperature range in each cylinder, limiting condensation losses, and yields a more even turning moment. Disadvantages include higher first cost, more complicated starting arrangements, and increased maintenance. The spread of superheating in the early twentieth century reduced the efficiency advantage of compounding.
