Paul Bach-y-Rita (4 April 1934 – 7 November 2006) was an American neuroscientist at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, regarded as a founder of the field of sensory substitution and one of the earliest champions of the modern idea of neuroplasticity — the brain's capacity to reorganise itself throughout life.

Sensory substitution

In the late 1960s Bach-y-Rita built the first tactile vision substitution system (TVSS): a camera whose image was converted into a pattern of vibrating or electrical points on a blind person's back or abdomen. With training, users learned to perceive objects in space from these patterns, reporting that the sensation seemed to come from the world in front of them rather than from their skin. His dictum summarised the principle: "We see with the brain, not with the eyes."

Later work

Later devices moved stimulation to the tongue, whose dense innervation and moist environment made it an ideal display surface; the BrainPort device applied this to balance disorders and vision. Bach-y-Rita's own recovery experiments — including work inspired by his father's recovery from a severe stroke — convinced him that intensive, appropriately designed rehabilitation could restore function long after injury, a view initially resisted but later widely accepted.
