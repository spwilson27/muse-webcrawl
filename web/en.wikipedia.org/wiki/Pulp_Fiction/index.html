Pulp Fiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Jules and Vincent in suits with guns drawn, in the cool tableau that became the film's poster and the nineties' defining image]

Pulp Fiction is a 1994 American crime film written and directed by Quentin Tarantino, whose interlocking, non-chronological stories of Los Angeles criminals — two philosophical hitmen, their boss and his wife, a boxer paid to lose — revived American independent cinema and won the Palme d'Or at Cannes and the Academy Award for Best Original Screenplay.

John Travolta's career-reviving Vincent Vega, Samuel L. Jackson's Jules Winnfield, Uma Thurman's Mia Wallace, Bruce Willis's Butch: the casting alone reordered Hollywood. The film's dialogue — long, digressive, pop-cultural — its surf-rock and soul soundtrack, its chaptered circular structure (the film ends where it begins, in the diner) made it the most imitated American film of its decade.

Making and impact

Tarantino, a video-store clerk turned screenwriter (Reservoir Dogs, 1992), wrote the script with Roger Avary. Made for about $8.5 million, it grossed over $200 million worldwide and proved Miramax's model of festival heat plus controversy marketing. Its violence — stylised, off-screen as often as on — and its talk re-ignited the perennial argument about cinema's morals while every young filmmaker in the world took notes.

Elements

The briefcase whose contents glow and are never explained (Tarantino has called it a MacGuffin and refused more), the dance contest at Jack Rabbit Slim's, the adrenaline shot, the gold watch monologue: the film is a necklace of set pieces whose order is its meaning — redemption (Jules's) placed before damnation (Vincent's) by the circular cut. It sits in the National Film Registry and every nineties canon.
