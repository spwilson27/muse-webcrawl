Duality (optimization)

In mathematical optimization theory, duality or the duality principle is the principle that optimization problems may be viewed from either of two perspectives, the primal problem or the dual problem. If the primal is a minimization problem then the dual is a maximization problem, and vice versa. Any feasible solution to the primal problem gives a bound on the optimal value of the dual problem, and any feasible solution to the dual problem gives a bound on the optimal value of the primal; this is known as weak duality.

Primal and dual problems

For a minimization problem in standard form, the dual problem is obtained by forming the Lagrangian function: each constraint is multiplied by a non-negative dual variable (Lagrange multiplier) and added to the objective. The dual function, defined as the infimum of the Lagrangian over the primal variables, is always concave, so the dual problem — maximizing the dual function — is a convex optimization problem even when the primal is not. Under suitable conditions, such as Slater's condition for convex problems, strong duality holds: the optimal values of the primal and dual problems are equal, and the difference between them (the duality gap) is zero.

Linear programming duality

The best-known case is linear programming, where the dual of a linear program is another linear program whose variables correspond to the primal constraints. The duality theorem of linear programming states that if either problem has an optimal solution, so does the other, and their objective values coincide. Dual variables in linear programming have an economic interpretation as shadow prices: the marginal value of relaxing a resource constraint. The complementary slackness conditions characterize optimal solutions of the pair jointly.

Applications

Duality is used throughout optimization and beyond: to derive algorithms (primal-dual methods), to prove optimality conditions such as the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions, to construct lower bounds in integer programming and combinatorial optimization, and in machine learning, where the dual formulation of the support vector machine leads to the kernel trick. Duality also appears in economics (prices as dual variables), game theory (the minimax theorem), and physics.
