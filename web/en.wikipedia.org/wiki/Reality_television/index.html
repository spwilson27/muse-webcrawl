Reality television

Reality television is a genre of television programming that documents purportedly unscripted real-life situations, often starring ordinary people rather than professional actors. Reality television emerged as a distinct genre in the early 1990s with shows such as The Real World, then achieved its greatest prominence during the 2000s with the success of series such as Survivor, Big Brother, The Amazing Race, American Idol, America's Next Top Model, The Apprentice, Dancing with the Stars and The Real Housewives franchise.

Although the genre has existed in some form since the early years of television, the current explosion of popularity is generally dated to around 2000. Reality television shows tend to be interspersed with "confessionals", short interview segments in which cast members reflect on the events shown. Competition-based reality shows typically feature the gradual elimination of participants, either by a panel of judges, by the viewership of the show, or by the contestants themselves.

History

Precursors

Television formats portraying ordinary people in unscripted situations are almost as old as the medium itself. Allen Funt's Candid Camera, in which bewildered bystanders were subjected to practical jokes, debuted in 1948. The 1973 PBS series An American Family, which followed the Loud family of Santa Barbara, California — including the on-camera breakup of the parents' marriage — is often cited as a foundational work of the genre. The police documentary series Cops, premiered in 1989, became an early and durable hit of unscripted programming.

1990s

The Real World (1992), created for MTV by Mary-Ellis Bunim and Jonathan Murray, placed seven strangers in a shared house and filmed their interactions; its famous opening narration — "the true story of seven strangers, picked to live in a house, work together and have their lives taped, to find out what happens when people stop being polite and start getting real" — became a template for the genre. The Dutch series Nummer 28 (1991) and the British programme Sylvania Waters (1992) were also influential.

2000s boom

The year 2000 saw the premiere of Survivor on CBS and of Big Brother internationally; both were massive ratings successes. Talent competitions such as Pop Idol (United Kingdom, 2001) and its American adaptation American Idol (2002) followed, alongside The Osbournes (2002), The Simple Life (2003) and Keeping Up with the Kardashians (2007), which made celebrities of their subjects. Lower production costs compared with scripted drama made the format especially attractive to networks.

Subgenres and formats

  • Documentary style ("docusoaps") — following people in their professional or private lives, e.g. Deadliest Catch, Ice Road Truckers.
  • Reality competition / game shows — contestants are eliminated over a season, e.g. Survivor, The Amazing Race, Top Chef, RuPaul's Drag Race.
  • Talent searches — e.g. American Idol, The Voice, Got Talent franchises.
  • Dating and relationship shows — e.g. The Bachelor, Love Island, Married at First Sight.
  • Makeover and lifestyle — e.g. Queer Eye, Extreme Makeover: Home Edition, home-renovation formats.
  • Hidden camera and social experiments — descendants of Candid Camera.
  • Celebrity reality — programmes built around already-famous participants.

Criticism

The genre is frequently criticised for heavy editing that manufactures storylines ("Frankenbiting" of dialogue), for placing participants in artificial or humiliating situations, for blurring the line between documentation and scripting, and for the psychological welfare of participants, some of whom have reported lasting harm. Writers have also noted that reality shows historically employed non-union labour and bypassed screenwriters, a point of contention during the 2007–08 Writers Guild of America strike.
