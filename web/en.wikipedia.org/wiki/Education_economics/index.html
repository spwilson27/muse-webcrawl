Economics of education

The economics of education or education economics is the study of economic issues relating to education, including the demand for education, the financing and provision of education, and the comparative efficiency of various educational programs and policies. From early works on the relationship between schooling and labor market outcomes for individuals, the field of the economics of education has grown rapidly to cover virtually all areas with linkages to education.

Average earnings typically rise with educational attainment, a central relationship studied in the economics of education.

Education as an investment

A dominant feature in the economics of education is the concept of human capital, popularized by Gary Becker and Theodore Schultz in the 1960s. Education is treated as an investment: individuals and societies incur costs (tuition, forgone earnings, public expenditure) in expectation of future benefits such as higher wages, greater productivity, better health, and lower crime. The earnings gains from schooling are commonly summarized by the Mincerian earnings function, developed by Jacob Mincer, which relates log earnings to years of schooling and experience. Empirical estimates typically find that each additional year of schooling raises earnings by roughly 5–15 percent, varying by country, level, and field of study.

Signaling versus human capital

A major debate concerns whether education raises earnings primarily by increasing skills (the human capital view) or by signaling pre-existing ability to employers, as proposed by Michael Spence. "Sheepskin effects" — discontinuous wage gains at credential completion — and the returns to GED certification are cited as evidence for signaling, while direct measures of skills and employer learning suggest human capital formation is the larger component.

Financing and provision

Because education generates positive externalities and credit markets for human capital are imperfect, most countries provide education publicly or subsidize it heavily. Research topics include school finance equalization, voucher programs and school choice, class size effects (notably the Tennessee STAR experiment), teacher quality and value-added measurement, the effects of education technology, and the economics of early childhood intervention, where programs such as the Perry Preschool Project have shown high rates of return in the work of James Heckman.

Education and growth

At the macroeconomic level, cross-country studies associate higher average schooling and measured cognitive skills with faster economic growth, though causality is debated. Education economics also studies peer effects, the effects of school accountability systems, and the distribution of educational opportunity by income, race, and gender.
