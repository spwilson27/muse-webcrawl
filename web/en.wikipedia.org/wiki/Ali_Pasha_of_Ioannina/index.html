Ali Pasha of Ioannina (1740 – 24 January 1822), commonly known as Ali Pasha of Yanina, was an Ottoman Albanian ruler who served as the de facto independent ruler of the Pashalik of Yanina in western Rumelia from 1788 to 1822. His court in Ioannina became a centre of power that rivalled the Sublime Porte itself, and he is remembered both for his administrative energy and for the cruelty with which he suppressed resistance.

Early life

Ali was born in Tepelena in southern Albania, the son of Veli Bey, a local clan leader. After his father's death the family lost much of its power, and the young Ali spent years as a brigand and mercenary before entering Ottoman service. Through a combination of ability, ruthlessness and well-timed marriages he steadily accumulated offices, becoming mutasarrıf of Trikala and then, in 1788, pasha of Ioannina.

Rule

From Ioannina, Ali extended his authority over most of mainland Greece, Albania and parts of Macedonia, ruling a territory of perhaps 1.5 million people. He built roads, aqueducts and fortifications, encouraged trade, and corresponded with Napoleon Bonaparte and the British government, playing the European powers against each other and against the Sultan. Travellers such as Lord Byron visited his court; Byron described him as a man of fierce energy and absolute power.

Ali's rule was also marked by notorious acts of violence, including the drowning of Greek women of Ioannina in Lake Pamvotida and his long, destructive campaigns against the Souliotes, whose strongholds he finally captured in 1803.

Downfall and death

In 1820 Sultan Mahmud II, alarmed at Ali's independence and his intrigues with the Greek secret society the Filiki Eteria, declared him a rebel and sent an army under Hurshid Pasha against him. Ali held out in Ioannina for nearly two years, and his revolt usefully distracted Ottoman forces during the opening of the Greek War of Independence. Betrayed by promises of a pardon, he was shot on 24 January 1822 in the monastery of St. Panteleimon on the island in Lake Pamvotida, where he had taken refuge. His head was sent to Constantinople.

Legacy

Ali Pasha remains a vivid figure in Greek, Albanian and Turkish memory and in European literature: he appears in The Count of Monte Cristo, and his fortress and mosque still dominate the castle of Ioannina. Historians debate whether he was a modernising state-builder or merely a brilliant tyrant; most conclude he was both.
