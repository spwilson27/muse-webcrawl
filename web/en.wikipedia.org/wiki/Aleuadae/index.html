The Aleuadae (Ancient Greek: Ἀλευάδαι) were an ancient Greek aristocratic family of Larissa in Thessaly, who traced their descent from Aleuas, a legendary descendant of Heracles. For centuries they were the most powerful family in Thessaly and leading figures of its aristocratic oligarchies.

History

The Aleuadae rose to prominence in the 6th century BC. Aleuas, surnamed "the Red," was credited in tradition with organizing the Thessalian League's military system of four tetrads (Thessaliotis, Hestiaeotis, Pelasgiotis and Phthiotis), each supplying contingents of cavalry and light troops. The family intermarried with other Thessalian houses, notably the Scopadae of Crannon, and dominated Larissa, one of the principal cities of the Thessalian plain.

During the Greco-Persian Wars, leading Aleuadae — including the brothers Thorax, Eurypylus and Thrasydaeus — were prominent "Medizers" who invited Xerxes to invade Greece and supported the Persian cause, hoping to extend their rule over all Thessaly. After the Persian defeat, the family recovered its position and continued to contend for leadership of the Thessalian League in the 5th and 4th centuries BC.

Later period

In the 360s BC, members of the family, facing the tyranny of Alexander of Pherae, appealed to Philip II of Macedon for intervention, an important step in Macedon's involvement in Thessalian and Greek affairs. The Aleuadae still appear in the Hellenistic period: Simus of Larissa, an Aleuad, was a leading statesman and contemporary of Philip V, maintaining close ties with the Macedonian court.

Cultural associations

The Aleuadae are remembered in lyric poetry: Simonides was associated with the Thessalian aristocracy, and Pindar's Pythian Ode 10 — his earliest surviving ode, composed in 498 BC — was written for Hippocleas of Thessaly and celebrates the patronage of Thorax and the Aleuadae.
