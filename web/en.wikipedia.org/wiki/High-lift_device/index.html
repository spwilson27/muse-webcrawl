High-lift device

In aircraft design, high-lift devices are components or mechanisms on an aircraft's wing that increase the amount of lift produced. They allow a wing optimised for efficient high-speed cruise flight to also generate enough lift at the low speeds required for take-off and landing.

Types

The most common high-lift devices are flaps on the trailing edge — plain, split, slotted and Fowler flaps — which increase wing camber and, in the Fowler design, wing area. Slats on the leading edge open a slot that re-energises the boundary layer and allows a higher angle of attack before stalling. Other devices include Krueger flaps, leading-edge droops and cuffs, vortex generators, and (historically) blown flaps using engine bleed air. Deploying these devices raises the maximum lift coefficient from roughly 1.6 for a clean wing to 3 or more, at the cost of greatly increased drag — which is itself useful on approach.

Operation

On airliners, flaps and slats are extended in stages during approach according to a flap schedule, and retracted after take-off as the aircraft accelerates. Asymmetric deployment is a serious failure mode guarded against by interconnect shafts and asymmetry detection systems.
