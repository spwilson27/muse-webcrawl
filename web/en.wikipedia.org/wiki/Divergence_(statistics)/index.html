Divergence (statistics)

A divergence measures how one probability distribution differs from another.

In information geometry and statistics, a divergence is a function which establishes the "distance" of one probability distribution to another on a statistical manifold. It is a weaker notion than a distance metric: divergences need not be symmetric and do not need to satisfy the triangle inequality. Nevertheless, they are widely used to quantify how dissimilar two distributions are.

Examples

The most famous divergence is the Kullback–Leibler divergence, which measures the expected excess surprise from using one distribution to approximate another and plays a central role in information theory and machine learning. Other examples include the total variation distance, Hellinger distance, Jensen–Shannon divergence, chi-squared divergence, and the family of f-divergences, of which several of these are special cases. The Bregman divergences form another important family, defined for convex functions over distributions or points in space.

Properties and uses

Divergences are always non-negative, and are zero exactly when the two distributions are equal (for most standard divergences). They appear in hypothesis testing, variational inference, model selection and information geometry, where the Fisher information metric can be derived from the local behaviour of a divergence. Because of their asymmetry, the direction of a divergence matters in applications such as variational Bayes, where minimising one direction produces different behaviour from the other.
