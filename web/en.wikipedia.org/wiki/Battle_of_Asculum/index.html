Battle of Asculum

The Battle of Asculum was fought in 279 BC between the Roman Republic and the forces of King Pyrrhus of Epirus, who had come to Italy to aid the Greek city of Tarentum. Though Pyrrhus won the battle, the heavy losses among his irreplaceable veteran troops gave rise to the expression "Pyrrhic victory".

Background

After his costly success at Heraclea in 280 BC, Pyrrhus advanced into Apulia and met a Roman army under the consuls Publius Decius Mus and Publius Sulpicius Saverrio near Asculum. The Romans had prepared anti-elephant wagons fitted with spikes, grappling hooks and pots of flaming material to counter Pyrrhus's war elephants.

The battle

Fighting lasted two days. On the first day, rough, wooded terrain along the river hampered Pyrrhus's cavalry and elephants, and the battle ended inconclusively. On the second day Pyrrhus seized the rough ground at dawn, forcing the Romans to fight in the open where his phalanx and elephants prevailed; the anti-elephant wagons were neutralised by light troops. Roman losses were about 6,000; Pyrrhus lost some 3,500 men, including many officers and veteran soldiers he could not replace in Italy. Plutarch reports his remark that one more such victory would ruin him.

Aftermath

Unable to force Rome to terms and overstretched in Italy, Pyrrhus accepted an invitation to intervene in Sicily against Carthage. He returned to Italy in 275 BC, was beaten at Beneventum, and withdrew to Greece, leaving Tarentum to fall to Rome in 272 BC.
