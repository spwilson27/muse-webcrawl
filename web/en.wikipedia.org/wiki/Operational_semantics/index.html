Operational semantics

Operational semantics is a category of formal programming language semantics in which certain desired properties of a program, such as being type safe, are verified by providing proofs based on the logical rules of the semantics. It is contrasted with denotational semantics, which maps programs to mathematical objects, and axiomatic semantics, which defines meaning through logical assertions about program states.

Approaches

Operational semantics comes in two main varieties. Structural operational semantics (also called small-step semantics), introduced by Gordon Plotkin in 1981, describes how the individual steps of a computation take place, expressing execution as a sequence of single transitions between configurations of an abstract machine. Natural semantics (also called big-step semantics), introduced by Gilles Kahn, relates the initial configuration directly to the final result of the computation, without describing the intermediate steps.

Transition systems

An operational semantics is typically given by a set of inference rules defining a transition relation. In small-step form, a judgment such as <e, σ> → <e′, σ′> states that expression e in state σ reduces in one step to e′ in state σ′. Evaluation proceeds by repeatedly applying the rules until a value is reached.

Applications

Operational semantics underlies the formal definitions of many real languages — for example, the Standard ML definition adopted a related style — and is widely used in research on type systems, program verification, and compiler correctness, because its rule format is well suited to inductive proofs.
