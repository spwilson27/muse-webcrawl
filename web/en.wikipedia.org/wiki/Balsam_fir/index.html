The balsam fir (Abies balsamea) is a North American fir, native to most of eastern and central Canada (from Newfoundland west to Alberta) and the northeastern United States (from Minnesota east to Maine, and south in the Appalachian Mountains to West Virginia). It is a medium-sized evergreen tree, typically 14–20 metres tall (exceptionally to 27 metres), with a narrow conical crown.

Description

The bark on young trees is smooth, grey, and marked by resin blisters — the source of Canada balsam, a resin once widely used as a mounting medium in microscopy and as a cement for optical lenses. The needles are flat, 15–30 mm long, dark green above with two white stomatal bands below, and strongly aromatic when crushed. The seed cones stand upright on the branches, 40–80 mm long, maturing from dark purple to brown in a single season and disintegrating on the tree to release winged seeds.

Ecology

Balsam fir is a characteristic tree of the boreal forest and cool, moist lowlands, growing in pure stands or with spruce, paper birch, and aspen. It is shade tolerant but relatively short-lived and shallow-rooted, hence vulnerable to windthrow. It is the primary host of the spruce budworm, whose periodic outbreaks kill fir over vast areas; moose browse it heavily in winter, and its dense thickets shelter wildlife such as the Canada lynx and snowshoe hare.

Uses

  • A major pulpwood species for paper, and lumber for light construction.
  • One of the most popular Christmas trees for its fragrance, dark green colour, and good needle retention.
  • Canada balsam resin from the bark blisters; folk medicinal uses of the oleoresin.
  • Essential oil distilled from the needles, used in fragrances and air fresheners.

Balsam fir is the provincial tree of New Brunswick. Its close relative the Fraser fir (Abies fraseri) of the southern Appalachians is sometimes treated as a subspecies, A. balsamea subsp. fraseri.
