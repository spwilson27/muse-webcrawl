Non-functional requirement

In systems and software engineering, a non-functional requirement (NFR) is a requirement that specifies criteria that can be used to judge the operation of a system, rather than specific behaviours. They are contrasted with functional requirements, which define specific behaviour or functions. Non-functional requirements are sometimes informally called the "ilities" of a system, because many end in -ility: usability, availability, reliability, scalability, maintainability, and so on.

Examples

  • Performance — "The system shall respond to 95% of queries within 200 milliseconds under peak load."
  • Availability — "The service shall be available 99.95% of the time per calendar month."
  • Security — "All stored passwords shall be hashed with a salted, computationally expensive algorithm."
  • Usability — "A new user shall be able to complete checkout without training in under five minutes."
  • Scalability — "The system shall support 100,000 concurrent users without redesign."
  • Portability, compliance, auditability — constraints imposed by platforms, standards, or law.

Role in architecture

Non-functional requirements are often the principal drivers of software architecture: the choice of technologies and structures is usually dictated more by performance, security, and reliability targets than by features. Because they cross-cut the whole system, NFRs are sometimes called cross-cutting concerns or quality attributes. Well-formed NFRs should be measurable and testable; vague statements such as "the system shall be fast" cannot be verified and are a common source of project failure. Frameworks such as ISO/IEC 25010 (SQuaRE) provide standard quality models for classifying them.
