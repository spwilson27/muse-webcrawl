Charge conservation

In physics, charge conservation is the principle that the total electric charge in an isolated system never changes. The net quantity of electric charge — the amount of positive charge minus the amount of negative charge — is constant in the universe. Charge can be transferred between bodies, and particles of matter and antimatter can be created or annihilated, but only in pairs whose net charge is zero.

Continuity equation

Charge conservation is expressed locally by the continuity equation, which states that the rate of change of charge density ρ in a volume equals the net current flowing out: ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·J = 0, where J is the current density. This equation is implied by Maxwell's equations, and in turn charge conservation is required for their consistency with the Lorenz gauge condition.

Theoretical basis

By Noether's theorem, conservation of charge follows from the global U(1) gauge symmetry of electromagnetism: the laws of physics are unchanged by a uniform shift of the phase of charged particle wavefunctions. In the Standard Model, charge conservation is exact; proposed extensions such as grand unified theories predict proton decay, which would still conserve charge even while violating baryon number.

Experimental evidence

No violation of charge conservation has ever been observed. Processes such as beta decay conserve charge exactly (a neutron decays to a proton, electron and antineutrino: 0 = +1 − 1 + 0). Stringent limits come from searches for charge-non-conserving decays of the electron, which place its lifetime against such decays above roughly 1028 years.

Circuit analogue

In electrical engineering, the same principle underlies Kirchhoff's current law: the sum of currents entering a node equals the sum leaving it, because charge does not accumulate at the node in steady state.
