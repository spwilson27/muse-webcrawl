Biologist

A biologist is a scientist who studies living organisms and their relationship to their environment. Biologists work across the whole scale of life, from molecules and cells to organisms, populations, and ecosystems, and in many specialised sub-disciplines such as microbiology, botany, zoology, genetics, ecology, marine biology, and evolutionary biology.

Work

Research biologists design and conduct experiments, carry out field surveys, collect and analyse data, and publish findings in scientific journals. Many work in universities and public research institutes; others are employed in biotechnology, pharmaceuticals, agriculture, conservation, environmental consulting, healthcare, and science communication. Field biologists may spend long periods observing organisms in their natural habitats, while laboratory-based biologists use techniques such as microscopy, DNA sequencing, and computational modelling.

Training

Professional biologists usually hold at least a bachelor's degree in biology or a related life-science subject; research positions typically require a master's degree or doctorate. Some branches, such as clinical and medical laboratory science, are regulated or certified by professional bodies. The term "biologist" came into common use in the 19th century, as "biology" itself was coined around 1800 by scientists including Gottfried Reinhold Treviranus and Jean-Baptiste Lamarck.

History

Although the systematic study of life dates to naturalists such as Aristotle, the professional biologist emerged with 19th-century advances — cell theory, Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection, and experimental physiology — and expanded enormously with the molecular biology revolution of the 20th century.
