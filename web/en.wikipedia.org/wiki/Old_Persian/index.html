Old Persian is one of the two directly attested Old Iranian languages (the other being Avestan) and is known from inscriptions of the Achaemenid Empire, dating roughly from the late 6th to the 4th century BC. It was the vernacular of the imperial court in Persis (Fārs) and the ancestor of Middle Persian and, ultimately, of modern Persian (Farsi), though the administration of the empire mostly used Imperial Aramaic and Elamite.

Inscriptions and decipherment

The most famous text is the trilingual Behistun Inscription of Darius I, which recounts his accession and victories, with parallel versions in Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian. Copies of the Behistun text made by Henry Creswicke Rawlinson in the 1830s–40s were the key to deciphering cuneiform. Other major texts include the inscriptions of Cyrus at Pasargadae, of Darius and Xerxes at Persepolis, Naqsh-e Rostam and Suez, and royal foundation charters on stone, metal tablets and clay.

The script and language

Old Persian cuneiform is a semi-alphabetic script of 36 signs — three vowels, and consonant signs mostly representing a consonant plus inherent /a/ — plus logograms for words such as "king" and "god", and a word divider. It was probably created by order of Darius for monumental use. The language itself is inflected, with three genders, three numbers and up to seven cases, and a verb system already somewhat simplified compared with Avestan or Sanskrit. Characteristic formulas open the royal texts: "A great god is Ahura Mazda, who created this earth, who created yonder sky, who created man, who created happiness for man, who made Darius king."
