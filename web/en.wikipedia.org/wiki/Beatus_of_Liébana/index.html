Beatus of Liébana (Spanish: Beato de Liébana; c. 730 – c. 800) was a monk and theologian from the Monastery of Santo Toribio de Liébana in the Kingdom of Asturias, in what is now northern Spain. He is best known for his Commentary on the Apocalypse (Commentaria in Apocalypsin), composed around 776 and revised in subsequent editions, which became one of the most influential illustrated texts of the early medieval Iberian world.

Life

Little is known of Beatus's life. He was probably born in the Liébana valley and entered the monastery of San Martín de Turieno (later Santo Toribio). He is associated with the court of King Silo of Asturias and served as confessor to Queen Adosinda. In 785 he and Etherius of Osma wrote the Adversus Elipandum, a treatise defending orthodox Christology against the Adoptionist teachings of Elipandus of Toledo, which held that Christ was the adopted son of God.

Commentary on the Apocalypse

The Commentary is a compilation of patristic excerpts — drawing on authors such as Tyconius, Primasius, Apringius of Beja, and Jerome — woven around the text of the Book of Revelation. Beatus presented it as a work for the edification of his fellow monks. The text gained enormous popularity in Christian Iberia during the following centuries, and more than thirty medieval copies survive, many lavishly illuminated in the Mozarabic style. These illuminated copies are collectively known as Beatus manuscripts (Spanish: Beatos), including the Morgan Beatus, the Girona Beatus, and the Saint-Sever Beatus.

Legacy

The maps included in some Beatus manuscripts, showing the world as known to medieval Christians and the dispersion of the Apostles, are among the most famous examples of medieval cartography. Beatus's name also became attached to the manuscripts themselves, regardless of their textual relationship to his original commentary.
