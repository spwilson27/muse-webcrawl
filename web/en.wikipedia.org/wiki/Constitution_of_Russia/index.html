Constitution of Russia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Constitution of the Russian Federation was adopted by national referendum on 12 December 1993, in the aftermath of the constitutional crisis of that year in which President Boris Yeltsin's forces shelled the parliament building. It established a strong-presidency system (often classed as super-presidential): the president determines the basic direction of domestic and foreign policy, appoints the prime minister subject to the Duma's consent, may dissolve the Duma in defined conflicts, issues decrees with the force of law, and commands the armed forces; the Federal Assembly (State Duma and Federation Council) legislates and the Constitutional Court reviews constitutionality.

The text proclaims a democratic, federal, rule-of-law state with separation of powers and an extensive rights catalogue, and designates its first two chapters unamendable except by a new constitutional assembly. In practice, amendments lengthened presidential and Duma terms (2008: six and five years), merged the high courts (2014) and, in 2020, enacted a package that “zeroed” the incumbent president's term count (allowing two further terms), prioritised Russian law over international court rulings, and entrenched social and “traditional values” provisions, approved in an “all-Russian vote” whose procedures were themselves contested. The gap between the constitution's liberal text and the system's practice is the central datum of Politics of Russia.
