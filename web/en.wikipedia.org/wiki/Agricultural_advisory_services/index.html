Agricultural advisory services

Agricultural advisory services, historically called agricultural extension, are services that support farmers and other rural actors by providing knowledge, information, skills, and advice to improve agricultural productivity, livelihoods, and sustainability. They link research institutions, governments, private companies, and non-governmental organizations with farming communities.

History

Modern extension systems arose in the 19th century — for example, with itinerant agricultural lecturers in Britain and Ireland — and were institutionalized in the United States by the Smith–Lever Act of 1914, which created the Cooperative Extension Service linking land-grant universities to county agents. After the Second World War, extension became a key instrument of development policy, most famously through the Training and Visit system promoted by the World Bank in the 1970s and 1980s.

Methods

Advisory methods include farm visits, demonstration plots, farmer field schools, radio and television programs, printed materials, and increasingly digital tools such as mobile-phone messaging, call centres, and smartphone apps delivering weather, pest, and market information. Participatory approaches — in which advisors act as facilitators of farmer experimentation rather than lecturers — have gained prominence since the 1990s.

Organization and challenges

Services may be publicly funded, privately provided by input suppliers, agribusinesses, or consultants, or delivered by NGOs and producer organizations; many countries operate pluralistic systems combining all of these. Persistent challenges include reaching women and remote farmers, financing, advisor training, and adapting advice to climate change.
