The Mark of Zorro is a 1919 story written by American pulp writer Johnston McCulley. It was first published in the magazine All-Story Weekly as a five-part serial under the title The Curse of Capistrano, running from August 9 to September 6, 1919. The story introduced the character Zorro, the masked outlaw hero of Spanish California, and is considered a landmark of pulp adventure fiction. Following the success of the 1920 Douglas Fairbanks film adaptation, The Mark of Zorro, the story was republished as a novel under that title in 1924.

Plot summary

Set in the pueblo of Los Angeles in Spanish California around the early 19th century, the novel follows Don Diego de la Vega, a wealthy young caballero who appears to his friends as a lazy, effeminate idler indifferent to the injustices around him. In secret, Diego is Zorro ("the Fox"), a masked swordsman dressed in black who rides out at night to defend the poor and oppressed — Native Americans, friars, and peasants — against the corrupt soldiers and officials of the governor's regime, in particular the brutal Sergeant Pedro Gonzales and Captain Ramon.

Zorro's signature is the letter "Z" cut with the tip of his sword into the cheek or clothing of his enemies. Aided by his deaf-mute servant Bernardo and by the Franciscan friar Padre Felipe, Zorro repeatedly humiliates the presidio's soldiers. Diego, in his public persona, courts the beautiful Lolita Pulido, whose impoverished noble family has fallen out of favour with the governor; as Zorro, he also woos her, and she finds herself torn between the languid don and the dashing outlaw — not knowing they are the same man. The story culminates in a revolt of the caballeros against the governor, Zorro's unmasking before Lolita and her family, and Diego's promise to lay down the mask now that justice has been restored.

Publication history

McCulley wrote the story for All-Story Weekly, one of the leading pulp magazines published by the Frank A. Munsey Company. The five installments appeared under the title The Curse of Capistrano. Douglas Fairbanks purchased the film rights, and his 1920 silent film The Mark of Zorro was a major commercial success that effectively launched the swashbuckler genre in American cinema. To capitalize on the film, McCulley's story was reissued in book form in 1924 as The Mark of Zorro, published by Grosset & Dunlap, and the new title has been used in most subsequent reprints.

Legacy

The novel established many of the enduring elements of the Zorro mythos: the black costume and mask, the rapier-cut "Z", the secret identity concealed behind a foppish alter ego, and the loyal mute servant. McCulley went on to write more than sixty further Zorro stories. The character and the dual-identity structure of the novel were also a acknowledged influence on later masked heroes; Bob Kane cited Zorro, and the Fairbanks film in particular, as an inspiration for Batman, whose origin story includes a young Bruce Wayne leaving a screening of The Mark of Zorro on the night his parents are murdered.
