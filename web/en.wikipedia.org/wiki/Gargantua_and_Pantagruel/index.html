Gargantua and Pantagruel

The Life of Gargantua and of Pantagruel (French: La vie de Gargantua et de Pantagruel) is a pentalogy of novels written in the 16th century by François Rabelais, which tells of the adventures of two giants, Gargantua and his son Pantagruel. The text is written in an amusing, extravagant, and satirical vein, features much erudite wordplay, and is renowned for its earthy humour and its celebration of learning, appetite and physical life. The adjective "gargantuan" derives from the first giant's name.

The books

The first book published was Pantagruel (1532), issued under the anagrammatic pseudonym Alcofrybas Nasier. It follows the young giant Pantagruel through his education in Paris, his friendship with the rogue Panurge, and his war against the Dipsodes. Gargantua (1534), nominally a prequel, recounts the birth, education and wars of Pantagruel's father, and contains the famous description of the Abbey of Thélème, whose single rule is "Do what thou wilt" (Fay ce que vouldras).

The Third Book (1546) centres on Panurge's question of whether he should marry, consulting a parade of authorities; the Fourth Book (1552) sends Panurge and Pantagruel on a sea voyage toward the Oracle of the Divine Bottle. A Fifth Book, published posthumously in 1564, completes the voyage, though Rabelais's authorship of all of it has long been questioned.

Themes and style

Rabelais satirises scholastic education, monasticism, lawyers, theologians and the Sorbonne, which condemned his books; he was protected by powerful patrons including Cardinal Jean du Bellay. His humanist programme favours education of the whole person — languages, science, physical training — and his prose mixes Latin, Greek, dialects, invented words, lists and puns. The Russian theorist Mikhail Bakhtin famously analysed the work in Rabelais and His World as the supreme expression of carnival and grotesque realism.

Influence

The novels stand at the head of the European comic novel tradition, influencing writers from Laurence Sterne to James Joyce. The first English translation, by Sir Thomas Urquhart (1653) with Peter Anthony Motteux, is itself celebrated as a masterpiece of translation.
