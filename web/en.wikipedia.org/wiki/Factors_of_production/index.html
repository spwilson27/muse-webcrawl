In economics, factors of production, resources, or inputs are what is used in the production process to produce output — that is, finished goods and services. The utilised amounts of the various inputs determine the quantity of output according to the relationship called the production function.

There are three basic resources or factors of production: land, labour and capital; all of these are factors of production used in the production of goods and services. The classical economics of Adam Smith, David Ricardo and Karl Marx regarded land, labour and capital as the primary factors; in neoclassical economics, entrepreneurship is sometimes added as a fourth factor.

Land

Land includes not just the site of production but natural resources above or below the soil — fields, forests, minerals, water resources and other gifts of nature. The payment for the use of land is called rent. Land differs from other factors in being fixed in total supply.

Labour

Labour consists of human effort — physical and mental — devoted to production. The payment for labour is wages (and salaries). The quality of labour can be improved through education and training, a process economists call human capital formation.

Capital

Capital goods — buildings, machinery, tools, equipment and infrastructure — are produced means of production, themselves outputs of earlier economic activity. Economists distinguish capital from financial capital (money and financial assets), which itself produces nothing but is used to purchase capital goods. The payment for capital is interest.

Entrepreneurship

Entrepreneurship is the combination of the other factors in order to produce goods or services for the marketplace, bearing risk and uncertainty in return for profit. Economists such as Alfred Marshall treated organisation or entrepreneurship as a distinct fourth factor of production.

Modern interpretations

Knowledge and technology are sometimes treated as additional factors, particularly in endogenous growth theory. Energy is occasionally considered a primary factor, since every production process requires it. The relative importance of the factors varies by industry: farming is land-intensive, services are labour-intensive, and heavy manufacturing is capital-intensive.
