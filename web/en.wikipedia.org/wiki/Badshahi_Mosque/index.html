The Badshahi Mosque (Urdu: بادشاہی مسجد, "Imperial Mosque") is a Mughal-era congregational mosque in Lahore, Pakistan, located west of Lahore Fort and facing the historic Hazuri Bagh. Commissioned by the emperor Aurangzeb and completed in 1673, it was the largest mosque in the world at the time of its construction and remained so for over three centuries; it is now among the largest mosques in South Asia and an iconic symbol of Lahore.

Architecture

Built of red sandstone with white marble inlay, the mosque follows classic Mughal design: a vast courtyard entered through a monumental gateway reached by a flight of steps, a prayer hall divided into seven bays, three bulbous white marble domes, and four minarets at the corners of the hall with four smaller minarets at the gateway. The prayer chamber is richly decorated with carved marble and fresco work. The mosque's layout mirrors that of the Jama Masjid in Delhi, built by Aurangzeb's father, Shah Jahan.

History

After the decline of the Mughal Empire, the mosque was used as a garrison during the Sikh Empire and the British Raj, when its courtyard served military purposes. The author John Burke photographed it in the 19th century. Extensive repairs began under British administration and continued after Pakistan's independence, restoring the mosque as a place of worship. Relics attributed to the Prophet Muhammad and his family are displayed in a small museum in the gateway.
