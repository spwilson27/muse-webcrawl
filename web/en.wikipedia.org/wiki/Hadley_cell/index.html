Hadley cell

The Hadley cell, also known as the Hadley circulation, is a global-scale tropical atmospheric circulation in which air rises near the equator, flows poleward high in the troposphere, sinks in the subtropics around 30° latitude, and returns towards the equator near the surface. It is the largest and most important of the three great circulation cells in each hemisphere, dominating the tropics and subtropics. The cell is named after George Hadley, who described the mechanism in 1735 to explain the trade winds.

Mechanism

Intense solar heating near the equator warms moist air, which rises in deep convection, producing the heavy rainfall of the intertropical convergence zone (ITCZ). At the tropopause the air spreads poleward, gradually cooling and subsiding. Where it sinks, in the subtropical highs, it creates the arid belts that contain most of the world's great deserts. The surface return flow is deflected by the Coriolis effect into the easterly trade winds.

Seasonal and climatic behaviour

The Hadley cell migrates seasonally with the thermal equator, and the two hemispheric cells merge into a single cross-equatorial cell around the solstices. Observations indicate that the cells have widened by roughly a degree of latitude or more since the late twentieth century, a change associated with climate warming and with shifts in storm tracks and subtropical dry zones. The strength of the circulation also varies with El Niño and La Niña events.

History of the theory

Hadley's 1735 explanation invoked the conservation of angular velocity as air moved between latitudes — essentially the modern Coriolis explanation — but it was largely ignored for a century. William Ferrel and later nineteenth-century meteorologists incorporated the cell into the three-cell model of atmospheric circulation alongside the Ferrel and polar cells.
