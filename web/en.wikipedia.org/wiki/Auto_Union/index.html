Auto Union AG was a German automobile manufacturer formed in 1932 from the merger of four companies — Audi, DKW, Horch, and Wanderer — during the Great Depression. Its emblem of four interlocking rings, one for each founding marque, survives today as the logo of Audi. Auto Union is especially famous for its mid-engined Grand Prix racing cars of 1934–1939, the Silver Arrows that rivalled Mercedes-Benz.

Formation and pre-war years

The merger, brokered by the State Bank of Saxony, kept the four brands in separate market segments: Horch for luxury cars, Wanderer for mid-range cars, Audi for the upper-middle class, and DKW — already a giant in motorcycles and small two-stroke cars — for inexpensive volume models. DKW's front-wheel-drive designs and two-stroke engines dominated the company's sales.

The Grand Prix cars

With funding from the Nazi government's motor-racing programme, Ferdinand Porsche designed the Auto Union racing cars: mid-engined, supercharged V16 (Type A–C) and later V12 (Type D) machines of fearsome power and handling. Driven by Hans Stuck, Bernd Rosemeyer, and Tazio Nuvolari, they won numerous Grands Prix between 1934 and 1939 in the "Silver Arrow" duel with Mercedes-Benz. Rosemeyer was killed in a land-speed-record attempt in 1938.

Post-war rebirth in the West

The original Auto Union's Saxon plants were dismantled by the Soviet occupation, and the company was struck from the Chemnitz register in 1948. Executives and engineers re-founded Auto Union GmbH in Ingolstadt, Bavaria, in 1949, reviving DKW two-stroke cars and motorcycles. Acquired by Daimler-Benz in 1958 and sold to Volkswagen in 1964–66, the Ingolstadt company launched the first modern Audi in 1965 and merged with NSU in 1969 to form Audi NSU Auto Union AG — today's Audi AG.
