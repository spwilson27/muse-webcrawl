Crystal LaBeija (died 1982) was an American drag queen and trans woman who founded the House of LaBeija in 1972, establishing the "house" system that became a foundation of New York City ball culture. She is remembered as one of the founding mothers of the modern drag ball scene.

Life and career

LaBeija competed in the New York drag pageant circuit of the 1960s, which was dominated by white queens and judges. In 1967 she was a contestant in the Miss All-America Camp Beauty Contest documented in Frank Simon's 1968 film The Queen. When the title went to a white contestant, Rachel Harlow, LaBeija famously walked out in protest on camera, denouncing the pageant as rigged against Black and Latina performers.

In response, she and Lottie LaBeija organized their own ball — billed as "Crystal & Lottie LaBeija presents the first annual House of LaBeija Ball" in Harlem in 1972. The "house" — a chosen-family structure led by a "mother" — offered support, mentorship and a competitive banner for its "children", and the model spread through Black and Latino LGBTQ communities, evolving into the ball culture later documented in Paris Is Burning (1990) and the television series Pose (2018–2021).

Legacy

The House of LaBeija survives as the first house of ball culture, and Crystal LaBeija is honoured as a foundational figure in LGBTQ history. She died in 1982.
