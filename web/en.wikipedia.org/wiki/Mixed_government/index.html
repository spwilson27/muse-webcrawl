Mixed government

Mixed government (or a mixed constitution) is a form of government that combines elements of democracy, aristocracy, and monarchy, in the classical theory supposedly making impossible their respective degenerations — mob rule, oligarchy, and tyranny. The idea, developed by Aristotle and Polybius, became one of the most influential concepts in Western constitutional history, underpinning the analysis of the Roman Republic, the Venetian Republic, and the English constitution, and feeding into the modern doctrines of separation of powers and checks and balances.

Classical theory

Aristotle classified constitutions by whether the one, the few, or the many rule, and whether they rule for the common good or their own: kingship degenerates into tyranny, aristocracy into oligarchy, and the moderate "polity" into democracy in its bad sense. In the Politics he praised a blend of oligarchic and democratic features. Polybius, in Book VI of his Histories, explained Rome's stability and success by its mixture: the consuls supplied the monarchical element, the Senate the aristocratic, and the assemblies and tribunes the democratic, each checking the others and interrupting the cycle of constitutional decay (anacyclosis).

Medieval and early modern development

The mixed constitution was invoked for the estates of medieval kingdoms and was central to civic republican thought. In England, the doctrine crystallised in the claim that King, Lords, and Commons together embodied monarchy, aristocracy, and democracy in balance — a claim deployed by royalists in the Answer to the Nineteen Propositions (1642) and later celebrated by Blackstone. James Harrington and later Montesquieu transformed it: Montesquieu's The Spirit of the Laws recast mixture in functional terms as the separation of legislative, executive, and judicial powers, the form in which it entered the United States Constitution.

Decline and legacy

The rise of popular sovereignty made the idea of balancing social orders seem obsolete: if all power derives from the people, mixing "elements" tied to estates loses meaning. Yet the institutional inheritance — bicameralism, independent courts, federalism — embodies the mixed constitution's practical aim of preventing any single power from ruling unchecked, and modern constitutionalism is often described as its heir.
