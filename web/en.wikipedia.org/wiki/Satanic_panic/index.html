Satanic panic

The Satanic panic was a moral panic that swept the United States in the 1980s and spread to other English-speaking countries, centred on unfounded allegations that vast networks of Satanic cults were ritually abusing children. It led to numerous criminal prosecutions — most famously the McMartin preschool trial, the longest and most expensive criminal trial in American history at the time — nearly all of which ended in acquittal, dismissal, or convictions later overturned. No credible evidence of organised Satanic ritual abuse was ever found.

Origins

The panic was catalysed by the 1980 book Michelle Remembers, in which a Canadian patient, with her psychiatrist (later her husband), recounted "recovered memories" of childhood ritual abuse that were widely promoted before being discredited. Talk shows, evangelical preachers, law-enforcement seminars and some therapists amplified claims that daycare centres, heavy metal music, role-playing games such as Dungeons & Dragons, and horror films were gateways to a Satanic conspiracy.

Cases and investigations

Major cases included McMartin (California), the Kern County cases, Little Rascals (North Carolina), and the West Memphis Three murders in Arkansas, where a teenager's interest in the occult was central evidence. Techniques used on child witnesses — repeated suggestive questioning, anatomical dolls and demands for disclosure — were later shown to produce false allegations. An FBI report by Kenneth Lanning (1992) concluded there was no evidence of organised ritual-abuse conspiracies, and reviews in Britain reached similar conclusions.

Legacy

The panic is now a standard example in discussions of moral panics, false memory syndrome and miscarriages of justice. Many wrongly convicted defendants spent years in prison before release. Scholars draw parallels with later conspiracy movements; several key figures of the panic resurfaced in the QAnon era, which revived ritual-abuse narratives.
