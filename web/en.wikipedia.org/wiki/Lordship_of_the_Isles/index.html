The Lordship of the Isles was a semi-independent Gaelic-Norse lordship in the Hebrides and the western seaboard of Scotland during the later Middle Ages. Ruled by the chiefs of Clan Donald (MacDonalds), who styled themselves Dominus Insularum — "Lord of the Isles" — the lordship functioned at its height almost as an independent maritime kingdom within Scotland.

Origins

The lordship grew out of the realm of Somerled (died 1164), a Norse-Gaelic warlord who carved out a sea-kingdom in Argyll and the Isles. His descendants divided his territories; the MacDonald line took Islay and the southern Hebrides. After the Treaty of Perth (1266), by which Norway ceded the Hebrides to Scotland, the MacDonald chiefs nominally became vassals of the Scottish Crown while retaining practical autonomy.

The MacDonald ascendancy

John of Islay (c. 1318–1387) was the first to style himself Lord of the Isles in formal documents. Under John and his successors the lordship expanded to include the Earldom of Ross, conquered mainland territories in the north-west, and claims in Ireland. The Lords governed from Finlaggan on Islay, where a council of the Isles — including the great chiefs such as the MacLeods, MacLeans, and Mackenzies — met to dispense justice and enact statutes.

The lordship was a centre of Gaelic culture: its court patronised bards, harpists, physicians (the Beaton medical kindred), and sculptors of the Iona school. Gaelic was the language of government, and the Lords cultivated links with Ireland as much as with Edinburgh.

Conflict with the Crown

Relations with the Scottish kings deteriorated as the Crown sought to assert control. Alexander of Islay's ambitions led to the Battle of Harlaw (1411). In 1462 John II of Islay concluded the Treaty of Westminster-Ardtornish with Edward IV of England, agreeing to partition Scotland with English help — a treasonable act that, when discovered in 1475, cost him the Earldom of Ross.

After further rebellions by his son Angus Óg and the disputed succession, James IV in 1493 finally forfeited the lordship and annexed its territories to the Crown. Later risings, including Donald Dubh's in 1545, failed to restore it.

Legacy

The title "Lord of the Isles" became one of the titles of the Scottish (and later British) monarch's heir; it is now held by the Prince of Wales (Duke of Rothesay in Scotland). The lordship's memory remains central to the history of Clan Donald and to Gaelic Scotland's medieval identity.
