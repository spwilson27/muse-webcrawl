Cassowary

The southern cassowary is one of three living cassowary species.

Cassowaries are large flightless birds of the genus Casuarius, native to the tropical forests of New Guinea and northeastern Australia. The three living species—the southern (or double-wattled) cassowary, the northern (single-wattled) cassowary, and the dwarf cassowary—are ratites related to emus, ostriches, and kiwis.

Description

Cassowaries have coarse black plumage, bare blue and red skin on the neck, and a prominent keratin casque on the head. Females are larger and more brightly coloured than males. Their powerful legs bear dagger-like inner claws that can exceed 12 centimetres (5 inches) and can be dangerous if the bird is cornered or provoked.

Behaviour and ecology

Generally shy and solitary, cassowaries feed mainly on fallen fruit, supplemented by fungi, invertebrates, and small vertebrates. As large frugivores they disperse the seeds of many rainforest plants, some of which germinate better after passing through the bird. Males incubate the eggs and rear the striped chicks for many months.

Conservation

Habitat loss, vehicle strikes, and dog attacks threaten cassowaries, particularly the southern cassowary in Australia, which is listed as endangered under Australian law.
