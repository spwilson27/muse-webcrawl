Chain Home

Chain Home transmitter towers

Chain Home, or CH for short, was the codename for the ring of coastal early warning radar stations built by the Royal Air Force (RAF) before and during the Second World War to detect and track aircraft. The system is best known for its role in the Battle of Britain in 1940, when it gave Fighter Command early warning of incoming German air raids, allowing its outnumbered fighter force to be used efficiently.

Chain Home was the first national radar air defence system to be deployed anywhere in the world, and is generally credited as the first fully integrated air defence network. Operating at around 20–30 MHz in the HF band, it was a crude system by later standards, with transmitter towers some 110 metres (350 ft) tall and wooden receiver towers, but it proved decisive in practice.

Development

British research into radar began in 1935, following a memorandum by Robert Watson-Watt demonstrating that radio echoes from aircraft could be detected. Tests at Orford Ness and later at Bawdsey Manor in Suffolk led to a working system. Under pressure of the approaching war, the Air Ministry ordered a chain of stations along the south and east coasts of Britain. By the outbreak of war in September 1939, 18 stations were operational, and coverage was extended around much of the coastline during 1940.

Operation

Chain Home stations transmitted pulses of radio energy; echoes reflected from aircraft were received on separate arrays and displayed on a cathode ray tube, giving range. Direction was estimated by comparing signals from crossed receiver aerials using a goniometer, and height could be estimated roughly from signal strength. Information from the stations was telephoned to filter rooms and then to Fighter Command headquarters and group and sector operations rooms, where it was combined with reports from the Observer Corps in the "Dowding system" of air defence control.

A companion system, Chain Home Low (CHL), operating at higher frequencies, was introduced to detect low-flying aircraft that flew beneath CH coverage.

Battle of Britain

During the Battle of Britain in the summer and autumn of 1940, Chain Home gave warning of raids forming over France, typically allowing RAF controllers 20 minutes or more to scramble fighters. The Luftwaffe attacked several radar stations in August 1940 but failed to put the system out of action for long, and largely abandoned attacks on the stations. Historians regard Chain Home and the Dowding system as a critical factor in the RAF's victory.

Later use

Chain Home remained in service through the war, supplemented and eventually replaced by more advanced systems such as the AMES Type 7 ground-controlled interception radar. After the war, parts of the network were adapted during the early Cold War (the ROTOR programme) before final decommissioning. Few complete stations survive; the transmitter block at Bawdsey is preserved as a museum.
