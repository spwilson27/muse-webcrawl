The War of the Eight Princes (traditional Chinese: 八王之亂; simplified Chinese: 八王之乱; pinyin: Bā Wáng zhī Luàn), also known as the Rebellion of the Eight Kings or the Rebellion of the Eight Princes, was a series of civil wars among kings (wang) of the Chinese Western Jin dynasty from 291 to 306 CE. The conflicts were largely confined to the imperial family, the House of Sima, and devastated the Jin heartland in northern China.

Background

The Western Jin founder, Emperor Wu (Sima Yan), had enfeoffed his many sons and relatives as princes with substantial military commands, hoping they would shield the throne. His heir, Emperor Hui, was developmentally disabled and unable to rule, so power was contested by regents and by the emperor's wife, Empress Jia Nanfeng, whose coup in 291 — using the Prince of Chu as her instrument and then destroying him — opened the succession of struggles.

Course of the war

The eight princes most prominently involved were Sima Liang (Prince of Ru'nan), Sima Wei (Prince of Chu), Sima Lun (Prince of Zhao), Sima Jiong (Prince of Qi), Sima Ying (Prince of Chengdu), Sima Yong (Prince of Hejian), Sima Yue (Prince of Donghai) and Sima Ai (Prince of Changsha). Sima Lun briefly usurped the throne in 301 before being overthrown. Thereafter the struggle centred on Luoyang and Chang'an: Sima Ying and Sima Yong fought Sima Yue for control of the puppet emperor, armies increasingly recruited Xiongnu, Xianbei and other non-Han ("Wu Hu") troops, and famine and massacre depopulated whole regions.

Consequences

The war exhausted the Western Jin state and destroyed its military reserves. In its aftermath the Xiongnu leader Liu Yuan declared himself king and founded Han-Zhao; Luoyang fell in 311 and Chang'an in 316, ending the Western Jin. The surviving court fled south to found the Eastern Jin, while the north entered the era of the Sixteen Kingdoms. The War of the Eight Princes is conventionally counted among the most destructive civil wars in Chinese history.
