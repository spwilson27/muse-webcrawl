Gene-culture coevolution, also known as dual inheritance theory, is a branch of evolutionary theory that treats culture as a second system of inheritance — transmitted by teaching, imitation and other social learning — that evolves alongside, and interacts with, genetic inheritance. Culturally transmitted practices can change the selection pressures on genes, while evolved psychological biases shape which cultural variants spread.

Overview

The framework was formalised in the 1970s and 1980s by Luigi Luca Cavalli-Sforza and Marcus Feldman, who modelled cultural transmission mathematically, and by Robert Boyd and Peter Richerson, who emphasised transmission biases such as conformity and prestige bias. Culture in this view is information stored in brains and artefacts that affects behaviour and can be analysed with population models analogous to population genetics.

Examples

The canonical case is lactase persistence: the cultural practice of dairying and milk-drinking in pastoralist populations of Europe, Africa and the Middle East created selection favouring alleles that keep the lactase enzyme active in adults — and different mutations arose independently in different dairying regions, demonstrating culture driving genetic change.

Other studied cases include yam cultivation increasing mosquito breeding sites and thereby selection for the sickle-cell trait in West Africa, starch-rich agricultural diets and amylase gene copy number, and cultural norms of cousin marriage affecting genetic structure.

Criticism and relation to other fields

The theory overlaps with niche construction theory, memetics and evolutionary psychology, from which it differs in emphasis. Critics have questioned how faithfully culture is transmitted and whether population-genetic analogies apply; supporters respond that the models need only statistical regularities, not perfect copying.
