Lifting-line theory

Lifting-line theory
DeveloperLudwig Prandtl (1918–1919)
FieldAerodynamics

Lifting-line theory is a mathematical model in aerodynamics, developed by Ludwig Prandtl and his colleagues at Göttingen, that describes the lift distribution and induced drag of a finite wing. The wing is modelled as a "lifting line" of bound vorticity, shedding a sheet of trailing vortices from its trailing edge.

The model

Circulation Γ varies along the span; by Helmholtz's theorems, changes in circulation shed trailing vortices whose combined effect induces a downward velocity (downwash) at the wing. The downwash reduces the effective angle of attack and tilts the lift vector backward, producing induced drag. Expanding the circulation as a Fourier sine series yields simple expressions for total lift and induced drag.

Key results

The theory shows that induced drag is minimised by an elliptical spanwise lift distribution, that induced drag coefficient varies as the square of the lift coefficient divided by the aspect ratio (CD,i = CL² / (π·AR·e)), and that it explains tip vortices and the behaviour of high-aspect-ratio wings such as those of gliders. It remains accurate for straight, unswept wings of moderate-to-high aspect ratio at low angles of attack.

Limitations

The model breaks down for low-aspect-ratio, swept or delta wings, and near stall, where three-dimensional and viscous effects dominate; extensions such as lifting-surface theory and vortex lattice methods address these cases.
