American Zoetrope

American Zoetrope (also known as Zoetrope Studios) is an American film production company based in San Francisco, California. It was founded in 1969 by filmmakers Francis Ford Coppola and George Lucas as an independent studio where young directors could work outside the Hollywood studio system. The company takes its name from the zoetrope, a pre-film animation device, one of which Coppola had received as a gift.

History

Originally located in a warehouse on Folsom Street, American Zoetrope's first productions included Lucas's THX 1138 (1971), made under a development arrangement with Warner Bros. When the studio reacted negatively to the slate of projects, the deal collapsed and Coppola turned to directing The Godfather (1972) for Paramount to keep the company afloat. Through the 1970s the studio produced The Conversation (1974), Apocalypse Now (1979) and films by directors including John Milius and Carroll Ballard (The Black Stallion).

In the early 1980s the company expanded ambitiously as Zoetrope Studios after purchasing the Hollywood General Studios lot, but the commercial failure of the musical One from the Heart (1982) led to bankruptcy proceedings and the loss of the lot. Coppola continued producing and directing through American Zoetrope in San Francisco, later making films such as Bram Stoker's Dracula (1992), The Virgin Suicides (1999, directed by Sofia Coppola), Lost in Translation (2003, executive produced) and Megalopolis (2024).

Legacy

American Zoetrope was central to the New Hollywood movement and nurtured a generation of filmmakers and craftspeople, including Walter Murch and sound and editing innovators. The company is headquartered in the Sentinel Building, the green copper-clad flatiron also known as the Columbus Tower, which Coppola purchased in the 1970s. The building also houses the offices of the literary magazine Zoetrope: All-Story, founded by Coppola in 1997.
