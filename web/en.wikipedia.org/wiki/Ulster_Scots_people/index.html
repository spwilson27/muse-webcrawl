The Ulster Scots (Ulster Scots: Ulstèr-Scotch), often known in North America as the Scotch-Irish, are an ethnic group in Ireland descended mainly from Lowland Scottish settlers who migrated to the province of Ulster during the 17th century, above all during the Plantation of Ulster (from 1609) and the large migrations of the 1690s.

Ulster Scots heritage signage in County Antrim.

History

Scottish settlement in Ulster began on a large scale with the Plantation following the Flight of the Earls (1607). Settlers came chiefly from the Scottish Lowlands and Borders, bringing Presbyterianism, the Scots language (in its Ulster form, Ulster Scots), and distinctive farming and linen-working practices. Many Ulster Scots later emigrated to North America; in the 18th century perhaps 200,000 or more migrated, forming the Scotch-Irish, who played a major role in the American frontier, the American Revolution, and supplied numerous US presidents of Ulster Scots descent.

Culture

Ulster Scots culture includes Highland-style pipe and flute bands, Scottish country dancing, Burns Night celebrations, and a literary tradition in Ulster Scots verse by the "weaver poets" of the 18th and 19th centuries. Following the Good Friday Agreement of 1998, which recognised Ulster Scots language and culture, public bodies such as the Ulster-Scots Agency (Tha Boord o Ulstèr-Scotch) were established to promote it. Census returns in Northern Ireland show tens of thousands claiming some knowledge of Ulster Scots.
