TALEN

Transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs) are restriction enzymes that can be engineered to cut specific sequences of DNA. They are used as genome-editing tools, alongside zinc-finger nucleases and the later CRISPR/Cas systems.

Structure

A TALEN is made by fusing a DNA-binding domain derived from TAL effectors — proteins secreted by Xanthomonas bacteria to manipulate plant genes — to a DNA cleavage domain, typically from the FokI nuclease. The TAL effector's binding specificity is determined by a central repeat region in which two variable amino acids (the repeat-variable diresidue) in each ~34-residue repeat specify one DNA base pair, a code deciphered in 2009. Because FokI cuts only as a dimer, TALENs are used in pairs binding opposite strands flanking the target site.

Applications

TALENs have been used to knock out or modify genes in plants, animals, and cultured cells, including early clinical work on engineered T-cell therapies. Although CRISPR systems have largely displaced TALENs because they are easier to design, TALENs remain useful where their specificity profile is advantageous.
