Stellar cycle

The stellar cycle, or life cycle of a star, describes the sequence of changes a star undergoes over its lifetime, from its formation in a collapsing cloud of gas and dust to its final state as a compact remnant. A star's initial mass determines its path: low-mass stars end as white dwarfs, while massive stars explode as supernovae, leaving neutron stars or black holes.

Formation

Stars form in giant molecular clouds. When a dense core collapses under gravity it heats into a protostar surrounded by an accretion disc. When core temperatures reach roughly 10 million kelvin, hydrogen fusion ignites and the star joins the main sequence, fusing hydrogen into helium in its core for most of its life — about 10 billion years for the Sun.

Post-main-sequence

When core hydrogen is exhausted, the core contracts and heats, hydrogen burning moves into a shell, and the star expands into a red giant. Stars like the Sun then ignite helium (the helium flash in low-mass stars), later shed their outer layers as a planetary nebula, and leave a cooling white dwarf. Massive stars fuse progressively heavier elements up to iron, then collapse catastrophically: the resulting supernova disperses heavy elements into space and leaves a neutron star or black hole.

Recycling

Material expelled by dying stars enriches the interstellar medium, seeding future generations of stars and planets — the "stellar cycle" in the galactic sense.
