Sarcoidosis, also known as Besnier–Boeck–Schaumann disease, is a disease involving abnormal collections of inflammatory cells that form lumps known as granulomata. The disease usually begins in the lungs, skin, or lymph nodes and can affect many organs, most notably the lungs and lymph nodes of the chest. Less commonly it affects the eyes, liver, heart, and the nervous system.

Signs and symptoms

Signs and symptoms of sarcoidosis vary with the organs involved. Roughly half of people show no symptoms; others develop a persistent dry cough, breathlessness, fatigue, weight loss, fever, or skin problems such as tender red bumps on the shins (erythema nodosum). A classic acute presentation combining fever, joint pain, enlarged hilar lymph nodes, and erythema nodosum is called Löfgren syndrome and often resolves on its own.

Cause

The cause of sarcoidosis is unknown. Some believe it may be due to an immune reaction to a trigger such as an infection or chemicals in genetically susceptible individuals. Family cases suggest a hereditary component; certain HLA alleles are associated with susceptibility and prognosis.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is partly one of exclusion and typically requires a biopsy showing non-caseating epithelioid granulomas, together with compatible clinical and radiological findings, such as bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy on a chest radiograph. Other granulomatous diseases, including tuberculosis, must be ruled out.

Treatment and prognosis

Many cases resolve without treatment. When treatment is needed, corticosteroids such as prednisone are first-line; second-line agents include methotrexate and biologic therapies such as infliximab. Mortality is low overall, though pulmonary fibrosis and cardiac involvement can be serious.

Epidemiology

Sarcoidosis occurs worldwide in all races, with higher rates reported among people of Northern European and African ancestry. It most commonly begins in young adults between the ages of 20 and 49.
