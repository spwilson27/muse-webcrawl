Phytophthora infestans

Phytophthora infestans is an oomycete (water mold) that causes the serious potato disease known as late blight or potato blight. Late blight was a major culprit in the 1840s European potato failure that caused the Great Famine of Ireland and the Highland potato famine in Scotland. It remains the most devastating disease of potatoes worldwide, costing billions of dollars annually in crop losses and control measures. The organism also infects tomatoes and other members of the nightshade family (Solanaceae).

Biology

Despite its fungus-like appearance, P. infestans is an oomycete, more closely related to brown algae than to true fungi. It reproduces asexually by sporangia produced on infected leaves in cool, wet weather; sporangia are spread by wind and rain splash and may release motile zoospores that swim to infection sites. When both mating types (A1 and A2) are present—now the case on most continents—sexual reproduction produces thick-walled oospores that survive in soil.

Disease cycle and symptoms

Infection begins as pale green, water-soaked lesions on leaves, often with a white mildew growth on the underside in humid conditions. Lesions turn brown-black and can destroy a field's foliage within days under favourable conditions (cool nights, warm days, leaf wetness). Tubers develop a reddish-brown granular rot and are vulnerable to secondary bacterial soft rot. The 19th-century pandemics were caused by the HERB-1 lineage; modern populations are more diverse and aggressive, with strains such as US-23 and the European "Blue 13" lineage.

History

The pathogen originated in the central Mexican highlands, the centre of diversity of wild potatoes. It spread to North America and then Europe in the 1840s, where susceptible potato varieties (notably the Irish 'Lumper') and wet weather produced catastrophe: about one million people died in Ireland and another million emigrated. Anton de Bary established the organism's role in the disease in 1861, an early milestone in germ theory and plant pathology, naming it Phytophthora ("plant destroyer").

Management

Control relies on integrated measures: certified seed, resistant varieties (bred using resistance genes from wild relatives such as Solanum demissum), destruction of cull piles and volunteer plants, canopy management, and fungicides including protectants and systemic oomycete-targeted products. Resistance management is a continuing struggle because the pathogen evolves rapidly; its genome, sequenced in 2009, revealed an unusually large, repeat-rich genome rich in effector genes that suppress plant immunity.
