Mnemosyne (Ancient Greek: Μνημοσύνη, Mnēmosýnē, "remembrance" or "memory") is the personification of memory in Greek mythology. A Titaness, she was the daughter of Uranus (Sky) and Gaia (Earth), and the mother of the nine Muses by Zeus. Her name derives from the Greek word mnēmē, "memory", and is the source of the English word "mnemonic".

Mythology

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Zeus lay with Mnemosyne for nine consecutive nights in the mountains of Pieria, and after a year she bore the nine Muses — Calliope, Clio, Euterpe, Thalia, Melpomene, Terpsichore, Erato, Polyhymnia and Urania — whose singing brings forgetfulness of sorrows. Because their mother is Memory itself, the Muses were understood to grant poets and kings the power of authoritative speech and recollection of the past.

Mnemosyne also presided over the spring or pool of Mnemosyne in Hades, contrasted with the waters of Lethe (Forgetfulness). In the Orphic tradition, initiates were instructed to avoid the spring of Lethe after death and instead to drink from the cold water flowing from the Lake of Mnemosyne, so that they would retain their memory and identity in the afterlife. Inscriptions with these instructions — the Orphic gold tablets — have been found in graves around the Greek world, from Hipponion in Italy to Thessaly and Crete.

Cult and legacy

Mnemosyne received modest cult honours, often alongside the Muses, for example at the sanctuary of the Muses in the Valley of the Muses near Thespiae. The Roman equivalent was Moneta, an epithet of Juno that was also associated with memory. Mnemosyne's name survives in terms such as "mnemonic device", and culturally in Aby Warburg's unfinished Mnemosyne Atlas (1924–1929), an influential picture atlas tracing the survival of classical imagery in Western art. The main-belt asteroid 57 Mnemosyne, discovered in 1859, is named after her.
