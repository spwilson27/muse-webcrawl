Norris–LaGuardia Act

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Norris–LaGuardia Act (also known as the Anti-Injunction Bill) is a 1932 United States federal law relating to labor law. It outlawed the "yellow-dog contract" — an agreement between an employer and an employee in which the employee agrees, as a condition of employment, not to join a labor union — and it sharply limited the jurisdiction and authority of the federal courts to issue injunctions against nonviolent labor disputes.

Provisions

The Act declared yellow-dog contracts unenforceable in federal court, stated that workers were free to form unions without employer interference, and barred federal courts from issuing injunctions in labor disputes except under strict procedural safeguards, including testimony in open court and specific findings of fact about unlawful acts and irreparable injury. It also guaranteed trial by jury in contempt cases arising from labor disputes.

History

The law was sponsored by Senator George W. Norris of Nebraska and Representative Fiorello H. La Guardia of New York, both progressive Republicans. It was signed by President Herbert Hoover on 23 March 1932. It is widely regarded as the first piece of New Deal-style labor legislation, preceding the National Industrial Recovery Act and the National Labor Relations Act of 1935 (the Wagner Act), which built upon its framework.

Impact

By removing the injunction as employers' principal anti-union weapon and by denying enforcement to yellow-dog contracts, the Act fundamentally shifted the federal government's posture toward organized labor from suppression toward protection of collective bargaining.
