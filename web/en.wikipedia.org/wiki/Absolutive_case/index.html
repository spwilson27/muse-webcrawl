Absolutive case

In grammar, the absolutive case is the unmarked grammatical case used for the subject of an intransitive verb and the object of a transitive verb in ergative–absolutive languages. It contrasts with the ergative case, which marks the subject (agent) of a transitive verb.

Alignment

Languages organise core arguments in different ways. Nominative–accusative languages group the intransitive subject with the transitive agent (nominative) against the object (accusative). Ergative–absolutive languages instead group the intransitive subject with the transitive object (absolutive) against the agent (ergative). The absolutive is typically the citation form of the noun and may carry no overt marking, while the ergative bears a special suffix or particle.

Languages

Well-known ergative languages include Basque, Georgian and other Kartvelian languages, many Mayan languages, Inuktitut and other Inuit languages, Dyirbal and other Australian Aboriginal languages, and (in restricted tenses) Hindi-Urdu and Pashto. In Basque, for example, gizona "the man" is absolutive, while the transitive agent takes the ergative gizonak.

Terminology

The term "absolutive" was applied to this case because it was originally used for the form of the noun standing "absolutely", outside a transitive relation. Some linguists prefer to analyse certain so-called ergative systems instead in terms of active–stative alignment or Austronesian alignment, in which the absolutive corresponds to the direct case.
