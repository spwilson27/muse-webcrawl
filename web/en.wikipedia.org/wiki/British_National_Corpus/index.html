The British National Corpus (BNC) is a 100-million-word collection of samples of written and spoken language from a wide range of sources, designed to represent a broad cross-section of British English of the late 20th century. It was compiled by a consortium of publishers, universities and the British Library, and was completed in 1994. The corpus is encoded in XML and annotated with part-of-speech information, and it has been one of the most widely used resources in lexicography, language teaching and natural language processing.

Composition

Written texts make up 90 per cent of the corpus: extracts from regional and national newspapers, specialist journals and academic books, popular fiction, school and university essays, letters and memoranda, and many other kinds of text, most dating from 1985 onwards. The spoken component, 10 per cent, comprises orthographic transcriptions of informal conversation — recorded by volunteers selected to give a demographic balance of age, region and social class — together with speech from business and government meetings, radio phone-ins and classroom lessons.

Development and editions

Work on the corpus began in 1991, funded by the Department of Trade and Industry and the Science and Engineering Research Council under a collaborative programme. The first edition was released in 1995, with a second edition (BNC World) in 2001 and the BNC XML Edition in 2007. Access is free for research in the UK through the Oxford Text Archive and via online interfaces such as BNCweb and the corpus tools hosted by Lancaster University and Brigham Young University.

Uses

The BNC provided the evidence base for several major learners' dictionaries and grammars — including the Longman Grammar of Spoken and Written English — because it allowed statements about frequency, collocation and register differences between speech and writing to be checked against a large balanced sample. A successor project, the Spoken British National Corpus 2014 (Spoken BNC2014), compiled by Lancaster University and Cambridge University Press, gathered recordings of everyday conversation to update the spoken component.
