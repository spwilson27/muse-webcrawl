The Strauss–Howe generational theory, devised by William Strauss and Neil Howe, describes a theorised recurring generation cycle in American and Western history. According to the theory, historical events are associated with recurring generational personas ("archetypes"), and these unfold in a cycle of four stages, called "turnings", lasting roughly 80 to 90 years in total—a period the authors call a saeculum.

The four turnings

  1. High — a post-crisis era of strengthening institutions and weakening individualism; society feels upbeat about collective direction.
  2. Awakening — a spiritual upheaval in which institutions are attacked in the name of personal and spiritual autonomy.
  3. Unravelling — an era of strong individualism and weak institutions; the social order of the High decays.
  4. Crisis (the "Fourth Turning") — a period of major upheaval in which institutional life is destroyed and rebuilt in response to a perceived threat to the nation's survival.

Generational archetypes

Each turning is associated with a dominant generational archetype, recurring in fixed order: Prophet (born during a High), Nomad (born during an Awakening), Hero (born during an Unravelling), and Artist (born during a Crisis). In the authors' schema, recent American generations include the "Silent Generation" (Artist), the Baby Boomers (Prophet), Generation X (Nomad), and Millennials (Hero).

Origins

Strauss and Howe introduced the theory in Generations: The History of America's Future, 1584 to 2069 (1991) and popularised its prediction of a coming crisis in The Fourth Turning (1997). William Strauss died in 2007; Howe has continued developing and applying the framework.

Criticism

Historians and social scientists have criticised the theory as overly deterministic, unfalsifiable, and based on selective readings of Anglo-American history, with archetypal descriptions vague enough to fit almost any cohort. Supporters value it as a heuristic for thinking about social mood and institutional cycles.
