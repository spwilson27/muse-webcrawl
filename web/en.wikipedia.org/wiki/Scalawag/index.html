Scalawag

A Reconstruction-era cartoon: "scalawag" was a pejorative for white Southern Republicans

In United States history, the pejorative scalawag (sometimes spelled scallywag) referred to white Southerners who supported Reconstruction policies and efforts after the American Civil War, often in coalition with freedmen and Northern newcomers known as "carpetbaggers". The term was used by Southern Democrats who opposed Reconstruction and sought to restore white supremacy and Democratic rule.

Origins of the term

Before the Civil War, "scalawag" was a slang term for a scrawny or worthless animal, especially undersized cattle — derived in part from Scalloway in the Shetland Islands, known for small ponies. During Reconstruction, Southern newspapers applied it to white Southerners who cooperated with the Republican Party.

Who the scalawags were

Scalawags were a diverse group. Many were non-slaveholding small farmers from the upcountry and Appalachian regions who had opposed secession and resented the planter elite. Others included former Whigs, merchants, and professionals who saw cooperation with Reconstruction governments as a path to economic development, public schools, and infrastructure investment. Some were former Confederate officers and planters who accepted the war's outcome and sought roles in the new order.

Role in Reconstruction

Scalawags formed a crucial part of the Republican coalition in the South alongside African American voters and Northern migrants. They held offices at every level, including governorships — James L. Alcorn of Mississippi and William G. Brownlow of Tennessee are prominent examples. Scalawag-supported governments established public school systems, modernised state constitutions, and extended civil rights, though they were also criticised for corruption and high taxes.

Decline

As "Redeemer" Democrats regained power state by state in the 1870s, using a combination of electoral organisation, economic pressure, and paramilitary violence by groups such as the Ku Klux Klan and White Leagues, the scalawag coalition collapsed. Many scalawags returned to the Democratic Party or left politics.

Historiography

Early Dunning School historians portrayed scalawags as corrupt traitors to their race and region. Revisionist scholarship from the mid-20th century onward presented a more complex picture, emphasising their Unionism, economic grievances against the planter class, and genuine divisions within white Southern society.
