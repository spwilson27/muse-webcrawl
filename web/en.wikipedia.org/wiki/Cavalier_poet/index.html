Cavalier poet

The Cavalier poets were a group of English poets of the seventeenth century associated with the court of Charles I and the Royalist ("Cavalier") cause in the English Civil War. Writing in the tradition of Ben Jonson — many styled themselves "Sons of Ben" — they favoured polished, witty lyric verse on themes of love, honour, loyalty, and the enjoyment of the present moment.

Principal figures

The leading Cavalier poets include Robert Herrick, whose Hesperides (1648) contains "To the Virgins, to Make Much of Time" ("Gather ye rosebuds while ye may"); Richard Lovelace, author of "To Althea, from Prison" and "To Lucasta, Going to the Warres"; Sir John Suckling, the most dashing of the group; Thomas Carew; and Edmund Waller. Their verse is characterised by classical restraint, smooth metre, and conversational elegance, in deliberate contrast to the intellectual complexity of the metaphysical poets such as John Donne.

Themes and style

The carpe diem motif — seize the day — runs through Cavalier lyric, alongside codes of honour, courtly love, and loyalty to the king. During the Civil War and Commonwealth, their poetry took on elegiac and defiant tones, lamenting a lost courtly world; Lovelace wrote his most famous lines while imprisoned by Parliament.

Legacy

The Cavalier lyric helped establish the polished song-like poem in English, and its best pieces have remained anthology favourites. The label "Cavalier" was applied retrospectively, but the poets' shared milieu, politics, and Jonsonian inheritance make them a genuinely coherent school.
