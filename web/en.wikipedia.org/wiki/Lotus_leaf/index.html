Lotus leaf

The lotus leaf is the foliage of Nelumbo nucifera, the sacred lotus, an aquatic plant of Asia. The leaves are large, round and peltate, floating on the water surface or held high above it on long stalks, and they are famous for their extraordinary water repellency — the so-called lotus effect.

The lotus effect

The leaf surface is covered with microscopic papillae coated in hydrophobic wax crystals, producing a hierarchical micro- and nano-scale roughness. Water droplets therefore sit almost as spheres with contact angles above 150 degrees, rolling off at the slightest tilt and picking up dirt particles as they go. This self-cleaning superhydrophobicity, described scientifically in the 1970s by Wilhelm Barthlott and colleagues, keeps the leaf clean in muddy water.

Uses and biomimicry

In Asian cuisines, lotus leaves are used as aromatic wrappers for steaming food, such as sticky rice. In technology, the leaf's structure has inspired self-cleaning paints, coatings, glass and textiles — a classic example of biomimetics, in which an evolved natural surface informs engineered materials.
