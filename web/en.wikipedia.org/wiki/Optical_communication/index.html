Optical communication

Optical communication, also known as optical telecommunication, is communication over a distance using light to carry information. It can be performed visually or by using electronic devices. The earliest basic forms of optical communication date back several millennia, while the first electrical device created to do so was the photophone, invented in 1880.

Historical forms

Smoke signals, beacon fires, signal flags and semaphore lines are all visual optical communication systems. Claude Chappe's semaphore telegraph network covered France in the 1790s, and naval flag signalling remains in use. The heliograph used mirrors to flash sunlight in Morse code. In 1880 Alexander Graham Bell and Charles Sumner Tainter demonstrated the photophone, which transmitted speech on a beam of light; Bell regarded it as his greatest invention.

Fibre-optic communication

Modern optical communication is dominated by fiber-optic communication, in which infrared light pulses travel through hair-thin strands of glass fibre. Key enabling technologies were the laser (1960) and low-loss optical fibre demonstrated by Corning in 1970, following the theoretical proposals of Charles Kao. Fibre systems use wavelength-division multiplexing to carry many channels simultaneously, and erbium-doped fibre amplifiers to extend reach; undersea fibre cables now carry the great majority of intercontinental internet traffic, with capacities of tens of terabits per second per cable.

Free-space optical communication

Free-space optics transmits light through air or vacuum rather than fibre, using lasers for point-to-point links between buildings, satellites and deep-space probes. NASA's laser communications demonstrations have achieved high data rates from lunar and interplanetary distances. Visible light communication (VLC, or Li-Fi) uses modulated LED lighting for short-range indoor links.
