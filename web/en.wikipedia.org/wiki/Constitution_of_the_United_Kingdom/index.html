Constitution of the United Kingdom

The constitution of the United Kingdom comprises the written and unwritten arrangements that establish the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland as a political body. Unlike in most countries, no attempt has been made to codify such arrangements into a single document, and so it is sometimes referred to as an "uncodified" or de facto constitution.

Its sources are diverse: Acts of Parliament, court judgments, parliamentary conventions, and authoritative works of constitutional law. Because Parliament is sovereign, the constitution is not entrenched: Parliament can change it by ordinary legislation, and no Parliament can bind its successors.

Key principles

  • Parliamentary sovereignty — Parliament is the supreme legal authority and can make or unmake any law.
  • The rule of law — government must act according to law, a principle described by A. V. Dicey as a central pillar of the constitution.
  • Unitary state — power is concentrated in Westminster, though since 1998 significant powers have been devolved to Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.
  • Constitutional monarchy — the monarch is head of state, while executive power is exercised by ministers accountable to Parliament.

Fundamental documents

Documents of constitutional significance include Magna Carta (1215), the Petition of Right (1628), the Habeas Corpus Act 1679, the Bill of Rights 1689 and Claim of Right Act 1689, the Acts of Union 1707, the Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949, the Human Rights Act 1998, the Scotland Act 1998, and the Constitutional Reform Act 2005, which created the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom.

Devolution

Since 1998, legislatures in Edinburgh, Cardiff and Belfast have exercised devolved powers over areas such as health and education, while Westminster retains "reserved" matters including foreign affairs and defence. The arrangement rests on the continuing sovereignty of the UK Parliament.
