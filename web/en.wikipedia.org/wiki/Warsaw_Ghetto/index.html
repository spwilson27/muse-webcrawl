Warsaw Ghetto

The Warsaw Ghetto was the largest of the Nazi ghettos during World War II. It was established by Nazi Germany in occupied Warsaw, the capital of Poland, in October–November 1940. More than 400,000 Jews — about 30 percent of the city's population — were imprisoned in an area of roughly 3.4 km² (1.3 sq mi), surrounded by a wall topped with barbed wire and guarded by German and Polish police.

Conditions

Starvation was deliberate policy: official rations for Jews fell to under 200 calories a day, and disease — especially typhus — spread through the overcrowded housing, where an average of seven people shared a room. Smuggling food from the "Aryan side" kept many alive. About 100,000 ghetto residents died of starvation and disease before deportations began. The Judenrat, led by Adam Czerniaków, administered internal affairs under German orders; Czerniaków killed himself in July 1942 rather than deliver children for deportation.

Deportations

During the Grossaktion of summer 1942, roughly 265,000 Jews were deported from the Umschlagplatz to the Treblinka extermination camp and murdered. By early 1943 only some 50,000–70,000 remained.

Uprising

On 19 April 1943, when German forces entered to liquidate the ghetto, the Jewish Combat Organisation (ŻOB) under Mordechai Anielewicz and the Jewish Military Union rose in the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising — the largest act of Jewish resistance during the Holocaust. Jürgen Stroop's forces burned the ghetto block by block; the uprising was crushed by 16 May 1943, when the Great Synagogue was blown up. Some 13,000 Jews were killed in the fighting and almost all the rest deported to camps. Emanuel Ringelblum's Oyneg Shabes archive, buried in milk cans, preserved the ghetto's own record of its life and destruction.
