Vietnamese calendar

The Vietnamese calendar (lịch Việt Nam) is a lunisolar calendar used primarily to determine traditional festivals and holidays in Vietnam, such as Tết (Vietnamese New Year) and the Mid-Autumn Festival. It is based on the Chinese calendar, with modifications reflecting local observations made at the meridian of Vietnam.

History

Vietnam adopted the Chinese calendar during the period of Chinese domination. After gaining independence in the 10th century, Vietnamese dynasties issued their own calendars, computed by court astronomers. Vietnamese calendars diverged from the Chinese in details such as the timing of the new year, because the calculations are performed for a different longitude: the day begins at midnight local time, and the winter solstice month (the eleventh month) is determined by observations at Vietnam's meridian.

Famous differences from the Chinese calendar occurred in 1968, when Tết was celebrated on different days in Vietnam and China, and again in 1985 and 2007, when the Vietnamese and Chinese New Years fell a month apart.

Structure

Like the Chinese calendar, the Vietnamese calendar is lunisolar: months begin on the day of the new moon and normally have 29 or 30 days. A leap month is inserted seven times in a 19-year cycle to keep the calendar in step with the solar year. The year is divided into 24 solar terms (tiết khí).

Years are named using the sexagenary cycle, combining the ten Heavenly Stems (Thiên Can) and twelve Earthly Branches (Địa Chi), the latter corresponding to the twelve zodiac animals.

Differences from the Chinese zodiac

The Vietnamese zodiac differs from the Chinese in one animal: the fourth animal is the cat instead of the rabbit. A common folk explanation holds that the Chinese word for rabbit sounded similar to the Vietnamese word for cat, or that the cat was favoured because it protected rice fields from rats. The buffalo also replaces the ox in the Vietnamese cycle.

Use today

The Gregorian calendar is used for civil purposes in Vietnam, but the lunar calendar remains central to cultural life: weddings, funerals, ancestor worship and festivals are scheduled by it, and most Vietnamese calendars print both systems side by side.
