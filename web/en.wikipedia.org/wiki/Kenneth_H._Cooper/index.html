Kenneth Henry Cooper (March 4, 1931 – February 18, 2025) was an American physician and former United States Air Force officer who popularised the concept of aerobics for physical fitness. His 1968 bestseller Aerobics introduced a point system for quantifying exercise and is widely credited with launching the modern running and fitness boom.

Career

Cooper earned his medical degree from the University of Oklahoma and a doctorate in public health from Harvard. While serving as a flight surgeon and director of the Aerospace Medicine Laboratory in San Antonio, he developed fitness standards for astronauts and aircrew, including the 12-minute run test — the Cooper test — which estimates maximal oxygen uptake (VO2 max) from the distance covered in twelve minutes. After leaving the Air Force as a lieutenant colonel, he founded the Cooper Aerobics Center and the non-profit Cooper Institute in Dallas, Texas, in 1970, conducting landmark longitudinal studies linking cardiorespiratory fitness to reduced mortality.

Books and influence

Cooper wrote more than a dozen books, including Aerobics (1968), The New Aerobics (1970), Running Without Fear (1985) and Regaining the Power of Youth at Any Age (1998). Brazilian football coach Cláudio Coutinho adopted Cooper's methods for the Brazil national team that won the 1970 World Cup, and the Portuguese-derived word "cooper" became a common term for jogging in Brazil. His work helped shift preventive medicine toward regular physical exercise.
