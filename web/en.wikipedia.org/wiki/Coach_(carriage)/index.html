Coach (carriage)

A coach is a large, closed, four-wheeled, horse-drawn carriage for the conveyance of passengers. The word derives from the Hungarian kocsi, meaning a wagon from the village of Kocs in Hungary, where such vehicles were first made in the 15th century. Coaches became the principal means of long-distance road transport in Europe and North America until the coming of the railways in the 19th century.

History

The earliest coaches were little more than covered wagons, but by the 17th century improvements such as steel springs and leather thoroughbrace suspension made long journeys tolerable. Coaches were driven by a coachman seated on a box at the front, and could carry passengers inside and sometimes on the roof.

Types of coach

The stagecoach ran on scheduled routes between coaching inns, carrying passengers and mail; the mail coach, introduced in Britain in 1784, combined passenger service with the fast transport of mail. Private coaches included the barouche, the landau, and the state coach used for ceremonial occasions, such as the Gold State Coach used by British monarchs since 1762.

Decline and legacy

Railways displaced the stagecoach from long-distance routes in the mid-19th century, though coaches continued in use for local transport and ceremony. The word "coach" survived into the age of motor transport, applied to motor coaches (buses) and to railway passenger carriages, and figuratively to a class of air travel.
