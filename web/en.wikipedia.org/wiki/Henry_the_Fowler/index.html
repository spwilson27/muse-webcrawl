Henry the Fowler (German: Heinrich der Vogler or Heinrich der Finkler; c. 876 – 2 July 936) was the duke of Saxony from 912 and the king of East Francia from 919 until his death in 936. As the first non-Frankish ruler of East Francia, he is considered the founder of the Ottonian dynasty of German kings and emperors and is often regarded as the founder of the medieval German state. He was an able ruler who consolidated his kingdom, defended it against the Magyar invasions, expanded its territory eastward, and passed a strengthened crown to his son Otto I.

Accession

Henry succeeded his father Otto the Illustrious as duke of Saxony in 912. When King Conrad I died in December 918, he is said to have designated Henry as his successor. Henry was elected king at Fritzlar in May 919 by the Saxons and Franks. According to a later legend, he received the delegation while setting bird snares — hence his epithet "the Fowler". Unlike his Carolingian predecessors, Henry reportedly declined anointing by the archbishop of Mainz, preferring to rule as first among equals with the dukes.

Consolidation of the realm

Henry recognised the stem duchies and allowed the dukes substantial autonomy in exchange for loyalty. He won over Burchard II of Swabia, defeated and won the friendship of Eberhard of Franconia, and in 921 secured recognition from Charles the Simple of West Francia by the Treaty of Bonn. In 925 he brought Lotharingia back under East Frankish control after its duke Gilbert submitted — and gave him his daughter Gerberga in marriage.

Defence against the Magyars

The gravest external threat came from the Magyar horsemen of Hungary, whose raids had devastated German lands for decades. In 924 Henry concluded a nine-year truce, paying tribute, and used the respite to build fortified settlements (the Burgenordnung), garrison them with soldier-farmers, and train a heavy cavalry force. When the truce expired in 933 and Henry refused further tribute, he defeated the Magyars decisively at the Battle of Riade, ending the large-scale invasions of his reign.

Expansion and death

In the east, Henry campaigned against the Slavic peoples beyond the Elbe, capturing Brandenburg and establishing the margraviate system that his successors extended. He also intervened in Denmark and Bohemia, compelling Wenceslaus I of Bohemia to acknowledge his overlordship. Henry died of a stroke at Memleben on 2 July 936 and was buried at Quedlinburg Abbey. His succession arrangements — designating Otto as sole heir — averted a division of the kingdom.
