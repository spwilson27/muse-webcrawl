Rosser Reeves

Born18 September 1910, Danville, Virginia, U.S.
Died24 January 1984 (aged 73)
OccupationAdvertising executive, author

Rosser Reeves (September 18, 1910 – January 24, 1984) was an American advertising executive and pioneer of television advertising. As chairman of the Ted Bates agency, he became the leading exponent of the "hard sell" and of the concept he called the Unique Selling Proposition (USP): every advertisement must offer the consumer one specific benefit, state it relentlessly, and be built around a proposition competitors could not or did not offer.

Career

Reeves was born in Danville, Virginia, and attended the University of Virginia before dropping out after being caught cheating in a poker game, as he himself told it. He worked as a journalist and in Richmond advertising before joining the Ted Bates agency in New York in 1940, rising to become its creative head and eventually chairman.

At Bates, Reeves created some of the most famous — and most repetitive — campaigns of early television. For Anacin pain reliever he hammered home the claim that it contained "the ingredients doctors recommend most" and worked "fast, fast, fast", with spots showing a hammer pounding inside a head. For Colgate he devised the dental-cream claim based on "Gardol"; for M&M's, "The milk chocolate melts in your mouth — not in your hand"; and for Bic pens, demonstrations of pens being fired from crossbows and still writing. His agency was among the first major shops to embrace television.

Reality in Advertising

In 1961 Reeves codified his philosophy in the best-selling book Reality in Advertising, which remains a classic of the field. He argued that advertising works through a single strong claim repeated until it achieves "penetration", and that originality and entertainment were worthless unless they reinforced the selling proposition. He distinguished the USP from empty "vampire" creativity that audiences remembered while forgetting the product.

Reeves was also involved in politics, contributing to the pioneering spot-television campaign of Dwight D. Eisenhower in 1952 ("Eisenhower Answers America"). A man of varied interests, he was a serious chess and backgammon player and a published poet. His hard-sell school fell out of fashion during the 1960s "creative revolution" led by figures such as Bill Bernbach and David Ogilvy, but Reeves's USP concept remains a standard term in marketing.
