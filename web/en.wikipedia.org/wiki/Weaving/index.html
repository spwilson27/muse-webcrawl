Weaving

Weaving
MaterialsWarp and weft yarns (cotton, wool, silk, linen, synthetics)
EquipmentLoom
Basic structuresPlain weave, twill, satin

Weaving is a method of textile production in which two distinct sets of yarns or threads are interlaced at right angles to form a fabric or cloth. The longitudinal threads are called the warp and the lateral threads the weft or filling. The way the warp and weft interlace determines the weave structure — plain weave, twill and satin being the fundamental types — and cloth is usually woven on a loom, a device that holds the warp threads in tension while the weft is inserted.

History

Weaving is among the oldest surviving human technologies, practised in the Neolithic period; woven flax fibres dated to around 34,000 years ago have been reported from Dzudzuana Cave in Georgia, and loom weights and spindle whorls are common archaeological finds. Weaving was a household craft for millennia — in Greek myth Athena and Arachne contested at the loom — and became a major urban trade in medieval Europe. Mechanisation transformed it during the Industrial Revolution: Edmund Cartwright's power loom (1785), the Jacquard head (1804) for patterned weaving using punched cards, and the Lancashire and Lowell mill systems made woven cloth a mass-produced commodity, at heavy social cost to displaced handloom weavers such as the Luddites and Silesian weavers.

Process

Warp yarns are first wound onto a warp beam and threaded through heddles and a reed. Weaving proceeds by three primary motions: shedding (separating warp threads to form an opening), picking (inserting the weft, traditionally with a shuttle, in modern looms by air jet, water jet, rapier or projectile) and beating-up (pushing the new weft pick into place). Pattern possibilities range from simple checks and stripes to tapestry, brocade, velvet (with an extra pile warp) and double cloth.

Modern industry

Today almost all commercial weaving is done by computerised shuttleless looms at speeds exceeding a thousand picks per minute, while hand weaving continues as an artisan craft and art form worldwide. Woven fabrics dominate apparel, upholstery, sailcloth, geotextiles and technical composites.
