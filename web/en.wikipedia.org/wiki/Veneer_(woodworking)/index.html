Veneer (woodworking)

In woodworking, veneer refers to thin slices of wood, usually thinner than 3 mm, that are glued onto core panels of wood, particleboard or medium-density fibreboard to produce flat surfaces such as doors, cabinet panels and furniture tops.

Production

Veneer is obtained by peeling a tree trunk on a rotary lathe or by slicing large rectangular blocks of wood known as flitches. The appearance of the grain depends on how the log is cut: rotary cutting produces a broad, variegated figure, while quarter slicing yields straight stripes. Species prized for figure include walnut burl, bird's-eye maple, oak, mahogany and exotic tropical hardwoods.

Uses

Veneering allows attractive or rare woods to cover stable, inexpensive substrates, and allows matching of grain patterns across large surfaces through techniques such as book-matching. It has been used since ancient Egyptian times, reached high art in marquetry and the furniture of the 18th century, and remains standard in both fine furniture and mass-produced flat-pack goods, where printed or melamine surfaces sometimes imitate it.
