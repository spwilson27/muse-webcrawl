Friedman test

The Friedman test is a non-parametric statistical test developed by Milton Friedman in 1937. It is used to detect differences in treatments across multiple test attempts, and is an alternative to the repeated measures ANOVA when the assumption of normality is in doubt. The procedure involves ranking each block (or row) together and considering the values of ranks by columns.

Procedure

Given data from n blocks (for example, subjects) each measured under k treatments, observations within each block are ranked from 1 to k, with average ranks assigned to ties. The test statistic compares the sums of ranks across treatments: if treatments have no effect, the rank sums should be approximately equal. Under the null hypothesis the statistic approximately follows a chi-squared distribution with k − 1 degrees of freedom; for small samples, exact tables or the Iman–Davenport F-statistic refinement are preferred.

Use and follow-up

The Friedman test is widely used in sensory evaluation, clinical studies and machine learning, where it is commonly used to compare multiple algorithms over multiple data sets. A significant result indicates that at least one treatment differs, after which post hoc tests — such as the Nemenyi test or pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with correction for multiple comparisons — identify which treatments differ.
