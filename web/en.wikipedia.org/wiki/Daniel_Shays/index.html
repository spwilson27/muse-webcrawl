Daniel Shays (August 1747 – September 29, 1825) was an American soldier and farmer from Massachusetts. He served as a captain in the Continental Army during the American Revolutionary War, fighting at the battles of Lexington and Concord, Bunker Hill, and Saratoga. After the war he became the namesake leader of Shays's Rebellion (1786–1787), an armed uprising of indebted farmers in western Massachusetts protesting high taxes and debt foreclosures.

Early life and military service

Shays was born in Hopkinton, Massachusetts, to Irish immigrant parents. He worked as a farm laborer and enlisted in the colonial militia at the outbreak of the Revolution, rising to captain in the 5th Massachusetts Regiment. He was reportedly presented with a ceremonial sword by the Marquis de Lafayette in recognition of his service.

Shays's Rebellion

Like many veterans, Shays returned from the war to find himself in debt and was taken to court for non-payment. In 1786 he joined protests that closed courts in western Massachusetts to prevent foreclosure proceedings. In January 1787 he led some 1,200–1,500 men in an attempt to seize the federal arsenal at Springfield; the attack was repelled by militia artillery, and the rebellion was soon dispersed by a privately funded army under Benjamin Lincoln.

Later life

Shays fled to Vermont and was sentenced to death in absentia, but he petitioned for and received a pardon in 1788. He later moved to Sparta, New York, where he lived in poverty, receiving a small pension for his Revolutionary service. He died in 1825. The rebellion he led alarmed national leaders and is widely credited with strengthening support for replacing the Articles of Confederation with the United States Constitution.
