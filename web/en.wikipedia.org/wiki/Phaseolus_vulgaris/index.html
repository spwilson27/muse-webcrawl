Phaseolus vulgaris

Phaseolus vulgaris, the common bean, is a herbaceous annual plant grown worldwide for its edible dry seeds (beans) or its unripe fruit (green beans). It is a member of the legume family Fabaceae and one of the most important food legumes, especially in Latin America and Africa. Nearly all beans familiar in Western cooking — kidney, pinto, navy, black, and many others — are cultivars of this single species.

Domestication

The wild ancestor of P. vulgaris is native to the Americas. Genetic and archaeological evidence indicates two independent domestications, in Mesoamerica and in the southern Andes, producing two major gene pools with distinct seed sizes and characteristics. Beans spread to Europe after the Columbian exchange and thence across the world.

Cultivation and varieties

Common beans grow as bush plants or climbing vines with trifoliate leaves and white, pink, or purple flowers. Well-known cultivar groups include kidney beans, pinto beans, black beans, navy (haricot) beans, cranberry or borlotti beans, and French/green beans harvested immature. Like other legumes, the plants fix nitrogen via root-nodule bacteria, improving soil fertility.

Nutrition and toxicity

Dry beans are rich in protein, complex carbohydrates, folate, iron, and dietary fibre. Raw or undercooked beans — especially red kidney beans — contain the lectin phytohaemagglutinin, which causes severe gastrointestinal illness and must be destroyed by thorough boiling.
