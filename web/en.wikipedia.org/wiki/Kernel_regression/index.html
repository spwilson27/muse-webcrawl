Kernel regression

Kernel regression is a non-parametric technique in statistics to estimate the conditional expectation of a random variable. The objective is to find a non-linear relation between a pair of random variables X and Y.

Introduction

In any nonparametric regression, the conditional expectation of a variable Y relative to a variable X may be written:

E(Y | X) = m(X)

where m is an unknown function. Kernel regression estimates m by locally weighting the observed data points with a kernel function, so that observations closer to the point of interest receive greater weight. Unlike linear regression, no global functional form — such as a straight line — is assumed in advance; the shape of the curve is determined by the data themselves.

The technique is closely related to kernel density estimation and to smoothing methods such as moving averages and splines. Its accuracy depends strongly on the choice of the kernel and, most importantly, on the bandwidth parameter, which controls the degree of smoothing.

Nadaraya–Watson kernel regression

The Nadaraya–Watson estimator, proposed independently by Nadaraya (1964) and Watson (1964), estimates m(x) as a locally weighted average using a kernel as a weighting function:

m̂(x) = Σ Kh(x − xi) yi / Σ Kh(x − xi)

where Kh is a kernel with bandwidth h. Common choices of kernel include the Gaussian kernel and the Epanechnikov kernel. The denominator is a weighting term that acts as a normalizing constant and is related to the estimated density of X at the point x.

Priestley–Chao kernel estimator

The Priestley–Chao estimator, introduced by M. B. Priestley and M. T. Chao in 1972, was designed for cases in which the design points are ordered, as in time series. It weights successive differences of the ordered observations by kernel weights and does not require dividing by an estimated density, which can make it more stable in some settings.

Gasser–Müller kernel estimator

The Gasser–Müller estimator, proposed by Theo Gasser and Hans-Georg Müller, integrates the kernel over intervals defined by midpoints between consecutive design points. It was developed partly to reduce the boundary bias that simple kernel smoothers exhibit near the edges of the data range.

Bandwidth selection

The choice of bandwidth h governs the bias–variance tradeoff: a small bandwidth yields a curve that tracks the data closely but is noisy (high variance), whereas a large bandwidth oversmooths, washing out genuine structure (high bias). Practical methods include cross-validation, plug-in rules based on estimated curvature, and rules of thumb analogous to those used in kernel density estimation.
