Carbon-fiber-reinforced polymers

Carbon fiber reinforced polymer (CFRP), also called carbon fiber reinforced plastic or simply carbon fiber, is an extremely strong and light fiber-reinforced plastic that contains carbon fibers embedded in a polymer resin matrix, most commonly epoxy. The composite combines the high tensile strength and low weight of carbon fibers with a matrix that holds them in alignment and transfers loads between them. CFRP has one of the highest strength-to-weight ratios of any structural material and is used extensively in aerospace, motorsport, high-end bicycles, sporting goods, and increasingly in automotive and civil engineering applications.

Manufacturing

Carbon fibers are produced from precursors such as polyacrylonitrile (PAN) or pitch by oxidisation, carbonisation, and graphitisation, yielding filaments about 5–10 micrometres in diameter. The fibers are woven into cloth or used as unidirectional tape, then combined with resin by processes including:

  • Prepreg lay-up — resin-impregnated sheets are stacked in chosen orientations and cured under heat and pressure in an autoclave.
  • Resin transfer moulding and vacuum infusion — dry fiber is placed in a mould and resin injected or drawn through.
  • Filament winding — for pressure vessels and tubes.
  • Pultrusion — for constant-section profiles used in construction.
  • Forged/compression-moulded carbon — chopped fibers in resin pressed in a mould, giving a marbled appearance.

Properties are strongly anisotropic: a laminate is stiff and strong along its fiber directions, so engineers tailor ply orientations (0°, ±45°, 90°) to the loads.

Applications

The Boeing 787 and Airbus A350 use CFRP for about half their airframe weight, including fuselage barrels and wings. Formula One monocoques have been carbon since the McLaren MP4/1 of 1981, dramatically improving driver survival. Other uses include bicycle frames, tennis rackets, fishing rods, wind-turbine blades, pressure vessels for hydrogen storage, prosthetics, and strengthening wraps for concrete bridges and buildings. Drawbacks include high cost, brittleness under impact (with hard-to-detect internal delamination), galvanic corrosion when joined to aluminium, and difficult recycling.
