Karl Anders Ericsson (23 October 1947 – 17 June 2020) was a Swedish cognitive psychologist best known for his research on expertise and human performance. As Conradi Eminent Scholar at Florida State University, he founded the modern scientific study of expert performance and developed the concept of deliberate practice — structured, feedback-rich training activities designed specifically to improve performance.

Research

Ericsson earned his doctorate at Stockholm University in 1976 and worked with Nobel laureate Herbert A. Simon at Carnegie Mellon University, with whom he developed protocol analysis — the use of verbal reports as data — set out in their book Protocol Analysis (1984). With Walter Kintsch he proposed "long-term working memory" to explain how experts circumvent normal memory limits, building on his earlier case study of a runner (subject SF) who, after some 230 hours of practice, expanded his digit span from 7 to over 80 digits using mnemonic chunking.

Deliberate practice and the 10,000 hours

In a landmark 1993 paper with Ralf Krampe and Clemens Tesch-Römer, studying violin students at a Berlin music academy, Ericsson argued that what distinguished the best performers was not innate talent but the accumulated amount of solitary deliberate practice — the top group had averaged about 10,000 hours by age twenty. Malcolm Gladwell popularised this finding in Outliers (2008) as the "10,000-hour rule". Ericsson objected to the popularisation: the figure was an average, not a magic threshold; the practice must be deliberate, with a teacher, feedback, and work on specific weaknesses; and Gladwell had applied the idea to fields (such as entrepreneurship) where Ericsson's research did not apply. Ericsson set out his own account for general readers in Peak: Secrets from the New Science of Expertise (2016), co-authored with Robert Pool.

Debate

Later meta-analyses, notably by Brooke Macnamara and colleagues, found that deliberate practice explains a substantial but variable share of performance differences — large in games and music, smaller in education and professional work — prompting debate about the roles of working memory, starting age, and other factors. Ericsson maintained that properly measured deliberate practice remained the dominant known contributor to individual differences in expert performance.
