The Mark of Zorro is a 1920 American silent swashbuckler film directed by Fred Niblo and starring Douglas Fairbanks, who also produced the film and co-wrote the screenplay. It is based on the 1919 novella The Curse of Capistrano by Johnston McCulley, which introduced the character of Zorro. The film was the first production released by United Artists to become a major commercial success and established Fairbanks's swashbuckling screen persona.

Plot

In Spanish-controlled California of the early 19th century, the masked swordsman Zorro ("the Fox") emerges to defend the oppressed natives and peasants against the tyrannical Governor Alvarado and his henchman Captain Juan Ramon. Zorro signs his work by carving a "Z" with his blade. By day he is Don Diego Vega, who feigns laziness and foppishness to conceal his identity, courting the impoverished noblewoman Lolita Pulido while defending her family as Zorro. After a series of duels, rescues, and chases, Zorro rallies the caballeros to depose the governor, reveals his identity, and wins Lolita's hand.

Legacy

The film was a landmark in the development of the action-adventure genre and popularised the dual-identity masked hero; Fairbanks's acrobatic stunt work set a new standard for screen swashbuckling. Zorro became one of the most enduring characters in popular fiction, and the film influenced later masked heroes including Batman, whose creators cited Zorro as an inspiration—indeed, in Batman lore, the film Bruce Wayne saw on the night his parents were murdered is The Mark of Zorro. Fairbanks made a sequel, Don Q, Son of Zorro, in 1925. The 1920 film has been preserved in various restorations and remains the best-known of the silent Zorro adaptations.
