The Quiet Revolution (French: Révolution tranquille) was a period of intense socio-political and cultural change in the Canadian province of Quebec during the 1960s. It transformed a society long dominated by the Roman Catholic Church, rural conservatism, and the patronage regime of premier Maurice Duplessis into a modern, secular, nationalist, and interventionist state.

Course of the revolution

The revolution is conventionally dated from the 1960 provincial election that brought Jean Lesage's Liberal Party to power on the slogan "It's time for a change". The new government rapidly expanded the role of the provincial state: it created a Ministry of Education (1964) and reformed schooling after the Parent Report, founded the Société générale de financement, established a modern civil service, and, under minister René Lévesque, completed the nationalisation of private electricity companies into Hydro-Québec in 1962–63 under the slogan Maîtres chez nous — "masters in our own house". Quebec also opted out of federal programmes, created its own pension plan (1966), and liberalised its labour code.

Effects

Church attendance and vocations collapsed, the birth rate fell from among Canada's highest to among its lowest, and francophones moved into senior positions in an economy long dominated by anglophone capital. The transformation was accompanied by a new assertive Quebec nationalism: the Quiet Revolution laid the groundwork for the sovereignty movement, the election of Lévesque's Parti Québécois in 1976, and the referendums of 1980 and 1995. The term "Quiet Revolution" captures the paradox that Quebec's society was revolutionised within a decade, largely without violence.
