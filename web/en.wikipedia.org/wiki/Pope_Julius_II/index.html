Pope Julius II (Latin: Iulius II; Italian: Giulio II; born Giuliano della Rovere, 5 December 1443 – 21 February 1513), nicknamed the Warrior Pope and il papa terribile ("the terrible pope"), was head of the Catholic Church and ruler of the Papal States from 1503 to his death. As a cardinal he was a nephew of Pope Sixtus IV and the great rival of Rodrigo Borgia (Pope Alexander VI), during whose pontificate he lived in exile in France. Julius is remembered as one of the most powerful and influential popes: he personally led armies to secure the Papal States, shifted the alliances of the Italian Wars, and commissioned the supreme masterpieces of High Renaissance art.

Pontificate and wars

Elected in the shortest conclave of the era, Julius set out to restore papal control over the Papal States, expelling Cesare Borgia and reducing rebellious barons and petty tyrants. He formed the League of Cambrai (1508) with France, Spain, and the Empire against Venice, then reversed course, proclaiming a Holy League (1511) with Venice and Spain to drive the French — his former allies — out of Italy ("Fuori i barbari!"). He campaigned in person, in armour, at the sieges of Mirandola and elsewhere, an unprecedented spectacle for a pope. In 1512 he convened the Fifth Council of the Lateran to reform the Church and counter the schismatic Council of Pisa. Julius also founded the Swiss Guard in 1506.

Patronage of the arts

Julius's patronage defined the High Renaissance in Rome. In 1506 he laid the foundation stone of the new St Peter's Basilica, designed by Bramante — the largest church in Christendom. He commissioned Michelangelo to paint the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel (1508–1512) and to carve his (ultimately much-reduced) tomb, including the statue of Moses. Raphael he employed to fresco the papal apartments, the Stanza della Segnatura among them, producing The School of Athens and Disputation of the Holy Sacrament. His programme aimed to make Rome, and the papacy, the centre of a renewed Christian empire.

Legacy

Julius left the Papal States territorially consolidated and the papal treasury full, but his militarism and worldliness drew fierce criticism — Erasmus satirized him in the dialogue Julius Excluded from Heaven — and his wars helped set the pattern of foreign domination of Italy for three centuries. Machiavelli admired his impetuous energy while noting that fortune favoured his boldness. He is generally ranked among the most consequential Renaissance popes.
