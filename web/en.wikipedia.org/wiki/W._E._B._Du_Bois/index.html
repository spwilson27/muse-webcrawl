William Edward Burghardt Du Bois (1868–1963) was an American sociologist, historian, author and civil rights activist. One of the founders of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) in 1909, he was the leading Black intellectual of his era and a foundational figure in American sociology.

Early life and education

Du Bois was born in Great Barrington, Massachusetts, and graduated from Fisk University before becoming, in 1895, the first African American to earn a PhD from Harvard University. His dissertation, The Suppression of the African Slave-Trade to the United States of America, 1638–1870, launched a scholarly career that included the pioneering empirical study The Philadelphia Negro (1899) and a long professorship at Atlanta University.

The Souls of Black Folk and the Niagara Movement

His 1903 essay collection The Souls of Black Folk introduced the concepts of "double consciousness" and the "veil", and famously declared that "the problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color-line". Du Bois broke publicly with Booker T. Washington's accommodationist strategy, demanding full political rights; in 1905 he helped found the Niagara Movement, and in 1909 the interracial NAACP, whose magazine The Crisis he edited until 1934. His advocacy operated within the constitutional frame created by the Fourteenth Amendment, whose promises of equal protection he insisted America honour.

Later career and Pan-Africanism

Du Bois organised a series of Pan-African Congresses and increasingly embraced socialist and anti-colonial positions. His monumental Black Reconstruction in America (1935) rewrote the history of the post-Civil War era. Harassed during the McCarthy era — he was indicted in 1951 as an unregistered foreign agent and acquitted — he emigrated to Ghana at Kwame Nkrumah's invitation in 1961, took Ghanaian citizenship, and died in Accra on 27 August 1963, on the eve of the March on Washington.

Legacy

Du Bois is credited with founding American scientific sociology, and his ideas on race, identity and democracy remain central to the humanities and social sciences.
