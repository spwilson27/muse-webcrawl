Hōryū-ji

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hōryū-ji (Japanese: 法隆寺, "Temple of the Flourishing Dharma") is a Buddhist temple in Ikaruga, Nara Prefecture, Japan. Founded in 607 by Prince Shōtoku and Empress Suiko, it is famous for containing some of the oldest surviving wooden buildings in the world, dating from the Asuka period. The temple complex covers about 187,000 square metres and was among the first sites in Japan inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List, in 1993, as part of the "Buddhist Monuments in the Hōryū-ji Area".

History

The original temple, known as Wakakusa-garan, burned down in 670 according to the Nihon Shoki, though some scholars debate whether a fire occurred. The temple was rebuilt by the early 8th century, and its five-storey pagoda (gojū-no-tō) and main hall (kondō) survive from that era. Hōryū-ji was closely associated with Prince Shōtoku, regarded as a patron of Japanese Buddhism, and became a centre of learning for the Hossō school.

Architecture and treasures

The western precinct (Sai-in) contains the pagoda and main hall arranged side by side, an arrangement characteristic of early Japanese temple planning. The kondō preserves celebrated wall paintings of the Pure Land paradise, damaged by fire in 1949 — an incident that spurred Japan's modern cultural property protection law. The eastern precinct (Tō-in) centres on the octagonal Yumedono, the Hall of Dreams, associated with Prince Shōtoku. The temple's treasure house holds masterpieces such as the Shaka triad by the sculptor Tori Busshi and the Kudara Kannon.

Significance

Hōryū-ji is a cornerstone of Japanese Buddhist art and architecture and remains an active temple of the Shōtoku-shū sect, which it heads.
