Order of succession

A line of succession depicted as a family tree

An order of succession or right of succession is the line of individuals entitled to hold a high office when it becomes vacated, such as the head of state or an honour such as a title of nobility. The sequence may be regulated by descent, entail, or statute, and it determines who inherits a throne, office, or property when the incumbent dies, abdicates, is deposed, or is otherwise removed.

Hereditary succession

In monarchies, succession is usually hereditary and governed by primogeniture. Under male-preference primogeniture, sons take precedence over daughters; under absolute primogeniture — adopted by Sweden (1980), the Netherlands, Belgium, Denmark, and (for those born after 2011) the United Kingdom — the eldest child succeeds regardless of sex. Other systems include agnatic succession (Salic law), which excludes women entirely, and systems of election or rotation among branches of a family.

Presidential lines of succession

Republics also maintain statutory succession orders for executive offices. In the United States, the Presidential Succession Act of 1947 places the Vice President first, followed by the Speaker of the House, the President pro tempore of the Senate, and then cabinet secretaries in the order their departments were created.

Related concepts

An heir apparent is first in line and cannot be displaced by the birth of another; an heir presumptive may be. Lines of succession can extend to dozens or even thousands of traced individuals, as with the British line, which includes all legitimate descendants of Sophia, Electress of Hanover.
