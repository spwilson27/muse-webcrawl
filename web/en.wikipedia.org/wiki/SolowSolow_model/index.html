Solow–Swan model

This article is about the exogenous economic growth model also known as the Solow growth model. (Article stored under the concatenated slug "SolowSolow".)

The Solow–Swan model, or Solow growth model, is a standard model of long-run economic growth in neoclassical economics. Published independently by Robert Solow (1956) and Trevor Swan (1956), it explains growth through capital accumulation, labour (population) growth, and an exogenously given rate of technological progress. It remains the starting point for most modern analysis of economic growth, and Solow received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1987 for his contributions to growth theory.

The Solow diagram: the economy converges to a steady state where investment equals break-even depreciation and dilution.

The model

Output is produced with a neoclassical production function, commonly Cobb–Douglas, Y = Kα(AL)1−α, exhibiting diminishing returns to capital. A constant fraction s of output is saved and invested; capital depreciates at rate δ; the labour force grows at rate n and labour-augmenting technology at rate g. In terms of capital per effective worker, accumulation follows k̇ = s f(k) − (n + g + δ)k.

The economy converges to a steady state in which capital per effective worker is constant. In steady state, output per worker grows exactly at the rate of technological progress g — so sustained growth in living standards comes only from technology, which the model takes as given ("exogenous"). Capital accumulation alone cannot sustain growth because of diminishing returns.

Implications

  • Conditional convergence: poorer economies with the same fundamentals should grow faster and catch up toward their steady states — an implication tested extensively in the empirical growth literature (e.g. Mankiw, Romer and Weil, 1992, who augmented the model with human capital).
  • Saving: a higher saving rate raises the level of income but not its long-run growth rate; the "golden rule" saving rate maximises steady-state consumption.
  • Growth accounting: Solow's related 1957 work attributed a large share of US growth to a residual — total factor productivity — motivating later endogenous growth theory (Romer, Lucas), which seeks to explain technology itself.
