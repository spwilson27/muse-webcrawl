Multisample anti-aliasing (MSAA) is a technique of spatial anti-aliasing used in real-time computer graphics to remove jaggies — the stair-step artefacts on the edges of rendered polygons. It is a more efficient relative of supersampling anti-aliasing (SSAA), popularized by graphics hardware in the early 2000s through APIs such as Direct3D and OpenGL.

How it works

In classic supersampling, the scene is rendered at a higher resolution and downsampled, so the fragment (pixel) shader runs many times per pixel. MSAA instead separates coverage sampling from shading: the GPU evaluates coverage, depth and stencil at multiple sub-pixel sample positions per pixel (2×, 4×, 8× and higher), but runs the fragment shader only once per pixel for each primitive covering it, sharing the resulting colour among the covered samples. When a pixel straddles a polygon edge, its samples are split between the two surfaces and are averaged at resolve time, producing a smooth edge at a fraction of the cost of full supersampling.

Advantages and limitations

MSAA greatly reduces edge aliasing on geometry with modest performance and memory costs (though the multi-sampled framebuffer still increases memory bandwidth). Its main limitation is that it does not anti-alias within polygons: texture aliasing, shader aliasing and transparency effects (such as alpha-tested foliage) receive no benefit, because only one shaded value is produced per pixel. This weakness, together with the rise of deferred rendering engines — in which MSAA is harder to apply — led games to adopt post-process methods such as FXAA and temporal anti-aliasing (TAA).
