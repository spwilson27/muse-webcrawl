Wormwood (Artemisia absinthium), also called common wormwood or absinthium, is a species of flowering plant in the aster family, Asteraceae, native to temperate regions of Eurasia and North Africa and widely naturalised elsewhere. It is best known as a key ingredient of the spirit absinthe and as one of the most bitter substances used in traditional medicine and flavouring.

Description

Wormwood is a herbaceous perennial growing 0.8–1.2 m tall, with a fibrous rootstock and straight, grooved stems. The leaves are silvery-green, covered in fine silky hairs, spirally arranged and deeply divided; the small, pale yellow, globular flower heads hang in leafy panicles and appear in summer. The whole plant is intensely bitter owing to compounds including absinthin, and it contains the monoterpene thujone in its essential oil.

Uses

Wormwood has been used since antiquity as a vermifuge (to expel intestinal worms — hence its name), a digestive bitter and a treatment for fevers. Its most famous use is in absinthe, the anise-flavoured spirit popular in 19th-century France, in which wormwood is combined with green anise and fennel; it is also used in vermouth, bitters and some other liqueurs. Thujone was once blamed for absinthe's supposed hallucinogenic effects, though modern analysis shows historic absinthes contained only small amounts and their effects are attributable chiefly to their high alcohol content.

Cultivation and toxicity

The plant prefers dry, sunny, uncultivated ground and is easily grown in poor soils; it spreads readily and can be invasive. Pure wormwood oil is toxic, and concentrated preparations should not be ingested; the herb is regulated as a food flavouring in many jurisdictions.

In culture

Wormwood is mentioned in the Bible as a symbol of bitterness and calamity — notably in the Book of Revelation, where a star named Wormwood falls and poisons the waters — and appears frequently in literature as an emblem of grief and gall.
