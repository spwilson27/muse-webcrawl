Gyrification

Gyrification is the process of forming the characteristic folds of the cerebral cortex. The peak of such a fold is called a gyrus (plural: gyri), and its valley is called a sulcus (plural: sulci). Neurons of the cerebral cortex reside in a thin layer of grey matter, only 2–4 millimetres thick, at the surface of the brain. This layer is thrown into folds in most mammals, greatly increasing the surface area — and thus the number of cortical neurons — that can fit within the skull.

Gyri and sulci of the human brain

Development

The human cortex is smooth (lissencephalic) early in gestation; primary sulci appear from around the twentieth week of fetal development, and gyrification proceeds rapidly in the third trimester, driven by differential growth of the cortical sheet, proliferation of progenitor cells in the outer subventricular zone, and mechanical tension along axonal connections, among other proposed mechanisms. The degree of folding is quantified by the gyrification index — the ratio of the total cortical surface area to the area of its exposed outer hull — which is about 2.5 in humans and higher in some cetaceans.

Clinical significance

Abnormal gyrification is associated with neurodevelopmental disorders. In lissencephaly ("smooth brain"), failed neuronal migration leaves the cortex nearly unfolded, causing severe intellectual disability. Polymicrogyria involves excessive small folds, and pachygyria abnormally thick, reduced folds. Differences in folding patterns have also been studied in relation to schizophrenia, autism and other conditions, and cortical folding continues to be refined in childhood and adolescence.
