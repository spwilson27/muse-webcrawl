The 1949 Geneva Convention standardised rules for international road traffic after the Second World War.

The Geneva Convention on Road Traffic, formally the Convention on Road Traffic, is an international treaty concluded at Geneva on 19 September 1949 and entered into force on 26 March 1952. Prepared under the auspices of the United Nations, it promotes the development and safety of international road traffic by establishing uniform rules on the admission of vehicles and drivers across borders, driving permits, vehicle registration, and basic rules of the road. It updated and replaced earlier instruments, including the 1926 Paris conventions on motor traffic and road traffic.

Background

International motoring grew rapidly after the Second World War, and motorists crossing borders faced a patchwork of national rules. A United Nations conference on road and motor transport, held in Geneva in August–September 1949, adopted the Convention together with protocols on road signs and signals and on the countries and territories then in a state of war-related transition. The Convention sought a middle way between total uniformity and respect for national law: contracting states undertook to admit each other's vehicles and drivers provided common minimum conditions were met.

Main provisions

  • Admission of vehicles — motor vehicles and trailers registered in a contracting state may circulate in other contracting states if they meet the Convention's technical conditions and carry a registration certificate, registration plates and a distinguishing sign of the country of registration (the oval country sticker).
  • Driving permits — drivers holding a valid domestic permit meeting the Convention's standards may drive in other contracting states; the Convention also created the model International Driving Permit, a multilingual translation booklet valid for a maximum of one year.
  • Rules of the road — basic obligations such as keeping to a consistent side of the road within a territory, rules for overtaking, and requirements for vehicle lighting and brakes.
  • Distinguishing signs — the annexed list of national distinguishing signs (e.g. F for France, D for Germany, GB for the United Kingdom) displayed on vehicles abroad.

Relation to the Vienna Convention

In 1968 a further conference in Vienna adopted the Vienna Convention on Road Traffic, which provides more detailed and prescriptive rules and a three-year international permit. Many states are party to both conventions; between parties to both, the Vienna Convention generally governs, while the Geneva Convention continues to regulate relations with states — such as the United States — that adhere only to the 1949 instrument. Because major motoring countries remain Geneva-only, the 1949 Convention's one-year IDP remains the most widely accepted format worldwide.

Parties

The Convention has been ratified or acceded to by around one hundred states and remains one of the foundational instruments of international road traffic law, alongside the companion conventions on road signs and signals and the customs conventions governing temporary importation of vehicles. Its framework supports cross-border motoring, tourism and haulage, and underpins the global system of road traffic safety cooperation.
