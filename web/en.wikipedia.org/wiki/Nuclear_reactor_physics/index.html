Nuclear reactor physics

Nuclear reactor physics is the field of physics that studies and deals with the application of neutron behaviour and nuclear chain reactions in nuclear reactors. It provides the theoretical basis for reactor design, covering how neutrons are produced by fission, slowed down (moderated), absorbed, or leak from the core, and how these processes determine whether a reactor is critical, subcritical or supercritical.

Key concepts

The central quantity is the effective multiplication factor keff: when it equals one, the chain reaction is self-sustaining (critical). Reactor physicists use the four-factor formula and transport or diffusion theory to model neutron populations across energy groups and space. Reactivity feedback effects — such as Doppler broadening in fuel, moderator temperature and density changes, and void coefficients — govern stability and safety. Delayed neutrons, a small fraction of neutrons emitted seconds after fission by fission-product nuclei, make practical control of reactors possible. Other central topics include fuel burnup and depletion, fission-product poisoning by strong absorbers such as xenon-135 and samarium-149, control rod worth, and the kinetics of reactor transients.

Methods

Calculations range from analytic diffusion models to large-scale Monte Carlo simulations of neutron transport (for example with codes such as MCNP or OpenMC) and deterministic lattice and core simulators used in design and licensing.
