Kanban

Kanban (Japanese: 看板, "signboard" or "billboard") is a scheduling and workflow system for lean manufacturing and just-in-time production, developed at Toyota by Taiichi Ohno in the late 1940s–1950s. In the original manufacturing system, kanban are physical cards that signal a downstream process's consumption of parts and authorise the upstream process to produce a replacement quantity — a "pull" system, in contrast to "push" production based on forecasts.

Principles

The number of kanban cards in circulation caps work-in-progress inventory: no card, no production. This limits overproduction, exposes bottlenecks and defects quickly, and drives continuous improvement (kaizen).

Kanban in knowledge work

Since the 2000s, the concept has been adapted to software development and knowledge work (notably by David J. Anderson) as the Kanban Method: work items are visualised as cards on a board with columns such as "To do", "Doing" and "Done"; explicit limits on work in progress, management of flow, and continuous feedback loops are central practices. Kanban boards, physical and digital (e.g. Trello-style tools), are now ubiquitous in agile teams.
