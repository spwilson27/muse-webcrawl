Cryptochrome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cryptochromes are flavoproteins involved in light sensing and circadian rhythms.

Cryptochromes are a class of flavoproteins — proteins containing the light-absorbing cofactor flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) — found in plants, animals, and some bacteria. They are sensitive to blue light and are evolutionarily related to photolyases, enzymes that repair ultraviolet-damaged DNA, though most cryptochromes have lost that repair function. Their name reflects both their prevalence in cryptogams (plants that reproduce by spores) and their historically elusive ("cryptic") nature.

Functions

In plants

Cryptochromes mediate many blue-light responses, including the inhibition of stem elongation, the opening of stomata, the regulation of flowering time, and the entrainment of the circadian clock. The model plant Arabidopsis thaliana has two main cryptochromes, CRY1 and CRY2, which work together with the red-light receptors called phytochromes to coordinate growth with the light environment.

In animals

In animals, cryptochromes are core components of the circadian clock. In mammals, the proteins CRY1 and CRY2 (encoded by the Cry1 and Cry2 genes) act in the suprachiasmatic nucleus and peripheral tissues as transcriptional repressors: they partner with PER proteins to inhibit the CLOCK–BMAL1 complex that drives their own expression, forming the negative feedback loop that generates roughly 24-hour rhythms. Unlike plant cryptochromes, mammalian CRY1 and CRY2 do not require light for this clock function.

Magnetoreception

Cryptochromes are leading candidates for the sensory molecules behind magnetoreception — the ability of migratory birds and other animals to detect Earth's magnetic field. According to the radical pair mechanism, blue light triggers electron transfer in cryptochrome in the bird's retina, producing pairs of radicals whose chemistry is subtly influenced by the direction of the magnetic field, potentially giving the animal a visual sense of compass direction. Experiments showing that weak radio-frequency fields disrupt bird orientation behaviour support a radical-pair basis, though the hypothesis remains under active investigation.

Discovery

Blue-light responses in plants were observed as early as Darwin's experiments on phototropism, but the receptor was identified only in the 1990s, when the HY4 gene of Arabidopsis was shown to encode a photolyase-like protein, subsequently named cryptochrome 1. Animal cryptochromes were identified shortly afterward through their sequence similarity and their role in circadian rhythms.
