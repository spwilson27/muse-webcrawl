Carnot's theorem

Carnot's theorem (thermodynamics)
Proposed byNicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot
Year1824
FieldThermodynamics

Carnot's theorem, developed by Sadi Carnot in his 1824 work Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire, states that no heat engine operating between two thermal reservoirs can be more efficient than a reversible engine operating between the same reservoirs, and that all reversible engines operating between the same reservoirs have the same efficiency.

Statement

The maximum possible efficiency of any heat engine depends only on the absolute temperatures of the hot reservoir (TH) and cold reservoir (TC): ηmax = 1 − TC/TH. This value, the Carnot efficiency, is achieved by the idealised Carnot cycle.

Proof idea

Carnot's argument proceeds by contradiction: if a more efficient engine existed, it could drive a reversible engine backwards as a refrigerator, producing a net transfer of heat from cold to hot without work input — an impossibility. The same coupling argument shows all reversible engines share the same efficiency, since each could otherwise drive the other.

Other theorems named for Carnot

Lazare Carnot, Sadi's father, is the namesake of a theorem in geometry relating perpendiculars drawn to the sides of a triangle, and of a result about conic sections. In geometry, "Carnot's theorem" most often refers to Lazare's criterion for three perpendiculars from points on the sides of a triangle to be concurrent.

Significance

Carnot's theorem laid the foundations for the second law of thermodynamics and the concept of absolute temperature, later formalised by Kelvin and Clausius.
