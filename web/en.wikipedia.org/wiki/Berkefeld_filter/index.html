Berkefeld filter

A Berkefeld filter is a water filter made of diatomaceous earth (kieselguhr). It was invented in Germany in 1891 and named after the mine in Celle from which the diatomaceous earth was obtained. The filter element is a hollow ceramic "candle" through which water passes under gravity or pressure; the fine pores of the diatomaceous earth strain out bacteria and suspended particles.

History

Berkefeld filters were developed in the wake of the 1892 Hamburg cholera epidemic, which starkly contrasted with low cholera rates in neighbouring Altona, where water was filtered. They were marketed in Britain by the Berkefeld Filter Co. and widely adopted by households, armies, and travellers. During the First World War, ceramic candle filters of this type were standard equipment for producing drinking water in the field.

Design and grades

The candles were produced in grades of increasing fineness, historically designated V (coarse), N (medium), and W (fine). The finest grades can remove most bacteria, including the cholera vibrio, but they do not reliably remove viruses, and the candles must be scrubbed and boiled periodically to restore flow and maintain performance. Modern descendants of the design include gravity-fed ceramic filter systems sold for domestic and emergency use.
