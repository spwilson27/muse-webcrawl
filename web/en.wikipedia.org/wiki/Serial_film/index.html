Serial film

A serial film, film serial, movie serial, or chapter play is a motion picture form popular during the first half of the 20th century. A serial is typically shown in a collection of episodes called chapters, each screened at a theatre once a week, usually accompanied by a cartoon, a newsreel and a feature film in a programme of matinee entertainment.

Format

Chapters were usually fifteen to twenty minutes long and characteristically ended in a cliffhanger: the hero or heroine was left in an apparently inescapable peril, whose resolution opened the following chapter. Serials ran for ten to fifteen chapters, concluding with a final episode in which the villain was unmasked and defeated. Chapter titles and recaps at the start of each instalment helped audiences follow the story.

History

The serial began in the silent era with productions such as What Happened to Mary (1912) and the enormously popular The Perils of Pauline (1914). French studios produced influential serials such as Fantômas and Les Vampires. In the sound era, studios including Universal Pictures, Columbia Pictures and Republic Pictures specialised in the form. Republic in particular became known for action serials based on comic-book and pulp heroes, such as Flash Gordon (1936), The Lone Ranger (1938), Adventures of Captain Marvel (1941) and Spy Smasher (1942).

Rising production costs and the arrival of television ended serial production in the mid-1950s; the last American serial was Blazing the Overland Trail (1956). The cliffhanger structure survived in television drama and influenced later adventure films — notably the Indiana Jones series.
