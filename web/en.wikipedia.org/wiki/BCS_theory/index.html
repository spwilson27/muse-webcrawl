BCS theory

BCS theory, or Bardeen–Cooper–Schrieffer theory (named after John Bardeen, Leon Cooper and John Robert Schrieffer), is the first microscopic theory of Superconductivity. Published in 1957, it describes superconductivity as a macroscopic quantum phenomenon in which electrons near the Fermi surface pair up into Cooper pairs through an attractive interaction mediated by lattice vibrations (phonons). The condensed pairs behave as bosons and can flow without resistance below a critical temperature.

History

Superconductivity had been discovered in 1911 by Heike Kamerlingh Onnes, but for nearly half a century it resisted microscopic explanation. In 1956 Cooper showed that the Fermi sea is unstable to arbitrarily weak attractive interactions, forming bound pairs. Bardeen, Cooper and Schrieffer extended this insight to the many-body problem in their 1957 paper "Theory of Superconductivity". The three shared the 1972 Nobel Prize in Physics for the theory; Bardeen remains the only person to have won the Physics prize twice.

Main conclusions

The theory predicts an energy gap in the excitation spectrum at the Fermi level, an exponentially suppressed specific heat below the transition, the Meissner effect, and a universal ratio between the zero-temperature gap and the critical temperature, 2Δ(0)/kBTc ≈ 3.53 for weakly coupled superconductors. It also explains the isotope effect, in which Tc varies with the atomic mass of the lattice ions, implicating phonons as the pairing glue.

Limitations and extensions

BCS theory in its original form applies to conventional, phonon-mediated superconductors. Strong-coupling corrections are handled by Eliashberg theory. The unconventional pairing states found in high-temperature superconductors and heavy-fermion compounds are not explained by the simple phonon mechanism, although the language of pairing and gaps developed by BCS theory remains the starting point for their description.
