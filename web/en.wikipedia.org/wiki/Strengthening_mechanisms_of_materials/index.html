Strengthening mechanisms of materials

Strengthening mechanisms are methods used in materials science to increase the strength of materials, particularly metals, by hindering the movement of dislocations through the crystal lattice. Because plastic deformation occurs by dislocation motion, anything that impedes that motion raises the yield strength — usually at the cost of ductility.

Mechanisms

  • Work hardening (strain hardening): plastic deformation increases dislocation density, and interactions between dislocations impede further motion.
  • Solid solution strengthening: alloying atoms of a different size distort the lattice and create stress fields that obstruct dislocations.
  • Precipitation hardening: fine second-phase particles block dislocation motion; dislocations must either cut through or bow around them.
  • Grain boundary strengthening: reducing grain size means dislocations pile up at more boundaries; the effect follows the Hall–Petch relationship.
  • Martensitic and transformation strengthening: phase transformations produce hard phases and internal stresses, as in quenched steel.

These mechanisms are often combined in commercial alloys, and their contributions to strength can be approximately additive.
