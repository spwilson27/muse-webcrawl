Gastric bypass surgery

Gastric bypass surgery is a type of bariatric (weight-loss) surgery in which the stomach is divided into a small upper pouch and a much larger lower remnant, and the small intestine is rearranged to connect to both — the Roux-en-Y configuration. The procedure both restricts food intake and reduces nutrient absorption, and it produces powerful hormonal effects that frequently improve or remit type 2 diabetes.

Procedure

In the standard Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, the surgeon creates a small stomach pouch of roughly 30 ml and connects it directly to the middle portion of the small intestine (the "Roux limb"), so food bypasses most of the stomach, the duodenum and the upper jejunum. Digestive juices from the bypassed stomach and duodenum join the food stream further downstream. The operation is usually performed laparoscopically.

Outcomes

Patients typically lose 60–70% of excess body weight, with substantial long-term improvements in type 2 diabetes, hypertension, sleep apnoea and cardiovascular risk. Lifelong vitamin and mineral supplementation and follow-up are required, since malabsorption can cause deficiencies of iron, vitamin B12, folate and calcium.

Risks and complications

Complications include anastomotic leaks, strictures, marginal ulcers, internal hernias, gallstones, and the "dumping syndrome" — nausea, cramps and diarrhoea after eating sugary foods. Operative mortality in modern series is low, comparable to other major elective abdominal surgery.
