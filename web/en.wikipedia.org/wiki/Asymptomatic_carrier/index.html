Asymptomatic carrier

An asymptomatic carrier is a person or other organism that has become infected by a pathogen, but shows no symptoms. Although unaffected by the pathogen themselves, carriers are able to transmit it to others, and so play an important role in the spread of infectious disease. Asymptomatic carriers are critical in the transmission of common infectious diseases such as typhoid, HIV, C. difficile, influenzas, cholera, tuberculosis, and COVID-19.

Types of carrier

Carriers may be classified by their stage of infection. A person in the incubation period who will later develop symptoms is sometimes called a pre-symptomatic carrier, while one who never develops symptoms throughout the course of infection is a true asymptomatic carrier. Others become carriers after recovering from illness, harbouring and shedding the pathogen for weeks, years, or even a lifetime, as occurs with typhoid fever (where the bacteria persist in the gallbladder) and some cases of hepatitis B and salmonellosis.

Typhoid Mary

The most famous asymptomatic carrier was Mary Mallon, known as "Typhoid Mary", an Irish-born cook working in the New York area in the early 20th century. Although healthy herself, she infected dozens of people with typhoid fever through her work preparing food. After refusing to stop working as a cook or undergo gallbladder removal, she was forcibly quarantined by health authorities for much of the rest of her life, dying in isolation on North Brother Island in 1938. Her case established legal precedents for the state's power to restrict carriers of disease and remains a touchstone in public-health ethics.

Public health significance

Because carriers cannot be identified by symptoms, control of diseases with frequent asymptomatic transmission depends on testing, contact tracing, vaccination, and hygiene measures such as food-handler screening. Carriers of pathogens such as MRSA and Clostridioides difficile are a particular concern in hospitals, where they can seed outbreaks among vulnerable patients.
