Algorithmic bias

Algorithmic bias describes systematic and repeatable errors in a computer system that create unfair outcomes, such as privileging one arbitrary group of users over others. It can arise when an algorithm is trained on biased or unrepresentative data, when its design encodes the assumptions or prejudices of its creators, or when it is deployed in a context different from the one it was designed for.

Sources

Bias can enter through historical bias in source data (for example, arrest records reflecting policing patterns rather than crime rates), representation bias in sampling, measurement bias in labels and proxies, and aggregation bias when one model is applied to groups with different characteristics. Feedback loops can amplify initial disparities, as when predictive-policing systems direct more patrols to areas already heavily policed.

Examples

Well-documented cases include risk-assessment tools used in criminal justice (such as COMPAS, debated after a 2016 ProPublica analysis), facial recognition systems with higher error rates for darker-skinned faces, a recruiting tool discontinued by Amazon after it was found to downgrade women's résumés, and healthcare algorithms that underestimated the needs of Black patients because they used cost as a proxy for illness.

Responses

Responses include algorithmic fairness research (defining metrics such as demographic parity and equalised odds, which provably cannot all be satisfied at once), auditing and impact assessments, transparency requirements, and regulation such as the EU AI Act.
