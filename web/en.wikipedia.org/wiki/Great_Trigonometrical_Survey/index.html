Great Trigonometrical Survey

The Great Trigonometrical Survey was a project of the Survey of India to measure the entire Indian subcontinent with scientific precision. Begun in 1802 by infantry officer William Lambton, under the sponsorship of the British East India Company, it consumed most of the 19th century and produced the first accurate measurements of the Himalayan peaks, including Everest.

The Great Arc

The survey's backbone was the Great Arc, a chain of triangles running some 2,400 km from Cape Comorin at the southern tip of India to the Himalayas. Teams hauled enormous theodolites — the largest weighing half a tonne and requiring a dozen men to carry — up onto towers and hilltops, measuring carefully surveyed baselines and observing angles over decades, often in malarial jungle where many surveyors died.

Measuring Everest

From plains over 100 miles away, survey "computers" under Radhanath Sickdar calculated the height of Peak XV and announced in 1856 that it was the highest mountain in the world at 29,002 feet (8,839.8 m). Surveyor General Andrew Waugh named it Mount Everest after his predecessor, George Everest.

Legacy

The survey produced accurate maps of India, fixed the shape of the Earth more precisely (revealing the deflection of the plumb line near the Himalayas, which fed the theory of isostasy), and remains one of the largest scientific undertakings of its century.
