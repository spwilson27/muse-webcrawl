The Rice Terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras are a set of monumental irrigated rice terraces carved into the mountains of Ifugao province in the Cordillera Administrative Region of northern Luzon, Philippines. Built and maintained by the Ifugao people over many centuries, the terraces were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1995 — the first property ever included in the cultural landscape category of the list.

Description

The inscription covers five terrace clusters: Nagacadan (Kiangan), Hungduan, Pula (Bangaan), Batad and Mayoyao. The terraces follow the contours of steep slopes at elevations between about 700 and 1,500 metres, their retaining walls built of stone and mud, in places as high as a multi-storey building. A complex traditional water-management system channels water from protected watershed forests (muyung) above the terraces through bamboo pipes and channels, distributing it evenly and carrying silt that fertilises the fields. The system also incorporates settlements, sacred groves and burial grounds, forming an integrated cultural landscape.

History

Terrace farming in the Cordilleras is of great antiquity, though scholars debate the precise chronology: some accounts describe them as roughly 2,000 years old, while archaeological work suggests the large-scale wetland terrace systems intensified during and after the period of Spanish colonisation. Knowledge of construction and maintenance — including the ritual calendar that governs planting and harvest — is passed down by oral tradition within Ifugao families. The famous Banaue terraces, often photographed, lie in the same province though outside the five inscribed clusters.

Conservation

The site was placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger in 2001 because of deteriorating terraces, out-migration of young Ifugao to cities, and the loss of traditional practices. Following government and community conservation programmes, irrigation repairs and a management plan, it was removed from the danger list in 2012. Challenges persist, including the introduction of giant earthworms that damage terrace walls, climate change, and pressure from tourism.
