Lysergic acid diethylamide

Lysergic acid diethylamide, commonly known as LSD (from German Lysergsäure-diethylamid) and colloquially as acid, is a potent psychedelic drug. Effects typically include intensified thoughts, emotions, and sensory perception. At sufficiently high dosages, LSD manifests primarily visual, as well as auditory, tactile, and proprioceptive hallucinations, and in some cases experiences of ego dissolution. It is classified as a classic psychedelic and acts mainly as an agonist at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors.

History

LSD was first synthesized by the Swiss chemist Albert Hofmann at Sandoz Laboratories in Basel on 16 November 1938, as the 25th compound in a series of lysergic acid derivatives investigated for circulatory and respiratory stimulant properties. Its psychedelic effects were discovered five years later, on 16 April 1943, when Hofmann accidentally absorbed a small amount through his skin. Three days later, on 19 April 1943 — an event commemorated as "Bicycle Day" — he deliberately ingested 250 micrograms and rode home by bicycle while experiencing the drug's effects. Sandoz marketed the drug under the name Delysid from 1947, promoting it for psychiatric use.

During the 1950s and 1960s, LSD was studied extensively in psychotherapy and was also investigated by intelligence agencies, most notoriously in the CIA's MKUltra programme. Figures such as Timothy Leary popularised the drug, which became emblematic of the 1960s counterculture. Growing recreational use led to prohibition: it was criminalised in the United States in the late 1960s and is controlled under the 1971 UN Convention on Psychotropic Substances.

Effects

LSD is active at extremely low doses — a typical dose is around 100 micrograms. Physical effects include pupil dilation, increased heart rate and blood pressure, and reduced appetite. Psychological effects include visual patterning, altered sense of time, synaesthesia-like experiences, and profound changes in cognition. Adverse reactions can include anxiety, paranoia and, in rare cases, persistent perceptual changes known as hallucinogen persisting perception disorder (HPPD). LSD is not considered addictive and has low toxicity; lethal overdose in humans is exceedingly rare.

Pharmacology

LSD binds to most serotonin receptor subtypes, dopamine receptors and adrenoceptors. Its psychedelic effects are attributed chiefly to agonism at the 5-HT2A receptor. The molecule is structurally related to the neurotransmitter serotonin and to the ergot alkaloids found in the ergot fungus. It is metabolised in the liver and excreted in the urine.

Research

After decades in which research was effectively halted, clinical studies of LSD resumed in the 21st century, investigating its potential in treating anxiety, depression, cluster headaches and alcohol dependence. Switzerland authorized limited medical use under special licences, and several clinical trials have reported reductions in anxiety associated with life-threatening illness.
