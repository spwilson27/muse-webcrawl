Waste sorting

Source separation using color-coded containers for different waste fractions.

Waste sorting is the process by which waste is separated into different elements. Sorting can occur manually at the household and be collected through curbside collection schemes, or automatically separated in materials recovery facilities or mechanical biological treatment systems. The quality of sorting strongly affects the quality of materials that can be recycled.

Source separation

In many municipalities, households separate waste into fractions such as paper and cardboard, glass, metal, plastics, organic (food and garden) waste, and residual waste, each placed in a dedicated container, often color-coded. Source separation produces cleaner material streams than sorting mixed waste later, but depends on public participation and education. Contamination — placing the wrong material in a bin — remains a major problem, a behavior sometimes called "wishcycling".

Mechanical sorting

At a materials recovery facility (MRF), mixed recyclables are sorted mechanically and manually. Screens separate material by size; rotating discs sort containers from flat paper; magnets extract ferrous metal; eddy-current separators eject aluminium; air jets and near-infrared optical sorters identify and separate different plastic polymers. In single-stream systems all recyclables are collected together, reducing collection costs but increasing processing cost and contamination; dual-stream systems keep fibers separate from containers.

Automatic and advanced sorting

Modern plants increasingly use artificial-intelligence-guided robotic pickers and spectroscopic sorters to raise purity and capture additional fractions, including flexible films and mixed plastics destined for chemical recycling. Hazardous fractions such as batteries and electronic waste require dedicated collection to avoid fires and pollution.
