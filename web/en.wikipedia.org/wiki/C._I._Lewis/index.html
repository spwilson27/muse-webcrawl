Clarence Irving Lewis (April 12, 1883 – February 3, 1964), usually cited as C. I. Lewis, was an American philosopher, a founder of modal logic, and the formulator of "conceptual pragmatism." He taught for most of his career at Harvard University and was a leading figure of American philosophy between the world wars.

Modal logic

Dissatisfied with the paradoxes of material implication in the Principia Mathematica of Russell and Whitehead, Lewis developed a logic of strict implication, in which "p strictly implies q" means that q is deducible from p in a stronger, modal sense. In a series of papers from 1912 and in A Survey of Symbolic Logic (1918) and Symbolic Logic (1932, with C. H. Langford), he presented the axiom systems known as S1 through S5 — among which S4 and S5 remain the most studied — effectively creating modern modal logic and inaugurating the study of necessity and possibility as logical operators.

Epistemology and conceptual pragmatism

In Mind and the World-Order (1929), Lewis argued that knowledge has two elements: concepts, which the mind supplies a priori and which we choose on pragmatic grounds, and the "given" of raw experience, which interpretation organises. Because alternative conceptual schemes can organise the same given, the a priori is "made" rather than discovered — a pluralism of frameworks comparable to, though distinct from, later logical empiricist views. In An Analysis of Knowledge and Valuation (1946) he extended the account to ethics, defending a naturalistic but non-reductive theory of value.

Life and influence

Lewis studied at Harvard under Josiah Royce, taught at the University of California, Berkeley (1911–1920), and then returned to Harvard, where he taught until 1948 and trained a generation of philosophers including Wilfrid Sellars, Roderick Chisholm, and Nelson Goodman. His modal logic, dormant as a philosophical research programme for some years, was revived in the 1950s by possible-worlds semantics, which made Lewis's systems central to contemporary metaphysics and philosophical logic.
