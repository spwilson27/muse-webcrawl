Edward Wadie Said (1 November 1935 – 25 September 2003) was a Palestinian-American academic, literary critic and public intellectual. A professor of literature at Columbia University for forty years, he was a founder of the academic field of postcolonial studies and one of the most influential humanities scholars of the late twentieth century. As a public intellectual he was a prominent advocate of the Palestinian cause and a member of the Palestinian National Council from 1977 to 1991.

Life

Said was born in Jerusalem into a wealthy Arab Christian family and was educated in Cairo and the United States, taking his bachelor's degree at Princeton and his doctorate at Harvard. He joined the Columbia faculty in 1963 and taught there until his death from leukaemia in 2003. His memoir Out of Place (1999) recounts the displacement of his upbringing between worlds.

Orientalism

Said's landmark book Orientalism (1978) argued that Western scholarship, literature and art had constructed "the Orient" as Europe's inferior, exotic Other — a discourse of knowledge inseparable from imperial power. Drawing on Michel Foucault's ideas about discourse and Antonio Gramsci's concept of cultural hegemony, the book transformed the study of colonial history, Middle Eastern studies and literary criticism, and remains both foundational and fiercely debated.

Other work

In Culture and Imperialism (1993) Said extended the argument to the canonical novels of Austen, Conrad and others, reading them "contrapuntally" against the colonial histories they presuppose. A serious amateur pianist and friend of Daniel Barenboim, he co-founded with Barenboim the West–Eastern Divan Orchestra, bringing together Arab and Israeli musicians. His other books include The Question of Palestine (1979), Covering Islam (1981) and Humanism and Democratic Criticism (2004).
