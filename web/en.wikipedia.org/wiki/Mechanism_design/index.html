Mechanism design

[Image: A mechanism maps agents' private information and messages into allocations and payments]
Mechanism design works backwards from desired outcomes to rules of a game

Mechanism design, sometimes called reverse game theory, is a field in economics and game theory that takes an engineering approach to designing economic mechanisms or incentives to achieve desired objectives, where players act rationally. Whereas game theory starts with rules and predicts behaviour, mechanism design starts with a social goal and asks what rules (mechanisms) would implement it when participants hold private information and act in their own interest.

Core ideas

A mechanism specifies the messages agents may send and how outcomes (allocations and payments) depend on those messages. Two foundational results structure the field. The revelation principle says that for any mechanism there is an equivalent "direct" mechanism in which agents simply report their private information and truth-telling is an equilibrium, so the designer can restrict attention to such mechanisms. Incentive compatibility requires that agents find honesty (or the intended strategy) optimal; combined with participation constraints it characterises what outcomes are implementable.

Applications

Mechanism design underlies modern auction theory — including the Vickrey auction and the revenue-equivalence and Myerson optimal-auction results — and has been applied to spectrum licence auctions, electricity markets, school-choice and kidney-exchange matching (via its close relative, matching theory), voting rules, public-goods provision and online advertising auctions. Leonid Hurwicz, Eric Maskin and Roger Myerson received the 2007 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for laying the foundations of the theory.

Key results

The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem shows limits: with unrestricted preferences over three or more outcomes, the only strategy-proof voting rules are dictatorial. Green–Laffont results show that efficient, strategy-proof mechanisms generally cannot also balance budgets. These impossibility results guide designers toward second-best mechanisms trading off efficiency, truthfulness and feasibility.
