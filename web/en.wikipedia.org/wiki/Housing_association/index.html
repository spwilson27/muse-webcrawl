Housing association

Housing associations are independent, not-for-profit organisations that provide low-cost social housing for people in housing need. In the United Kingdom they are the main providers of new social housing and, following large-scale transfers of council housing stock since the late 1980s, manage the majority of social rented homes — around 2.5 million homes housing more than five million people in England alone. Similar non-profit housing bodies exist in the Netherlands (woningcorporaties), which own roughly a third of the national housing stock, and in other European countries.

History

Housing associations grew out of 19th-century philanthropic and charitable trusts, such as the Peabody Trust and the Guinness Trust, which built model dwellings for the working poor. In Britain the sector expanded with state grants under the Housing Act 1974, and from 1988 associations could combine public grant with private finance, sharply increasing development. Council tenants voted in many areas to transfer their homes to associations, which could borrow against the stock.

Funding and regulation

Associations are regulated bodies — in England by the Regulator of Social Housing — and are formally known as private registered providers of social housing or registered social landlords. They finance development through a mix of government grant, rental income and private borrowing, and many also build homes for market sale or shared ownership to cross-subsidise social housing. Surpluses must be reinvested in housing purposes rather than distributed to shareholders.

Roles

Beyond building and managing homes at below-market rents (social and "affordable" rent), associations provide supported housing for elderly, disabled and vulnerable people, run community investment programmes, and are central to regeneration and decarbonisation of the housing stock.
