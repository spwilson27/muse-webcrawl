Shiva is one of the principal deities of Hinduism, the destroyer and transformer within the Trimūrti, alongside Brahma the creator and Vishnu the preserver. For Shaivas he is the Supreme Being: at once the terrifying ascetic smeared with ash on Mount Kailash, the cosmic dancer Naṭarāja whose dance creates and destroys the universe, the benevolent lord of Varanasi, and the devoted husband of Parvati and father of Ganesha and Kartikeya.

Forms and myths

Shiva's mythology holds asceticism and household life in tension. He burned Kāma, god of love, to ash with his third eye; he caught the falling river Ganga in his matted hair to break her descent to earth; he drank the world-destroying poison halāhala at the churning of the ocean, which turned his throat blue. As Ardhanārīśvara he is half male, half female. His emblem, the liṅga, set in the yoni base, is the focus of worship in Shaiva temples, aniconic and ubiquitous.

Worship

Shaivism is among the oldest and largest Hindu traditions, with major centres at Varanasi, Kedarnath and the twelve jyotirliṅga shrines; the Kumbh Mela draws Shaiva ascetic orders (including the ash-covered Nāga sadhus) in vast numbers. His great festival is Mahāśivarātri, the night of Shiva. Through yoga traditions — Shiva is the first yogi, Ādi Yogi — his image as meditating ascetic has travelled worldwide, and the bronze Naṭarāja of the Chola dynasty is one of the most celebrated works of Indian art.
