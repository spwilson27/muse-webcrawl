Population exchange between Greece and Turkey

The population exchange between Greece and Turkey of 1923 was a compulsory, internationally sanctioned transfer of populations agreed in the aftermath of the Greco-Turkish War (1919–1922). Under a convention signed at Lausanne on 30 January 1923, Orthodox Christians living in Turkey were to move to Greece and Muslims living in Greece to Turkey, with religion — not language or ethnicity — as the criterion of belonging.

Background

The exchange largely ratified a flight that had already happened. Following the Greek army's defeat in Anatolia and the burning of Smyrna (İzmir) in September 1922, hundreds of thousands of Greeks and Armenians fled or were driven from Turkey. Fridtjof Nansen, the League of Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, proposed a formal exchange to stabilise both states and settle the refugees.

Terms and implementation

The convention exempted two communities: the Greek Orthodox of Istanbul (Constantinople) and the islands of Imbros and Tenedos, and the Muslims of Western Thrace. Everyone else — including Turkish-speaking Orthodox (Karamanlides) sent to Greece and Greek-speaking Muslims from Crete sent to Turkey — was uprooted. A Mixed Commission supervised liquidations of property, though compensation was rarely adequate. Greece's population rose by roughly a quarter almost overnight; refugee neighbourhoods such as Nea Smyrni and Nea Ionia grew around Athens, and Greek Macedonia and Thrace were transformed demographically.

Legacy

At the time praised as a humane solution, the exchange entrenched the idea of ethnically homogeneous nation-states and was later cited as a precedent — admiringly by some interwar planners, critically by later historians — for forced transfers. Its long-term ethnic homogenisation of both countries is a central fact of modern Greek and Turkish history, and its exceptions shaped later crises, including the Istanbul pogrom of 1955 that drove out most of Istanbul's remaining Greeks.
