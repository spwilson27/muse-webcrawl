Decipherment is the analysis of documents written in ancient languages or scripts that are no longer understood. It is distinguished from the interpretation of an unknown language that is written in a known script: decipherment requires recovering both the script's sign values and, usually, the underlying language. Famous decipherments include Egyptian hieroglyphs, Mesopotamian cuneiform, Linear B, and the Maya script.

Methods

Successful decipherments typically depend on some combination of bilingual or multilingual texts (such as the Rosetta Stone), proper names that can be recognised across languages, a large enough corpus of inscriptions, statistical analysis of sign frequencies and distributions, and a plausible hypothesis about the language being recorded. Cryptanalytic techniques borrowed from code-breaking — such as Alice Kober's analysis of inflection patterns in Linear B using punched-card tabulations — have proved decisive.

Famous decipherments

  • Egyptian hieroglyphs — deciphered in the 1820s by Jean-François Champollion using the Rosetta Stone, a decree of Ptolemy V inscribed in hieroglyphic, Demotic, and Greek.
  • Cuneiform — Old Persian cuneiform was read in the early 19th century by Georg Friedrich Grotefend and Henry Rawlinson, opening the way to Akkadian and Sumerian.
  • Linear B — deciphered in 1952 by Michael Ventris, building on Alice Kober's work, and shown to record an early form of Greek.
  • Maya script — Yuri Knorozov demonstrated in the 1950s that the script was logosyllabic; most texts can now be read.

Undeciphered scripts

Several writing systems remain undeciphered, including the Indus script, Linear A, Rongorongo of Easter Island, the Voynich manuscript, and the Phaistos Disc. Claims of decipherment are frequent but require independent verification and consistent readings of new texts before they are accepted by specialists.
