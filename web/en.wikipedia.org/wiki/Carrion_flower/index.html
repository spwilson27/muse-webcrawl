Carrion flower

Carrion flowers, also known as corpse flowers or stinking flowers, are flowers that emit an odor that smells like rotting flesh. Carrion flowers attract mostly scavenging flies and beetles as pollinators. Some species may trap the insects temporarily to ensure the gathering and transfer of pollen.

Examples

Well-known carrion flowers include the titan arum (Amorphophallus titanum), which produces one of the largest unbranched inflorescences in the world; Rafflesia arnoldii, which bears the largest individual flower; and members of the genus Stapelia, succulent plants from Africa whose star-shaped, hairy, flesh-coloured blooms mimic decaying meat. Other examples occur in the genera Huernia, Orbea, Caralluma, and in Hydnora and pawpaw (Asimina).

Mechanism

The flowers produce compounds such as dimethyl trisulfide, isovaleric acid, and indole that mimic the smell of carrion and dung. Some generate heat (thermogenesis), which helps volatilize the scent and imitates the warmth of fresh carrion. Blow flies and carrion beetles, expecting food or a place to lay eggs, carry pollen between flowers.

Cultivation

Despite their smell, many carrion flowers are popular in cultivation among succulent collectors and botanical gardens, where titan arum blooms draw large crowds.
