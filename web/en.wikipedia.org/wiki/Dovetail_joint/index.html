Dovetail joint

A dovetail joint is a joinery technique most commonly used in woodworking, including furniture, cabinets and log buildings. The joint is formed by interlocking fan-shaped "tails" cut into the end of one board with matching "pins" on the board it joins, so named because the tails resemble the shape of a dove's tail. Dovetails are renowned for their resistance to being pulled apart (tensile strength) and are considered a hallmark of fine craftsmanship.

History

Dovetail joints predate written history; examples have been found in ancient Egyptian furniture, including pieces from the tomb of Pharaoh Tutankhamun, and in ancient Chinese architecture. The joint became a signature of European cabinet-making from the 17th century onwards.

Types

  • Through dovetail — the end grain of both boards is visible; used for carcass construction.
  • Half-blind (lap) dovetail — the joint is hidden from the front; the classic drawer joint.
  • Secret mitred dovetail — fully hidden, used in the finest work.
  • Sliding dovetail — a long tapered socket into which a matching tail slides, used for shelves and casework.

Construction

Dovetails may be cut by hand with saw and chisel — traditionally regarded as a test of a joiner's skill — or machined with a router and jig, which made the joint common in mass-produced furniture.
