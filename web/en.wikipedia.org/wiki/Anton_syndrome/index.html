Anton syndrome, also known as Anton's blindness or Anton–Babinski syndrome, is a rare condition in which a person is blind — typically because of damage to both occipital lobes of the brain — yet firmly denies being unable to see and confabulates visual experiences. It is the best-known form of anosognosia (lack of awareness of a deficit) applied to vision, and is classified as a type of visual anosognosia. Patients may bump into furniture or walls while insisting they can see, offering invented descriptions of the room and of people present.

Causes and mechanism

The most common cause is bilateral occipital stroke, for example following occlusion of the posterior cerebral arteries; other causes include trauma and haemorrhage. The eyes and optic pathways may remain intact, so the defect is cortical. Denial is thought to arise because the damaged visual cortex is disconnected from intact speech and monitoring areas, which then generate explanations for the absent input without registering the loss.

History

The syndrome is named for the Austrian neurologist and psychiatrist Gabriel Anton, who described cases of unawareness of blindness (and of deafness) in the 1890s; Joseph Babinski applied the term anosognosia to similar phenomena in 1914. The dramatist and documentarian Alan Bennett and popular accounts in neurology, such as those of Oliver Sacks, have brought the condition to wide attention.
