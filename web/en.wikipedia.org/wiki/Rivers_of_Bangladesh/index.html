Rivers of Bangladesh

Bangladesh is a riverine country, sometimes called the "land of rivers." Some 700 rivers, including tributaries and distributaries, flow through its territory, with a total length of roughly 24,000 kilometres. Most of the country is formed from sediment deposited by these rivers as they drain the Himalaya and surrounding highlands into the Bay of Bengal.

The great rivers

Three great rivers dominate the hydrology of Bangladesh:

  • The Ganges, which enters from India and is known in Bangladesh as the Padma.
  • The Brahmaputra, which enters from the northeast and, after meeting the Teesta, flows as the Jamuna.
  • The Meghna, formed in the haor wetlands of the northeast and fed by rivers from the hills of Meghalaya and Tripura.

The Padma and Jamuna join near Goalundo, and their combined flow meets the Meghna near Chandpur. The resulting Lower Meghna is one of the largest rivers on Earth by discharge, carrying a combined flow that, at its monsoon peak, ranks among the top few rivers in the world. The rivers together carry on the order of a billion tonnes of sediment each year, continually building the Bengal Delta, the largest river delta in the world.

Importance

Rivers are the arteries of Bangladeshi life. They provide water for irrigation and rice cultivation, support one of the world's largest inland capture fisheries, and carry millions of passengers and much of the nation's freight on ferries and country boats. Annual monsoon flooding replenishes soils across the floodplains, though severe floods can inundate a third or more of the country. River erosion is a persistent hazard, displacing thousands of people each year as channels migrate across the delta.

Management issues

Most of Bangladesh's major rivers originate outside its borders, making water management an international question. The sharing of the Ganges' dry-season flow with India was addressed by the 1996 Ganges Water Sharing Treaty, while the proposed and completed barrages and embankments in the region remain politically sensitive. Within the country, embankments, polders, and barrages such as the Farakka Barrage upstream in India have reshaped the regime of many rivers, altering salinity in the southwest and affecting the Sundarbans mangrove forest.
