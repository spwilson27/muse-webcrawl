Chicago school (architecture)

Activec. 1880s – 1910s (first school); 1940s–1970s (second school)

The Chicago school refers to a school of architects active in Chicago in the late 19th and early 20th centuries who pioneered the skyscraper after the Great Chicago Fire of 1871, making Chicago a laboratory of modern commercial architecture.

Characteristics

The first Chicago school combined steel-frame (and iron-frame) construction, masonry cladding, large plate-glass "Chicago windows", and restrained ornament, freeing exterior walls from load-bearing functions. William Le Baron Jenney's Home Insurance Building (1885) is often called the first skyscraper. Louis Sullivan articulated the principle "form follows function", and the firm of Burnham and Root produced landmarks such as the Monadnock Building. A "second Chicago school" of the postwar decades, led by Ludwig Mies van der Rohe and firms like SOM, extended the steel-and-glass grid worldwide.

Legacy

The school laid the technical and aesthetic groundwork for modernist high-rise architecture, though many of its buildings were demolished in the 20th century; survivors are protected as landmarks.
