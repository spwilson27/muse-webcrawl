Neuropsychology

Neuropsychology is a branch of psychology and neuroscience concerned with how the structure and function of the brain relate to cognition and behaviour. Clinical neuropsychologists assess and treat the cognitive, emotional, and behavioural consequences of brain injury and neurological disease, while experimental neuropsychologists study brain–behaviour relationships in both healthy and damaged brains.

History

The discipline grew out of nineteenth-century lesion studies. Paul Broca's 1861 description of a patient who had lost expressive speech after damage to the left frontal lobe, and Carl Wernicke's subsequent account of receptive aphasia, established that specific cognitive functions could be localised. Alexander Luria's work with brain-injured soldiers in the Second World War produced an influential theory of functional brain systems, and in the English-speaking world Norman Geschwind revived behavioural neurology. Brenda Milner's studies of the amnesic patient H.M. demonstrated the role of the medial temporal lobes in memory formation.

Assessment

Neuropsychological assessment uses standardised tests of intelligence, memory, language, executive function, attention, processing speed, visuospatial ability, and motor function, interpreted against normative data and the patient's estimated premorbid level. Batteries such as the WAIS and WMS, and flexible hypothesis-testing approaches in the tradition of Edith Kaplan and Muriel Lezak, are used to diagnose and characterise conditions including dementia, stroke, traumatic brain injury, epilepsy, and developmental disorders, and to plan rehabilitation and guide decisions about capacity and return to work or school.

Methods

Beyond testing, the field draws on lesion analysis, structural and functional neuroimaging (MRI, fMRI, PET), electrophysiology, and, in research settings, methods such as transcranial magnetic stimulation. Cognitive neuropsychology uses dissociations between impaired and spared abilities in individual patients to test models of normal cognitive architecture — for example, distinguishing separate routes in reading aloud informed models of acquired dyslexia.

Related topics include neurotoxicity and neuroticism, a personality trait studied in differential psychology.
