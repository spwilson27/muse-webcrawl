Haemolytic anaemia

Haemolytic anaemia
SpecialtyHaematology
SymptomsFatigue, pallor, jaundice, dark urine, enlarged spleen
CausesInherited defects, autoimmune disease, infection, drugs, mechanical destruction
Key testsHigh reticulocytes, raised LDH, low haptoglobin, raised unconjugated bilirubin, Coombs test

Haemolytic anaemia (American English: hemolytic anemia) is a form of anaemia due to haemolysis — the abnormal breakdown of red blood cells (RBCs), either within the blood vessels (intravascular haemolysis) or elsewhere in the body (extravascular, mainly in the spleen and liver). Normally red blood cells live about 120 days; in haemolytic anaemia destruction outpaces the bone marrow's ability to replace them.

Causes

Causes are classified as intrinsic (inherited defects of the red cell) and extrinsic (acquired factors). Intrinsic causes include membrane disorders such as hereditary spherocytosis; enzyme deficiencies such as glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency and pyruvate kinase deficiency; and haemoglobinopathies such as sickle cell disease and thalassaemia. Extrinsic causes include autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (warm and cold antibody types), microangiopathic processes (such as thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura and haemolytic uraemic syndrome), infections including malaria, drugs and toxins, hypersplenism, and mechanical destruction by prosthetic heart valves.

Signs and investigation

Patients present with symptoms of anaemia — fatigue, pallor, breathlessness — together with jaundice from increased bilirubin production, splenomegaly, and in chronic cases pigment gallstones. Haemolysis is confirmed by a raised reticulocyte count, increased lactate dehydrogenase, reduced haptoglobin, and raised unconjugated bilirubin; the blood film may show spherocytes, schistocytes, or sickle cells, and the direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test identifies immune causes.

Treatment

Management depends on the cause: folic acid supplementation to support increased red cell production, corticosteroids and other immunosuppression for autoimmune haemolysis, avoidance of triggers in G6PD deficiency, transfusion when necessary, and splenectomy for selected cases such as hereditary spherocytosis and refractory autoimmune disease.
