Prairie dog

Scientific classification
KingdomAnimalia
FamilySciuridae
GenusCynomys

Prairie dogs (genus Cynomys) are herbivorous burrowing ground squirrels native to the grasslands of North America. Despite the name, they are not dogs; early explorers named them for their habitat and their bark-like alarm calls. There are five recognised species: black-tailed, white-tailed, Gunnison's, Utah, and Mexican prairie dogs.

Social structure

Prairie dogs live in large colonies called "towns", which may extend over hundreds of hectares and contain thousands of individuals. Within a town, the basic social unit is a family group (a coterie for black-tailed prairie dogs) typically consisting of one adult male, several adult females, and their offspring. Their burrow systems are elaborate, with multiple entrances, listening posts, nesting chambers, and air-flow engineering that drives ventilation through the tunnels.

Communication

Prairie dogs are among the most communicative mammals known. Their alarm calls encode information about the type, size, speed, and even colour of an approaching predator, and research by biologist Con Slobodchikoff has shown that the calls function as a descriptive vocabulary that can incorporate new terms for novel objects.

Ecology

Prairie dogs are a keystone species of the North American prairie. Their grazing and burrowing create habitat used by burrowing owls, black-footed ferrets, swift foxes, and many other species; they are also an important prey base for raptors, coyotes, and badgers. Populations have fallen drastically since the nineteenth century owing to habitat conversion, poisoning campaigns, recreational shooting, and sylvatic plague; the Mexican and Utah prairie dogs are listed as threatened or endangered.
