Cult film

A cult film or cult movie, also commonly referred to as a cult classic, is a film that has acquired a cult following. Cult films are known for their dedicated, passionate fanbase which forms an elaborate subculture, with members engaging in repeated viewings, dialogue-quoting, and audience participation.

Definitions of the term are not always consistent: inclusive definitions allow nearly any film with a fandom to be described as a cult film, while some academics restrict the label to films that failed commercially on release, were transgressive in content, or were produced outside the mainstream studio system. What unites most accounts is an unusually devoted community of viewers organized around the film.

Definition

Film scholar Ernest Mathijs and others have argued that cult status is best understood as a property of a film's reception rather than of the film itself: a cult film is one around which a community of viewers performs its devotion through rituals such as marathon screenings, fan fiction, conventions, and memorabilia collecting. Transgression — of taste, genre, morality, or technique — is a recurring feature.

History

Cult followings formed around films as early as the silent era, but the phenomenon crystallized in the 1970s with repertory cinemas and campus film societies. Films that had been dismissed on release, such as Harold and Maude (1971), found second lives through word of mouth and repeat attendance.

Midnight movies

The midnight movie circuit, popularized by New York's Elgin Theater beginning with El Topo (1970), gave exhibitors a slot for provocative, low-budget, or strange films. Eraserhead (1977), Pink Flamingos (1972), and above all The Rocky Horror Picture Show (1975) — the longest-running theatrical release in history, still screened with live shadow casts — defined the format.

Home media and the Internet

The arrival of VHS in the 1980s, and later DVD, streaming, and social media, made obscurity itself reproducible: fans could now discover, trade, and evangelize neglected films worldwide. Box-office failures such as The Shawshank Redemption (1994) and The Big Lebowski (1998) became cult staples through home viewing.

Examples

Frequently cited cult films include Plan 9 from Outer Space (1957), The Room (2003), Donnie Darko (2001), Fight Club (1999), Blade Runner (1982), and This Is Spinal Tap (1984). Many belong to horror, science fiction, exploitation, and comedy genres, though no genre has a monopoly on cult status.
