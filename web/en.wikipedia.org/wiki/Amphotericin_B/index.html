Amphotericin B

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Amphotericin B, a mainstay treatment for life-threatening systemic fungal infections.

Amphotericin B is an antifungal medication used for serious systemic fungal infections and for visceral leishmaniasis. A polyene macrolide isolated in the 1950s from the bacterium Streptomyces nodosus found in soil from Venezuela's Orinoco basin, it has been called the "gold standard" of antifungal therapy because of its broad spectrum and the rarity of resistance.

Mechanism

Amphotericin B binds to ergosterol, the principal sterol of fungal cell membranes, forming pores that leak ions and kill the cell. Its weaker affinity for cholesterol in human membranes accounts for much of its toxicity.

Uses and formulations

It is administered intravenously for invasive aspergillosis, cryptococcal meningitis, mucormycosis, and other deep mycoses, and is used in much of the world — especially where newer drugs are unaffordable — as first-line treatment for visceral leishmaniasis. Lipid formulations (liposomal amphotericin B, sold as AmBisome, and lipid complex preparations) reduce toxicity and allow higher doses. Oral and topical preparations are poorly absorbed and rarely used systemically.

Adverse effects

Conventional amphotericin B deoxycholate is notorious for infusion reactions (fever, rigors — "shake and bake") and dose-related kidney damage and electrolyte wasting, which require careful monitoring. Lipid formulations have softened but not eliminated these problems. Despite its toxicity, it remains indispensable because few alternatives cover the same range of fungi.
