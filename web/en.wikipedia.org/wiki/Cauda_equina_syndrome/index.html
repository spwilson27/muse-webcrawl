Cauda equina syndrome (CES) is a serious neurological condition caused by compression of the bundle of spinal nerves below the end of the spinal cord — the cauda equina ("horse's tail") — within the lumbar spinal canal. It is regarded as a surgical emergency because delayed treatment risks permanent paralysis and loss of bladder and bowel control.

Signs and symptoms

Characteristic "red flag" features include severe low back pain, sciatica (often bilateral), numbness in the saddle distribution (the perineum and inner thighs), weakness in the legs, and disturbance of bladder function — typically urinary retention progressing to overflow incontinence — or of bowel and sexual function.

Causes

The commonest cause is a large central herniation of a lumbar intervertebral disc. Other causes include spinal stenosis, tumours, infection (such as an epidural abscess), trauma with fracture or haematoma, and complications of spinal procedures.

Diagnosis and treatment

Suspected CES requires urgent MRI of the lumbosacral spine. Confirmed compression is treated with emergency surgical decompression, usually by laminectomy; outcomes are best when surgery is performed promptly, and the timing of intervention in incomplete cases has been the subject of extensive clinical and medico-legal debate.
