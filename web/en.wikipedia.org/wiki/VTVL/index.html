VTVL

VTVL (vertical takeoff, vertical landing) is a form of takeoff and landing for rockets in which the vehicle ascends vertically under rocket power and returns to Earth vertically, descending tail-first on a pillar of thrust to a soft landing. The concept was long a staple of science fiction and early rocket experiments, and has become practical in the 21st century as a key technology for reusable launch vehicles, dramatically reducing the cost of access to space.

History

Early concepts included the Soviet and American proposals of the mid-20th century, and the Apollo Lunar Module landed vertically on rocket power (though it took off from the Moon rather than returning through an atmosphere). The McDonnell Douglas DC-X ("Delta Clipper") demonstrated rapid-turnaround VTVL flight in the 1990s. The approach reached operational maturity with SpaceX's Grasshopper test vehicle and the first successful recovery of a Falcon 9 first stage in December 2015, after which powered vertical landings of orbital booster stages — on land and on autonomous drone ships at sea — became routine. Blue Origin's suborbital New Shepard also flies a VTVL profile.

Technology

VTVL requires rocket engines that can throttle deeply and restart reliably, aerodynamic control surfaces such as grid fins for steering during atmospheric descent, guidance able to target a precise landing point, and landing legs deployed before touchdown. A returning booster typically performs a series of burns: a boostback burn to shape its trajectory, a reentry burn to slow its fall through the upper atmosphere, and a final landing burn that brings it to a hover or direct touchdown — the latter sometimes called a "hoverslam" or "suicide burn" because the burn must be timed to reach zero velocity exactly at the surface.
