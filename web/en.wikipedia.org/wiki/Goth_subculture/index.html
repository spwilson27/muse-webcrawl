Goth subculture

The goth subculture is a music-based subculture that emerged in the United Kingdom in the late 1970s and early 1980s from the post-punk scene, centred on gothic rock bands such as Bauhaus, Siouxsie and the Banshees, The Cure, Joy Division and The Sisters of Mercy. It is characterised by an aesthetic of dark clothing and makeup, and by tastes for gothic literature, horror, romanticism and the macabre. The subculture spread internationally and persists through clubs, festivals (notably Germany's Wave-Gotik-Treffen in Leipzig) and online communities, with many internal variants.

Origins

The term "gothic rock" was applied to music by Bauhaus — whose 1979 debut single "Bela Lugosi's Dead" is treated as the genre's founding record — and their contemporaries. London's Batcave club (1982) gave the emerging scene a home and a name, blending glam, punk and horror-film imagery. Early fans were called goths by association with the music, and a distinct identity crystallised in the mid-1980s.

Music and fashion

Goth music spans the tribal drums and bass-led sound of early gothic rock, the ethereal textures of Cocteau Twins and Dead Can Dance, deathrock in the United States, and later industrial and electronic offshoots. Fashion typically features black clothing, fishnets, leather and PVC, silver (often religious or occult) jewellery, pale foundation with dark eyeliner and lipstick, and hairstyles ranging from backcombed "deathhawks" to Victorian and Edwardian revival styles. Later branches include romantic goth, cybergoth, gothic Lolita (in Japan) and "trad goth" revivals.

Culture and reception

Studies of the subculture note its longevity, its members' frequent continuation into adulthood, and its emphasis on creativity, tolerance and individualism. Goths have periodically faced moral panic and harassment — most notoriously in the aftermath of the 1999 Columbine massacre (whose perpetrators were wrongly identified as goths) and in the 2007 murder of Sophie Lancaster in England, which led to campaigning for hate-crime recognition.
