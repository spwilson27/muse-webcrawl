Reticle

A reticle, or reticule also known as a graticule, is a pattern of fine lines or markings built into the eyepiece of an optical device such as a telescopic sight, spotting scope, theodolite, microscope, or the screen of an oscilloscope, to provide measurement references during visual examinations. Today, engraved lines or embedded fibers are commonly used in such devices.

Uses

The best-known use is in telescopic sights, where the reticle — often called crosshairs — is used to aim a firearm. Reticle designs range from a simple pair of crossed lines to duplex, mil-dot, and Christmas-tree patterns with graduated markings used to estimate range and compensate for bullet drop and wind drift. In surveying and microscopy, reticles provide scales for measuring angles, distances, and specimen sizes.

Construction

Early reticles were made from fine threads, spider silk, or human hair stretched across the focal plane. Modern reticles are typically etched onto a glass plate or formed from thin metal foil, and may be illuminated by a battery-powered LED or by ambient light collected through a fiber optic, as in some holographic and red-dot sights.

Focal plane position

In variable-magnification scopes the reticle may be placed in the first focal plane, where it scales with the image so that its graduations remain accurate at any magnification, or in the second focal plane, where it stays the same size and its graduations are accurate at only one magnification.
