Wall drawing

Wall drawings are works of art made directly on a wall, rather than on a portable support. The category spans prehistoric cave painting, Renaissance fresco, modern murals, and the conceptual wall drawings of the late 20th century, in which the work exists as a set of instructions to be executed on a wall.

History

The oldest known wall drawings are the paintings in caves such as Chauvet and Lascaux in France, made tens of thousands of years ago. Egyptian tombs, Roman villas, and medieval churches all carried painted walls, and fresco — painting into wet plaster — became the dominant monumental technique of the Renaissance, exemplified by Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel ceiling.

Sol LeWitt and conceptual wall drawings

The term "wall drawing" is most closely associated with the American artist Sol LeWitt, who from 1968 onwards produced more than 1,200 wall drawings. LeWitt's works are not objects but instructions: written directions and diagrams that others execute on the wall, often in graphite, coloured pencil, or acrylic paint. The drawings are temporary, painted over at the end of an exhibition, and can be re-executed elsewhere. The idea, LeWitt argued, becomes "a machine that makes the art".

Other forms

Contemporary wall drawing includes murals and street art, children's chalk drawings, architectural drafting on walls during construction, and decorative wall painting in domestic interiors. Digital projection has added a further medium, in which drawn images are projected onto walls without physical contact.

Conservation

Because wall drawings are inseparable from their architectural support, they present particular conservation problems: they cannot be moved, they are vulnerable to building decay and renovation, and — in the case of LeWitt's works — their authenticity resides in the instructions and their authorised execution rather than in any single physical instance.
