Bell test

A Bell test, also called a Bell inequality experiment or Bell experiment, is a real-world physics experiment designed to test the theory of quantum mechanics against the concept of local realism. The experiments test whether nature obeys Bell's theorem, which shows that quantum predictions for entangled particles cannot be reproduced by any theory with local hidden variables.

Background

In 1964, physicist John Stewart Bell proved that correlations between measurements on entangled particles, made with independently chosen settings, must satisfy an inequality if local realism holds — but that quantum mechanics predicts violations of that inequality. The first experimental tests were performed in the 1970s by John Clauser and Stuart Freedman, followed by the influential experiments of Alain Aspect in the early 1980s.

Loopholes

Early tests left open "loopholes" — the locality loophole (settings and outcomes possibly communicating), the detection loophole (unrepresentative detected subsample), and the freedom-of-choice loophole. In 2015, several experiments (at Delft, NIST, and Vienna) closed the major loopholes simultaneously, using entangled electrons or photons, fast random setting choices, and high-efficiency detection.

Results and significance

All well-designed experiments have found violations of Bell inequalities in agreement with quantum theory, ruling out local hidden-variable descriptions of nature. The work was recognized by the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics, awarded to Aspect, Clauser and Anton Zeilinger for experiments with entangled photons and the violation of Bell inequalities.
