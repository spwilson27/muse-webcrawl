In spectral graph theory, a Ramanujan graph is a regular graph whose spectral gap is almost as large as possible. Such graphs are excellent expanders, making them valuable in combinatorics, number theory, and theoretical computer science. They are named after the Indian mathematician Srinivasa Ramanujan, because the definition rests on the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture, proved by Pierre Deligne.

Definition

Let G be a connected d-regular graph with n vertices, and let λ1 ≥ λ2 ≥ … ≥ λn be the eigenvalues of its adjacency matrix. Because G is connected and d-regular, λ1 = d. Define λ(G) = max{|λi| : λi ≠ ±d}. The graph G is called a Ramanujan graph if λ(G) ≤ 2√(d − 1). By the Alon–Boppana bound, 2√(d − 1) is asymptotically the best possible value, so Ramanujan graphs achieve an essentially optimal spectral gap.

Constructions

The first explicit constructions of infinite families of Ramanujan graphs were given by Alexander Lubotzky, Ralph Phillips, and Peter Sarnak in 1988, and independently by Grigory Margulis. These constructions use deep results from number theory, in particular the Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture for modular forms, together with the theory of quaternion algebras and Cayley graphs of projective special linear groups. Later, Marcus, Spielman, and Srivastava proved the existence of bipartite Ramanujan graphs of every degree using interlacing families of polynomials.

Applications

Because of their optimal expansion properties, Ramanujan graphs are used in derandomization, error-correcting codes, pseudorandom generators, and the design of efficient communication networks. Their vertices and edges also model the structure of certain cryptographic hash functions.
