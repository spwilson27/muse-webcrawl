Romanization

Romanization (in British English also Romanisation) is the process by which the peoples conquered by ancient Rome adopted Roman and Latin culture, language, law and institutions. The term is used by historians to describe the spread of Roman urban life, citizenship, dress, religion and material culture across Italy and the provinces of the Roman Empire.

Mechanisms

Romanization proceeded through the founding of colonies of veterans, the building of cities with forums, baths, amphitheatres and aqueducts, the granting of citizenship and Latin rights to local elites, service in the Roman army, taxation in coin, and the adoption of Latin as the language of government — in the West eventually giving rise to the Romance languages. Local aristocrats often Romanized voluntarily, building villas and taking Roman names to advance within the imperial order.

Debate

Older scholarship, such as Francis Haverfield's, portrayed Romanization as a one-way civilizing mission. Modern historians stress negotiation and diversity: provincial cultures hybridized, persisted, or selectively appropriated Roman forms — a process sometimes reframed as "becoming Roman" or, in the eastern empire, where Greek remained dominant, as only shallow Romanization. Regional outcomes varied greatly, from deeply Latinized Gaul and Hispania to areas where indigenous languages and cults endured for centuries.

Other senses

The word is also used for later or unrelated processes, including the adoption of Roman Catholic practice, and in linguistics for the transliteration of other writing systems into the Latin alphabet (romanization of Chinese, Japanese, Korean, etc.), which is usually spelled with a lower-case r.
