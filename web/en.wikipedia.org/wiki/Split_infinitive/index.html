Split infinitive

A split infinitive is a grammatical construction in English in which an adverb or adverbial phrase comes between the particle to and the infinitive verb, as in "to boldly go." The construction has existed in English since the Middle Ages but became a target of prescriptive criticism in the nineteenth century, when some grammarians argued, by analogy with Latin, that the infinitive should be kept whole.

History of the controversy

Split infinitives are attested from the thirteenth century and appear in the works of writers such as John Wycliffe and later Daniel Defoe and Benjamin Franklin. Criticism emerged in the 1800s; an 1834 magazine letter and textbooks such as Henry Alford's A Plea for the Queen's English (1864) popularised the objection. The objection rested on the fact that in Latin the infinitive is a single word and therefore cannot be split — an argument most modern linguists reject, since English to is a separate particle.

Modern usage

Modern reference grammars accept the split infinitive as standard. Sometimes splitting is necessary to avoid ambiguity or awkwardness: in "to more than double," there is no natural alternative placement. Style guides generally advise avoiding a split only when placing the adverb elsewhere reads equally well and preserves the meaning.

Examples

The most famous split infinitive is the Star Trek line "to boldly go where no man has gone before." Other common examples include "to really enjoy," "to fully understand," and "to gradually decline."
