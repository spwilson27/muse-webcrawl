Hierarchy of genres


History painting topped the hierarchy

A hierarchy of genres is any formal ranking of the different types or genres in an art form in terms of their perceived value or prestige. The best-known example is the hierarchy of subject matter in European academic painting, formalized by the French Academy in the 17th century, particularly in the writings of André Félibien (1667).

The academic hierarchy

In Félibien's formulation, the ranking from highest to lowest was:

  1. History painting — grand narratives from history, mythology, religion, and allegory, involving multiple figures;
  2. Portraiture;
  3. Genre painting — scenes of everyday life;
  4. Landscape;
  5. Animal painting;
  6. Still life — at the bottom, requiring the least intellectual invention.

The hierarchy reflected the belief that art's highest purpose was the depiction of significant human action and moral exemplars. It governed the French Academy's teaching, the hanging of pictures at exhibitions such as the Paris Salon, and the prestige and prices accorded to different kinds of work.

Decline and influence

During the 19th century the hierarchy was increasingly challenged — landscape and genre painters gained status, and movements from Romanticism to Impressionism rejected academic rankings altogether. Analogous hierarchies exist in other arts: in literature, epic and tragedy traditionally ranked above comedy and the novel; in Chinese painting, landscape (shanshui) held the highest prestige.
