Identification friend or foe

Identification, friend or foe (IFF) is an identification system designed for command and control that enables military and civilian air traffic control systems to identify aircraft, vehicles, and forces as friendly, and to determine their bearing and range from the interrogator. IFF uses an interrogator that sends coded radio pulses and a transponder aboard the target that replies with an identifying code; targets that do not reply correctly may be treated as hostile. The system is a secondary surveillance radar, dependent on the target's cooperation rather than on reflected echoes alone.

History

IFF was developed during the Second World War in response to the difficulty of distinguishing friendly from enemy aircraft on radar, a problem made urgent during the Battle of Britain. The British IFF Mark I and Mark II systems were followed by the Mark III, standardized among the Allies, which became the basis of postwar civil secondary surveillance radar. In 1940 the first active IFF transponder systems were flight-tested in Britain and the United States; wartime failures of identification nonetheless contributed to friendly-fire incidents, such as the 1943 friendly-fire disaster during the invasion of Sicily, when Allied anti-aircraft fire shot down American transport aircraft.

Modes of operation

Civil modes include Mode A (identity code), Mode C (pressure altitude), and Mode S, which gives each aircraft a unique 24-bit address and selective interrogation. Military modes include Mode 1 (mission), Mode 2 (unit), Mode 3 (shared with civil Mode A), and the encrypted Mode 4 and Mode 5, which use cryptographic challenge-and-reply authentication to prevent spoofing. Modern Mode 5, fielded by NATO and allied forces, adds improved secure identification and data such as GPS position.

Limitations and use

Because IFF relies on a functioning, correctly set transponder, silence does not prove hostility; failures, battle damage, or code errors have repeatedly contributed to fratricide, including the 1994 shootdown of two U.S. Army Black Hawk helicopters by U.S. Air Force fighters over Iraq and the 2003 Patriot missile incidents. IFF is therefore used as one input among several in rules of engagement, and modern combat identification increasingly combines IFF with data links, satellite tracking, and non-cooperative target recognition.
