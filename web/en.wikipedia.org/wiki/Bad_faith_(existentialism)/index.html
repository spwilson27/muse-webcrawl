Bad faith (existentialism)

Bad faith (French: mauvaise foi) is a central concept in the existentialist philosophy of Jean-Paul Sartre, denoting a form of self-deception in which a person denies their own freedom and responsibility by pretending to be determined by their circumstances, social role, or nature. Sartre developed the idea most fully in Being and Nothingness (1943).

The concept

For Sartre, human consciousness is characterised by a tension between facticity (the given facts of one's situation — body, past, social position) and transcendence (the freedom to interpret and go beyond those facts). Bad faith occurs when a person flees this tension by collapsing one side into the other: treating oneself as a mere thing wholly defined by one's role, or, conversely, pretending one's situation imposes no constraints at all. Because the deceiver and the deceived are the same person, bad faith is a paradoxical "lie to oneself" that Sartre treats as a project of consciousness rather than a simple falsehood.

Examples

Sartre's famous example is a café waiter who plays at being a waiter too completely — moving with exaggerated precision, as if his role were an essence he must perform rather than a job he freely chooses. Another example concerns a woman on a date who leaves her hand resting in her companion's hand while refusing to notice the romantic significance of the gesture, treating her body as a mere object separate from herself.

Related thought

Simone de Beauvoir applied cognate ideas in The Ethics of Ambiguity (1947) and The Second Sex (1949), analysing how the oppressed may be complicit in, or forced into, attitudes of bad faith. The concept contrasts with authenticity — the lucid acceptance of one's freedom and responsibility. It differs from ordinary lying and from Freudian unconscious self-deception, since Sartre denies the existence of an unconscious censor.
