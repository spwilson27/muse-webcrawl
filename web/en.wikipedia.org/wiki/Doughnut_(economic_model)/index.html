The Doughnut, or Doughnut economics, is a visual framework for sustainable development proposed by the British economist Kate Raworth. It depicts a "safe and just space for humanity" as the ring of a doughnut: inside the hole lies human deprivation — falling below a social foundation of well-being — while beyond the outer crust lies ecological overshoot — exceeding an ecological ceiling of planetary pressure. A regenerative and distributive economy, Raworth argues, should bring everyone into the Doughnut's ring without overshooting Earth's life-supporting systems.

Origins

Raworth first published the diagram in a 2012 Oxfam discussion paper, "A Safe and Just Space for Humanity," drawing its outer boundary from the planetary boundaries framework developed by Johan Rockström and colleagues at the Stockholm Resilience Centre, and its inner boundary from the social priorities that fed into the UN Sustainable Development Goals. She developed the ideas in her 2017 book Doughnut Economics: Seven Ways to Think Like a 21st-Century Economist, which critiques mainstream economics' fixation on GDP growth and argues for economies designed to be distributive and regenerative by design.

The framework

The social foundation comprises twelve dimensions derived from internationally agreed minimum standards — food, water, health, education, income and work, peace and justice, political voice, social equity, gender equality, housing, networks and energy. The ecological ceiling comprises the nine planetary boundaries: climate change, biodiversity loss, nitrogen and phosphorus loading, ocean acidification, land-use change, freshwater use, ozone depletion, air pollution and chemical pollution. In Raworth's global "portrait," humanity currently falls short on most social dimensions while overshooting several boundaries simultaneously — the central predicament the model is meant to communicate.

Applications and reception

The Doughnut Economics Action Lab (DEAL), co-founded by Raworth, works with cities and regions to "downscale" the Doughnut to local portraits; Amsterdam formally adopted a city Doughnut in 2020 as a framework for its post-COVID recovery and circular-economy strategy, and Brussels, Copenhagen and others followed with adaptations. Supporters praise the model as an intuitive compass beyond GDP; critics note that aggregating social and ecological indicators into national "portraits" involves contestable weighting, that the model is a dashboard rather than a theory of change, and that it says little about the political economy of getting inside the ring. Raworth herself presents it as a framework for rethinking goals rather than a complete policy programme.
