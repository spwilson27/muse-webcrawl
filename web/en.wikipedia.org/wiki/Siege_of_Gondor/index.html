Siege of Gondor

The Siege of Gondor is a fictional siege in J. R. R. Tolkien's novel The Lord of the Rings (1954–55). It forms the climax of the first part of The Return of the King, in which the forces of Sauron, led by the Witch-king of Angmar, besiege Minas Tirith, the capital of Gondor. The siege culminates in the Battle of the Pelennor Fields, where the Rohirrim under King Théoden relieve the city at dawn.

In the narrative

Following the capture of Osgiliath and the crossing of the Anduin, Sauron's armies occupy the Pelennor Fields on 13 March T.A. 3019. Earlier, Faramir is wounded in the retreat from the Rammas Echor and carried into the city; the Steward Denethor, despairing after seeing a vision in a palantír, later attempts to burn himself and the wounded Faramir on a pyre, and dies by self-immolation while Faramir is rescued by Gandalf and Beregond.

On 14 March the besiegers surround Minas Tirith, filling the trenches with fire and bombarding the first circle with catapults, including the severed heads of fallen Gondorians. The great battering ram Grond, hauled by huge beasts and wielded by mountain-trolls, breaks the city gate at dawn on 15 March, aided by the Witch-king's sorcery. The Witch-king enters the gateway and confronts Gandalf — but is interrupted by the horns of Rohan.

Battle of the Pelennor Fields

The arrival of 6,000 Rohirrim turns the siege into an open battle on the Pelennor. Théoden is slain by the Witch-king, who is himself killed by Éowyn and the hobbit Merry, fulfilling Glorfindel's prophecy. Prince Imrahil sallies from the city; later in the day Aragorn arrives by ship up the Anduin with reinforcements from southern Gondor, having captured the Corsairs' fleet. The combined hosts destroy Sauron's army, though at heavy cost.

Analysis

Commentators note Tolkien's drawing on historical sieges — including the sieges of Vienna and Constantinople — and on his First World War experience for the atmosphere of dread, the field hospitals of the Houses of Healing, and morale under bombardment. The siege's structure, interlaced with the parallel quest of Frodo and Sam, is a frequently discussed feature of the novel's narrative technique.
