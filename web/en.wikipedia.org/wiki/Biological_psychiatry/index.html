Biological psychiatry

Hero image: functional brain imaging, a central tool in the biological investigation of mental illness.

Biological psychiatry is an approach to psychiatry that aims to understand mental disorder in terms of the biological function of the nervous system. It is interdisciplinary, drawing on neuroscience, biochemistry, genetics, physiology, neurology, and pharmacology, and seeks to identify biomarkers, neural circuits, and genetic contributions underlying conditions such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and major depression.

History

Biological explanations of mental illness are ancient — Hippocratic physicians attributed madness to humoral imbalance — but the modern field took shape in the nineteenth century, when psychiatrists such as Wilhelm Griesinger declared that "mental diseases are brain diseases" and Emil Kraepelin classified psychoses on the basis of course and outcome. The discovery that general paresis of the insane was caused by syphilis gave early proof that a severe psychiatric syndrome could have a specific biological cause.

The field was transformed in the 1950s by the serendipitous discovery of chlorpromazine for schizophrenia and imipramine and iproniazid for depression, which gave rise to the monoamine hypotheses — that mood and psychotic disorders involve dysregulation of neurotransmitters such as dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine. Later work added genetics (twin and genome-wide association studies), neuroimaging, and neuroendocrinology. The journal Biological Psychiatry, founded in 1969, is the field's flagship publication.

Concepts and criticism

Biological psychiatry underpins modern diagnostic manuals and drug development, but it has been criticized for reductionism: the simple "chemical imbalance" account of depression is regarded by many researchers as an oversimplification, and no validated biomarker yet diagnoses any major psychiatric disorder. Frameworks such as the NIMH Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) attempt to ground diagnosis in dimensions of neural function rather than symptom categories, integrating biological with psychological and social levels of analysis in a biopsychosocial model.
