Adjunct professor

TypeContingent academic appointment
EmploymentPart-time, per-course contract
TenureNone (non-tenure-track)

An adjunct professor is a type of academic appointment in higher education, most common in the United States and Canada. In North American usage, adjunct professors are part-time, non-tenure-track faculty hired on limited contracts, usually paid per course, to teach specific classes. The growing reliance on adjunct labour — sometimes called the "adjunctification" of higher education — has been a subject of debate about academic employment conditions.

Role and conditions

Adjuncts typically carry teaching loads only, without the research or service obligations of tenure-track faculty, and usually receive lower per-course pay and few benefits. Many hold advanced degrees and teach at several institutions simultaneously. In the United States, contingent faculty of all types make up a large share of instructional staff at colleges and universities.

Other uses of the title

Outside North America, "adjunct professor" can denote an honorary or part-time senior rank: for example, a practicing professional (a physician, lawyer, or engineer) affiliated with a university, or, in some European systems, a mid-career research position roughly equivalent to associate professor. These usages differ substantially from the North American contingent model.

Criticism and reform

Critics argue that heavy reliance on adjuncts undermines academic freedom, student mentoring, and job security, while defenders cite flexibility and cost control. Unionization drives and institutional conversions of adjunct lines to full-time positions have been responses to these concerns.
