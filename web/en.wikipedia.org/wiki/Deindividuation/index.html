Deindividuation

Deindividuation

Deindividuation is a concept in social psychology that is generally thought of as the loss of self-awareness in groups, although this is often disputed, as the concept requires further validation. American social psychologist Leon Festinger and colleagues first coined the term in 1952. It describes a psychological state in which individuals engaged in group behaviour lose their sense of individual identity and personal responsibility, becoming more likely to act on impulses they would ordinarily restrain.

Overview

Deindividuation is characterised by a diminished sense of personal identity, reduced concern for social evaluation, and weakened restraints against deviant behaviour. It is most likely to occur in group contexts that provide anonymity, diffuse responsibility, involve high levels of arousal, and focus attention on the group rather than the self. Classic examples include crowds, mobs, military units in uniform, and anonymous online communities.

The phenomenon has roots in Gustave Le Bon's 1895 work on crowd psychology, which proposed that individuals in a crowd submerge their personal identity into a collective "group mind". Festinger, Pepitone and Newcomb developed the idea experimentally in the early 1950s, and Philip Zimbardo expanded the theory in 1969, identifying input variables such as anonymity, group size, arousal and altered time perspective.

Theoretical developments

Edward Diener's work in the 1970s shifted emphasis from anonymity itself to reduced private self-awareness as the core mechanism: when people stop monitoring their own behaviour against internal standards, self-regulation fails. The social identity model of deindividuation effects (SIDE), developed by Russell Spears, Martin Lea and Stephen Reicher, challenges the classic account, arguing that anonymity in groups does not produce a loss of identity but a shift from personal identity to a salient social identity, so behaviour becomes more norm-governed — governed by group norms rather than individual ones.

Applications

Deindividuation has been invoked to explain behaviour ranging from rioting, looting and lynch mobs to online disinhibition and "flaming" in anonymous internet forums. Diener's Halloween studies found that children in costumes and in groups were more likely to steal extra sweets and money when anonymous. In organisational settings, uniforms, masks and remote communication have all been studied as deindividuating conditions. Critics note that laboratory manipulations of anonymity often produce modest effects, and that many crowd behaviours are orderly and purposive rather than impulsive.
