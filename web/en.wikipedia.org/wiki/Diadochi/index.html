The Diadochi (singular: Diadochus; from Greek Διάδοχοι, 'successors') were the rival generals, families, and friends of Alexander the Great who fought for control over his empire after his death in 323 BC. The Wars of the Diadochi mark the beginning of the Hellenistic period, from the Mediterranean to the Indus River Valley.

The most notable Diadochi include Ptolemy, Antigonus, Lysimachus, Seleucus, and Cassander, whose competing claims produced some forty years of wars before a rough equilibrium of Hellenistic kingdoms emerged.

Partition of Babylon

After Alexander died at Babylon in June 323 BC, his generals divided the empire's satrapies at the Partition of Babylon, nominally ruling for Alexander's unborn son and his half-brother Philip III Arrhidaeus, with Perdiccas as regent. Rivalries quickly escalated into open war after Perdiccas was assassinated in Egypt in 321/320 BC, leading to the Partition of Triparadisus.

Wars

A series of conflicts (322–281 BC) followed. Antigonus Monophthalmus, the most powerful of the successors, was defeated and killed at the Battle of Ipsus in 301 BC by a coalition of Lysimachus, Seleucus, Cassander, and Ptolemy. The last surviving Diadochus, Seleucus, was assassinated in 281 BC after defeating Lysimachus at Corupedium, closing the era.

Hellenistic kingdoms

The settlement produced the great Hellenistic states: the Ptolemaic Kingdom in Egypt, the Seleucid Empire in Asia, Antigonid Macedon, and later the Attalid kingdom of Pergamon — powers that dominated the eastern Mediterranean until the rise of Rome.
