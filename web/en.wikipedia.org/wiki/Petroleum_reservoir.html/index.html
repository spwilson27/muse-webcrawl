Petroleum reservoir

A petroleum reservoir, or oil and gas reservoir, is a subsurface accumulation of hydrocarbons contained in porous or fractured rock formations, sealed beneath an impermeable caprock. The reservoir rock — commonly sandstone, limestone, or dolomite — must be porous enough to store fluids and permeable enough to let them flow to production wells.

Formation

A complete petroleum system requires an organic-rich source rock that generated hydrocarbons through burial and heating, migration pathways, a reservoir rock, a trap (structural, such as an anticline or fault block, or stratigraphic), and a seal. Within a reservoir, fluids segregate by density: gas on top, oil in the middle, and formation water below, separated by gas–oil and oil–water contacts.

Reservoir drive and recovery

Production is driven by natural mechanisms — solution gas drive, gas cap drive, water drive, and gravity drainage — supplemented by pressure maintenance (water or gas injection) and enhanced oil recovery methods such as thermal recovery, CO2 injection, and chemical flooding. Recovery factors vary widely, commonly 20–40% of oil in place for conventional reservoirs.

Estimation

Reserves are estimated from geological, geophysical, and production data and classified as proved, probable, or possible. The largest conventional reservoirs include Saudi Arabia's Ghawar field and other giant fields of the Middle East.
