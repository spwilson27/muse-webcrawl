Compliance (psychology)

Compliance in psychology refers to changing one's behaviour in response to a direct request or suggestion from another person, without that person necessarily holding authority. It is one of three types of social influence identified by Herbert Kelman in 1958, alongside identification and internalisation.

Compliance techniques

Research has identified several techniques that reliably increase compliance:

  • Foot-in-the-door: agreeing to a small initial request increases the chance of agreeing to a larger one later (Freedman & Fraser, 1966).
  • Door-in-the-face: a large request that is refused is followed by a smaller, target request, which is then more likely to be accepted.
  • Low-ball: agreement is obtained before hidden costs or conditions are revealed.
  • Reciprocity: people feel obliged to return favours, so a small gift or concession increases compliance.
  • That's-not-all: an offer is sweetened before the target has a chance to respond.

Foundations

Robert Cialdini's work on persuasion summarised the underlying principles as reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, liking, authority, and scarcity. Compliance differs from obedience, which involves commands from an authority figure (as in Milgram's experiments), and from conformity, which involves adjusting to group norms without any explicit request.
