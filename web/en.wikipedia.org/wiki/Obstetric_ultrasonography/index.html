Obstetric ultrasonography, or prenatal ultrasound, is the use of medical ultrasonography in pregnancy, in which sound waves are used to create real-time visual images of the developing embryo or fetus in the uterus. It is a standard part of prenatal care in many countries, used to confirm pregnancy, establish gestational age, detect multiple pregnancies and screen for congenital abnormalities. The procedure involves no ionising radiation and has an excellent safety record at diagnostic intensities.

History

Medical ultrasound was adapted for obstetrics in the 1950s, notably by the Scottish physician Ian Donald and engineer Tom Brown in Glasgow, who published landmark work on fetal imaging in 1958. Through the 1970s real-time grey-scale scanners replaced static images, and by the 1980s ultrasound examination had become routine in antenatal care in most developed countries.

Procedure

A transducer is moved over the abdomen with a coupling gel, or a transvaginal probe is used in early pregnancy for higher resolution. Reflected high-frequency sound waves are converted into a two-dimensional image; Doppler modes can display blood flow and the fetal heartbeat, and 3D/4D modes reconstruct surface views. A full bladder may be requested for early transabdominal scans to improve the acoustic window.

Timing and uses

  • First trimester (6–13 weeks) — confirm intrauterine pregnancy, viability, number of fetuses; the 11–13+6 week scan measures nuchal translucency as part of screening for chromosomal conditions such as Down syndrome.
  • Second trimester (18–22 weeks) — the anomaly or anatomy scan systematically examines fetal structures, the placenta and amniotic fluid.
  • Third trimester — growth scans, presentation, placental position and Doppler assessment where indicated.

Dating and measurements

Crown–rump length in the first trimester dates a pregnancy to within about five to seven days. Later measurements include biparietal diameter, head and abdominal circumference and femur length, combined into estimates of fetal weight. Ultrasound can also determine fetal sex from the mid-trimester scan, subject to local law and policy.

Safety

No adverse effects on mother or fetus have been demonstrated for diagnostic ultrasound used appropriately. Professional bodies such as AIUM and ISUOG endorse the ALARA principle — exposure as low as reasonably achievable — and discourage non-medical "keepsake" scanning with prolonged exposure.
