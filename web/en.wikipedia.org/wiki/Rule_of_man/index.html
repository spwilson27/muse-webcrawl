Rule of man

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The rule of man is a political and legal concept describing a system in which authority is exercised according to the personal will of rulers rather than by fixed, impartial law. It is most often used as the contrasting term to the rule of law: where the rule of law subordinates government to general rules known in advance, the rule of man makes outcomes depend on who decides.

The contrast is ancient. In Chinese political thought, rénzhì (rule of man) was debated against fǎzhì (rule of law or legalism) from the classical period onward; Confucian writers often held that good government depends on virtuous rulers, while Legalist thinkers such as Han Fei argued for impersonal laws and techniques of administration. In Western thought, Aristotle's preference for the rule of law over the rule of any individual, and James Harrington's seventeenth-century call for an “empire of laws and not of men”, frame the same opposition.

Usage

The phrase is usually pejorative in modern constitutional writing, implying arbitrariness, favouritism and unpredictability. Yet theorists note that no legal order eliminates human discretion entirely: judges interpret, officials enforce selectively, and emergencies strain rules. The realistic debate is therefore about how far discretion should be structured and reviewable, rather than about a literal choice between laws and men. Political scientists also use the concept descriptively for personalist and patrimonial regimes, where authority inheres in a leader rather than in offices.
