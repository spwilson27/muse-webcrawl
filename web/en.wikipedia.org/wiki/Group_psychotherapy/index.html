Group psychotherapy or group therapy is a form of psychotherapy in which one or more therapists treat a small group of clients together as a group. The term can refer to any form of psychotherapy delivered in a group format, including art therapy, cognitive behavioural therapy, and interpersonal therapy, but often applies to psychodynamic group therapy, where the group context and group processes are explicitly utilised as mechanisms of change.

The broader concept of any form of group treatment, including support groups, skills-training groups, and psychoeducation groups, is also in use. Practitioners include psychologists, psychiatrists, clinical social workers, mental health counsellors, and psychiatric nurses.

History

The founders of group psychotherapy in the United States were Joseph H. Pratt, Trigant Burrow, and Paul Schilder in the early twentieth century. Their method attracted worldwide attention from the specialty after the Second World War, when group treatment proved efficient for large numbers of war veterans. Jacob L. Moreno is credited with developing psychodrama, a specific form of group psychotherapy. In the United Kingdom, group analysis was developed by S. H. Foulkes and others at the Group Analytic Society.

Therapeutic factors

Irvin Yalom proposed a set of therapeutic factors through which change occurs in group therapy: universality, altruism, instillation of hope, imparting information, corrective recapitulation of the primary family group, development of socialising techniques, imitative behaviour, cohesiveness, existential factors, catharsis, interpersonal learning, and self-understanding.

Forms

Groups may be open or closed, homogeneous or heterogeneous, time-limited or open-ended. Common formats include outpatient therapy groups, inpatient groups, self-help and support groups such as Alcoholics Anonymous, and structured skills groups used in dialectical behaviour therapy and cognitive behavioural therapy.

Evidence

Meta-analyses generally find group therapy to be about as effective as individual therapy for a wide range of conditions, including depression, anxiety disorders, and post-traumatic stress disorder, with the advantages of lower cost and the therapeutic benefits of peer interaction.
