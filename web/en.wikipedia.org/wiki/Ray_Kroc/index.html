Raymond Albert Kroc (October 5, 1902 – January 14, 1984) was an American businessman. He joined the California company McDonald's in 1954, after the McDonald brothers had franchised 6 locations out from their original 1940 operation in San Bernardino. This set the stage for national expansion with the help of Kroc, eventually leading to a global franchise, making it the most successful fast food corporation in the world. Kroc was included in Time 100, the list of the most important people of the century, and amassed a fortune during his lifetime. He owned the San Diego Padres baseball team from 1974 until his death in 1984.

Early life and career

Kroc was born in Oak Park, Illinois, to Czech-American parents. During World War I he lied about his age and trained as a Red Cross ambulance driver (in the same unit, briefly, as Walt Disney), though the war ended before he was sent overseas. Between the wars he worked as a paper cup salesman for the Lily-Tulip Cup Company, a jazz musician, and a radio music director. From 1939 he became the exclusive distributor of the Multimixer, a machine that could mix five milkshakes at once, and spent 15 years selling it to restaurants across America.

McDonald's

In 1954 Kroc visited the McDonald brothers' San Bernardino restaurant, puzzled why a single stand needed eight Multimixers. Impressed by Richard and Maurice McDonald's "Speedee Service System" — a streamlined, assembly-line operation serving a tiny menu of high-quality hamburgers, fries and shakes quickly and cheaply — he proposed franchising the concept nationally. The brothers agreed, and Kroc founded the McDonald's System, Inc. in 1955, opening his first franchised restaurant in Des Plaines, Illinois, on 15 April 1955.

Kroc's franchising model was transformed when his associate Harry Sonneborn devised Franchise Realty Corporation, which bought or leased the land under franchisees' restaurants and collected rent — in Sonneborn's phrase, they were really in the real estate business. Frustrated by the limits of his agreement with the brothers, Kroc bought them out in 1961 for $2.7 million, plus, by the brothers' account, a handshake promise of a continuing royalty that was never paid; the original San Bernardino restaurant was excluded from the deal and eventually closed after Kroc opened a competing McDonald's across the street. Under Kroc, McDonald's standardised every detail of operations, expanded to thousands of outlets, went public in 1965, and spread internationally.

Later life and legacy

Kroc stepped down as president in 1968 and as chairman in 1977, becoming senior chairman. In 1974 he bought the San Diego Padres baseball team. He died of heart failure in San Diego on 14 January 1984. His third wife, Joan Kroc, inherited much of his fortune and became one of America's most notable philanthropists, leaving bequests of well over a billion dollars, including $1.5 billion to the Salvation Army. Kroc's role in McDonald's is contested terrain: he long promoted himself as the company's founder — a title the McDonald brothers' partisans dispute — and his story is dramatised in the 2016 film The Founder.
