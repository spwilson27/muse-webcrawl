Heroic couplet

A heroic couplet is a traditional form for English poetry, commonly used in epic and narrative poetry, and consisting of a pair of rhyming lines in iambic pentameter. The form was pioneered by Geoffrey Chaucer in the Legend of Good Women and the Canterbury Tales, and since the Restoration it has usually been a closed couplet, in which each pair of lines forms a complete grammatical unit and a self-contained thought.

History

Although Chaucer introduced the rhymed pentameter couplet into English, the heroic couplet took its characteristic polish in the seventeenth century, in the work of John Denham, whose Cooper's Hill (1642) was admired for its balanced phrasing, and Edmund Waller. John Dryden established the form as the dominant verse of the Augustan age, using it for satire, translation, and drama in his heroic plays.

Pope and the Augustans

The heroic couplet reached its height with Alexander Pope, whose Essay on Criticism (1711), The Rape of the Lock (1712–14), and translations of Homer are considered the supreme achievement of the form. Pope perfected the closed couplet's antithesis, parallelism, and caesura, as in his famous definition of wit:

True Wit is Nature to advantage dress'd,
What oft was thought, but ne'er so well express'd.

Samuel Johnson, Oliver Goldsmith, and George Crabb continued to write in heroic couplets, though the Romantics largely abandoned the form in favour of blank verse and lyric metres, finding it artificial and restrictive. Twentieth-century poets occasionally revived it for ironic and epigrammatic purposes.

Form

Each couplet consists of two lines of ten syllables, rhyming together, arranged in five iambic feet (an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed one). In the closed heroic couplet the sense is complete within the two lines, and a caesura — a pause, often marked by punctuation — typically falls in each line, letting poets balance clauses against one another for effects of wit, contrast, and epigrammatic point.
