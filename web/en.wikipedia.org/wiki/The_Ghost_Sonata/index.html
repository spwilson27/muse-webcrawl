The Ghost Sonata (Swedish: Spöksonaten) is a play in three acts by the Swedish playwright August Strindberg, written in 1907 as the third of his "chamber plays" for his intimate theatre in Stockholm, where it premiered on 21 January 1908. One of the key texts of modernist and expressionist theatre, the play presents a dream-like, hallucinatory world in which a student, Arkenholz, is drawn into a fine Stockholm house whose inhabitants are revealed as vampires, ghosts and living corpses bound together by old crimes and debts.

Plot

The young Student, who has spent the night helping rescue victims of a collapsed house, meets the sinister old Jacob Hummel, who claims a debt over him and arranges for him to attend the "ghost supper" at the house of Colonel Alsing. Inside, the household is a web of decay: the Colonel's wife, the Mummy, lives in a closet and speaks like a parrot; the Colonel's supposed daughter, the Young Lady, is in fact Hummel's daughter and is slowly dying in a room saturated with the household's sickness and deceit. Hummel, who has ruined many lives, is unmasked by the Mummy and hangs himself. The Student's hopes of a pure love with the Young Lady dissolve as he sees her world is irredeemable; she dies, and the play ends with the Student's speech over her body, quoting the Song of the Sun, as the backdrop of Böcklin's painting Isle of the Dead appears.

Structure and influences

Strindberg structured the play like a sonata — hence the title, which also alludes to Beethoven's Geistersonate (the "Ghost" Trio, Op. 70 No. 1) and Gespenstersonate. The three scenes correspond to musical movements and motifs recur throughout. The play draws on themes of vampirism and karmic debt partly inspired by Strindberg's reading of Emanuel Swedenborg and by his own "Inferno crisis" of the 1890s.

Reception and adaptations

Initially baffling to audiences, the play came to be regarded as one of Strindberg's masterpieces and a founding work of theatrical modernism, influencing expressionist and absurdist drama. Ingmar Bergman directed it for the stage four times and made a television version. The composer Aribert Reimann adapted it as the opera Die Gespenstersonate (1984).
