The Morris Minor is a British economy family car that was manufactured by Morris Motors between 1948 and 1971. It made its debut at the Earls Court Motor Show, London, in October 1948 and was designed by Alec Issigonis, later famous for the Mini. More than 1.6 million were built in three series: the Series MM (1948–1953), the Series II (1952–1956) and the Minor 1000 (1956–1971). The Minor was the first British car to sell over a million units and is considered a classic example of automotive design, much like the Volkswagen Beetle in Germany and the Citroën 2CV in France.

Design

For its time the Minor was advanced: unitary (monocoque) construction, independent front suspension by torsion bars, rack-and-pinion steering, and roadholding that reviewers praised for being far better than its rivals'. Issigonis widened the car by four inches at a late stage of development — production cars retain a raised strip down the centre of the bonnet where the extra width was accommodated. The original engine was the pre-war 918 cc side-valve flat-four-plan unit derived from the Morris Eight; later cars received Austin-designed A-series overhead-valve engines of 803, 948 and finally 1,098 cc.

Variants

Beyond the saloons, the range included a two-door convertible (tourer), the beloved Traveller estate with structural ash wood framing to its rear body, and light commercial versions: a van and a pick-up widely used by the Post Office and small businesses. Coachbuilders and enthusiasts produced many conversions. The Minor was assembled abroad, including in Australia and New Zealand, and sold widely throughout the Commonwealth.

Legacy

The millionth Minor, built in 1960, was celebrated with a limited run of 350 lilac-painted "Minor Million" cars. Production ended in 1971, but the Minor remains one of Britain's most popular classic cars, supported by an active owners' club, abundant spares supply, and a reputation for simplicity, charm, and durability.
