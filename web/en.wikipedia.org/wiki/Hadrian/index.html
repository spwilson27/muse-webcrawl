Hadrian

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Hadrian's Wall crossing the Northumberland crags at Housesteads]

Hadrian (AD 76–138; Publius Aelius Hadrianus) was Roman emperor from 117 to 138, the third of the 'Five Good Emperors'. Adopted — amid enduring suspicion about the adoption's circumstances — by his cousin and guardian Trajan, he abandoned Trajan's new Mesopotamian conquests as indefensible and spent his reign consolidating the empire: travelling almost continuously through the provinces for over a decade, inspecting armies and founding cities, and fixing the frontiers with works like Hadrian's Wall in Britain (from 122). A Hellenophile of formidable intellect — architect, poet, hunter — he rebuilt the Pantheon in Rome, raised the Temple of Venus and Rome, and in Judaea provoked, then crushed with terrible loss, the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–136).

The travelling emperor

Hadrian's journeys (121–125, 128–134) took him from Britain to Egypt, Athens (his beloved city, honoured with the Olympieion's completion and a new quarter) to the Danube. Everywhere he reviewed troops, remitted taxes, founded baths, aqueducts and colonies, and indulged his passions: architecture (his Villa at Tivoli is a museum of the empire in miniature) and the Bithynian youth Antinous, whose drowning in the Nile (130) plunged him into grief, a empire-wide cult, and the foundation of Antinoopolis. His beard — the first worn by an emperor in generations — reset imperial fashion for a century.

Last years

The Bar Kokhba war devastated Judaea (Cassius Dio gives 580,000 Jewish dead, a number treated with caution) and ended Jewish Jerusalem — refounded as pagan Aelia Capitolina. Failing in health, Hadrian executed suspected rivals, adopted the blameless Antoninus Pius on condition he adopt Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus — the settlement that gave Rome its golden half-century — and died at Baiae in July 138, embittered and in pain. The Senate reluctantly deified him; his great mausoleum became Castel Sant'Angelo.
