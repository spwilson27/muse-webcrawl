Hypernatremia

Hypernatremia
DefinitionSerum sodium > 145 mmol/L
SpecialtyNephrology, internal medicine
SymptomsThirst, confusion, irritability, seizures in severe cases
CausesWater loss, impaired thirst, diabetes insipidus, excess salt intake

Hypernatremia is an elevated concentration of sodium in the blood.

Hypernatremia, also spelled hypernatraemia, is a high concentration of sodium in the blood, defined as a serum sodium level above 145 mmol/L. It is fundamentally a disorder of water balance: it almost always reflects a deficit of water relative to sodium rather than sodium excess. Early symptoms may include a strong feeling of thirst, weakness, nausea and loss of appetite; severe hypernatremia causes confusion, muscle twitching, seizures and coma as brain cells shrink from water loss.

Causes

Causes are grouped by the patient's volume status. Hypovolemic hypernatremia results from combined water and sodium loss with water loss predominating — for example diarrhoea, vomiting, sweating, burns or osmotic diuresis from hyperglycaemia. Euvolemic hypernatremia arises from pure water loss, classically diabetes insipidus (central or nephrogenic), in which the kidneys cannot concentrate urine. Hypervolemic hypernatremia, the rarest form, follows administration of hypertonic saline or sodium bicarbonate, or mineralocorticoid excess. In all groups, impaired access to water or a blunted thirst mechanism — common in the elderly and in infants — is usually required for the condition to develop.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by blood electrolytes, with urine osmolality and sodium helping to identify the cause. Treatment centres on replacing the free-water deficit, usually with oral water or intravenous hypotonic fluids such as 5% dextrose. Correction must not be too rapid in chronic hypernatremia: lowering serum sodium faster than about 10–12 mmol/L per day risks cerebral oedema, because adapted brain cells have accumulated intracellular solutes. The underlying cause, such as diabetes insipidus, is treated in parallel (e.g. desmopressin for the central form).

Epidemiology

Hypernatremia is common in hospitalised patients, particularly the elderly and critically ill, and is associated with increased mortality, though it is rarely the direct cause of death.
