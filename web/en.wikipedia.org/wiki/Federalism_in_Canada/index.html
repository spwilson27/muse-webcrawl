Federalism in Canada

Established1867, British North America Act (Constitution Act, 1867)
Orders of governmentFederal; 10 provinces; 3 territories
Key sectionsss. 91–92, Constitution Act, 1867

Federalism in Canada is the system by which legislative authority is divided between the Parliament of Canada and the legislatures of the provinces, as set out chiefly in sections 91 and 92 of the Constitution Act, 1867.

Division of powers

Section 91 grants the federal Parliament power over matters of national scope — trade and commerce, defence, criminal law, banking, currency, and a general "peace, order, and good government" power. Section 92 assigns the provinces property and civil rights, health care, education, municipalities, natural resources, and most local matters. Some powers, such as agriculture and immigration, are concurrent. The three northern territories, by contrast, exercise powers delegated by Parliament rather than constitutionally guaranteed.

Development

Although the founders of 1867 designed a relatively centralized federation, decisions of the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council and, later, the Supreme Court of Canada shifted power toward the provinces, producing one of the world's most decentralized federations. Fiscal arrangements — equalization payments, conditional grants, and tax-point transfers — are a permanent arena of federal–provincial negotiation, as are the claims of Quebec, recognized in practice through asymmetrical arrangements, and the growing constitutional role of Indigenous governments and rights under section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982.
