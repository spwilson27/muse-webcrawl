Poet

A poet is a person who studies and creates poetry. Poets may describe themselves as such or be described as such by others. A poet may simply be a writer of poetry, or may perform their art to an audience.

History

Poetry, and the figure of the poet, predate literacy itself: in oral cultures, poet-singers such as the bards of Celtic tradition, the aoidos of ancient Greece, and the griots of West Africa preserved genealogy, myth, and history in verse. The word "poet" derives from the Greek poiētēs (ποιητής), "maker," from poiein, "to make" — emphasising the poet as a craftsman of language. In ancient Rome, poets such as Virgil and Horace enjoyed the patronage of the wealthy; in medieval Europe, troubadours and minnesingers composed lyric verse for courts, while in Persia and the Arab world figures such as Rumi, Hafez, and Omar Khayyam achieved lasting fame.

Role and status

The social standing of poets has varied widely. In some societies the poet held a near-prophetic status — the Roman vates was both poet and seer — while in others poets depended on patronage or struggled in poverty, giving rise to the romantic image of the starving artist. Many states appoint a poet laureate, a poet officially recognised to compose works for national occasions; the United Kingdom and the United States both maintain such posts.

Forms and practice

Poets work in forms ranging from highly structured verse — sonnets, villanelles, ghazals, haiku — to free verse and prose poetry. Performance traditions include the poetry reading, slam poetry competitions, and the modern phenomenon of poets publishing short-form work through social media. Notable poets across history include Homer, Sappho, Dante Alighieri, William Shakespeare, Emily Dickinson, Rabindranath Tagore, and Pablo Neruda.
