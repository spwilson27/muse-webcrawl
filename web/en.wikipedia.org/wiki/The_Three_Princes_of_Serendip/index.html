The Three Princes of Serendip

Origin: Persian fairy tale

First European publication: Venice, 1557 (Michele Tramezzino)

Source collection: Peregrinaggio di tre giovani figliuoli del re di Serendippo

Legacy: Origin of the word "serendipity"

The Three Princes of Serendip is the English version of a Persian fairy tale, published in Venice in 1557 by Michele Tramezzino as part of the Peregrinaggio di tre giovani figliuoli del re di Serendippo, adapted from Amir Khusrau's Hasht-Bihisht (1302). Serendip is the old Persian name for Sri Lanka. The tale tells of three princes, educated in all the sciences and arts, whom their father sends travelling so that experience may complete their education.

The camel episode

In the most famous episode, the princes are accused of stealing a camel they have never seen. They astound their accusers by describing the animal in detail — that it is lame, blind in one eye, missing a tooth, carrying butter on one side and honey on the other, and ridden by a pregnant woman — all deduced from tracks and signs along the road. When the camel is found, their powers of observation are vindicated.

Serendipity

The English writer Horace Walpole coined the word serendipity in a letter of 28 January 1754 to Horace Mann, explaining that he had formed it from the tale's title because its heroes "were always making discoveries, by accidents and sagacity, of things they were not in quest of." The episode of the lost camel is also a recognised ancestor of detective fiction: Voltaire adapted it in Zadig (1747), and the method of reasoning from traces has been linked to figures from Cuvier to Sherlock Holmes.
