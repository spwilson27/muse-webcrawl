Portico

Portico
OriginAncient Greek and Roman architecture
FeaturesRoof supported by columns; often pedimented
Related termsStoa, loggia, veranda, porte-cochère

A portico is a porch leading to the entrance of a building, or extended as a colonnade, with a roof structure over a walkway supported by columns or enclosed by walls. Rooted in ancient Greek temple architecture, the portico became a defining feature of Roman, Palladian, Neoclassical and Greek Revival buildings.

History

The concept derives from the Greek stoa and the columned porches (pronaos) of temples. The Romans adopted and monumentalised it: the Pantheon in Rome (c. 118–125 AD) preserves the most famous ancient portico, an eight-column granite front supporting a triangular pediment bearing Agrippa's inscription. Porticos provided a shaded, ceremonial threshold between public space and the interior.

Revived by Andrea Palladio in the villas of the Veneto, the temple-front portico spread through European and American architecture. It became ubiquitous in Georgian and Neoclassical design and in the Greek Revival houses, courthouses and plantation homes of the United States; the north and south porticos of the White House are celebrated examples.

Types

Porticos are described by the number of their columns: tetrastyle (four), hexastyle (six), octastyle (eight) and decastyle (ten). A portico may be prostyle, with columns standing free in front of the building, and is typically crowned by a pediment. Related forms include the two-storey portico, the porte-cochère for vehicles, and the recessed portico set within the building's facade.
