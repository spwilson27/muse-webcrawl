The Almogavars (Catalan: almogàvers) were a class of soldiers from the Crown of Aragon, well known during the Christian Reconquista of the Iberian Peninsula. They were frontier soldiers who originally lived in the Pyrenean and Ebro frontier regions and served as light infantry shock troops. Fierce and highly mobile, they became famous throughout the Mediterranean world in the late 13th and early 14th centuries, most notably as the core of the Catalan Company led by Roger de Flor.

Origins

The name derives from the Arabic al-mughāwir, meaning "the one who makes incursions into enemy territory". The Almogavars arose from the harsh frontier lifestyle of the borderlands between Christian and Muslim Spain, where peasants and shepherds learned to live by raiding. By the reign of James I of Aragon they were already an established part of Aragonese armies, and Peter III of Aragon employed them extensively in the conquest of Sicily after the Sicilian Vespers of 1282.

Equipment and tactics

The Almogavars were lightly equipped compared with knights or regular infantry. Their typical armament consisted of a short spear or javelins (azcona), a long knife or short sword (coltell), a small round shield, and sometimes a sling. They wore little armour, often only a leather jerkin and a distinctive knotted headband. Their battle cry was Desperta ferro! ("Awake iron!"), shouted while striking their weapons against stones to produce sparks.

Operating in rough terrain, they relied on ambush, speed and ferocity. Contemporary chronicler Bernat Desclot and Ramon Muntaner, who served with them, praised their endurance: they could march for days on little food and fight effectively against much heavier forces.

The Catalan Company

After the Peace of Caltabellotta (1302) ended the war in Sicily, some 6,500 Almogavars under Roger de Flor sailed east to serve the Byzantine emperor Andronikos II Palaiologos against the Ottoman Turks. Their successes in Anatolia were followed by conflict with the Byzantines; after Roger's assassination in 1305, the Company devastated Thrace and Macedonia before moving into Greece, where at the Battle of Halmyros (1311) they destroyed the chivalry of the Duchy of Athens and seized the duchy for themselves, ruling Athens and Thebes for much of the 14th century.

Legacy

The Almogavars' reputation for ruthlessness — the phrase "Catalan vengeance" entered Greek and Balkan folklore — long outlived their political power. Modern Spanish regular army units, including paratroop and mountain troops, have at times revived the name and traditions of the Almogavars.
