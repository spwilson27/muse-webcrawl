Carnot's theorem

Carnot's theorem, developed in 1824 by Nicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot, is a principle of thermodynamics that specifies the maximum possible efficiency of any heat engine operating between two temperatures.

Statement

The theorem states:

  • No heat engine operating between two heat reservoirs can be more efficient than a Carnot engine operating between the same reservoirs.
  • All Carnot engines operating between the same two reservoirs have the same efficiency, regardless of the working substance.

The maximum efficiency depends only on the absolute temperatures of the hot and cold reservoirs: η = 1 − TC/TH.

Significance

Carnot's theorem, published in his Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire, founded the science of thermodynamics. It implies that no engine can convert all heat into work, a result later connected to the second law of thermodynamics and the concept of entropy developed by Clausius and Kelvin. A related result, Carnot's rule, concerns the theoretical maximum of reversible cycles such as the Carnot cycle.
