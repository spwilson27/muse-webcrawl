Light cavalry

Historically, cavalry (from the French word cavalerie) are soldiers or warriors who fight mounted on horseback. Light cavalry comprises lightly armed and armoured troops mounted on horses, as opposed to heavy cavalry, where the riders (and sometimes the warhorses) are heavily armored. The missions of the light cavalry were primarily raiding, reconnaissance, screening, skirmishing, patrolling, and tactical communications. Prior to the early modern period, light cavalry were also used for pursuit after a battle.

Roles

Light cavalry were valued for speed and endurance rather than shock action. Their tasks included scouting ahead of the army, screening the advance from enemy observation, harassing enemy supply lines and foragers, carrying orders, and pursuing a defeated enemy to convert a retreat into a rout. In battle they often skirmished on the flanks and exploited breakthroughs.

Historical types

Famous types of light cavalry include the Hungarian hussars, whose dress and tactics were adopted across Europe; the Polish ulans armed with lances; the Cossacks of the Russian steppe; the chasseurs à cheval of France; and the light dragoons of Britain and the United States, who were originally mounted infantry. In the Napoleonic Wars most armies maintained both light regiments for outpost and reconnaissance work and heavy regiments of cuirassiers and heavy dragoons for battlefield charges.

Decline

The spread of rifled firearms and machine guns in the later 19th and early 20th centuries ended the era of mounted battlefield cavalry. During the First World War light cavalry still performed reconnaissance and raiding on more open fronts, but horses were progressively replaced by armoured cars, tanks, and aircraft; many historic light cavalry regiments survive today as armoured reconnaissance units.
