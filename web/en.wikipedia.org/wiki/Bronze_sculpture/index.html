Bronze sculpture
MediumBronze (copper–tin alloy)
Principal techniqueLost-wax casting
PeriodBronze Age to present

Bronze sculpture is the use of bronze in sculptures, in cast metal statuary and reliefs. Bronze has been used for major works of art since antiquity because of its strength, durability and ability to reproduce fine detail. Common bronze alloys have the desirable property of expanding slightly just before they set, thus filling the finest details of a mould.

Process

Most large bronze sculptures are made by the lost-wax casting (cire-perdue) process. A model is made in clay or wax; a mould is taken from it; the wax is melted out and replaced by molten bronze; after cooling, the mould is broken away and the metal is chased, cleaned and given a surface patina by chemical treatment. Large works are cast in sections and joined, and are usually hollow to save weight and metal.

History

Bronze casting flourished in ancient China, whose ritual vessels reached extraordinary sophistication, and in the ancient Greek world, where life-size bronzes such as the Riace bronzes and the Charioteer of Delphi survive. Bronze was the prestige medium for Roman imperial portraiture. In the Renaissance, sculptors such as Ghiberti, Donatello and Cellini revived large-scale bronze casting in Italy. Auguste Rodin and his successors made bronze the dominant medium of modern figurative sculpture.

Properties

Bronze resists corrosion and can stand outdoors for centuries, gradually developing a brown or green patina. Its tensile strength allows self-supporting compositions — rearing horses, outstretched arms — that would be impossible in marble without supports.
