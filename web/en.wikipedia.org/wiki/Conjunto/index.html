Accordion and bajo sexto — the core of the conjunto sound

The term conjunto (Spanish for "group" or "ensemble") refers to several types of small musical ensembles and to the distinctive Mexican-American musical tradition of South Texas built around the button accordion and the bajo sexto. Conjunto music developed among working-class Tejanos in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, fusing Mexican ranchera and corrido traditions with the polkas, waltzes and schottisches brought by German, Czech and Polish immigrants.

History

German immigrants introduced the diatonic button accordion to northern Mexico and Texas in the late 1800s, where it was adopted by Mexican musicians for dance music at cantinas, weddings and community halls. The classic conjunto instrumentation crystallised in the 1930s and 1940s: accordion, bajo sexto (a twelve-string bass guitar), tololoche (upright bass), and later a drum kit. Narciso Martínez, "El Huracán del Valle", and his bajo sexto player Santiago Almeida made the first conjunto recordings in 1936 and defined the style's instrumental core, emphasising melody on accordion against the bajo sexto's combined rhythm and harmony. Valerio Longoria added sung lyrics and boleros in the 1940s–50s, and the electric conjunto of the postwar decades, exemplified by Tony de la Rosa, amplified the accordion and added full drum kits.

Style and legacy

Conjunto repertoire centres on polkas, rancheras, redovas, huapangos and corridos — narrative ballads of border life. Virtuoso accordionists such as Flaco Jiménez, who carried the music to international audiences through work with Ry Cooder and the Texas Tornados, and Esteban "Steve" Jordan, known for his jazz- and rock-influenced experimentation, expanded its reach. Conjunto is distinguished from the smoother, keyboard- and synthesiser-driven sound of modern Tejano music and from Mexican norteño, its close cousin. The tradition is celebrated at festivals such as San Antonio's Tejano Conjunto Festival (founded 1982) and by the Guadalupe Cultural Arts Center; conjunto pioneers have been honoured with National Heritage Fellowships and Grammy Awards.
