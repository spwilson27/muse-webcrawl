Richard Serra (November 2, 1938 – March 26, 2024) was an American artist known for his large-scale abstract sculptures made for architectural, urban and landscape settings. Working principally with weathering (Cor-Ten) steel plate — propped, rolled, torqued and forged — he created some of the most monumental public art of the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, exploring weight, balance, gravity and the viewer's movement through space.

Early work

Born in San Francisco, Serra studied English literature, worked in steel mills to support himself, and took an MFA at Yale (1964), where Josef Albers was an influence. In the late 1960s he became a leading figure in Process art, making works from molten lead splashed against a wall (Splash Piece) and from rubber, neon and scatterings of material, and compiling his famous Verb List (1967–68) of actions — "to roll, to crease, to fold…" — as a program for sculpture.

Major works and the Tilted Arc controversy

From the 1970s Serra built site-specific steel works including Shift in Ontario and Strike in various versions. His Tilted Arc (1981), a 120-foot curving steel wall across Federal Plaza in New York, provoked public hearings and lawsuits; it was removed in 1989, the defining legal battle over public art in America. Later triumphs included the torqued ellipses and spirals of The Matter of Time (2005) at the Guggenheim Museum Bilbao, East-West/West-East (2014) in the Qatari desert, and works at Dia Beacon and in Iceland (Áfangar).
