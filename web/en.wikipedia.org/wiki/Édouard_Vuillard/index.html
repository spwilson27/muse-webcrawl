Jean-Édouard Vuillard (11 November 1868 – 21 June 1940) was a French painter, decorative artist, and printmaker. A leading member of Les Nabis, the group of young Post-Impressionist avant-garde artists who flourished in the 1890s, he is best known for intimate domestic interiors — "intimism" — painted with dense patterning, muted harmonies, and cropped, snapshot-like compositions.

Early life and the Nabis

Vuillard grew up in Paris, where his mother ran a dressmaking business; the fabrics and sewing workshop of his childhood recur throughout his art. He studied at the Lycée Condorcet alongside Ker-Xavier Roussel and Maurice Denis, then at the Académie Julian and the École des Beaux-Arts. With Denis, Roussel, Pierre Bonnard and Paul Sérusier he formed Les Nabis, inspired by Gauguin's Synthetism, and Vuillard shared a studio with Bonnard.

Work

In the 1890s Vuillard painted small, jewel-like interiors of his family, the Natanson circle of La Revue Blanche, and Parisian theatre scenes; he also produced lithograph suites such as Paysages et intérieurs (1899) and the nine large decorative panels of Public Gardens (1894). After 1900 his manner became more naturalistic, and he painted society portraits, still lifes, and large decorative schemes, including for the Palais de Chaillot (1937). A keen amateur photographer, he used his Kodak camera to compose paintings.

Death and legacy

Vuillard died at La Baule in June 1940, having fled Paris before the German occupation. Major retrospectives, notably the 2003–04 exhibition in Paris, Washington and elsewhere, re-established his reputation as one of the most subtle painters of modern French art.
