The Salon des Indépendants is an annual art exhibition in Paris, founded in 1884 by the Société des Artistes Indépendants. Under its founding principle — no jury and no prizes — any artist who pays the fee may exhibit, making it a landmark of artistic freedom in reaction to the rigid selection of the official Paris Salon.

Origins

In 1884, a group of artists including Georges Seurat, Paul Signac, Charles Angrand and Odilon Redon, frustrated by mass rejections at the official Salon, formed the Société des Artistes Indépendants and held their first exhibition in December 1884. The salon became a principal venue for Neo-Impressionism: Seurat's monumental A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte was shown there in 1886.

Development

During the early 20th century the Indépendants exhibited Post-Impressionists, Fauves and Cubists. Henri Rousseau showed there regularly from 1886. In 1911 the salon's display of Cubist works by Gleizes, Metzinger, Léger and others caused a public sensation. Marcel Duchamp's Nude Descending a Staircase controversy began when the work was pressured out of the 1912 Indépendants.

Today

The Société des Artistes Indépendants still organises the salon, now typically held at the Grand Palais Éphémère or similar Paris venues, and remains open to all exhibitors under its original democratic principle. A related but separate exhibition, the Salon d'Automne, was founded in 1903.
