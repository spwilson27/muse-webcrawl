Tinamou

Tinamous are ground-dwelling birds of the family Tinamidae, the sole family in the order Tinamiformes. Native to Mexico, Central America and South America, the roughly 46 species range from the large great tinamou to small dwarf species. Despite their superficial resemblance to quail or partridges, tinamous are paleognaths — among the closest living relatives of the flightless ratites, with which they form the group Palaeognathae — yet unlike ratites, tinamous can fly, albeit weakly and reluctantly.

Description

Tinamous are stocky, cryptically plumaged birds with short tails, strong legs and a slightly downcurved bill. They are shy and secretive, more often heard — producing clear, flute-like whistles — than seen. Their eggs are famous for their glossy, solid colours, ranging from bright green and turquoise to chocolate brown, and are incubated by the male, who also raises the chicks.

Behaviour and ecology

They feed on seeds, fruit, roots and small invertebrates. Forest species tend to be solitary, while those of open grassland, such as the elegant crested tinamou, are more social. Tinamous are hunted for food in much of Latin America, and habitat loss threatens several species.

Evolution

Molecular studies place tinamous within the ratites, suggesting that flight was lost independently several times among paleognaths. Fossil tinamous are known from the Miocene of South America.
