Mixing (music production)

Audio mixing is the process by which multiple sounds are combined into one or more audio channels, most commonly a two-channel stereo mixdown of a multitrack recording. In the process, the relative levels, frequency content, dynamics and panoramic position of the source signals are adjusted and enhanced. Mixing is practised by a mix engineer (or mixing engineer) working on a mixing console or in a digital audio workstation (DAW), and it sits between tracking (recording) and mastering in the production chain.

Techniques

The mixer's core tools are level balance (faders), equalisation (shaping each sound's spectrum so elements do not mask one another), dynamics processing (compression and limiting to control level variation), panning (placing sounds across the stereo field), and time-based effects such as reverb and delay to create a sense of space and depth. Automation allows levels and effects to change over the course of a song. Mix engineers monitor on nearfield loudspeakers and check the mix on headphones and small speakers to ensure translation to consumer systems.

History

Before multitrack tape, mixing meant balancing musicians in the room during a single live performance recorded to one track. Les Paul's multitrack experiments and the 4-, 8-, 16- and 24-track studios of the 1960s–80s progressively moved mixing to a separate, creative stage — exemplified by the Beatles' and George Martin's Abbey Road work and by dub reggae pioneers such as King Tubby, who treated the console itself as an instrument. Since the 1990s most mixing has moved "in the box" to DAWs with plug-in emulations of classic hardware, though hybrid analogue-digital studios remain common.
