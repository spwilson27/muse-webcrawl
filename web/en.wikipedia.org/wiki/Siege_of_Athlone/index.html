Siege of Athlone

The siege of Athlone was a major episode of the Williamite War in Ireland (1689–1691), fought over the strategically vital crossing of the River Shannon at Athlone. The town was besieged twice: a first Williamite assault under James Douglas failed in July 1690, while a second, famous assault in June–July 1691 under Godert de Ginkel succeeded in one of the most celebrated storming operations of the war.

Background

Athlone guarded the principal bridge over the Shannon, the gateway between Leinster and Connacht. Holding it was essential to the Jacobite defence of the west of Ireland after the Williamite victory at the Boyne in 1690.

The 1690 siege

In July 1690 a Williamite force under James Douglas battered the town but was forced to withdraw after about a week when the Jacobite garrison, commanded by Colonel Richard Grace, repaired breaches and reinforcements approached. The repulse was a notable Jacobite success.

The 1691 assault

Returning in June 1691, Ginkel bombarded the town from the Leinster bank for days with a heavy artillery train. On 30 June, in a daring operation, picked Williamite troops — led by volunteers including Danish and Dutch soldiers — waded and stormed across the partly demolished bridge under intense fire, seized the Connacht-side ramparts, and took the town. The assault is often described as one of the most remarkable feats of arms of the seventeenth century. The fall of Athlone opened Connacht to the Williamites and led directly to the decisive Battle of Aughrim a fortnight later.
