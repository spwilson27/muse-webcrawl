Amphibious warfare is a type of offensive military operation that uses naval ships to project ground and air power onto a hostile or potentially hostile shore. It involves the landing of troops, armour, and supplies from the sea against a defended coastline, and is generally considered among the most complex of all military operations, requiring precise coordination between naval, ground, and air forces.

History

Seaborne invasions are as old as warfare itself: the Persians landed at Marathon in 490 BC, Alexander the Great conducted sieges supported from the sea, and Viking raiders exploited shallow-draft longships to strike deep inland. Major early-modern operations included the Spanish Armada's planned invasion of England (1588) and numerous colonial expeditions. The British landing at Quebec in 1759, culminating in the Battle of the Plains of Abraham, is often cited as a model early amphibious operation.

The First World War

The Gallipoli Campaign of 1915–16, an attempt by Britain, France, and ANZAC troops to seize the Dardanelles, ended in costly failure and convinced many planners that opposed landings were nearly impossible. Smaller operations, such as the German Operation Albion in the Baltic (1917), showed that with naval superiority and surprise, landings could still succeed.

The Second World War

The Second World War transformed amphibious warfare. Purpose-built equipment appeared: landing craft such as the LCVP ("Higgins boat"), amphibious tractors (LVTs), and DUKW trucks; specialist ships such as LSTs; and techniques including naval bombardment, underwater demolition teams, and artificial harbours. The United States Marine Corps, which had codified amphibious doctrine in the interwar years, conducted a vast island-hopping campaign in the Pacific — Guadalcanal, Tarawa, Iwo Jima, and Okinawa among them. In Europe, the Allies landed in North Africa (Operation Torch, 1942), Sicily and Italy (1943), and most famously in Normandy on D-Day, 6 June 1944, the largest seaborne invasion in history.

Korea and the Cold War

General Douglas MacArthur's landing at Inchon in September 1950 reversed the course of the Korean War and is regarded as a classic of operational manoeuvre. The helicopter introduced vertical envelopment — landing troops behind beaches by air — first used at scale by the French in Algeria and by the United States in Vietnam. Britain's 1982 Falklands War landings at San Carlos demonstrated that amphibious operations remained viable in the missile age, though ships proved vulnerable to air attack.

Modern amphibious operations

Modern forces emphasise over-the-horizon assault, using tilt-rotor aircraft, air-cushion landing craft (LCACs), and amphibious assault ships (LHDs/LHAs) that function as small aircraft carriers. The United States, China, Russia, the United Kingdom, France, Japan, South Korea, and several other states maintain dedicated amphibious forces, most often built around marine infantry. Amphibious ships are also widely used for humanitarian assistance and disaster relief.
