Winslow Homer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Snap the Whip (1872): barefoot schoolboys tumbling from a hand-holding chain in a sunlit meadow]

Winslow Homer (24 February 1836 – 29 September 1910) was an American landscape painter and illustrator, considered one of the foremost painters in nineteenth-century America. Largely self-taught, he began as a commercial illustrator for Harper's Weekly — famously covering the Civil War — and became the leading American watercolourist, ending his career with the great, elemental seascapes of Prouts Neck, Maine.

His best-known works include Snap the Whip (1872), Breezing Up (A Fair Wind) (1873–76), The Gulf Stream (1899), The Fog Warning (1885), the Adirondack hunting scenes, and the late marines such as Northeaster (1895).

Illustrator to painter

Homer was apprenticed to a Boston lithographer at 19 and freelanced for Harper's, which sent him to the front in 1861; war subjects like Prisoners from the Front (1866) established his reputation in oils. After a year in Paris he settled into the sunlit rural and schoolroom scenes of the 1870s — children, country schools and leisure — that made him popular.

Prouts Neck and the sea

An 18-month stay at Cullercoats in England (1881–82), among fisherfolk facing a dangerous sea, darkened his art. From 1883 he lived at Prouts Neck, Maine, painting fishermen, lifesaving crews and finally the sea alone against the rocks — his watercolours, refreshed by trips to Florida, the Caribbean and Canada, remaining virtuoso performances to the end.
