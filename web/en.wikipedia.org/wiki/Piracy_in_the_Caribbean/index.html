Piracy in the Caribbean

Piracy in the Caribbean refers to the practice of robbery and criminal violence at sea in the Caribbean region, most famously during the period roughly from the 1650s to the 1730s — the so-called Golden Age of Piracy. Pirates preyed on the Spanish treasure fleets carrying silver and gold from the Americas to Europe, as well as on merchant shipping of all nations.

Origins

Caribbean piracy grew out of the buccaneers, hunters and privateers based on Hispaniola and Tortuga who attacked Spanish shipping during the 17th century. Figures such as Henry Morgan, operating with semi-official sanction from England, sacked Spanish ports including Portobelo and Panama City.

The Golden Age

After the War of the Spanish Succession ended in 1713, thousands of unemployed privateers turned to piracy. Pirates established bases at Nassau in the Bahamas and operated across the Caribbean and the North American coast. Notorious captains included Edward Teach (Blackbeard), Bartholomew Roberts, Calico Jack Rackham, and the female pirates Anne Bonny and Mary Read.

Suppression

From 1718, a concerted campaign by the Royal Navy and colonial governors, including Woodes Rogers's governorship of the Bahamas and the offer of a royal pardon (the King's Pardon of 1717), broke the pirate republics. Mass trials and executions in the early 1720s ended the Golden Age, though sporadic piracy continued into the 19th century.

In popular culture

Caribbean piracy inspired Stevenson's Treasure Island, the play and novel Peter Pan, and Disney's Pirates of the Caribbean film series, which fixed the popular image of the pirate — tricorn hats, treasure maps, and the Jolly Roger.
