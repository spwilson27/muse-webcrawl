Politics of Australia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The politics of Australia are conducted in a federal constitutional monarchy and parliamentary democracy under the Constitution of 1901. The Commonwealth Parliament comprises the House of Representatives (elected by preferential/instant-runoff voting in single-member divisions) and a powerful Senate (elected by proportional representation, the single transferable vote, with equal state representation); six states and two mainland territories have their own parliaments. The governor-general represents the Crown; the prime minister and cabinet govern from the House. Voting is compulsory (since 1924) and administered by an independent electoral commission.

The party system pairs the centre-left Australian Labor Party against a long-standing Coalition of the Liberal and National parties, with Greens and independents growing (notably the “teal” community independents of 2022). Constitutional landmarks include the 1975 dismissal crisis (the governor-general's dismissal of the Whitlam government), the failed 1999 republic referendum, Indigenous recognition politics (the unsuccessful 2023 Voice referendum), and persistent questions of federal fiscal imbalance. Australia's combination — Westminster cabinet government, a strong elected Senate, federalism and compulsory voting — is sometimes labelled the “Washminster mutation”, and its preferential ballot is exported as a reform model (see Ranked voting if built; otherwise Electoral systems).
