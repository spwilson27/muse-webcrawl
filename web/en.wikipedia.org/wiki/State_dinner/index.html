State banquet

A state banquet or state dinner is an official dinner hosted by a head of state in their official residence for another head of state, or sometimes a head of government, as part of a state visit. It is among the highest honours one state can render to another's leader: guest lists, seating plans, menus and toasts are governed by strict diplomatic protocol, and the events typically feature military pageantry, national anthems, and speeches exchanged between the two heads of state.

United Kingdom

In the United Kingdom, state banquets are hosted by the monarch, traditionally in the Ballroom at Buckingham Palace (or at Windsor Castle), for around 170 guests seated at a horseshoe-shaped table set with the Grand Service of silver-gilt. The banquet forms the centrepiece of an incoming state visit, with the host and guest exchanging speeches and toasts, and the national anthems played by a military orchestra. Guests include members of the royal family, senior politicians, diplomats and notable figures with links to the visiting country.

United States

At the White House, state dinners are held in the State Dining Room (about 140 guests) or, for larger events, in a pavilion on the South Lawn. The first White House state dinner was hosted by President Ulysses S. Grant in 1874 for King Kalākaua of Hawaii. Preparation — coordinated by the White House Chief Usher, the Social Secretary, the Executive Chef and the State Department's Office of Protocol — takes months; the menu typically showcases American cuisine and wines, and entertainment by American performers follows the dinner. Invitations are prized markers of political and social standing.

Other countries

Most states maintain analogous rituals: France hosts state dinners at the Élysée Palace, Germany at Bellevue Palace or Schloss Augustusburg, Japan at the Imperial Palace or the Akasaka State Guest House, and China in the Great Hall of the People, where the state banquet for US President Richard Nixon in 1972 marked a turning point in Sino-American relations. Toasting customs, dress codes (often white tie or national dress) and the number of courses vary by national tradition, but the diplomatic function — honouring the guest and displaying friendship — is universal.
