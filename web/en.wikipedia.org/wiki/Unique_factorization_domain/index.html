Unique factorization domain

In mathematics, a unique factorization domain (UFD), sometimes called a factorial ring following Bourbaki, is a ring in which a statement analogous to the fundamental theorem of arithmetic holds. Specifically, a UFD is an integral domain in which every nonzero non-unit element can be written as a product of prime elements (or equivalently, irreducible elements), uniquely up to order and units.

UFDs sit between principal ideal domains and GCD domains in the hierarchy of rings.

Definition

Formally, an integral domain R is a UFD if every non-zero non-unit element x of R can be written as a product x = p1···pn of prime elements of R, and this representation is unique in the sense that if x = q1···qm is another such product, then m = n and after reordering each pi differs from the corresponding qi only by a unit factor.

Properties and examples

  • Every principal ideal domain (PID) is a UFD; in particular, the integers, and polynomial rings over a field in one variable, are UFDs.
  • A polynomial ring over a UFD is itself a UFD; hence polynomial rings in finitely many variables over a field or over the integers are UFDs.
  • Formal power series rings over a field or a discrete valuation ring are UFDs.
  • Every UFD is integrally closed (normal) and is a GCD domain: any two elements have a greatest common divisor.
  • Not every ring of algebraic integers is a UFD; for example, in ℤ[√−5] the element 6 factors both as 2·3 and as (1+√−5)(1−√−5). Such failures motivated the development of ideal theory.

Equivalent characterizations

A domain is a UFD if and only if every irreducible element is prime; also, if and only if it is a GCD domain in which every ascending chain of principal ideals terminates, or equivalently in which every nonzero non-unit factors into irreducibles and irreducibles are prime.
