Estates-General of 1789

Estates-General of 1789
Convened: 5 May 1789, Versailles
Called by: Louis XVI
Cause: Financial crisis of the French monarchy
Outcome: Formation of the National Assembly; start of the French Revolution
Previous Estates-General: 1614

The Estates-General of 1789 was the first meeting of the French Estates-General since 1614. Summoned by King Louis XVI to address the kingdom's financial crisis, it convened at Versailles on 5 May 1789. Its failure to resolve the deadlock over voting procedures led the Third Estate to proclaim itself the National Assembly, setting the French Revolution in motion.

Background

France's involvement in the American Revolutionary War and decades of fiscal imbalance had brought the monarchy close to bankruptcy. After attempts at reform by ministers such as Calonne and Jacques Necker failed in the Assembly of Notables, Louis XVI agreed in 1788 to summon the Estates-General, in which the clergy (First Estate), nobility (Second Estate) and commoners (Third Estate) would sit as separate orders.

Proceedings

Elections held across France produced lists of grievances, the cahiers de doléances. The Third Estate, which had been granted double representation, demanded voting by head rather than by order, which would have given it real influence. Deadlock lasted for weeks. On 17 June 1789 the Third Estate, joined by some clergy, declared itself the National Assembly; three days later, locked out of its hall, it swore the Tennis Court Oath not to disband until France had a constitution. In July the king ordered the other orders to join the Assembly, and the Estates-General effectively ceased to exist as the Revolution began.
