The Father (Swedish: Fadren) is a naturalistic tragedy by the Swedish playwright August Strindberg, written in 1887 and first performed in Copenhagen that year. It depicts a war of the sexes within a marriage: an army captain and his wife battle over their daughter's education, and the wife's insinuation that he may not be the child's father corrodes his reason until he is destroyed.

Plot

Captain Adolf, a cavalry officer and amateur scientist, insists that his daughter Bertha be raised in town as an atheist teacher; his wife Laura wants her kept at home as an artist. Under Swedish law of the time the husband's will prevails, so Laura counters by suggesting to the family doctor that her husband is mentally ill — citing his purchase of books and his monograph on meteorites — and, devastatingly, by letting him doubt whether he is really Bertha's father. Because paternity can never be proven, the doubt unmans him: the Captain suffers a breakdown, throws a lamp at Laura, and is eventually lured by his old nurse Margaret into a straitjacket, suffering a fatal stroke as Laura takes control of the household.

Themes and legacy

Written during Strindberg's marital crisis with Siri von Essen, the play fuses naturalist determinism with psychological warfare, and is paired with Miss Julie (1888) and Creditors (1889) as the summit of his early drama. Its concentrated, corrosive marital battle influenced twentieth-century drama from Ibsen's heirs to Albee's Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf?, a modern echo of the Jacobean domestic tragedy of The Changeling.
