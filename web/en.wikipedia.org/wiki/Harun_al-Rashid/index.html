Harun al-Rashid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A manuscript illustration of Harun al-Rashid receiving a delegation at the Baghdad court]

Harun al-Rashid (c. 763/766 – 809) was the fifth Abbasid caliph, ruling 786–809 from Baghdad, and the caliph of the Thousand and One Nights in the popular imagination. His reign marks the zenith of Abbasid power and splendour: the Barmakid viziers administered a vast empire, Baghdad's markets drew goods from China to the Atlantic, embassies exchanged gifts with Charlemagne (including the elephant Abul-Abbas, delivered to Aachen in 802) and with Tang China, and the court's patronage launched the translation movement of the Islamic Golden Age. His death on campaign at Tus in 809, having divided the empire between his sons al-Amin and al-Ma'mun, triggered the civil war (the Fourth Fitna) that began the caliphate's long fragmentation.

The Baghdad of the Nights

Harun inherited a stable, rich empire from his father al-Mahdi and brother al-Hadi (whose brief reign ended mysteriously). With the Persian Barmakid family — Yahya ibn Khalid as vizier, his sons al-Fadl and Ja'far as the caliph's boon companions — he governed through a sophisticated bureaucracy; Ja'far's sudden execution and the Barmakids' destruction in 803 (over motives still debated: power, wealth, an alleged affair with Harun's sister Abbasa, a story now treated as romance) ended the vizierate's golden partnership. The Nights' image of Harun wandering Baghdad in disguise with Ja'far and the executioner Masrur is fiction — but fiction rooted in this court's real glamour.

War and succession

Harun campaigned in person against Byzantium (reaching Heraclea in 806, imposing tribute on Nikephoros I), patronised poets (Abu Nuwas), scholars and the Bayt al-Hikma's forerunners, and settled the succession — disastrously — by the Mecca documents (802): al-Amin to succeed in Iraq, al-Ma'mun to hold Khurasan in near-independence, a third son to hold the frontier. The arrangement guaranteed the war of 809–813 that ruined Baghdad's western half and al-Amin's life, from which the caliphate's unity never recovered.
