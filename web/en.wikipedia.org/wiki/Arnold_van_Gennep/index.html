Arnold van Gennep (23 April 1873 – 7 May 1957) was a Dutch-German-French ethnographer, anthropologist and folklorist. He is best known for his work Les rites de passage (The Rites of Passage, 1909), in which he analysed ceremonies accompanying changes of place, state, social position and age, and proposed that such rites share a universal three-part structure: separation, transition (liminality), and incorporation.

Biography

Van Gennep was born in Ludwigsburg in the Kingdom of Württemberg to a Dutch father and spent much of his childhood in France after his parents divorced and his mother remarried a French doctor. He studied at the Sorbonne and the École pratique des hautes études in Paris, learning several languages. Unable to obtain an academic post in France — in part because of friction with the school of Émile Durkheim — he worked as a translator, schoolteacher and government ethnographer; from 1912 to 1915 he held the chair of ethnography at the University of Neuchâtel in Switzerland, the first such chair in that country.

He spent much of his career studying the folklore of rural France, producing the multi-volume Manuel de folklore français contemporain (1937–1958), much of it written at his home in Bourg-la-Reine, where he died in 1957.

The Rites of Passage

In Les rites de passage van Gennep classified the rituals that mark transitions in human life — birth, puberty, marriage, funerals, seasonal changes and territorial passage — and argued they follow a common pattern:

  • Preliminal rites — rites of separation from a previous world;
  • Liminal rites — rites enacted during the transitional, in-between stage;
  • Postliminal rites — rites of incorporation into a new world.

The concept of the liminal phase was later taken up and greatly expanded by the anthropologist Victor Turner, making van Gennep's book one of the most influential works of twentieth-century anthropology, although its significance was recognised only after its translation into English in 1960.
