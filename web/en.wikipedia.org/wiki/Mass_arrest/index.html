Mass arrest

A mass arrest occurs when law enforcement officers detain a large number of people at once, typically during a protest, demonstration, or riot control operation. Rather than identifying individual suspects for specific offences, police may encircle a crowd — a tactic known as "kettling" — and arrest everyone within it, or arrest large groups preventively before an event.

Practice

Mass arrests are used by police forces in many countries to clear demonstrations, at international summit protests, and during states of emergency. Detainees are commonly photographed, handcuffed with plastic ties, transported in buses, and processed at temporary facilities; frequently a large proportion are released without charge or have charges later dropped.

Criticism and legality

Civil liberties organisations criticise mass arrests as indiscriminate, arguing they sweep up bystanders, journalists, and legal observers along with offenders, chill lawful assembly, and frequently fail to produce convictions. Courts in several jurisdictions have found specific mass arrests unlawful and awarded damages — for example after the 2001 Genoa G8 protests, the 2002 Washington, D.C. Pershing Park arrests, the 2004 New York Republican National Convention arrests, and the 2010 Toronto G20 summit, which saw the largest mass arrest in Canadian history. Supporters argue the tactic can prevent violence when a crowd as a whole poses a threat.

Notable examples

  • The arrest of nearly 700 protesters on the Brooklyn Bridge during Occupy Wall Street in 2011.
  • Mass detentions during the 2019 Hong Kong protests.
  • Large-scale arrests of anti-war protesters in Russia following the 2022 invasion of Ukraine.
