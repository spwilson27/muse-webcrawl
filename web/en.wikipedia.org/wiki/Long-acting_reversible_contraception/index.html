Long-acting reversible contraception

Long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARC) are methods of birth control that provide effective contraception for an extended period without requiring user action. They include intrauterine devices (IUDs) and subdermal contraceptive implants. Once inserted, they are among the most effective forms of reversible contraception, with typical-use failure rates below 1 percent per year — comparable to sterilisation — because their effectiveness does not depend on the user remembering pills or using a method correctly each time.

Methods

Intrauterine devices are small T-shaped devices placed in the uterus. Hormonal IUDs release levonorgestrel and are approved for three to eight years depending on the product; the copper IUD, which contains no hormones, can remain effective for ten years or more. Contraceptive implants are matchstick-sized rods placed under the skin of the upper arm that release the progestin etonogestrel and are approved for up to three to five years. Fertility returns promptly after removal of any LARC method.

Use and recommendations

Major medical organisations, including the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists and the American Academy of Pediatrics, recommend LARCs as a first-line contraceptive option, including for adolescents and women who have not had children. Programmes that removed cost barriers, such as the Contraceptive CHOICE Project in St. Louis, found high uptake of LARCs and large reductions in unintended and teenage pregnancies. Side effects are usually limited to changes in bleeding patterns, and serious complications are rare; IUDs do not protect against sexually transmitted infections.
