The Gospel of Thomas (also known as the Coptic Gospel of Thomas) is an extra-canonical sayings gospel. It was discovered near Nag Hammadi, Egypt, in December 1945 among a group of books known as the Nag Hammadi library. Scholars speculate that the works were buried in response to a letter from Bishop Athanasius declaring a strict canon of Christian scripture.

The Coptic-language text, the second of seven tractates in what modern scholars have designated Codex II, is composed of 114 sayings attributed to Jesus. Almost half of these sayings resemble those found in the canonical Gospels, while the other sayings were previously unknown. Its place of origin may have been Syria, where Thomasine traditions were strong.

Discovery

The Nag Hammadi library, a collection of thirteen papyrus codices, was discovered by local peasants near the Egyptian town of Nag Hammadi in 1945. The Gospel of Thomas was identified in Codex II. Earlier, three Greek fragments of a similar sayings collection had been found at Oxyrhynchus in 1897 and 1904, later identified as parts of the Thomas tradition.

Contents

The text begins: "These are the secret sayings which the living Jesus spoke and which Didymos Judas Thomas wrote down." Unlike the canonical Gospels, Thomas contains no narrative of Jesus' birth, ministry, crucifixion, or resurrection; instead it presents sayings (logia), parables, and dialogues that emphasize self-knowledge and the Kingdom of God present within and around the believer.

Dating and scholarship

Scholars disagree about the date of composition. Some place it as early as the middle of the 1st century, arguing that some sayings preserve very early oral tradition; others date it to the early or mid 2nd century, seeing dependence on the synoptic Gospels or the Diatessaron. The question of whether Thomas is influenced by Gnosticism is also debated; many scholars now classify it as an ascetic, wisdom-oriented text rather than strictly Gnostic.
