Port

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Gantry cranes loading a container ship at a major port at dusk]
A container terminal at work.

A port is a maritime facility where ships load and discharge cargo and passengers, typically comprising docks, wharves, piers, terminals, warehouses and equipment such as cranes and tugboats. Ports sit at the junction of sea and land transport — roads, railways and pipelines in the hinterland. The world\'s busiest container port is Shanghai; the busiest by cargo tonnage is usually Ningbo-Zhoushan, also in China.

Types of port

Seaports divide into container, bulk (grain, ore, coal), liquid bulk (oil, LNG), ferry, cruise and fishing ports, plus inland \'dry ports\' linked by rail. Some are natural harbours (Sydney, Rio de Janeiro); others are wholly artificial, built behind breakwaters (e.g. Rotterdam\'s Maasvlakte extensions into the North Sea).

Operations

A port authority owns or regulates the land while private terminal operators run berths. Pilots guide ships in, tugs assist berthing, and stevedores work cargo with ship-to-shore cranes. Customs, immigration and quarantine functions make ports border checkpoints.

Economic role

Ports cluster industry — refineries, steelworks, logistics parks — and anchor port cities such as Singapore, Rotterdam, Hamburg and Busan. About 80–90% of world trade by volume passes by sea, making efficient ports critical to national economies.
