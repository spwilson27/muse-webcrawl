Shasta Dam (called Kennett Dam before its construction) is a concrete arch-gravity dam across the Sacramento River in Northern California. Standing 602 feet (183.5 m) high, it impounds Shasta Lake, the largest reservoir in California, and is the centerpiece of the Central Valley Project, providing flood control, irrigation water, municipal and industrial supply, and hydroelectric power.

Construction

Built by the United States Bureau of Reclamation between 1938 and 1945, the dam required some 6.27 million cubic yards of concrete, delivered by a celebrated aerial tramway system of buckets from the batch plant at Redding. The construction town of Shasta Dam Village (now part of Shasta Lake city) housed thousands of workers. The dam was completed two years ahead of schedule during World War II; its powerhouse has a generating capacity of about 710 megawatts.

Impacts

The reservoir submerged lands of the Winnemem Wintu people, including burial grounds and sacred sites, without a congressionally ratified treaty settlement — a grievance that continues today. The dam also blocked salmon from their historical spawning grounds in the upper Sacramento, McCloud, and Pit Rivers; the winter-run Chinook salmon is now endangered, and cold-water releases below the dam are managed to support downstream runs. Proposals to raise the dam by up to 18.5 feet to enlarge storage have been studied for decades and remain controversial.
