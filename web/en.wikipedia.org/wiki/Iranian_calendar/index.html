Iranian calendars

The Iranian calendars or Iranian chronology (Persian: گاه‌شماری ایرانی) are a succession of calendars created and used for over two millennia in Iran, also known as Persia. One of the longest chronological records in human history, the Iranian calendar has been modified many times for administrative, climatic, and religious purposes.

The modern calendar: Solar Hijri

The calendar in official use in Iran and Afghanistan today is the Solar Hijri calendar. It is a solar calendar that begins at the vernal equinox (Nowruz), as determined by astronomical observation at Tehran. Its epoch is the Hijra of Muhammad in 622 CE. The year consists of twelve months: the first six have 31 days, the next five have 30 days, and the last has 29 days (30 in leap years). The calendar was adopted in its modern form in 1925 in Iran and 1957 in Afghanistan; its mathematical structure reflects reforms associated historically with Omar Khayyam and the Jalali calendar of 1079.

Leap years and accuracy

The Solar Hijri calendar uses a sophisticated intercalation scheme based on the true equinox, making it exceptionally accurate — more closely aligned with the tropical year than the Gregorian calendar over very long timescales. Leap years follow a pattern governed by 33-year sub-cycles within a larger cycle, but ultimately depend on the observed moment of the equinox.

Historical calendars

Earlier Iranian calendars include the Old Persian calendar of the Achaemenid era, the Zoroastrian religious calendar of 365 days with twelve months of thirty days plus five epagomenal (Gatha) days, and the Jalali calendar commissioned by Sultan Malik-Shah I and computed by a team including Omar Khayyam.

Months

The twelve Solar Hijri month names are: Farvardin, Ordibehesht, Khordad, Tir, Mordad, Shahrivar, Mehr, Aban, Azar, Dey, Bahman, and Esfand.
