Stoma

In anatomy, a stoma (from the Greek stóma, "mouth"; plural stomata) is any opening in the body. In surgery, the term most commonly denotes an artificial opening created between a hollow organ and the body surface, usually on the abdomen, to allow the discharge of bodily wastes when the normal route is diseased, obstructed or has been removed.

Types

  • Colostomy — an opening from the colon; output is usually formed stool.
  • Ileostomy — an opening from the ileum, typically in the right lower abdomen; output is liquid.
  • Urostomy — diversion of urine, often using an ileal conduit.
  • Tracheostomy — an opening into the trachea in the neck for breathing.
  • Gastrostomy — an opening into the stomach, used for feeding (e.g. PEG tubes).

Indications and care

Stomas may be temporary or permanent and are created in the treatment of colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease), diverticular disease, trauma and congenital conditions. Effluent is collected in an external appliance (stoma bag) adhered to the skin around the stoma. Specialist stoma care nurses support the estimated 200,000-plus people living with a stoma in the United Kingdom and over a million in the United States.

Other uses of the term

In botany, stomata are the pores in leaves through which gas exchange occurs; in zoology the term describes mouth-like openings in some invertebrates.
