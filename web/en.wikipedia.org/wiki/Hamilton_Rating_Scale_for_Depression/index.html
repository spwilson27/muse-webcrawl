The Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression (HAM-D or HRSD) is a clinician-administered questionnaire used to measure the severity of depressive symptoms. Developed by Max Hamilton and published in 1960, it became the most widely used outcome measure in antidepressant clinical trials and is often treated as a historical "gold standard" for depression assessment.

Structure and scoring

The standard 17-item version rates symptoms such as depressed mood, guilt, suicidal ideation, insomnia (early, middle, and late), work and interests, psychomotor retardation and agitation, anxiety, somatic symptoms, weight loss, and insight. Items are scored 0–2 or 0–4, giving a maximum of 52. Conventional cut-offs describe 0–7 as normal/remission, 8–16 as mild, 17–23 as moderate, and 24 or more as severe depression. Longer 21- and 24-item versions add items such as helplessness, hopelessness, and worthlessness.

Criticism

The scale has been criticised for emphasising insomnia and somatic symptoms, under-weighting atypical features such as hypersomnia and increased appetite, and for items that conflate anxiety with depression. Its sensitivity to sedative side effects and the variability of unstructured administration led to structured interview guides and to competition from scales such as the MADRS and self-report instruments like the PHQ-9. Nevertheless, the HAM-D remains a benchmark endpoint in regulatory trials.
