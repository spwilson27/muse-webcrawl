Sewerage

A Victorian brick sewer tunnel

Sewerage is the infrastructure that conveys sewage or surface runoff using sewers — the network of pipes, pumps, manholes, and related works that carries wastewater from buildings to treatment or disposal. It is distinct from sewage (the waste itself) and from sewer (an individual conduit). Sewerage is a core element of urban sanitation, and its development in the 19th century produced enormous gains in public health by separating drinking water from human waste.

Types of system

In a separate system, sanitary sewers carry domestic and industrial wastewater to a treatment plant, while storm drains carry rainwater directly to watercourses. In a combined system, prevalent in older cities, one pipe carries both; during heavy rain the excess may discharge untreated through combined sewer overflows, a persistent pollution problem. Most systems flow by gravity, supplemented by pumping stations where terrain requires; alternatives for low-density areas include vacuum and pressure sewers and simplified (condominial) sewerage. Flows arriving at the works undergo wastewater treatment — screening, sedimentation, biological treatment such as activated sludge, and disinfection — before discharge or reuse.

History

Sophisticated drainage existed in the Indus Valley civilisation and in Rome, whose Cloaca Maxima still partly functions. Most pre-industrial cities, however, relied on cesspits and open channels. The cholera epidemics and the "Great Stink" of 1858 in London spurred Joseph Bazalgette's great intercepting sewer system for the Metropolitan Board of Works; Paris, Hamburg, and American cities built comparable networks in the later 19th century. The arrival of treatment in the early 20th century, and stricter environmental laws after 1970, progressively reduced the pollution load from sewerage outfalls.

Challenges

Modern challenges include ageing pipes and infiltration, blockages caused by fats, oils, grease and wet wipes ("fatbergs"), micropollutants such as pharmaceuticals, the detection of pathogens in wastewater (wastewater-based epidemiology, prominent during the COVID-19 pandemic), and adapting networks to more intense rainfall under climate change. Billions of people worldwide still lack safely managed sanitation, relying instead on on-site systems such as septic tanks and pit latrines.
