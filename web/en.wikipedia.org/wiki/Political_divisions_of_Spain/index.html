Political divisions of Spain

The political divisions of Spain are based on a model of territorial organisation established by the Spanish Constitution of 1978. Spain is divided into 17 autonomous communities (comunidades autónomas) and 2 autonomous cities, Ceuta and Melilla. The autonomous communities are in turn divided into 50 provinces, and the provinces into municipalities. This decentralised system is often described as the "State of Autonomies" (Estado de las Autonomías), one of the most decentralised systems of government in Europe.

Autonomous communities

The 17 autonomous communities are: Andalusia, Aragon, Asturias, the Balearic Islands, the Basque Country, the Canary Islands, Cantabria, Castile and León, Castilla–La Mancha, Catalonia, the Valencian Community, Extremadura, Galicia, La Rioja, Madrid, Murcia and Navarre. Each has its own statute of autonomy, parliament and government, with competences that vary — the Basque Country and Navarre enjoy a special fiscal regime (the concierto económico), while Catalonia, the Basque Country and Galicia are recognised as historic nationalities.

Provinces

The 50 provinces date mainly from the territorial division of 1833 designed by Javier de Burgos. Most autonomous communities comprise several provinces, but seven are single-province communities. Provincial councils (diputaciones) coordinate municipal services, though their role is debated.

Municipalities

Municipalities (municipios) are the basic local entities, governed by elected councils and mayors. Spain has around 8,100 municipalities, many of them very small villages, alongside large cities such as Madrid and Barcelona.

Other entities

Comarcas (county-like groupings) have official status in some communities such as Catalonia and Aragon. The island councils of the Balearics (consells insulars) and the Canaries (cabildos) form additional tiers of insular government.
