Satisfaction with Life Scale

The Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) is a five-item psychological instrument designed to measure global cognitive judgments of one's life satisfaction. It was developed by psychologists Ed Diener, Robert A. Emmons, Randy J. Larsen, and Sharon Griffin, and published in 1985 in the Journal of Personality Assessment. It is among the most widely used measures in the scientific study of subjective well-being.

The scale

Respondents rate their agreement with five statements on a seven-point scale from "strongly disagree" (1) to "strongly agree" (7). The items are:

  1. In most ways my life is close to my ideal.
  2. The conditions of my life are excellent.
  3. I am satisfied with my life.
  4. So far I have gotten the important things I want in life.
  5. If I could live my life over, I would change almost nothing.

Summed scores range from 5 to 35. In Diener's interpretation, scores of 20 represent a neutral point; 21–25 indicate slight satisfaction, 26–30 satisfaction, and 31–35 extreme satisfaction, while scores below 20 indicate degrees of dissatisfaction.

Psychometric properties and use

The SWLS deliberately measures the cognitive component of subjective well-being rather than momentary affect, leaving respondents to weight the domains of their lives as they see fit. Studies report good internal consistency and test–retest reliability, and the scale has been translated into many languages and used with populations ranging from students to the elderly and people with disabilities. It is frequently administered alongside measures of positive and negative affect, such as the PANAS, to give a fuller picture of subjective well-being.
