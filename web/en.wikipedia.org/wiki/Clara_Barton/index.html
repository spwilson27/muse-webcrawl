Clarissa Harlowe Barton (December 25, 1821 – April 12, 1912), better known as Clara Barton, was an American nurse who founded the American Red Cross. She was a hospital nurse in the American Civil War, a teacher, and a patent clerk. Since nursing education was not then very formalized and she did not attend nursing school, she provided self-taught nursing care. Barton is noteworthy for performing humanitarian work and civil rights advocacy at a time before women had the right to vote. She was inducted into the National Women's Hall of Fame in 1973.

Early life

Clarissa Harlowe Barton was born on Christmas Day 1821 in North Oxford, Massachusetts, the youngest of five children. Her father, Captain Stephen Barton, was a member of the local militia and a selectman. Her mother was Sarah Stone Barton. When she was three years old, Barton was sent to school with her brother Stephen, where she excelled at reading and spelling. At age eleven, she nursed her brother David back to health after he fell from a barn roof, an experience that foreshadowed her later calling.

Barton became a teacher in 1838 and worked in schools in Canada and west Georgia. In 1852 she founded a free school in Bordentown, New Jersey, which grew rapidly; she later moved to Washington, D.C., where she became a clerk in the U.S. Patent Office — one of the first women to hold a federal government job at a salary equal to a man's.

Civil War

When the Civil War began in April 1861, Barton began collecting and distributing supplies to wounded soldiers. In August 1862 she obtained permission to travel to the front lines, where she supplied and tended the wounded at battles including Cedar Mountain, Second Bull Run, Antietam, and Fredericksburg. She became known as the "Angel of the Battlefield." In 1864 she was placed in charge of hospitals for the Army of the James by Union General Benjamin Butler.

After the war, Barton ran the Office of Missing Soldiers in Washington, D.C., helping to locate and identify tens of thousands of missing and killed Union soldiers. In 1869 she traveled to Europe, where she became involved with the International Committee of the Red Cross and performed relief work during the Franco-Prussian War.

American Red Cross

Inspired by the European Red Cross, Barton campaigned for an American society. She founded the American Red Cross in Danbury, Connecticut, in 1881, at age 59, and became its first president. The organization expanded beyond wartime relief to provide aid in peacetime disasters, beginning with the Johnstown Flood of 1889. Barton led relief expeditions to disasters including the Galveston hurricane of 1900, her last field operation. She resigned as president in 1904 amid internal criticism of her management style.

Death and legacy

Barton died of tuberculosis at her home in Glen Echo, Maryland, on April 12, 1912, at the age of 90. Her home in Glen Echo is preserved as the Clara Barton National Historic Site. Numerous schools, hospitals, and streets bear her name, and Barton crater on Venus is named in her honor.
