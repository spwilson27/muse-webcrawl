Dermatomyositis

Dermatomyositis (DM) is a long-term inflammatory autoimmune disorder affecting the skin and muscles. It is one of the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, related to polymyositis, but distinguished by characteristic skin changes. Symptoms generally develop gradually and include a distinctive rash — often a violet-coloured (heliotrope) rash around the eyes and scaly red papules over the knuckles known as Gottron papules — together with progressive, symmetric weakness of the muscles closest to the trunk, such as those of the hips, thighs, shoulders and neck.

Signs and symptoms

The skin findings of dermatomyositis may precede or accompany the muscle disease. Besides the heliotrope rash and Gottron papules, patients may develop redness over sun-exposed areas, nailfold changes, and, especially in children, calcinosis — deposits of calcium in the skin and soft tissues. Muscle weakness can make climbing stairs, rising from a chair, or lifting the arms difficult, and involvement of the swallowing muscles or lungs (interstitial lung disease) may cause serious complications.

Causes and diagnosis

The condition is believed to result from an autoimmune attack on small blood vessels and muscle fibres, with genetic predisposition and environmental triggers such as infections or ultraviolet light implicated. In adults, dermatomyositis is associated with an increased risk of internal malignancy, so age-appropriate cancer screening is recommended. Diagnosis is based on the clinical picture, elevated muscle enzymes such as creatine kinase, electromyography, MRI, skin or muscle biopsy, and myositis-specific autoantibodies.

Treatment

There is no cure, but symptoms can usually be controlled. Treatment typically begins with high-dose corticosteroids such as prednisone, often combined with steroid-sparing immunosuppressants such as methotrexate or azathioprine; intravenous immunoglobulin and biologic agents may be used in refractory cases. Sun protection and physiotherapy are important parts of long-term management.
