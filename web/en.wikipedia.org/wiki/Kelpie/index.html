Kelpie

A kelpie, or water kelpie (Scottish Gaelic: colpach), is a shape-shifting spirit inhabiting lochs and pools of Scotland. It has usually been described as a grey or white horse-like creature, which can extend the length of its back to carry many riders, or as a hairy humanoid which appears by the water's edge. Kelpies are said to lure their victims — often children — onto their backs or into the water, where they drown and devour them, leaving only the entrails.

Folklore

The kelpie is the most common water spirit in Scottish folklore and is often associated with specific lochs, including Loch Ness. Stories about malevolent water horses were told to warn children away from dangerous waters and to keep them from wandering alone. In many tales the kelpie appears as a handsome pony; once a victim mounts, the creature's skin becomes adhesive and it plunges into the depths. A person who seizes the kelpie's bridle may gain control over it, and some tales tell of kelpies forced to work for lairds or millers.

The kelpie is closely related to the each-uisge of the Scottish Highlands, a still more dangerous water horse, and analogous creatures occur in other cultures, such as the Scandinavian bäckahäst and the Welsh ceffyl dŵr.

Etymology and interpretations

The word "kelpie" may derive from Scottish Gaelic calpach or cailpeach, meaning a heifer or colt. Folklorists have interpreted the tales variously as memories of water deities, personifications of floods and drowning hazards, or cautionary tales. Some Victorian ministers incorporated kelpie stories into sermons as images of demonic temptation.

In modern culture

The Kelpies are two 30-metre-high steel horse-head sculptures by Andy Scott, completed in 2013 at The Helix park near Falkirk, among the largest equine sculptures in the world, named after the mythical creatures. Kelpies also appear frequently in modern fantasy literature, film and games.
