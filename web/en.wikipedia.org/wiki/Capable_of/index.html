Capable of

Capable of is a phrase in English formed from the adjective capable (from Latin capabilis, "able to hold or contain," from capere, "to take") followed by the preposition of. It means having the ability, power or qualities necessary to do something, as in "capable of running a marathon," and can also mean susceptible to a quality or feeling, as in "capable of great kindness."

Usage

Grammatically, capable of is followed by a noun or a gerund (the -ing form of a verb): "capable of speech," "capable of flying." This distinguishes it from the near-synonymous adjective able, which takes the infinitive ("able to fly"). Usage writers have long noted this distinction as a point of English style. The construction is attested in English from the 16th and 17th centuries, and the noun form capability developed alongside it.

In philosophy and law

The concept of capability plays a role in several technical fields. In moral and legal contexts, being "capable of" an act or of forming intent relates to questions of legal capacity and responsibility. In development economics, Amartya Sen's capability approach evaluates wellbeing in terms of what people are actually able to do and to be — their substantive freedoms or "capabilities."
