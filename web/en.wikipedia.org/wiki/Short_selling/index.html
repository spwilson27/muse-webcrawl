Short selling

In finance, being short in an asset means investing in such a way that the investor will profit if the market value of the asset falls. Short selling (or shorting) a security means borrowing shares and selling them, in the expectation of buying them back later at a lower price and returning them to the lender, keeping the difference. It is the opposite of the conventional "long" position, in which an investor profits from a rise in price.

Mechanics

A short seller borrows shares through a broker, typically from another client's holdings, and sells them on the open market. At some later time the short seller must "cover" the position by repurchasing the shares and returning them. If the price has fallen, the short seller profits; if the price has risen, the short seller loses, and because a share price can in principle rise without limit, the potential loss on a short position is theoretically unlimited, whereas the maximum gain is capped at the proceeds of the initial sale (if the share becomes worthless). Short sellers pay interest and fees on the borrowed stock and must compensate the lender for any dividends paid during the loan.

Risks

Short positions are exposed to a short squeeze: if many short sellers rush to cover at once, their buying can drive the price sharply higher, compounding losses — as occurred famously with GameStop shares in January 2021. Brokers may also recall borrowed stock or issue margin calls, forcing a short seller to close a position at an unfavourable price.

Role and controversy

Supporters argue that short selling improves market efficiency and price discovery, provides liquidity, and helps expose fraud and overvaluation, as in the cases of Enron and Wirecard. Critics contend that it can drive down the prices of sound companies and amplify market panics. Regulators in many jurisdictions impose disclosure requirements, restrict "naked" short selling (selling shares not actually borrowed), and have occasionally banned short selling temporarily during financial crises, including in 2008.
