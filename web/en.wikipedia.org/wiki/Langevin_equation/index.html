Langevin equation

Overview
Field:Statistical physics, stochastic processes
Named after:Paul Langevin
Introduced:1908

In physics, a Langevin equation (named after Paul Langevin) is a stochastic differential equation describing how a system evolves when subjected to a combination of deterministic and fluctuating ("random") forces. The dependent variables in a Langevin equation typically are collective (macroscopic) variables changing only slowly in comparison to the other (microscopic) variables of the system. The fast variables are responsible for the stochastic nature of the Langevin equation.

The equation

For a Brownian particle of mass m with position x in a fluid, Newton's second law is augmented by a viscous drag force and a rapidly fluctuating random force ξ(t) arising from molecular collisions:

m dv/dt = −λv + ξ(t)

where v is the velocity, λ is a friction coefficient, and the noise ξ(t) is usually taken as Gaussian white noise with zero mean, whose strength is fixed by the fluctuation–dissipation theorem in terms of the temperature and the friction. Langevin introduced this description in 1908 as an alternative, simpler derivation of the properties of Brownian motion than Einstein's diffusion treatment.

Applications and generalisations

Langevin equations and their equivalents, such as the Fokker–Planck equation for the probability distribution, are standard tools in statistical mechanics, chemical kinetics, electrical engineering (Johnson noise), quantitative finance and molecular dynamics, where "Langevin dynamics" thermostats simulations. The harmonic oscillator subject to noise, the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, is the classic exactly solvable example.
