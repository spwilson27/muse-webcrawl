Hara Castle (Japanese: 原城, Hara-jō) was a Japanese castle on the Shimabara Peninsula in what is now Minamishimabara, Nagasaki Prefecture. Though modest in scale, it is famous as the final stronghold of the Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–1638, in which tens of thousands of Christian peasants and masterless samurai made their last stand against the Tokugawa shogunate. Its ruins are a National Historic Site and a component of the UNESCO World Heritage listing of Hidden Christian Sites in the Nagasaki Region.

History

A fortification existed on the coastal bluff at Hara from around 1500, built by the Arima clan, the Christian daimyo of the Shimabara domain. After the Arima were transferred, the Matsukura clan rebuilt the works in the early Edo period, but the castle was abandoned when the Matsukura moved their seat to Shimabara Castle in the 1620s, and its buildings were dismantled.

The Shimabara Rebellion

In December 1637, oppressed Christian peasants and rōnin rose against the Matsukura's taxation and persecution, rallying behind the charismatic teenager Amakusa Shirō. After early successes, the rebels — perhaps 27,000 to 37,000 people, including women and children — occupied the empty shell of Hara Castle, rapidly refortifying it with shogunal and Dutch-supplied supplies turned against their makers.

A shogunate army of over 100,000 besieged the castle through the winter, assisted by Dutch ships bombarding from the sea. Starved and out of ammunition, the defenders were overwhelmed in April 1638; virtually all were killed, and Amakusa Shirō was executed. The castle was demolished and the site left in ruins as a warning. The rebellion prompted the shogunate to complete Japan's seclusion policy and intensify the suppression of Christianity.

Archaeology

Excavations have uncovered stone walls, roof tiles, crucifixes, rosaries, and mass graves of the defenders, together with cannonballs from Dutch and government guns. The site preserves the layout of the honmaru and other enclosures on a promontory overlooking the Ariake Sea.
