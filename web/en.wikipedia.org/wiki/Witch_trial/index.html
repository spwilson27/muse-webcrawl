Witch trial

A witch trial is a legal proceeding against a person accused of witchcraft — the practice of harmful magic, often alleged to involve a pact with the Devil. Witch trials occurred sporadically in the ancient and medieval worlds, but reached their greatest intensity during the witch hunts of early modern Europe and colonial America, roughly between 1450 and 1750. Historians estimate that between about 40,000 and 60,000 people were executed for witchcraft in this period, the large majority of them women.

History

Background

In the later Middle Ages theologians developed the idea of witchcraft as a heretical, devil-worshipping conspiracy. The papal bull Summis desiderantes affectibus (1484) and Heinrich Kramer's Malleus Maleficarum (1486/87) helped authorise and systematise prosecutions. In regions using Roman-canon inquisitorial procedure, torture was routinely used to extract confessions and the names of accomplices, producing chain-reaction panics.

The great hunts

The most intense persecutions took place in the German lands of the Holy Roman Empire, France, Scotland and Switzerland. Major panics include the Trier trials (1581–1593), the Würzburg and Bamberg trials of the 1620s, and the Basque trials in Spain — where the inquisitor Alonso de Salazar Frías notably concluded the accusations were largely baseless. Scotland executed roughly 2,500 people, a very high rate for its population. England, where torture was limited, had lower numbers; its most famous cases include the Pendle witches (1612) and the trials conducted by the self-styled "Witchfinder General" Matthew Hopkins during the Civil War.

Colonial America

The Salem witch trials of 1692–93 in Massachusetts are the best-known American episode: nineteen people were hanged, one man was pressed to death, and several died in prison. Governor William Phips dissolved the special court and spectral evidence was repudiated; public apologies and compensation followed in later years.

Decline

Prosecutions waned in the late 17th and 18th centuries as judicial scepticism grew, evidentiary standards rose, and central authorities restrained local courts. England repealed its Witchcraft Act in 1736. The last executions in Europe are generally dated to the 1780s (Anna Göldi in Switzerland, 1782). In many jurisdictions modern parliaments and churches have since issued apologies or pardons.

Procedure and evidence

Accusations typically began with a charge of maleficium — harm to neighbours, livestock or crops. Tests included searching the body for a "witch's mark", pricking for insensible spots, swimming the accused, and weighing against a Bible. Under torture many accused confessed to attending sabbaths and named others, fuelling larger hunts.

Modern witchcraft accusations

Accusations and killings related to witchcraft beliefs continue in parts of sub-Saharan Africa, India, Nepal and Papua New Guinea, where they are treated as crimes and a human-rights concern rather than valid prosecutions.
