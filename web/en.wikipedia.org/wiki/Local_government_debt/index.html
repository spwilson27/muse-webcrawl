Local government debt

Local government debt refers to the financial obligations incurred by sub-national authorities — such as municipalities, counties, provinces, prefectures, and special-purpose districts — rather than by a national or sovereign government. Local governments borrow, typically by issuing municipal bonds or taking bank loans, to finance capital projects such as roads, schools, water systems, and public buildings, and in some cases to cover budget deficits.

Instruments

The most common instrument is the municipal bond, including general obligation bonds, secured by the issuer's taxing power, and revenue bonds, repaid from the income of the specific project financed. Other instruments include short-term tax and revenue anticipation notes, lease-revenue bonds, certificates of participation, and direct bank loans.

By country

China

Chinese local governments were historically barred from borrowing directly, and instead financed infrastructure through local government financing vehicles (LGFVs), corporate entities that raised off-balance-sheet debt. By the late 2010s this "hidden debt" had grown to tens of trillions of yuan, prompting a government-led debt-swap programme in which LGFV obligations were refinanced into lower-cost official municipal bonds, and periodic crackdowns on implicit guarantees.

United States

US state and local governments hold roughly $3 trillion in municipal securities. Issuance is concentrated among larger states such as California, New York, and Texas. Interest on most municipal bonds is exempt from federal income tax, which lowers borrowing costs. Notable municipal defaults and bankruptcies include Detroit (2013), the largest municipal bankruptcy in US history, and Jefferson County, Alabama (2011).

Japan

Japanese local governments borrow through local government bonds and the Fiscal Investment and Loan Program, under the oversight of the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications.

Risks

Because local governments generally cannot print currency and often face legal balanced-budget requirements, analysts watch debt-service ratios, pension and other post-employment liabilities, and reliance on implicit central-government guarantees — a common source of moral hazard.
