Strombolian eruptions are relatively mild volcanic eruptions, named for the volcano Stromboli in the Aeolian Islands of Italy, which has been erupting in this style almost continuously for centuries and was called the "Lighthouse of the Mediterranean" for its regular nighttime displays. Strombolian activity consists of discrete, rhythmic explosions — typically every few minutes to tens of minutes — that throw incandescent cinders, lapilli, and volcanic bombs in short arcs tens to a few hundred metres above the vent.

Mechanism

The eruptions are driven by the bursting of large gas bubbles ("slugs") that rise through low-viscosity basaltic to basaltic-andesite magma in the conduit and burst at the surface. Because the magma is fluid, gas can escape without shattering the melt into fine ash, so eruption columns remain small (usually under 1 km) and eruptions are short-lived and mildly explosive, rating VEI 1–2, occasionally 3.

Products and landforms

Ejected material falls close to the vent, building a cinder cone with steep flanks at the angle of repose (~33°). Fluid lava may also spill from the vent or the cone's base as short flows. Characteristic bombs include spindle, cow-dung, and bread-crust forms. Ash fall is limited compared with more explosive styles.

Examples

Besides Stromboli itself, classic Strombolian activity occurs at Yasur (Vanuatu), Erta Ale and Nyiragongo lava-lake volcanoes (in bursts), Pacaya (Guatemala), and frequently at Etna's summit craters. The 1943–1952 eruption of Parícutin in Mexico began with Strombolian activity that built its cinder cone. Strombolian behaviour often grades into Hawaiian fire-fountaining at higher discharge rates or into Vulcanian explosions when magma is more viscous or the conduit is blocked.
