Fold-and-cut theorem

Fold-and-cut theorem
FieldComputational geometry, origami mathematics
Proved byErik Demaine, Martin Demaine, Anna Lubiw (1999)
StatementAny polygonal shape can be cut from folded paper by one straight cut

The fold-and-cut theorem states that any shape with straight sides can be cut from a single (idealized) sheet of paper by folding it flat and making a single straight complete cut. Such shapes include polygons and sets of polygons, and even multiple disjoint shapes, provided their boundaries consist of line segments (possibly with holes). The resulting boundary after unfolding is exactly the set of cut edges.

History

The problem has a long popular history: it appears in an 18th-century Japanese mathematics book, in a 1922 puzzle of Harry Houdini cutting a five-pointed star with one snip, and in the (apocryphal) story of Betsy Ross cutting a star for the American flag. The general problem—characterizing what can be cut with one cut after folding—was posed as an open problem and solved in 1999 by Erik Demaine, Martin Demaine, and Anna Lubiw, who gave two constructive methods.

Methods

One algorithm folds the paper so that all edges of the desired pattern lie along a single line—the cut line—using the straight skeleton of the polygon and disk-packing techniques to compute a flat foldable crease pattern. A second approach folds the paper like a "universal molecule" in origami design. Both methods produce foldings with crease patterns computable in polynomial time, though the number of folds may be large and physically impractical.

Applications and related work

The fold-and-cut theorem is a landmark of mathematical origami and computational geometry. It connects to research on origami design algorithms, folding motions, and the complexity of flat foldability, and it is frequently used in science communication to show how folding transforms hard cutting problems into simple ones.
