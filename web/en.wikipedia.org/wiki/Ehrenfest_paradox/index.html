Ehrenfest paradox

The Ehrenfest paradox concerns the rotation of a "rigid" disc in the theory of relativity, and was proposed by Paul Ehrenfest in 1909 in his paper "Uniform Rotation of Rigid Bodies and the Theory of Relativity". It highlights the incompatibility between the notion of a Born-rigid rotating body and special relativity.

Statement

Consider a disc of radius R rotating with angular velocity ω. In the rest frame of its centre, an observer on the rim measures the circumference. Because the rim moves tangentially, Lorentz contraction should shorten the circumference as measured in the inertial frame:

C = 2πR / √(1 − ω²R²/c²)

while the radius, being perpendicular to the motion, is unchanged. The rotating observer would therefore measure a circumference-to-radius ratio different from π, implying a non-Euclidean geometry on the disc — in apparent conflict with the disc's behaviour as a rigid body in Euclidean space.

Resolution

The paradox is resolved by recognising that a perfectly rigid body cannot exist in relativity: stresses cannot propagate faster than light, so a disc cannot be spun up while remaining Born rigid. The geometry of the rotating disc, as described by the Langevin–Landau–Lifschitz metric, is genuinely non-Euclidean; the spatial geometry measured by co-rotating rulers is hyperbolic in character. Max Born, Gustav Herglotz, and others developed the theory of relativistic rigidity in response, and the discussion fed into Einstein's thinking on the way to general relativity, where the rotating disc became a heuristic bridge to curved spacetime geometry.
