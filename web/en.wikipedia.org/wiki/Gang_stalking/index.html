Gang stalking

Gang stalking (also termed organised stalking or group stalking) is a persecutory belief system in which people report being followed, surveilled and harassed by large numbers of people, acting in coordination, in everyday situations. Adherents, who often call themselves "targeted individuals" (TIs), attribute ordinary events — passing cars of a certain colour, coughs, gestures, vehicle movements — to a deliberate campaign intended to distress them. The beliefs are spread and reinforced largely through online communities.

Psychiatric perspective

Psychiatrists and psychologists who have studied self-described targeted individuals regard the experiences as consistent with persecutory delusions found in conditions such as schizophrenia, delusional disorder and severe anxiety or trauma-related disorders. Studies comparing "gang stalking" complainants with victims of stalking by a single known person find the group-stalking accounts are more likely to be associated with psychotic symptoms, depressive symptoms and post-traumatic stress. Clinical authors emphasise that the distress reported is real and that sufferers rarely seek or accept psychiatric explanations, in part because online communities validate the external-attribution account. Care is complicated because attempts by family and clinicians to question the belief may be absorbed into it as evidence of complicity.

Context

The concept emerged in the early 2000s and grew with social media. It is discussed alongside related belief systems — such as claims of electronic harassment and "V2K" (voice-to-skull) weapons — and in research on online radicalisation of grievance beliefs, misinformation and the social dynamics of support groups that reinforce delusional systems. Law-enforcement and journalistic investigations have found no substantiated evidence of organised stalking campaigns of the kind described.
