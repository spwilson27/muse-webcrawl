Jacques Ellul

Jacques Ellul (6 January 1912 – 19 May 1994) was a French philosopher, sociologist, lay theologian, and professor of law and social history at the University of Bordeaux. A prolific author of more than sixty books, he is best known for his critique of modern technology, above all in The Technological Society (1954; English translation 1964).

Life

Ellul was born in Bordeaux. During the Second World War he was active in the French Resistance and helped Jews escape deportation, work for which he was later recognised as Righteous Among the Nations by Yad Vashem. A member of the Reformed Church of France, he combined his academic career with theological writing and local political engagement, including environmental advocacy in the Aquitaine region.

Thought

Ellul argued that modern society is dominated by "technique" — not merely machines, but the ensemble of rationally ordered methods aimed at efficiency in every field of human activity. In his analysis, technique becomes autonomous and self-augmenting, escaping democratic and moral control and shaping the state, the economy, and culture in its own image. His other major works include Propaganda: The Formation of Men's Attitudes (1962), an influential study of mass persuasion in technological societies; The Political Illusion; and extensive biblical and theological studies in which he developed a Christian anarchist position. His dialectical pairing of sociological and theological writing is a hallmark of his work.

Influence

Ellul's writings influenced critics of technology and consumer society, as well as figures such as Ivan Illich and, by his own account, the Unabomber Theodore Kaczynski cited the English translation of The Technological Society as an influence. The International Jacques Ellul Society promotes study of his work.
