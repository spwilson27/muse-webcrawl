LocationNortheastern Alberta, Canada (around Fort McMurray)
ResourceBitumen (extra-heavy crude oil) mixed with sand and clay

The Athabasca oil sands (or tar sands) are a vast deposit of bitumen mixed with sand, clay and water in northeastern Alberta, Canada, centred on Fort McMurray along the Athabasca River. Together with the neighbouring Cold Lake and Peace River deposits, they make Canada's oil reserves the third or fourth largest in the world and underpin Canada's position as a top oil producer.

Extraction

About a fifth of the bitumen lies shallow enough for open-pit mining: the sand is trucked to plants where hot water separates the bitumen (the Clark hot water process). Deeper deposits are produced in situ, mainly by steam-assisted gravity drainage, in which paired horizontal wells inject steam to melt the bitumen so it drains to a producer well. Raw bitumen is too viscous to pipeline and is either upgraded into synthetic crude or diluted with condensate ("dilbit").

History

Indigenous peoples used surface bitumen to waterproof canoes long before European contact; fur traders reported the seeps in the 18th century. Commercial development began with the Great Canadian Oil Sands plant (now Suncor) in 1967 and Syncrude in 1978, expanding rapidly with high oil prices in the 2000s.

Environmental issues

The oil sands are Canada's fastest-growing source of greenhouse-gas emissions, owing to energy-intensive extraction and upgrading; they also generate large tailings ponds, disturb boreal forest and raise water-use and Indigenous-rights concerns, alongside major economic contributions and reclamation programmes.
