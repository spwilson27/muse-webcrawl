Unity of science

The unity of science is a thesis in the philosophy of science holding that all scientific disciplines can, in principle, be integrated into a single unified framework — typically by reduction of their laws and concepts to those of a more fundamental science, ultimately physics. The idea was a central program of the logical positivists of the Vienna Circle in the 1920s and 1930s, who promoted it through the International Encyclopedia of Unified Science (launched 1938), with contributors including Otto Neurath, Rudolf Carnap, and Charles W. Morris.

Forms of unity

Philosophers distinguish several senses of unity: ontological unity (the world consists of one kind of stuff, usually physical), reductive unity (theories of higher-level sciences can be derived from lower-level ones, as in Ernest Nagel's model of intertheoretic reduction), methodological unity (all sciences share one method), and linguistic unity (all scientific statements can be translated into a single physicalist language).

Criticism

Critics such as Paul Feyerabend and, later, proponents of the "disunity of science" (John Dupré, Nancy Cartwright) argued that the special sciences have autonomous laws and that the world may be "dappled" rather than governed by one set of universal principles. Jerry Fodor's influential 1974 essay "Special Sciences" defended the autonomy of disciplines like psychology and economics on the grounds of multiple realizability. More recent work often seeks weaker, local forms of integration — interfield theories, mechanisms, and consilience — rather than full reduction.
