The process of fermentation in winemaking turns grape juice into an alcoholic beverage. During fermentation, yeasts transform sugars present in the juice — chiefly glucose and fructose — into ethanol and carbon dioxide, while also producing heat and a host of flavour-active compounds including esters, higher alcohols, and glycerol. Fermentation is the defining biochemical step of wine production and one of the oldest biotechnologies known, practised for at least eight millennia.

The process

Primary (alcoholic) fermentation is usually carried out by the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae, either as ambient ("wild") yeast present on the grapes and in the winery, or as a cultured strain inoculated by the winemaker. Reds are fermented on their skins, which float as a "cap" that must be punched down or pumped over to extract colour, tannin, and flavour; whites are usually fermented from clarified juice at cooler temperatures (around 12–18 °C) to preserve aromatics, while reds ferment warmer (around 24–30 °C). Fermentations may "stick" — stop with residual sugar — if yeast runs out of nutrients, alcohol rises too high, or temperatures stray too far.

Malolactic conversion

Many wines, nearly all reds, undergo a secondary bacterial conversion in which lactic acid bacteria (especially Oenococcus oeni) convert sharp malic acid into softer lactic acid and CO2, deacidifying the wine and adding buttery notes through diacetyl. Winemakers may encourage or block this conversion depending on style.

Other fermentations

Carbonic maceration, used for Beaujolais Nouveau, ferments whole berries intracellularly before crushing. Sparkling wines undergo a second alcoholic fermentation in the bottle (traditional method) or tank (Charmat method) to trap CO2. In Sherry production, the yeast film called flor both ferments and protects the wine. Fermentation is ended when the winemaker's target is reached — by dryness, by fortification, by chilling and filtration, or historically by the addition of sulfur dioxide.
