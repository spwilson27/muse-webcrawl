Room clearing


Context: Military operations in urban terrain (MOUT), law-enforcement tactics
Team size: Typically a four-person stack
Key principles: Speed, surprise, violence of action; sectors of fire

Room clearing is a close-quarters combat tactic used by military and law-enforcement teams to enter, secure, and systematically clear a room or enclosed space that may contain hostile occupants. It forms a core drill of urban warfare training (MOUT/FIBUA) and of police tactical-unit and hostage-rescue operations.

Technique

A team assembles outside the entry point in a line called a "stack". On a signal — sometimes after a diversionary device such as a flash-bang grenade is thrown — the team enters in rapid sequence, each member moving along a predetermined path ("buttonhook" or "cross") to a point of domination in the room while engaging threats within an assigned sector of fire. Corners and the centre of the room are addressed in order of threat, and each member verbally confirms their sector is clear. The team then marks the room as cleared and proceeds to the next space, maintaining security on doors, corridors, and stairwells.

Principles and risks

Doctrine emphasises speed, surprise, and controlled aggression to overwhelm occupants before they can react. The doorway — the "fatal funnel" — is the most dangerous ground, since entrants are silhouetted and channelled; techniques aim to minimise time spent in it. Room clearing is among the most hazardous of infantry tasks: defenders hold prepared positions, fields of fire are short and crowded, and distinguishing combatants from non-combatants must happen in fractions of a second. Modern variations use limited-penetration methods, clearing as much of a room as possible from outside the threshold, and employ robots, cameras, and ballistic shields to reduce exposure.

Training

Forces train in purpose-built "shoot houses" with movable walls, first with blank or marking ammunition and role-players, progressing to live fire in specially armoured facilities. The drill is also standard in infantry, special forces, and SWAT selection and continuation training worldwide.
