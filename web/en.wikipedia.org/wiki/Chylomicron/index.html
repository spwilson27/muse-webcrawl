Chylomicrons (from the Greek chylo, juice or milky fluid, and micron, small particle) are lipoprotein particles that are composed chiefly of triglycerides (85–92%), phospholipids (6–12%), cholesterol (1–3%), and proteins (1–2%). They transport dietary lipids from the intestines to other locations in the body, and are the largest and least dense of the lipoprotein classes.

Formation

Chylomicrons are assembled in the endoplasmic reticulum of enterocytes, the absorptive cells of the small intestine. Dietary triglycerides are resynthesized in these cells and packaged with dietary cholesterol, fat-soluble vitamins, phospholipids, and one molecule of apolipoprotein B-48 (apoB-48), the structural protein that distinguishes chylomicrons. After exocytosis into the lacteals of the intestinal villi, they travel through the lymphatic system and enter the bloodstream at the left subclavian vein via the thoracic duct, bypassing the liver on first pass.

Metabolism

In the circulation, chylomicrons acquire apolipoproteins C-II and E from HDL. ApoC-II activates lipoprotein lipase in the capillaries of muscle and adipose tissue, which hydrolyzes the core triglycerides and releases free fatty acids for energy use or storage. As the particle shrinks it becomes a chylomicron remnant, enriched in cholesterol and apoE, which is rapidly taken up by the liver via remnant receptors. The half-life of chylomicrons in plasma is only about five to ten minutes.

Clinical significance

Defects in chylomicron metabolism can cause severe hypertriglyceridemia. Familial chylomicronemia syndrome, caused by deficiency of lipoprotein lipase or its activator apoC-II, leads to fasting chylomicron accumulation and recurrent pancreatitis. Failure to form chylomicrons, as in abetalipoproteinemia, results in fat malabsorption and deficiencies of fat-soluble vitamins.
