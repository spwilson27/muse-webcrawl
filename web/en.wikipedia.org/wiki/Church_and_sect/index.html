Church and sect

The church–sect typology is a sociological classification of religious organisations developed in the sociology of religion, principally by Max Weber and Ernst Troeltsch. It contrasts two ideal types: the church — large, inclusive, established and accommodating to the surrounding society — and the sect — small, exclusive, voluntary and in tension with society. The typology has been elaborated into a continuum (church – denomination – sect – cult) by later scholars such as H. Richard Niebuhr and Howard Becker.

[Image: A large established cathedral and a small sect meeting house]

Weber and Troeltsch

Weber introduced the distinction in The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1904–05) and his sociology of religion: a church is an institution into which one is born, dispensing grace impersonally; a sect is a voluntary association of the religiously qualified, membership in which must be earned and maintained by conduct. Troeltsch, in The Social Teaching of the Christian Churches (1912), systematised the contrast and added a third type, mysticism — individual, loose spiritual religion.

Development of the typology

H. Richard Niebuhr (The Social Sources of Denominationalism, 1929) argued that sects tend over generations to become denominations — church-like bodies — as members prosper and accommodate, a process sometimes called the sect-to-church cycle. Howard Becker extended the scheme to a fourfold sequence of cult, sect, denomination and ecclesia. Rodney Stark and William Sims Bainbridge reformulated the typology in terms of a single variable: the degree of tension between a religious group and its sociocultural environment.

Criticism

The typology reflects the European experience of established churches and fits other settings imperfectly; terms such as "sect" and "cult" carry pejorative connotations in everyday use; and many real organisations mix features of several types.
