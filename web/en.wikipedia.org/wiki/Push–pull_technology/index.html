Push–pull technology

Push–pull technology
TypeIntercropping-based integrated pest management strategy
Developed byICIPE (Kenya) with Rothamsted Research (UK) and partners
Key researchersZeyaur Khan and colleagues
Target cropsMaize, sorghum and other cereals
Target pestsStemborer moths; parasitic weed Striga; (later versions also fall armyworm)
Companion plantsDesmodium species (push intercrop); Napier grass or Brachiaria (pull border crop)

Push–pull technology is a strategy for controlling agricultural pests by using repellent "push" plants and trap "pull" plants, developed in Kenya for smallholder cereal farmers. In its classic form, maize or sorghum is intercropped with a legume of the genus Desmodium, whose volatile chemicals repel ("push") egg-laying stemborer moths away from the crop, and the field is surrounded by a border of Napier grass (Pennisetum purpureum), which attracts ("pulls") the moths to lay eggs on it — but on which most stemborer larvae fail to survive, because the grass produces a sticky, gum-like substance that traps them.

Mechanism

The system, developed from the 1990s by the International Centre of Insect Physiology and Ecology (ICIPE) in Nairobi with Rothamsted Research, exploits insect chemical ecology. Desmodium emits volatile compounds (including (E)-β-ocimene and (E)-4,8-dimethyl-1,3,7-nonatriene) that repel stemborer moths and also attract the parasitoid wasp Cotesia sesamiae, a natural enemy of the borers. Napier grass emits green-leaf volatiles that attract ovipositing moths, then kills most larvae. Remarkably, Desmodium root exudates also suppress the parasitic weed Striga hermonthica by inducing suicidal germination of its seeds, solving a second major constraint on African cereal yields.

Benefits and adoption

Field trials and farmer uptake in western Kenya and neighbouring countries showed substantially increased maize yields — often doubling or more in Striga-infested fields — along with fodder production (both Desmodium and Napier grass are livestock feeds), nitrogen fixation by the legume, improved soil cover and reduced erosion. The technology has been adopted by well over a hundred thousand smallholder households in East Africa. "Climate-adapted" versions substitute drought-tolerant partners — Desmodium intortum and Brachiaria (signal grass) — for drier areas, and later versions were found to also reduce damage by the invasive fall armyworm.
