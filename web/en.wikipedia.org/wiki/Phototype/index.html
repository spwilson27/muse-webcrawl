Phototype

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Phototype slugs being composed.

Phototype, also known as phototypesetting or filmsetting, is a method of typesetting that uses photography to project characters onto photographic paper or film, which is then used to make printing plates. It replaced hot metal typesetting as the dominant technology in the 1960s and 1970s before itself being displaced by digital desktop publishing in the 1980s and 1990s.

Technology

In a phototypesetter, characters are stored as images on a film strip, disc, drum, or glass grid. A light source projects each character through a lens onto photosensitive paper as the operator keyboards the text; the output is a galley of finished type, developed photographically and pasted up into pages for platemaking. Because characters are optical images, phototypesetting allowed easy scaling, slanting, and distortion of type, and made a far wider range of typefaces cheaply available than metal type had. Machines were produced by companies such as Compugraphic, Linotype (Linofilm), Mergenthaler, Monotype, and Berthold; later CRT-based systems displayed whole pages and output at high speed.

History

Experiments date to the late nineteenth century, but practical machines appeared after the Second World War: the Fotosetter (1946), the Photon (Lumitype) machine, and the Monophoto. Phototype, called "cold type" in contrast to hot metal, dominated newspaper, advertising, and book production for roughly three decades. Its final generation merged into digital typesetting, in which characters are stored as mathematical outlines — the direct ancestor of modern font technology.
