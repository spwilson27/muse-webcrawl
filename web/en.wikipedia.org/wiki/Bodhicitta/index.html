Avalokiteshvara, the bodhisattva of compassion, embodies bodhicitta.

In Mahayana Buddhism, bodhicitta (Sanskrit: बोधिचित्त, "mind of awakening" or "enlightenment-mind") is the mind that strives toward awakening and compassion for the benefit of all sentient beings. One who gives rise to bodhicitta and vows to work for the liberation of others is a bodhisattva. Bodhicitta is considered the defining motivation of the Mahayana path and the root of the bodhisattva's career through the perfections (paramitas).

Types

Traditions distinguish relative (or conventional) bodhicitta — the heartfelt aspiration and practical commitment to attain Buddhahood for others' sake — from absolute bodhicitta, the direct realization of emptiness (shunyata), the true nature of phenomena. Relative bodhicitta is further divided into aspiring bodhicitta (the wish, like intending to travel) and engaging bodhicitta (actually undertaking the journey, through practice of generosity, ethics, patience, diligence, meditation, and wisdom).

Generating bodhicitta

Classical methods include the sevenfold cause-and-effect instruction — recognizing all beings as one's mothers, remembering their kindness, resolving to repay it, and so on, culminating in the altruistic resolve — and equalizing and exchanging self and others, taught by the 8th-century master Shantideva in the Bodhicharyavatara and associated with the practice of tonglen (giving and taking). The 11th-century Indian master Atisha brought these teachings to Tibet, where they were organized into the lojong (mind training) tradition. In formal practice, bodhicitta is cultivated and sealed through the bodhisattva vows, often in a ritual with a preceptor, and is commonly generated at the start of meditation sessions through refuge and verses such as Shantideva's: "May I be a protector for those without protection..."

In practice

Bodhicitta frames the daily liturgies of Tibetan, Chinese, Korean, Japanese, and Vietnamese Mahayana communities, and is renewed in practices such as the recitation of Shantideva's text, the four immeasurables (loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity), and dedication of merit. Modern teachers emphasize that bodhicitta joins wisdom and compassion as "two wings" of the path: neither realization without compassion nor compassion without wisdom completes the Mahayana ideal.
