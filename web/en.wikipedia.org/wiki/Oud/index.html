Oud

Oud
ClassificationString instrument (plucked lute)
RelatedLute, barbat, qanbus, gambus
RegionMiddle East, North Africa, Central Asia

The oud (Arabic: عود, romanized: ʿūd) is a middle-eastern short-neck lute-type, pear-shaped, fretless stringed instrument with 11 or 13 strings, grouped in 5 or 6 courses. It is very similar to other types of lutes and to the Western lute. The modern oud and the European lute both descend from a common ancestor via diverging paths.

The oud is a defining instrument of Arabic, Turkish, Persian, Armenian, Greek and North African music, central to the maqam and taksim (improvisation) traditions. It is played with a long thin plectrum called a risha (Arabic) or mızrap (Turkish).

History

The oud descends from the Persian barbat, and its name derives from the Arabic al-ʿūd ("the wood"), distinguishing its wooden soundboard from skin-fronted ancestors. It was carried into al-Andalus, where players such as Ziryab (9th century) famously added a fifth pair of strings. Through Spain and Sicily the instrument entered Europe and evolved into the Renaissance lute; the word "lute" itself derives from al-ʿūd.

Construction and tuning

The body is built from thin strips of wood forming a rounded bowl; the soundboard (usually spruce) carries one large and two small carved rosettes. The short neck is fretless, allowing the quarter-tones of maqam music, and the pegbox is bent back at nearly 90 degrees. Regional schools differ: Arabic ouds are larger with a deeper bowl and are tuned in fourths (commonly C–F–A–d–g–c), while Turkish ouds are smaller and tuned a tone higher.
