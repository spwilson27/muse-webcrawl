Receptor (biochemistry)

In biochemistry and pharmacology, receptors are chemical structures, usually proteins, that receive chemical signals from outside a cell (or, for intracellular receptors, from within it) and initiate a response. When a signaling molecule — a ligand — binds to a receptor, it causes some form of cellular or tissue response, such as a change in the cell's membrane potential, enzyme activity, or gene expression.

Types

Cell-surface receptors span the plasma membrane and include three major classes: ion channel–linked receptors (such as the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor), which open a pore for ions; G protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs), the largest receptor family in humans and the target of a large share of modern drugs, which activate intracellular G proteins; and enzyme-linked receptors, such as receptor tyrosine kinases, whose intracellular domains have or recruit enzymatic activity. Intracellular receptors, including the nuclear receptors for steroid and thyroid hormones, reside in the cytoplasm or nucleus and directly regulate transcription.

Drug action

Molecules that activate receptors are called agonists; those that bind without activating and block agonists are antagonists; partial agonists produce a submaximal response. Receptor binding is characterized by affinity and efficacy, and dose–response relationships are fundamental to pharmacology. Cells regulate receptor numbers through upregulation and downregulation, contributing to tolerance and desensitization.
