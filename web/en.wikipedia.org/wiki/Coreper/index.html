Coreper

[Image: Permanent representatives of the EU member states meeting in Brussels]

Coreper, from the French Comité des représentants permanents, is the Committee of Permanent Representatives of the governments of the member states to the European Union. Based in Brussels, it is the Council of the European Union's most senior preparatory body: it prepares the work of all Council configurations, seeks agreement on dossiers before they reach ministers, and coordinates the work of the roughly 150 Council working parties beneath it. Article 240 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union provides its legal basis.

Structure

Coreper meets in two formations. Coreper II, composed of the member states' permanent representatives (ambassadors) themselves, deals with the more politically and financially significant business: general affairs, external relations, economic and financial matters, justice and home affairs, and preparation of European Council meetings. Coreper I, composed of the deputy permanent representatives, handles other areas such as agriculture, environment, transport, employment and the internal market. Coreper II is generally regarded as the more influential of the two. The chair is held by the permanent representative (or deputy) of the state holding the rotating presidency of the Council, except in the Foreign Affairs Council's preparatory bodies, which support the High Representative.

Functions

Almost every item on a Council agenda passes first through a working party, then through Coreper. Items on which agreement has effectively been reached are placed on the Council agenda as "A points", adopted without debate; contested dossiers are listed as "B points" for ministerial discussion. Because Coreper meets weekly and its members are senior diplomats with direct lines to their capitals, it can resolve most disagreements itself; ministers are estimated to rubber-stamp or settle on the basis of Coreper's groundwork the great majority of legislative files. Coreper also prepares the meetings of the European Council in its General Affairs Council formation's stead and liaises continuously with the European Parliament and the Commission.

Criticism

Coreper's centrality has attracted criticism on transparency grounds: much EU legislation is effectively agreed by unelected officials behind closed doors, with ministerial meetings then formally endorsing the outcomes. Defenders reply that the committee is accountable through the ministers who answer to national parliaments, and that its working methods are a practical necessity for legislating among 27 states.
