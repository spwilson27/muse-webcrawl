Friedrich Georg Wilhelm von Struve (15 April 1793 – 23 November 1864) was a Baltic German astronomer and geodesist. He was one of the leading astronomers of the 19th century, famous for his studies of double stars, his early measurement of stellar parallax, and the geodetic survey known as the Struve Geodetic Arc.

Career

Struve studied at the University of Dorpat (now Tartu, Estonia) and became director of the Dorpat Observatory in 1820. Using a fine Fraunhofer refractor, he surveyed double stars and published catalogues listing thousands of them, culminating in his Mensurae Micrometricae (1837). In the 1830s he measured the parallax of the star Vega — among the first successful stellar parallax determinations, published close in time to those of Bessel and Henderson.

In 1839 he became the founding director of the new Pulkovo Observatory near Saint Petersburg, which he built into one of the world's leading astronomical institutions. He also directed the triangulation of a meridian arc from the Arctic Ocean to the Black Sea — the Struve Geodetic Arc, a chain of survey points stretching some 2,820 km through ten countries, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Legacy

Struve founded a dynasty of astronomers: his son Otto Wilhelm von Struve succeeded him at Pulkovo, and his descendants included Hermann Struve and the American astronomer Otto Struve. The lunar crater Struve and the asteroid 768 Struveana are named in the family's honour.
