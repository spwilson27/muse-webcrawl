International Financial Reporting Standards

IFRS Accounting Standards are required or permitted in more than 140 jurisdictions

International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) are accounting standards issued by the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB), an independent body of the IFRS Foundation based in London. Their aim is to bring transparency, accountability and efficiency to financial markets by providing a common global language for business affairs, so that company accounts are understandable and comparable across international boundaries. The term IFRS encompasses both the standards issued by the IASB since 2001 (numbered IFRS 1 to IFRS 18) and the earlier International Accounting Standards (IAS) issued by its predecessor, the IASC.

Framework and major standards

IFRS is principles-based, founded on a Conceptual Framework that defines the objective of financial reporting and qualitative characteristics such as relevance and faithful representation. Widely applied standards include IFRS 9 (financial instruments), IFRS 15 (revenue from contracts with customers), IFRS 16 (leases, which brought most leases onto the balance sheet), IFRS 13 (fair value measurement), IFRS 10 (consolidated financial statements) and IAS 1 (presentation of financial statements). In 2023 the IFRS Foundation's new International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) issued its first sustainability disclosure standards, IFRS S1 and IFRS S2.

Adoption

More than 140 jurisdictions require IFRS for all or most publicly listed companies, including the European Union, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Brazil and South Korea. The United States retains its own US GAAP, issued by the FASB; a long-running convergence programme between the IASB and FASB produced partly converged standards on revenue and leases, but full convergence was not achieved. Listed companies in China and India use standards substantially converged with IFRS.
