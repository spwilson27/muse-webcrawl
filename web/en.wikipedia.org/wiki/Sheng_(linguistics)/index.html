Sheng (linguistics)

Sheng
RegionNairobi and other Kenyan urban areas
Base languagesSwahili and English
TypeSlang / mixed code

In linguistics, Sheng is a Swahili and English-based slang or cant spoken primarily by urban youth in Nairobi, Kenya, and increasingly in other Kenyan towns. The name is usually explained as a blend of Swahili and English. Sheng draws its grammar mainly from Swahili while mixing vocabulary from English, Swahili and Kenya's indigenous languages — such as Kikuyu, Luo, Luhya and Kamba — as well as borrowings mediated by popular culture.

Origins and development

Sheng emerged in the multicultural estates of Nairobi's Eastlands in the 1970s and 1980s, where children of different ethnic backgrounds needed a neutral common code. It spread through matatu (minibus) culture, schools and music, and is strongly associated with Kenyan hip hop and genge. Because its vocabulary changes rapidly and varies by neighbourhood and generation, Sheng marks in-group identity and excludes outsiders, including parents and teachers.

Features

Speakers coin words by borrowing, clipping, affixing Swahili morphology to English roots, and playful sound changes and reversals. Sheng is primarily an oral code used for informal conversation, comedy, advertising and lyrics; it is not standardised and has no official status, though media houses and advertisers use it to reach young audiences.

Debate

Educators and some commentators have worried that Sheng undermines proficiency in standard Swahili and English, while linguists argue it is a creative register that coexists with the standard languages and reflects Kenya's urban multilingualism. Related urban youth codes elsewhere in Africa, such as Tanzania's street Swahili and South Africa's tsotsitaal, show similar patterns.
