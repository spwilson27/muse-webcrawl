Geode

Geode
CategoryGeological formation
CompositionChalcedony, quartz, calcite, amethyst and other minerals

A geode is a geological secondary formation within sedimentary and volcanic rocks: a roughly spherical or oblong rock that is hollow inside and lined with crystals of minerals such as quartz, amethyst, calcite or agate. The word derives from the Greek ge-ōdēs, "earthlike".

Formation

Geodes form when mineral-rich groundwater deposits minerals on the inner walls of a cavity in rock. In volcanic rocks the cavity typically begins as a gas bubble in cooling lava; in sedimentary rocks it may be a hollow left by a dissolved fossil, concretion or tree root. Over thousands to millions of years, silica and other dissolved minerals precipitate in layers: an outer band of chalcedony commonly forms first, followed by inward-growing crystals. If the cavity fills completely, the result is called a nodule rather than a geode.

Distribution

Well-known geode localities include the Keokuk region of Iowa, Missouri and Illinois in the United States (Iowa designated the geode its state rock in 1967); Chihuahua, Mexico; Artigas, Uruguay, famous for large amethyst geodes; and volcanic districts in Brazil and Namibia. The giant Pulpí geode in Almería, Spain, lined with enormous gypsum crystals, is about 8 metres long.
