Social security

Social security is the protection that a society provides to individuals and households to assure access to health care and to guarantee income security, particularly in cases of old age, unemployment, sickness, invalidity, work injury, maternity or loss of a breadwinner, in the definition of the International Labour Organization. Systems of social security typically combine social insurance — benefits earned through contributions, such as pensions and unemployment insurance — with social assistance — tax-funded, often means-tested support. In the United States, "Social Security" is also the proper name of the federal Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance (OASDI) programme created by the Social Security Act of 1935.

History

Precursors include poor laws and mutual-aid friendly societies, but modern social security began with Otto von Bismarck's social insurance laws in Imperial Germany: health insurance (1883), accident insurance (1884) and old-age and disability insurance (1889), introduced partly to undercut the socialist movement. Britain followed with old-age pensions (1908) and National Insurance (1911). The term "social security" gained currency with the United States Social Security Act of 1935, part of Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal, and was cemented internationally by the Atlantic Charter (1941), the Beveridge Report (1942) in Britain, and Article 22 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948): "Everyone, as a member of society, has the right to social security."

Models

Comparative welfare-state research, notably Gøsta Esping-Andersen's The Three Worlds of Welfare Capitalism (1990), distinguishes liberal (means-tested, market-oriented), conservative-corporatist (contribution-based, status-preserving) and social-democratic (universal, generous) regimes. Funding may be pay-as-you-go, in which current workers' contributions pay current benefits, or funded through accumulated reserves; most pension systems are largely pay-as-you-go, raising debates about sustainability as populations age.
