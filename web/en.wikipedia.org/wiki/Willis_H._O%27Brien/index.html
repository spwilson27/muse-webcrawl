Willis Harold O'Brien (2 March 1886 – 8 November 1962) was an American film special-effects pioneer and stop-motion animator. He is celebrated for bringing dinosaurs and giant creatures to life in The Lost World (1925) and King Kong (1933), work that defined cinematic special effects for generations.

Early work

O'Brien worked variously as a cowboy, cartoonist and sculptor before making short stop-motion comedies for Edison in the 1910s. His feature breakthrough, The Lost World, adapted Arthur Conan Doyle's novel with remarkably lifelike animated dinosaurs and astonished audiences.

King Kong and later career

At RKO, O'Brien supervised the effects for King Kong (1933), animating Kong and the creatures of Skull Island with the assistance of model-maker Marcel Delgado; the film's success made him the industry's leading effects artist. Later credits include Son of Kong (1933), Mighty Joe Young (1949) — which won the Academy Award for Best Visual Effects — and The Black Scorpion (1957).

Legacy

O'Brien's protégé Ray Harryhausen carried his techniques forward, and his influence extends to modern motion-capture and digital creature effects. A posthumous Winsor McCay Award recognised his lifetime contribution to animation.
