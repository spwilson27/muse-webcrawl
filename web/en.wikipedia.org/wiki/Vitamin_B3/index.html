Vitamin B3

Vitamin B3, colloquially referred to as niacin, is a vitamin family that includes three forms, or vitamers: nicotinic acid (niacin), nicotinamide (niacinamide), and nicotinamide riboside. All three forms convert within the body to nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) and its phosphate NADP, coenzymes required for redox reactions in energy metabolism, DNA repair and cell signalling. The body can also synthesise NAD from the amino acid tryptophan. Niacin is sometimes defined narrowly as nicotinic acid alone, as opposed to the amide form.

Sources and requirements

Good sources include meat, poultry, fish such as tuna and salmon, peanuts, mushrooms, avocados, whole grains and fortified foods. Requirements are expressed in niacin equivalents (NE), counting 60 mg of dietary tryptophan as 1 mg of niacin; the U.S. Recommended Dietary Allowance for adults is 16 mg NE/day for men and 14 mg NE/day for women.

Deficiency

Severe deficiency causes pellagra, characterised by the "four Ds" — dermatitis (a photosensitive rash), diarrhoea, dementia, and, if untreated, death. Pellagra was epidemic in the early-20th-century American South among poor people subsisting on untreated maize, and was shown by Joseph Goldberger to be a dietary deficiency disease; it is now rare in developed countries except in alcoholism, malabsorption and certain metabolic disorders such as Hartnup disease and carcinoid syndrome.

Pharmacological use and toxicity

In gram doses, nicotinic acid (but not nicotinamide) was historically used to modify blood lipids — lowering LDL cholesterol and triglycerides and raising HDL — though large outcome trials found no added benefit on top of statins, and it commonly causes prostaglandin-mediated flushing of the face and neck. High-dose nicotinic acid can also cause liver toxicity, particularly in sustained-release preparations, and can worsen glucose control.
