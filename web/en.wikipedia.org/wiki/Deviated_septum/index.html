Deviated septum

SpecialtyOtorhinolaryngology
SymptomsOften none; nasal obstruction, nosebleeds, sinus infections
CausesDevelopment, trauma
TreatmentMedication, septoplasty

A deviated septum occurs when the thin wall between the nasal passages (the nasal septum) is displaced to one side. In many people, the nasal septum is off-center — or deviated — making one nasal passage smaller. Some degree of deviation is extremely common and often causes no symptoms at all.

Signs and symptoms

When a deviated septum is severe, it can block one side of the nose and reduce airflow, causing difficulty breathing through the nose. Other associated problems can include nasal congestion (often worse on one side), recurrent sinus infections, nosebleeds from dryness of the septal surface, facial pain or headaches in some cases, and noisy breathing during sleep or a preference for sleeping on a particular side.

Causes

A deviated septum may be present at birth, arising during fetal development, or may result from injury to the nose — during childbirth, contact sports, or accidents. The deviation can also become more noticeable with ageing, as nasal structures change over time.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually by physical examination of the nose, sometimes with a nasal speculum, endoscopy, or imaging. Treatment of symptoms may include decongestants, antihistamines and nasal steroid sprays to manage congestion, although these do not correct the deviation itself. Surgical correction, called septoplasty, straightens the septum by trimming, repositioning or replacing cartilage and bone, and is considered when symptoms are significant; it is sometimes combined with rhinoplasty (septorhinoplasty) when the external shape of the nose is also corrected.
