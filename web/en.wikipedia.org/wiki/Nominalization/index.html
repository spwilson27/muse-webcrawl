Nominalization

In linguistics, nominalization is the use of a word which is not a noun (typically a verb or an adjective) as a noun, or as the head of a noun phrase. In English this often involves derivational suffixes such as -tion, -ment, -ness and -ity: for example, the verb refuse becomes the noun refusal, and the adjective intense becomes intensity. Conversion, or zero derivation, produces nouns like a run from a verb with no change of form.

Types

Nominalizations may denote the action or process of the verb (the construction of the bridge), its result (a construction), or related states and qualities. Some languages, such as many Australian Aboriginal and Native American languages, make extensive grammatical use of nominalized clauses in place of subordinate clauses.

Style

Writing guides often criticise heavy nominalization — sometimes called "zombie nouns" — because turning verbs into nouns can obscure who is doing what and make prose dense, as in we made a decision in place of we decided. In academic and bureaucratic writing, however, nominalization serves useful functions, such as packaging previously stated information so it can be referred to and discussed.
