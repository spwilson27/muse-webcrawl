Soul theorem

The soul theorem is a major result in Riemannian geometry, proved by Jeff Cheeger and Detlef Gromoll in 1972. It describes the topology of a complete, non-compact Riemannian manifold of non-negative sectional curvature: such a manifold contains a compact, totally geodesic submanifold called its soul, and the manifold is diffeomorphic to the normal bundle of the soul.

Statement

Let M be a complete non-compact Riemannian manifold with sectional curvature K ≥ 0. Then there exists a compact, totally convex, totally geodesic submanifold S ⊂ M, called a soul of M, such that M is diffeomorphic to the normal bundle of S. In particular, M has the homotopy type of the compact manifold S, so its topology is strongly constrained: for example, its fundamental group and homology are those of a compact manifold of lower or equal dimension.

Consequences

If M has strictly positive curvature at even one point, then the soul reduces to a single point and M is diffeomorphic to Euclidean space — the soul conjecture, proposed by Cheeger and Gromoll and later proved by Grigori Perelman in 1994. If the curvature is positive everywhere and M is non-compact, the soul must be a point, so again M is diffeomorphic to ℝn. Sharafutdinov's retraction shows that the natural distance-non-increasing map from M onto its soul is a deformation retraction with additional geometric properties.

Examples

For flat Euclidean space, any point is a soul. For the product of a compact manifold of non-negative curvature with Euclidean space, the soul is the compact factor times a point. More subtle examples, constructed by Gromoll and Meyer and by Eschenburg, exhibit exotic spheres carrying metrics of non-negative curvature, where the soul is a lower-dimensional sphere.
