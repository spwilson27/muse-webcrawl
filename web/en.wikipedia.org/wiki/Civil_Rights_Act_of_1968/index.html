Civil Rights Act of 1968

The Civil Rights Act of 1968 is a landmark law in the United States signed into law by President Lyndon B. Johnson on April 11, 1968. Titles VIII and IX of the Act are commonly known as the Fair Housing Act, which prohibited discrimination concerning the sale, rental, and financing of housing based on race, religion, national origin, and (as amended in 1974) sex. The Act also included the Indian Civil Rights Act and provisions — the so-called "Rap Brown" law — making it a federal crime to cross state lines to incite a riot.

Background

Fair housing legislation had stalled in Congress for several years. The assassination of Martin Luther King Jr. on April 4, 1968, and the wave of civil unrest that followed, created momentum for passage; the House approved the Senate's version days later, and Johnson signed the bill a week after King's death, calling it a tribute to his legacy.

Provisions

The Fair Housing Act banned refusal to sell or rent a dwelling because of race, colour, religion, or national origin, discriminatory advertising, and blockbusting; disability and familial status were added as protected classes by amendments in 1988. The Indian Civil Rights Act applied most of the Bill of Rights' protections to individuals under tribal government jurisdiction. The Act expanded federal protection of civil rights workers and criminalised interstate travel in aid of rioting.
