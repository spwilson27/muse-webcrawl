The PlayStation (officially abbreviated as PS, and commonly as PS1 or by its codename PSX) is a home video game console developed and marketed by Sony Computer Entertainment. It was released in Japan on 3 December 1994 and in the rest of the world during 1995. The first console in the PlayStation series, it competed primarily with the Nintendo 64 and the Sega Saturn during the fifth generation of consoles.

History

The PlayStation originated when Sony engineer Ken Kutaragi, after watching his daughter play Nintendo's Famicom, proposed that Sony supply the audio chip for Nintendo's Super NES — a collaboration publicly announced in 1991 as a CD-ROM add-on called the Play Station. Nintendo abruptly repudiated the deal at the same trade show, turning instead to Philips; humiliated, Sony president Norio Ohga directed Kutaragi to develop a rival console. Using CD-ROMs — cheap to manufacture, roomy, and quick to press compared with cartridges — Sony courted third-party developers with favourable licensing and aggressive pricing.

Hardware

The console is built around a 32-bit MIPS R3000A CPU running at 33.87 MHz, a custom GPU capable of about 360,000 flat-shaded polygons per second, and 2 MB of main RAM. Games were distributed on CD-ROM, allowing full-motion video and CD-quality audio. The original controller introduced the now-iconic shape-symbol buttons (△ ○ ✕ □), designed by Teiyu Goto; the DualShock controller of 1997 added twin analogue sticks and vibration feedback, setting the template for modern gamepads.

Impact and legacy

The PlayStation was the first console to sell over 100 million units (102.49 million), and it shifted gaming decisively toward 3D graphics, CD media, and an older, mainstream audience marketed through clubs and lifestyle advertising. Landmark titles include Final Fantasy VII, Gran Turismo, Metal Gear Solid, Resident Evil, Tekken 3, Tomb Raider, and Crash Bandicoot. A smaller redesign, the PS One, followed in 2000, and the brand continued with the PlayStation 2, PlayStation 4, and PlayStation 5.
