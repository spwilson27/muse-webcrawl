Caodaism

Type: Monotheistic, syncretic religion
Founded: 1926, Tây Ninh, Vietnam
Founder: Ngô Văn Chiêu and others
Supreme being: Cao Đài (Đức Chí Tôn)
Adherents: Several million, mainly in Vietnam
Holy See: Tây Ninh Holy See

Caodaism (Vietnamese: Đạo Cao Đài, literally the "Religion of the Supreme Lord") is a monotheistic syncretic religion officially established in the city of Tây Ninh in southern Vietnam in 1926. The full name of the religion is Đại Đạo Tam Kỳ Phổ Độ ("The Great Faith for the Third Universal Redemption"). Adherents engage in practices such as prayer, veneration of ancestors, nonviolence and vegetarianism, with the goal of union with God and freedom from the cycle of birth and death.

Beliefs

Caodaists believe in a single supreme God, referred to as Cao Đài ("High Tower" or "Supreme Lord"), whose symbol is the Divine Eye — a left eye within a triangle. The faith synthesises elements of Buddhism, Taoism, Confucianism, Christianity and Islam, which are regarded as earlier revelations of the same universal truth. Cao Đài is said to have communicated directly with humanity three times: through figures such as Moses and Laozi in the first era, through Buddha, Jesus and Confucius in the second, and finally directly through spiritist séances in the third era, beginning in the 1920s.

The religion honours a hierarchy of divine beings, including the Jade Emperor figures of Chinese folk religion, the Mother Goddess (Phật Mẫu), and saints drawn from many traditions — among them Victor Hugo, Sun Yat-sen and the Vietnamese poet Nguyễn Bỉnh Khiêm, who appear together in a famous mural at the Tây Ninh Holy See signing a covenant with God.

History

The movement grew out of séances held in Saigon and southern Vietnam in the mid-1920s. Ngô Văn Chiêu, a colonial civil servant, received the first revelations in 1919; in 1926 a group including Lê Văn Trung formally declared the founding of the new religion at Tây Ninh, which became its Holy See. The faith expanded rapidly under French colonial rule, at its height claiming millions of adherents and maintaining its own army, administration and political presence in southern Vietnam.

After the Vietnam War and reunification in 1975, the Caodaist governing bodies were dissolved by the communist government and the Holy See's lands were confiscated; official recognition was restored in 1997, and worship resumed publicly. The largest branch remains centred on Tây Ninh, while smaller independent branches exist elsewhere in Vietnam and among the Vietnamese diaspora, particularly in the United States, Europe and Australia.

Practices and organisation

The priesthood is organised in a hierarchy modelled partly on the Roman Catholic Church, with a pope (Giáo Tông), cardinals, bishops and priests; notably, ordination is open to both men and women. Worship centres on prayer four times daily, dietary rules favouring vegetarianism, and the "Three Doctrines" and "Five Precepts" adapted from Buddhism and Confucian ethics. The Tây Ninh Holy See, a colourful temple complex blending Asian and European architectural styles, remains the spiritual centre of the faith and a popular destination for visitors.
