A money order is a payment order for a pre-specified amount of money. As it is required that the funds be prepaid for the amount shown on it, it is a more trusted method of payment than a cheque. The system of money orders dates back to 1792 in Britain, where a private company first operated it; it was not very profitable, but when the Post Office took over in 1838, the concept quickly gained popularity.

How it works

The purchaser pays the face value plus a small fee at a post office, bank, convenience store or money transfer agent, and receives a printed instrument naming a specific payee. Unlike a personal cheque, a money order cannot bounce, because the issuer already holds the funds. The payee deposits or cashes the money order at a bank or post office; many issuers limit a single money order to a fixed maximum (commonly $1,000 in the United States for domestic orders).

Postal money orders

Postal money orders are issued by national postal services and were for much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries a principal means of sending money safely through the mail, particularly for people without bank accounts. The United States Postal Service began issuing money orders in 1864, partly to reduce theft of cash sent by soldiers during the Civil War.

International money orders

International money orders allow payment between countries, often under agreements administered through the Universal Postal Union. They have largely been displaced by electronic transfers, wire services and mobile money, but remain in use for small remittances and official fees.

Decline

Usage has fallen steadily with the spread of debit cards, online banking and person-to-person payment apps, yet money orders persist for rent payments, transactions requiring guaranteed funds, and customers outside the banking system.
