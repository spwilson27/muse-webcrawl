Prisoner of conscience

[Image: The Amnesty International candle in barbed wire, the symbol associated with prisoners of conscience]

A prisoner of conscience (POC) is a person who has been imprisoned or otherwise physically restricted because of their political, religious or other conscientiously held beliefs, ethnic origin, sex, colour, language, national or social origin, economic status, birth, sexual orientation or other status, and who has neither used nor advocated violence or hatred. The term was introduced by Peter Benenson in his 1961 article "The Forgotten Prisoners" and became the founding concept of Amnesty International.

Definition

Amnesty International calls for the immediate and unconditional release of all prisoners of conscience. The definition deliberately excludes people who have used or advocated violence, a boundary that has occasionally proved controversial: the organisation's refusal to class Nelson Mandela as a prisoner of conscience after his conviction for sabotage in 1964, because the movement he led had turned to armed struggle, is the best-known example. The category overlaps with, but is narrower than, the broader notion of a political prisoner.

Campaigning

The classic Amnesty method built around the concept is the adoption of individual prisoners by local groups, whose members write letters to the authorities holding them, publicise their cases and support their families. Amnesty also distinguishes "prisoners of conscience" cases in its annual reports and urgent-action networks. Other organisations, including Helsinki Watch and its successors, used cognate categories when documenting the imprisonment of dissidents in the Soviet bloc.

Notable prisoners of conscience

People designated prisoners of conscience by Amnesty International have included the Burmese opposition leader Aung San Suu Kyi, the Czech playwright and later president Václav Havel, the Soviet dissident Andrei Sakharov, and the Chinese Nobel laureate Liu Xiaobo. Designations are sometimes contested by governments, which frequently characterise the individuals concerned as ordinary criminals; human rights organisations characterise such prosecutions as pretextual.

Criticism

Critics have questioned the non-violence boundary, the organisation's case-selection choices, and whether letter-writing campaigns measurably improve outcomes. Supporters respond that the concept's precision — its insistence on peaceful belief as the sole ground of imprisonment — is what gives it moral and legal force.
