Hill equation

"Hill equation" can refer to two distinct models named for Archibald V. Hill and George William Hill respectively.

Biochemistry

In biochemistry, the Hill equation describes the fraction of a macromolecule (such as an enzyme or receptor) saturated by a ligand as a function of ligand concentration: θ = [L]ⁿ / (K_d + [L]ⁿ), where θ is fractional saturation, [L] the ligand concentration, K_d a dissociation constant, and n the Hill coefficient. Hill proposed it in 1910 to describe the sigmoidal binding of oxygen to hemoglobin. A Hill coefficient of 1 indicates independent binding; greater than 1 indicates positive cooperativity (hemoglobin's is about 2.8–3); less than 1 indicates negative cooperativity. Plots of log(θ/(1−θ)) against log[L] (Hill plots) estimate n and K_d from experimental data, though the equation is empirical and does not correspond to a literal simultaneous binding of n ligands.

Differential equations

In mathematics, Hill's equation is a second-order linear ordinary differential equation of the form d²y/dt² + f(t)y = 0, where f is a periodic function. Named after G. W. Hill, who used it in 1886 in lunar theory, it generalizes the harmonic oscillator and Mathieu's equation, and its stability theory (Floquet theory) applies in celestial mechanics, accelerator physics, and solid-state physics.
