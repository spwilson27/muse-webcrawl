Thrust fault

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A thrust fault is a break in the Earth's crust across which older strata are pushed above younger strata, as the result of compressional forces. It is a type of reverse fault with a dip of 45 degrees or less. When the fault plane's dip is very shallow and the overlying block has been displaced many kilometres, the structure may form a nappe or overthrust sheet.

Geometry and mechanics

Thrust faults form where horizontal compression shortens the crust. The hanging wall moves up and over the footwall along a low-angle plane. Fault surfaces are rarely planar over long distances: they commonly step up through stratigraphic sections in a staircase pattern of flats (where the fault runs parallel to bedding) and ramps (where it cuts upsection). Folding above ramps produces characteristic fault-bend folds, and erosion of the uplifted block can isolate remnants of the thrust sheet as klippen.

Occurrence

Thrust faults are characteristic structures of fold and thrust belts at the fronts of mountain ranges built by continental collision or subduction-related compression — including the Alps, the Himalayas, the Appalachians and the Canadian Rockies. The Moine Thrust in north-west Scotland is one of the most famous thrust structures, where Precambrian rocks were pushed over Cambro-Ordovician strata. Large megathrust faults along subduction zones are the source of the world's greatest earthquakes, including the 2004 Sumatra and 2011 Tōhoku events.

Economic significance

Thrust faults can create structural traps for petroleum, stack reservoir rocks, and juxtapose older rocks against younger source rocks. Thrust-belt fields are important producers in regions such as the foothills of western Canada and the Zagros of Iran and Iraq.
