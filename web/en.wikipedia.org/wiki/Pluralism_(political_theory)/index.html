Pluralism (political theory)

Pluralism sees power as dispersed among competing groups.

Pluralism is a theory of politics holding that power in modern democracies is dispersed among a wide variety of organised groups — businesses, trade unions, professional associations, ethnic and religious communities, and public-interest groups — rather than concentrated in a single ruling elite. Public policy, on this account, emerges from bargaining and competition among groups within a framework of shared democratic rules, so that no single group consistently dominates.

Development

Pluralist ideas have roots in James Madison's analysis of factions in The Federalist No. 10 and in Alexis de Tocqueville's account of voluntary associations, and were developed by early twentieth-century thinkers such as Arthur Bentley and the English pluralists. The theory reached its classical form in post-war American political science, above all in Robert Dahl's Who Governs? (1961), a study of New Haven, Connecticut, which argued that different groups prevailed on different issues, and in Dahl's concept of "polyarchy." Charles Lindblom described policy-making as incremental "muddling through" among competing interests.

Key claims

  • Power is fragmented; resources (money, votes, expertise, organisation) are widely but unequally distributed.
  • Groups form freely; overlapping memberships moderate conflict.
  • Government acts as an arbiter and broker among group demands.
  • Potential groups and elections discipline leaders even where mobilised groups are few.

Criticism

Elite theorists such as C. Wright Mills, in The Power Elite (1956), argued that a cohesive network of corporate, military and political leaders dominates the United States, a thesis echoing Vilfredo Pareto's earlier theory of elites. Others charged pluralism with ignoring inequalities in group resources and organisation — E. E. Schattschneider quipped that "the flaw in the pluralist heaven is that the heavenly chorus sings with a strong upper-class accent" — and with neglecting "non-decisions" that keep issues off the agenda. Later scholars developed "neo-pluralist" accounts acknowledging the privileged position of business.
