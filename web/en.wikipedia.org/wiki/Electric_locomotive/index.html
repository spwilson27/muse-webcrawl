Electric locomotive

An electric locomotive is a locomotive powered by electricity from overhead lines, a third rail or on-board energy storage such as a battery or a supercapacitor. Locomotives with on-board fuelled prime movers, such as diesel engines or gas turbines, are classed as diesel-electric or gas turbine-electric and not as electric locomotives, because the electric generator/motor combination serves only as a power transmission system.

Electric locomotives benefit from the high efficiency of electric motors, often above 90%, and from regenerative braking, which returns energy to the supply during braking. They generally accelerate faster than diesel locomotives, produce no exhaust emissions at the point of use, and are well suited to busy commuter routes, steep gradients, and long tunnels. Their principal disadvantage is the capital cost of electrification infrastructure — catenary or third-rail systems, substations, and signalling immunisation.

History

The first known electric locomotive was built in 1837 by chemist Robert Davidson of Aberdeen, powered by galvanic cells. The first electric passenger train was presented by Werner von Siemens at Berlin in 1879, and electric traction spread rapidly in urban tramways and metros. Mainline electrification followed in tunnels and mountain districts — notably the Baltimore Belt Line in 1895 and the Simplon Tunnel route — and expanded widely in Europe and Japan during the 20th century. In Switzerland, coal shortages during the World Wars encouraged almost complete electrification of the national network.

Power supply

Common electrification systems include 1,500 V and 3,000 V DC, 15 kV AC at 16.7 Hz (used in Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Sweden, and Norway), and 25 kV AC at 50 or 60 Hz, now the standard for new high-speed and heavy-haul lines worldwide. Many modern locomotives are multi-system, able to operate under several voltages and frequencies for cross-border service. Current is collected by a pantograph from overhead catenary or by contact shoes from a third rail.

Types

Electric locomotives are built for passenger, freight, and shunting duties, ranging from small industrial units to freight designs of over 10,000 kW. Battery-electric locomotives are used in mines and on some metro systems, and hybrid designs combine external supply with onboard storage. In modern practice, much electric traction is provided instead by electric multiple units, in which the traction equipment is distributed along the train.
