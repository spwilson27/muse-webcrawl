English country house

An English country house is a large house or mansion in the English countryside, typically the principal residence of a landed family. Such houses were for centuries the powerhouses of rural society: centres of local administration, employment and patronage, set in landscaped parkland and supported by agricultural estates. Thousands were built between the sixteenth and early twentieth centuries in styles ranging from Tudor and Jacobean to Palladian, neoclassical and Gothic Revival.

Development

The country house emerged as fortified manor houses gave way to undefended residences after the Wars of the Roses. Elizabethan and Jacobean "prodigy houses" such as Hardwick Hall proclaimed the owner's status; after the Restoration, classical designs influenced by Inigo Jones and later by Palladio dominated, executed by architects such as William Kent, Robert Adam and Capability Brown, who reshaped the surrounding landscape. The Victorian era brought vast, service-intensive houses in eclectic styles.

The household

At its height the country house depended on a hierarchy of domestic servants — butler, housekeeper, cook, footmen, maids — often numbering in the dozens, with strict separation between family apartments, state rooms and the servants' wings and "below stairs" quarters. Social life revolved around shooting parties, balls and visits that could last for weeks.

Decline and survival

Agricultural depression from the 1870s, death duties from 1894, and the losses of two world wars undermined the economic basis of the country house. Well over a thousand were demolished during the twentieth century, a loss documented by the Victoria and Albert Museum's 1974 exhibition "The Destruction of the Country House". Survivors were sold, converted to schools, hotels and flats, or passed to the National Trust and English Heritage; many remain family homes opened to the public.

Examples

Notable houses include Blenheim Palace, Chatsworth House, Castle Howard, Harewood House, Waddesdon Manor, Petworth House and Knole.
