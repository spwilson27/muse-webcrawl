Adelbert Ames Jr. (August 19, 1880 – July 3, 1955) was an American scientist who made contributions to physics, physiology, ophthalmology, psychology and philosophy. He is best known as the inventor of the Ames room, a distorted chamber that creates a striking optical illusion of size, and for a series of demonstrations showing that perception is an active, assumption-driven process rather than a passive recording of sensation.

Life and career

Ames was the son of Adelbert Ames, a Civil War general and later governor and senator of Mississippi. He studied law briefly before turning to art and then to science, working first in optics and lens design; during the First World War he designed instruments for the military. From 1919 he was associated with the Dartmouth Eye Institute at Dartmouth College, where he conducted research on binocular vision, aniseikonia and the perception of space, and where he built his famous demonstration rooms in the 1930s and 1940s.

Perceptual demonstrations

Working with colleagues and students, Ames devised a set of demonstrations — including the Ames room, the rotating trapezoidal window (Ames window), the distorted chair, and the "Ames demonstrations in perception" later described by William H. Ittelson — intended to support the "transactional" theory of perception developed with Hadley Cantril and others. In this view, what a person perceives reflects unconscious assumptions drawn from past experience: when a room is secretly distorted so that its retinal image matches that of a normal rectangular room, observers assume the room is normal and consequently see people within it grow and shrink impossibly as they move.

Legacy

Ames's demonstrations became classics in the psychology of perception and remain standard teaching tools. His work influenced transactional psychology, ecological approaches to perception, and later constructivist accounts of visual experience.
