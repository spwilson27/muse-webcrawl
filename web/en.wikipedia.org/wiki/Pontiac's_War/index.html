Pontiac's War

Date: 1763–1766

Location: Great Lakes and Ohio Country, North America

Result: Military stalemate; Royal Proclamation of 1763

Pontiac's War, also called Pontiac's Conspiracy or Pontiac's Rebellion, was launched in 1763 by a confederation of Native American peoples of the Great Lakes, Illinois Country and Ohio Country against British rule following Britain's victory in the French and Indian War. It is named for the Ottawa leader Pontiac, the most prominent of its many leaders.

Causes

After France ceded its North American claims to Britain in 1763, British commander Jeffery Amherst imposed policies the Native nations found offensive: he restricted the sale of guns and ammunition, ended the French practice of gift-giving that underpinned diplomacy, and tolerated expanding settler encroachment. Religious revival led by the Lenape prophet Neolin, who preached rejection of European goods and ways, gave the resistance an ideological dimension.

Course of the war

Beginning with Pontiac's siege of Fort Detroit in May 1763, Native forces captured eight British forts — including Fort Sandusky, Fort Michilimackinac (taken by stratagem during a game of lacrosse) and Fort Pitt's satellite posts — and besieged Fort Pitt and Fort Niagara. British expeditions under Henry Bouquet relieved Fort Pitt after the Battle of Bushy Run. The war ended in negotiated settlements by 1766, as the confederacy fragmented and Pontiac made peace at Oswego.

Consequences

Although the British could not defeat the uprising outright, the war prompted the Royal Proclamation of 1763, which reserved lands west of the Appalachians for Native peoples — a measure that angered colonists and became one of the grievances leading toward the American Revolution. During the siege of Fort Pitt, British officers discussed — and on one documented occasion attempted — giving smallpox-infected blankets to Native envoys, an early recorded instance of attempted biological warfare.
