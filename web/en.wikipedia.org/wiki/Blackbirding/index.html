Blackbirding is the coercive recruitment — by deception or kidnapping — of Pacific Islanders to work as labourers on plantations, mainly in Queensland, Australia, and Fiji, during the second half of the nineteenth century. The term also covers the earlier seizure of Islanders for the guano islands and plantations of Peru. Although some Islanders signed on willingly, many were tricked aboard ships or taken by force, and the trade is now widely regarded as a form of slavery.

The Queensland trade

The trade began in 1863 when planter Robert Towns brought 67 Islanders to work his cotton plantation near Brisbane. Demand from the sugar industry drove expansion: between 1863 and 1904, roughly 62,000 South Sea Islanders were brought to Queensland, most from the Solomon Islands and Vanuatu (then the New Hebrides). Labourers, called "Kanakas," worked three-year terms for low wages under harsh conditions, with a high death rate from disease and overwork.

Regulation and end

Scandals — including the 1871 voyage of the brig Carl, whose crew murdered dozens of Islanders — prompted the British Pacific Islanders Protection Acts of 1872 and 1875 and the posting of a High Commissioner for the Western Pacific, but abuses continued. The trade ended with federation: the Pacific Island Labourers Act 1901, part of the White Australia policy, halted recruitment in 1904 and ordered the deportation of most Islanders by 1906, though some were exempted and remained.

Legacy

Descendants of those who stayed form the Australian South Sea Islander community, formally recognised by the Australian government in 1994 and by Queensland in 2000. The community continues to seek recognition of the trade's injustices.
