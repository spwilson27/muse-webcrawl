Dysentery (from Greek dys-, "bad", and enteron, "intestine") is an inflammatory and infectious condition of the intestine, especially the colon, that results in severe diarrhoea containing blood and mucus, accompanied by abdominal pain, fever and a painful urge to defecate (tenesmus). In extreme cases it can be fatal, particularly in young children, the elderly and the malnourished.

Causes

The two classic forms are bacillary dysentery, or shigellosis, caused by bacteria of the genus Shigella — of which Shigella dysenteriae type 1 causes the most severe, epidemic disease — and amoebic dysentery (amoebiasis), caused by the protozoan parasite Entamoeba histolytica, which can also produce liver abscesses. Other organisms, including Campylobacter and certain E. coli, can cause dysentery-like illness. Transmission is by the faecal–oral route through contaminated water, food and hands, flourishing where sanitation is poor.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on stool examination and culture or molecular tests to distinguish bacterial from amoebic infection — an important distinction, since amoebic disease requires specific antiparasitic drugs such as metronidazole. The foundation of treatment is replacement of lost fluids and electrolytes, usually oral rehydration therapy; antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin or azithromycin are used for shigellosis, though drug resistance is increasing.

History and epidemiology

Known historically as the "bloody flux", dysentery has devastated armies and cities from antiquity through the nineteenth century, killing more soldiers in many wars than combat did. It remains a major cause of childhood mortality in low-income settings; the World Health Organization estimates that diarrhoeal diseases, including dysentery, kill hundreds of thousands of children under five each year, most preventable with clean water, sanitation and hygiene.
