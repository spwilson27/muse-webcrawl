Hyperconjugation

In organic chemistry, hyperconjugation (or σ-conjugation) refers to the delocalization of electrons from a filled σ orbital — usually a C–H or C–C bond — into an adjacent empty or partially filled p orbital, π* antibonding orbital, or σ* orbital, producing a stabilizing interaction. The concept was developed by Robert S. Mulliken in the 1930s and 1940s and is closely related to the Baker–Nathan effect, the observed order of electron release by alkyl groups in certain reactions.

Applications

Hyperconjugation is invoked to explain several classic observations:

  • Carbocation stability: the stability order tertiary > secondary > primary carbocations, because more adjacent C–H bonds can donate into the empty p orbital.
  • Alkene stability: more substituted alkenes have lower heats of hydrogenation (Zaitsev's rule correlates).
  • The anomeric effect: the preference of electronegative substituents at the anomeric carbon of sugars for the axial orientation, explained by n → σ* donation (sometimes called negative hyperconjugation).
  • The gauche effect and staggered conformations of alkanes, where σ → σ* donation favors staggered over eclipsed geometries.
  • Shortened and strengthened bonds adjacent to π systems and unusual rotational barriers.

Interpretation

Modern computational descriptions frame hyperconjugation as donor–acceptor orbital interactions quantifiable by natural bond orbital (NBO) analysis. There has been long-standing debate over the relative importance of hyperconjugation versus steric and electrostatic effects — for example, in explaining the rotational barrier of ethane, where both hyperconjugative stabilization of the staggered form and steric repulsion arguments have been advanced. "Negative hyperconjugation" describes donation from a filled p or lone-pair orbital into an adjacent σ* orbital, important in the chemistry of carbanions and in the anomeric effect.
