A C corporation, under United States federal tax law, is any corporation that is taxed separately from its owners. It is distinguished from an S corporation, which generally allows income to pass through to shareholders for tax purposes. The designation derives from Subchapter C of Chapter 1 of the Internal Revenue Code, which governs the taxation of corporations.

Most large publicly traded US companies are C corporations.

Taxation

A C corporation pays corporate income tax on its profits at the entity level — a flat 21% federal rate since the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017. When profits are distributed as dividends, shareholders pay tax on them again, the so-called "double taxation" of corporate income. In return, C corporations face no limits on the number or type of shareholders, may issue multiple classes of stock, and can retain earnings for reinvestment.

Formation and use

Corporations are created under state law (Delaware being the most popular state of incorporation), and most are C corporations by default unless they elect S status and meet its restrictions. Venture-backed startups almost universally use the C corporation form because institutional investors prefer it, and founders may benefit from the qualified small business stock exclusion under Section 1202.
