Johan de Witt (24 September 1625 – 20 August 1672) was a Dutch statesman who, as Grand Pensionary of Holland, was the de facto leader of the Dutch Republic for nearly two decades during the first stadtholderless period. A skilled mathematician and administrator, he led the Republic through the first two Anglo-Dutch Wars and oversaw its commercial golden age. He was murdered, together with his brother Cornelis, by an Orangist mob in The Hague during the Rampjaar (disaster year) of 1672.

Early life

De Witt was born into a prominent Dordrecht regent family and studied law and mathematics at Leiden University, publishing an early treatise on geometry. He became pensionary of Dordrecht in 1650 and, aged only 27, Grand Pensionary of Holland in 1653 — the province that dominated the Republic's finances and policy.

Leadership

De Witt's domestic aim was to preserve republican government by the regent class and to exclude the House of Orange from the stadtholderate. He secured the Act of Seclusion (1654), secretly promising England that the young William of Orange would never become stadtholder. He reformed Dutch finances, built up the navy under admirals such as Michiel de Ruyter, and achieved a famous victory in the Second Anglo-Dutch War with the Raid on the Medway (1667). He also made lasting contributions to actuarial science, writing an early treatise on life annuities.

Downfall and murder

In 1672 the Republic was simultaneously attacked by France, England, Münster and Cologne. Public fury turned on de Witt; William III was made stadtholder, de Witt resigned, and Cornelis de Witt was arrested on a dubious treason charge. On 20 August 1672 Johan went to visit his brother in prison in The Hague, where a mob seized both brothers, shot and beat them to death, and mutilated their bodies — one of the most notorious political murders in Dutch history.
