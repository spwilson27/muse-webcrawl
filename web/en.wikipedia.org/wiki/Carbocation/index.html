Carbocation

Carbocation
DefinitionAn ion with a positively charged carbon atom
GeometryTrigonal planar (sp²) at the cationic centre
Stability orderTertiary > secondary > primary > methyl

A carbocation is a molecule in which a carbon atom bears a positive charge and has only six valence electrons, typically with three bonds and an empty p orbital in a trigonal planar (sp²) arrangement. Carbocations are important reactive intermediates in organic chemistry, central to mechanisms such as SN1 and E1 reactions, electrophilic additions and Wagner–Meerwein rearrangements.

Stability

Carbocation stability increases with alkyl substitution — tertiary > secondary > primary > methyl — because of electron donation by induction and hyperconjugation from adjacent C–H and C–C bonds. Resonance stabilisation is even stronger: allyl and benzyl cations, and cations adjacent to oxygen or nitrogen lone pairs, are especially stabilised. Vinyl and aryl cations are highly unstable. Carbocations frequently rearrange by hydride or alkyl shifts to give more stable cations.

History and observation

Carbocations were proposed as intermediates in the early 20th century (notably in the work of Meerwein and Whitmore). Long-lived carbocations in superacid media were directly observed by George Olah using NMR spectroscopy, work recognised by the 1994 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. "Non-classical" carbocations such as the 2-norbornyl cation, in which the charge is delocalised over a bridged structure, were the subject of a famous decades-long controversy.
