Yahweh

Yahweh was the national god of the ancient kingdoms of Israel and Judah, and is the God of the Hebrew Bible, from whom the God of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam is understood to descend. The name is conventionally reconstructed from the four-letter theonym YHWH (יהוה), the Tetragrammaton, with vowel points added from the Masoretic text's Adonai to give the scholarly reconstruction "Yahweh"; the name's original pronunciation is lost. Biblical tradition describes Yahweh revealing the name to Moses from the burning bush: "I AM THAT I AM" (ehyeh asher ehyeh, Exodus 3:14).

Origins and development

Scholars place Yahweh's earliest attested associations with the southern regions of Seir and Midian — Egyptian texts of the Late Bronze Age mention "the Shasu of Yhw". In early Israel Yahweh appears as a storm god and divine warrior, gradually absorbing attributes of El, the aged patriarch god of the Canaanite pantheon, with whom he came to be identified ("El Elyon", God Most High). Archaeological finds such as the Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions ("Yahweh and his Asherah") and the Khirbet el-Qom inscriptions suggest that, before the exilic period, some Israelites associated Yahweh with a consort, Asherah.

During and after the Babylonian exile (6th century BCE), Judahite religion moved decisively toward monotheism: Second Isaiah declares Yahweh the sole god, and the post-exilic Temple establishment under Ezra and Nehemiah consolidated an exclusive Yahwism that became Judaism. The Deuteronomistic History traces Israel's fate to fidelity or infidelity to Yahweh's covenant given at Sinai.

The name

By the Second Temple period, out of reverence, the name YHWH was no longer pronounced aloud, being substituted with Adonai ("my Lord") in reading — the origin of the written form "Jehovah" when medieval Christian scholars combined the consonants of YHWH with the vowels of Adonai. The Septuagint renders YHWH as Kyrios ("Lord"), a convention followed by most English Bibles.
