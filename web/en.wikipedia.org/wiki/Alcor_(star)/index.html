Alcor (designated 80 Ursae Majoris) is a binary star system in the constellation of Ursa Major. It is the fainter companion of Mizar, the second star from the end of the handle of the Big Dipper asterism. Mizar and Alcor together form a well-known naked-eye double star, used since antiquity as a test of eyesight.

Observational history

The ability to resolve Alcor from Mizar was traditionally considered a test of visual acuity, sometimes called the "Arab eye test". The name Alcor derives from the Arabic al-Khawar, meaning "the faint one". In 2009, astronomers discovered that Alcor itself has a companion, Alcor B, a red dwarf of about 0.3 solar masses, making the Mizar–Alcor system a sextuple star system overall.

Characteristics

Alcor A is an A-type main-sequence star somewhat hotter and more luminous than the Sun, with a mass of roughly 1.8 solar masses. It lies about 83 light-years from Earth and is a member of the Ursa Major Moving Group, a set of stars sharing a common motion through space and a common origin. Alcor and Mizar are separated by about one quarter of a light-year, and it remains debated whether they are gravitationally bound, though their shared proper motion and distance strongly suggest a physical association.

In culture

In Indian astronomy the pair represents the sage Vasishtha and his wife Arundhati, symbolising marital devotion; the pair is ritually shown to newlyweds. In Japanese folklore Alcor is associated with lifespan, and it was said that being unable to see it foretold death within the year.
