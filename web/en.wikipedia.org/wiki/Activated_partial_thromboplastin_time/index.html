The activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) is a blood test that characterises coagulation of the blood. It measures the time, in seconds, for a patient's plasma to clot after addition of a surface activator, partial thromboplastin (phospholipid) and calcium. The test evaluates the intrinsic pathway (factors VIII, IX, XI, XII and prekallikrein/HMWK) and the common pathway (factors II, V, X and fibrinogen).

Modern coagulation analysers measure the aPTT automatically from citrated plasma samples.

Method

Blood is collected into citrate anticoagulant, centrifuged to platelet-poor plasma, and incubated with an activator such as silica, kaolin or ellagic acid plus phospholipid; calcium is then added and the time to fibrin clot formation is recorded.

Interpretation

A prolonged aPTT may reflect factor deficiency (e.g., haemophilia A or B), von Willebrand disease, heparin therapy, lupus anticoagulant, or inhibitors. The aPTT is the standard test for monitoring intravenous unfractionated heparin, with therapeutic targets often expressed as a ratio of 1.5–2.5 times the control value. It is contrasted with the prothrombin time (PT/INR), which assesses the extrinsic and common pathways and monitors warfarin therapy.
