Denmark–Norway (Danish and Norwegian: Danmark–Norge) was a multi-national and multi-lingual real union consisting of the Kingdoms of Denmark and Norway, including the Norwegian dependencies of Iceland, Greenland, and the Faroe Islands, as well as the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein. The union endured, in its formal phase, from the 16th century until 1814.

History

Denmark and Norway had been joined in personal union since 1380 and formed part of the Kalmar Union with Sweden from 1397 to 1523. After Sweden's final secession under Gustav Vasa, Christian III reorganised the union in 1536–1537: Norway's council was abolished and Norway was declared a dependency of the Danish crown, though it retained its own laws and institutions and was later again recognised as a kingdom within the union.

The union was governed from Copenhagen, where the monarch resided and where the central administration, dominated by the Danish and German-speaking nobility, conducted affairs in Danish. Norway provided timber, fish, metals, and sailors, and the union maintained a common navy and foreign policy. The Danish East India Company and the Danish West India Company administered colonies in India (Tranquebar), the Caribbean (the Danish West Indies), and on the Gold Coast.

Dissolution

Denmark–Norway was drawn into the Napoleonic Wars on the French side after the British attacks on Copenhagen in 1801 and 1807. Defeated, Denmark was compelled by the Treaty of Kiel in January 1814 to cede Norway to Sweden. Norway briefly declared independence under Christian Frederick and adopted a constitution at Eidsvoll, but after a short war entered a looser personal union with Sweden that lasted until 1905. Denmark retained Iceland, Greenland, and the Faroe Islands.
