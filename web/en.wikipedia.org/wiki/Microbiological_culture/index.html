Microbiological culture

A microbiological culture, or microbial culture, is a method of multiplying microbial organisms by letting them reproduce in predetermined culture medium under controlled laboratory conditions. Microbial cultures are foundational to microbiology and are used to diagnose infectious diseases, study organisms, and produce substances such as antibiotics and enzymes.

Methods

Cultures may be grown in liquid medium (broth) or on solid medium, most commonly agar plates in Petri dishes. By streaking a sample across an agar surface, microbiologists can isolate individual colonies, each derived from a single cell, obtaining a pure culture of a single species. Selective and differential media favour particular organisms or make them distinguishable; enrichment cultures increase the numbers of fastidious organisms. Viruses and obligate intracellular bacteria cannot be grown on artificial media and instead require cell culture or embryonated eggs.

History

The techniques were developed in the late 19th century in the laboratory of Robert Koch: solid media (first gelatin, then agar, suggested by Fanny Hesse) and the Petri dish (invented by Julius Richard Petri) made it possible to isolate pure cultures, underpinning Koch's postulates and the germ theory of disease. Culture collections now preserve reference strains for research and industry.

Applications

Diagnostic laboratories culture clinical specimens to identify pathogens and test antibiotic sensitivity. Cultures are also used in food safety, environmental monitoring, biotechnology and the production of vaccines and fermented foods.
