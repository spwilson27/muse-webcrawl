Scotland's Great Trails

Scotland's Great Trails is the collective name for twenty-nine long-distance routes in Scotland, managed by NatureScot (formerly Scottish Natural Heritage). The trails are designated to a common standard, are waymarked with distinctive markers, and are intended for walking, though many sections are also suitable for cycling and horse riding. Together they run to more than 3,000 kilometres (1,900 miles) of paths across Scotland.

History and criteria

The first designated long-distance route in Scotland was the West Highland Way, opened in 1980. The network was formerly marketed as "Long Distance Routes" and administered under the Countryside (Scotland) Act. In 2010 the routes were relaunched under the "Scotland's Great Trails" brand by Scottish Natural Heritage, with additional routes — including long-distance paths managed by local authorities and community groups — admitted to the network if they meet criteria covering length (at least 25 miles), waymarking, path condition, services for walkers and promotion.

The trails

Well-known routes in the network include:

  • The West Highland Way (154 km), from Milngavie to Fort William, Scotland's most popular long-distance path.
  • The Great Glen Way (125 km), from Fort William to Inverness along Loch Ness.
  • The Southern Upland Way (344 km), a coast-to-coast route from Portpatrick to Cockburnspath, the longest trail in the network.
  • The Speyside Way, the Rob Roy Way, the Fife Coastal Path, the John Muir Way (215 km, opened 2014), the Hebridean Way, and the Cape Wrath Trail — the last widely regarded as one of Britain's toughest trails.

Access

Walkers benefit from Scotland's statutory right of responsible access, established by the Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003, which permits access to most land and inland water for recreation, subject to the Scottish Outdoor Access Code. The Great Trails network is promoted as a major asset for Scotland's tourism industry, attracting walkers from the UK and overseas.
