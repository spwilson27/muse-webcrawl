Kemalism (Turkish: Kemalizm, also Atatürkçülük) is the founding ideology of the Republic of Turkey, based on the principles and reforms of Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, the republic's first president. It is conventionally summarised in the "Six Arrows" (Altı Ok): republicanism, populism, nationalism, laicism (secularism), statism, and reformism (revolutionism).

The Six Arrows

Republicanism holds sovereignty to rest with the nation, expressed through a republic in place of the Ottoman sultanate, abolished in 1922. Populism asserts the equality of citizens before the law and rejects class privilege and theocracy. Nationalism defines Turkish identity in civic and cultural rather than ethnic terms and seeks national independence and unity—Atatürk's maxim was "Peace at home, peace in the world." Laicism mandates the strict separation of religion and state: the caliphate was abolished in 1924, religious courts and schools closed, and Islam placed under state supervision. Statism assigns the state a leading role in economic development where private capital is insufficient, producing state banks and factories in the 1930s. Reformism (or revolutionism) commits the republic to continuing modernisation and the defence of the reforms—including the Latin-based alphabet of 1928, the Swiss-derived civil code of 1926, and women's suffrage, granted for national elections in 1934.

History

The principles were adopted into the programme of the Republican People's Party in 1931 and written into the Turkish constitution in 1937; they remained in the constitutions of 1961 and 1982. The Turkish military long presented itself as guardian of Kemalist secularism, intervening in politics in 1960, 1971, 1980, and by memorandum in 1997. Since the 2000s the rise of the Justice and Development Party (AKP) has seen many Kemalist institutions curtailed, and the ideology's meaning and legacy remain contested in Turkish politics.
