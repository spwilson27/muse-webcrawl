The Book of the Dead is the modern name for an ancient Egyptian funerary text generally known in Egyptian as the Book of Coming Forth by Day. It is a collection of religious and magical spells, hymns and instructions intended to guide the deceased through the Duat (underworld) and into the afterlife. Rather than a single fixed book, it was a repertoire from which scribes selected chapters for individual manuscripts, typically written on papyrus and placed in the burial chamber or wrapped within the mummy's bandages.

Development

The Book of the Dead grew out of earlier funerary corpora: the Pyramid Texts of the Old Kingdom, inscribed inside royal pyramids, and the Coffin Texts of the Middle Kingdom. It emerged in a recognisable form around the beginning of the New Kingdom (c. 1550 BC), flourished through the Third Intermediate Period — when production of copies peaked — and continued in use until about 50 BC. In the Saite Period (26th Dynasty) the selection and ordering of spells became more standardised, producing the so-called "Saite recension" of about 192 numbered chapters in Karl Richard Lepsius's 1842 edition.

Contents and key spells

The spells cover protection against dangers in the afterlife, transformation into various animal and divine forms, preservation of the body, and the journey by the solar barque of Ra. The best-known section is Spell 125, the Judgment of Osiris: the deceased recites the "Negative Confession" before 42 assessor gods, declaring innocence of sins, while their heart is weighed against the feather of Ma'at. If the scales balance, the dead person is declared "true of voice" and admitted to the paradise of the Field of Reeds; if not, the heart is devoured by the monster Ammit. Spell 30B, inscribed on heart scarabs, implores the heart not to testify against its owner.

Production and famous copies

Copies range from deluxe illustrated papyri commissioned by wealthy officials to cheaper template versions with names inserted later. The Papyrus of Ani (c. 1250 BC, now in the British Museum) is the finest surviving example, noted for its detailed vignettes. The term "Book of the Dead" was coined by Lepsius from the name Egyptian tomb robbers reportedly gave such papyri — "kitab al-mayyitun", the book of the dead man.
