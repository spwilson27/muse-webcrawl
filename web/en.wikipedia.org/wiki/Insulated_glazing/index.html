Insulated glazing
Abbreviated IGU; commonly called double glazing or triple glazing.

Insulated glazing (IG) consists of two or more glass panes separated by a sealed space to reduce heat transfer across a building envelope. The assembly, called an insulated glazing unit (IGU), is most commonly double glazing (two panes) or triple glazing (three). The gap, typically 6–20 mm, is filled with dry air or a low-conductivity gas such as argon or krypton and is sealed at the edges with a spacer and sealants to keep out moisture; desiccant inside the spacer absorbs residual humidity.

Performance

Insulating performance is expressed as a U-factor (or U-value), the rate of heat transfer per unit area and temperature difference. A single pane has a U-value around 5.7 W/(m²·K); a standard double-glazed unit is about 2.8, and high-performance units with low-emissivity (low-E) coatings, gas fills, and warm-edge spacers reach 1.0 or below, with triple glazing lower still. Low-E coatings reflect long-wave infrared, improving winter insulation and, in solar-control variants, reducing summer heat gain. IGUs also reduce interior condensation and improve acoustic insulation, especially with laminated or dissimilar-thickness panes.

History and failure modes

Modern sealed IGUs were patented in the nineteenth century (Thomas Stetson, 1865) and commercialized in the 1930s–50s under names such as Thermopane. The characteristic failure is seal breakdown, permitting moist air ingress and fogging between panes; units are then replaced rather than repaired. Standards such as EN 1279 and ASTM E2190 govern durability testing. Vacuum glazing and aerogel glazing are advanced alternatives under development and niche use.
