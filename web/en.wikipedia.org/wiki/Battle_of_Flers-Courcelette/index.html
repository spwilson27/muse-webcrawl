Battle of Flers–Courcelette

The Battle of Flers–Courcelette was fought from 15 to 22 September 1916 during the Battle of the Somme in the First World War. British Fourth Army and Reserve Army troops, supported by the Canadian Corps and the New Zealand Division, attacked German defences between the villages of Flers and Courcelette in northern France. The battle is historically significant as the first in which tanks were used in warfare.

Background

After the costly attacks of July and August 1916, General Sir Douglas Haig planned a renewed offensive to break the German third position on the Somme. The attack would employ the new secret weapon — the tank — of which 49 Mark I machines were available, distributed among the attacking corps.

The battle

The assault opened at dawn on 15 September behind a creeping barrage. Of the tanks that started, many broke down or ditched before reaching the German lines, but several accompanied the infantry with dramatic local effect; one tank lumbered down the main street of Flers, prompting the famous report that "a tank is walking up the High Street of Flers with the British Army cheering behind it." The New Zealand Division captured Flers, Canadian troops took Courcelette with tank support, and advances of up to about 2,500 yards were made on parts of the front — among the deepest of the Somme offensive to that point. Fighting continued until 22 September as the British consolidated and repelled counter-attacks.

Aftermath

Allied casualties were heavy, on the order of 29,000 across the attacking armies. Although the tanks proved mechanically unreliable, their debut demonstrated the potential of armoured warfare and led to expanded production and, eventually, massed tank attacks such as Cambrai in 1917.
