Chinese studies

Chinese studies, commonly known as Sinology, is the academic study of China — its language, literature, history, philosophy, art, society and politics — primarily through Chinese-language sources. In China itself, the corresponding traditional discipline is called guóxué (國學, "national learning").

Origins

Western study of China began with 16th- and 17th-century missionaries, above all the Jesuits, whose letters, translations of Confucian texts and works such as Confucius Sinarum Philosophus (1687) shaped Europe's image of China. Matteo Ricci's mastery of classical Chinese and his accommodation strategy set an early model of linguistic, philological sinology. The first European university chair of Chinese was established at the Collège de France in 1814 for Jean-Pierre Abel-Rémusat.

Development

Nineteenth-century sinology, exemplified by scholars such as James Legge, who translated the Chinese classics into English, centred on philology and classical texts. In the 20th century the field broadened: archaeology, Dunhuang manuscript studies (following Aurel Stein and Paul Pelliot), social and economic history, and contemporary China studies expanded the discipline, especially in the United States after 1949, where area-studies funding created large China centres. Scholars such as Joseph Needham (Science and Civilisation in China) opened entirely new subfields.

Scope and methods

Modern Chinese studies combines language training with disciplines including history, literary studies, anthropology, political science and economics. Debate continues over the balance between classical philology and social-science approaches, and over the term "sinology" itself, which some regard as carrying Eurocentric connotations.
