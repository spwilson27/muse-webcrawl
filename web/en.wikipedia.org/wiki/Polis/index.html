Polis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Polis (Greek: πόλις, plural poleis) is the Greek word that referred to a member of the city-states of ancient Greece, but could also mean a city, a citizens' body, or an administrative structure. The polis emerged from the Greek Dark Ages and became the defining political unit of the Archaic and Classical Greek world; over a thousand poleis are attested, spread across mainland Greece, the Aegean, Ionia, Magna Graecia, and the Black Sea coasts.

Structure

A polis typically consisted of an urban center — often built around a fortified hilltop (the acropolis) and a public square (the agora) — together with its surrounding agricultural territory (the chora). Its defining feature was not size but citizenship: the polis was a community of citizens (politai) who governed themselves, however narrow or broad the franchise. Most poleis were small — populations of a few thousand were typical — while Athens, with perhaps 250,000–300,000 inhabitants at its height, and Syracuse and Corinth were exceptional.

Government

Poleis experimented with nearly every form of government known to political theory: monarchy, tyranny, aristocracy, oligarchy, and democracy. Aristotle's Politics systematically compared their constitutions. Features common to many poleis included a citizen assembly, a council, magistrates chosen by lot or election, and law courts. Sparta's dual kingship and mixed constitution, and Athens' direct democracy, represent two famous poles of the spectrum.

Society

Citizenship was generally restricted to free adult males born of citizen parents; women, resident foreigners (metics), and enslaved people — who made up a large fraction of the population — were excluded from political rights. The polis was also a religious community, bound by shared cults and patron deities, such as Athena at Athens.

Decline

The poleis retained local self-government under Macedonian domination after Chaeronea (338 BCE) and continued to flourish as civic entities under the Hellenistic kingdoms and the Roman Empire, though they lost independence in foreign policy. The ideal of the polis profoundly influenced Western political thought, from Aristotle and Plato through Renaissance republicanism to modern communitarianism.
