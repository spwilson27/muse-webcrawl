Allele

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An allele is a variant form of a given gene at a particular position (locus) on a chromosome. Diploid organisms inherit one allele from each parent at each locus; if the two are the same the organism is homozygous for that gene, if different, heterozygous. The combination of alleles is the genotype; the traits it produces, in interaction with the environment, are the phenotype — terms coined alongside 'gene' itself in 1909.

Mendel's experiments revealed that alleles can be dominant (expressed in a single copy, as with purple pea flowers) or recessive (expressed only when homozygous, as with white flowers). Many genes have numerous alleles in a population — the ABO blood group system has three common ones — and new alleles arise by Mutation. Allele frequencies changing over generations is precisely what evolution means in population genetics.

Effects

Alleles range from harmless variants (eye colour) to lethal recessives (Tay–Sachs) to protective ones: a single copy of the sickle-cell allele guards against malaria, while two copies cause disease — the classic case of balancing selection maintaining a harmful allele. The CCR5-Δ32 allele confers resistance to HIV.

Modern genetics

Genome-wide association studies hunt alleles statistically linked to diseases and traits; polygenic scores combine thousands of tiny allelic effects. In cancer, and in breeding and conservation, tracking alleles is the everyday currency of applied genetics.
