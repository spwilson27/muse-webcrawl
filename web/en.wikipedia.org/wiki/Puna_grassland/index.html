Puna grassland

The puna grassland ecoregion is a montane grassland and shrubland biome of the central Andes Mountains of South America. It encompasses the high-elevation plateau and slopes — the puna — lying between roughly 3,200 and 5,000 metres above sea level in Peru, Bolivia, Chile, and Argentina, above the treeline and below the permanent snowline of the Altiplano and surrounding cordilleras.

Climate and vegetation

The puna experiences large daily temperature swings, frequent night frosts, intense solar radiation, and a marked dry season. The northern, wetter puna receives up to 1,000 mm of rain a year, while the southern dry puna grades into salt flats and desert. Vegetation is dominated by tussock grasses such as ichu (Jarava ichu) and Festuca species, with cushion plants, rosette herbs, and shrubs such as tola. In wetter areas, high-elevation wetlands called bofedales support dense turf and are crucial dry-season grazing grounds.

Fauna

The puna is the home of the wild ancestors and relatives of the domesticated llama and alpaca — the vicuña and guanaco — which graze alongside domestic camelids herded by local people. Other characteristic animals include the Andean fox, puma, viscacha, the Andean mountain cat, and birds such as the Andean condor, Andean goose, puna ibis, and several species of flamingo on high saline lakes. Giant coots, horned coots, and the flightless Titicaca grebe inhabit plateau lakes.

Human use

Pastoralism of llamas and alpacas, and cultivation of hardy tubers and quinoa at lower margins, have shaped the puna for millennia. Overgrazing, burning, mining, and climate change threaten its wetlands and grasslands; a number of protected areas, including Lauca, Sajama, and Salinas y Aguada Blanca reserves, conserve parts of the ecoregion.
