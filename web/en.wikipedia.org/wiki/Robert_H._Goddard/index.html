Robert H. Goddard

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Goddard with the first liquid-fuel rocket, 1926.

Robert Hutchings Goddard (5 October 1882 – 10 August 1945) was an American engineer, professor and inventor credited with creating the first liquid-fuelled rocket. A physics professor at Clark University, he published his visionary 1919 monograph 'A Method of Reaching Extreme Altitudes', which was ridiculed by some newspapers for suggesting a rocket could work in the vacuum of space.

On 16 March 1926, at his aunt's farm in Auburn, Massachusetts, Goddard launched the first liquid-fuelled rocket: it flew for 2.5 seconds and rose 12.5 metres. Working with little funding — later supported by the Guggenheim family and championed by aviator Charles Lindbergh — he moved his tests to Roswell, New Mexico, and developed gyroscopic control, steerable thrust and multistage concepts that German rocketeers later used in the V-2. Under-recognised in life, he is now honoured alongside Konstantin Tsiolkovsky as a founder of modern rocketry; NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center bears his name.
