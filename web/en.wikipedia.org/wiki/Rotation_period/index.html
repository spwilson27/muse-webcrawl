Rotation period

In astronomy, the rotation period or spin period of a celestial object is the time it takes the object to complete one turn on its axis of rotation relative to the background stars (the sidereal rotation period). It differs from the object's solar day, which is measured relative to the Sun and includes the effect of the object's orbital motion.

Sidereal and solar periods

For Earth, the sidereal rotation period is about 23 hours 56 minutes 4 seconds, while the mean solar day is 24 hours: because Earth moves along its orbit while rotating, it must turn slightly more than 360 degrees for the Sun to return to the same position in the sky. For objects in prograde rotation the solar day is longer than the sidereal period; for retrograde rotators such as Venus it is shorter. Venus has a sidereal rotation period of about 243 Earth days (retrograde), yet a solar day of only about 117 Earth days. Mercury, locked in a 3:2 spin–orbit resonance, rotates three times for every two orbits, giving a solar day of 176 Earth days.

Measurement

Rotation periods of planets with visible surfaces are measured by tracking surface features; for gas giants, whose clouds move at different speeds at different latitudes, the period is defined by the rotation of the planet's magnetic field, generated in its interior. Radio emissions modulated by the magnetic field give precise values — about 9 h 55 m for Jupiter and 10 h 33 m for Saturn — while asteroid periods are derived from light curves as their irregular shapes vary in brightness. For the Sun itself, rotation varies from about 25 days at the equator to over 30 days near the poles (differential rotation).
