Asymptomatic

In medicine, a disease is considered asymptomatic if a patient is a carrier of the disease or infection but experiences no symptoms. A condition might be asymptomatic if it fails to show the noticeable symptoms with which it is usually associated. Asymptomatic infections are also called subclinical infections. The term is also used for subclinical changes detected by investigations, such as imaging or laboratory tests, before symptoms appear.

Importance

Knowing that a condition is asymptomatic is important for several reasons. Symptoms may develop later, so treatment or watchful waiting may be required. Some conditions, such as hypertension and hyperlipidemia, cause cumulative damage to the body over time despite causing no symptoms. An asymptomatic person may also be infectious and spread a disease to others without knowing it, as with many respiratory and sexually transmitted infections. Finally, a person's mental or medical problems may need to be treated even in the absence of symptoms.

Examples

Many conditions are frequently or typically asymptomatic. Examples include hypertension, many cases of type 2 diabetes in their early stages, glaucoma, coeliac disease, hereditary haemochromatosis, many infections such as herpes simplex, chlamydia, poliomyelitis (in most infections), hepatitis, HIV in its early stages, and a substantial proportion of COVID-19 infections. Cancers detected by screening programmes such as mammography and colonoscopy are often asymptomatic at detection, which is the rationale for screening.

Management

Management of asymptomatic conditions depends on the balance of benefits and harms of early treatment. Detection through screening can allow earlier, more effective intervention, but can also lead to overdiagnosis — the detection of abnormalities that would never have caused harm. Asymptomatic carriers of infectious diseases may be asked to take precautions to avoid transmission even though they feel entirely well.
