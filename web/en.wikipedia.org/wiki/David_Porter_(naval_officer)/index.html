David Porter (February 1, 1780 – March 3, 1843) was an officer in the United States Navy in the rank of captain and the honorary title of commodore. Porter commanded a number of U.S. naval ships, including the famous USS Constitution. He saw service in the Quasi-War with France, the First Barbary War, and in the War of 1812, during which, as captain of the frigate USS Essex, he made the first United States Pacific cruise and inflicted heavy losses on British whaling interests before being captured at Valparaíso in 1814.

Early service

Porter was born in Boston and went to sea as a boy, accompanying his father, a naval officer of the Revolutionary era, on voyages to the West Indies. Appointed a midshipman in 1798, he served in the Quasi-War against France aboard the frigate Constellation and the schooner Experiment, which he briefly commanded in action. During the First Barbary War he served in the Mediterranean squadron and was twice taken prisoner when the frigate Philadelphia ran aground and was captured off Tripoli in 1803; he remained a prisoner until 1805.

War of 1812 and the Essex cruise

Promoted to captain, Porter took command of the frigate Essex in 1812. After a successful Atlantic cruise, he rounded Cape Horn in early 1813 and began a celebrated campaign against British whalers in the Pacific, capturing numerous prizes, refitting at the Marquesas Islands (where he annexed Nuku Hiva to the United States, an action never ratified), and establishing the first American naval base in the Pacific. On 28 March 1814, blockaded at Valparaíso, Chile, Essex was attacked and captured by the British frigate HMS Phoebe and sloop HMS Cherub after a bloody fight in neutral waters. Porter returned home a hero.

Later career

After the war Porter sat on the Board of Navy Commissioners and in 1823 took command of the West India Squadron suppressing piracy in the Caribbean. Following an incident at Fajardo, Puerto Rico, where he landed men to demand an apology for the imprisonment of one of his officers, he was court-martialled and suspended. Porter resigned his commission and in 1826 accepted command of the navy of newly independent Mexico, serving until 1829. He later entered the diplomatic service, becoming United States chargé d'affaires to the Barbary state of Algiers and then minister resident to the Ottoman Empire, dying at Constantinople in 1843.

Family and legacy

Porter was the father of Admiral David Dixon Porter, a leading Union naval commander of the Civil War, and the foster father of Admiral David Farragut, the U.S. Navy's first admiral. Several U.S. Navy ships have been named USS Porter in honour of David Porter and his son.
