Faisal I bin al-Hussein bin Ali al-Hashemi (20 May 1885 – 8 September 1933) was King of Iraq from 1921 until his death in 1933. A member of the Hashemite family, he was briefly proclaimed King of Syria in 1920 before the French expelled him after the Battle of Maysalun.

Early life and the Arab Revolt

Faisal was the third son of Hussein bin Ali, Sharif of Mecca. During the First World War he became a leading commander of the Arab Revolt against the Ottoman Empire, working alongside T. E. Lawrence ("Lawrence of Arabia"). His forces captured Damascus in 1918, and he led the Arab delegation at the Paris Peace Conference of 1919.

King of Syria and expulsion

In March 1920 the Syrian National Congress proclaimed Faisal king of a united Syria. France, which had been granted the mandate for Syria under the Sykes–Picot Agreement, rejected the kingdom; after French forces defeated his army at Maysalun on 24 July 1920, Faisal was expelled and went into exile in Britain.

King of Iraq

Britain, holding the mandate for Mesopotamia and facing a costly revolt, offered Faisal the Iraqi throne. A referendum (of dubious reliability) endorsed him, and he was crowned in Baghdad on 23 August 1921. As king he balanced British influence against Iraqi nationalism, built up state institutions and an army, and negotiated the Anglo-Iraqi Treaty of 1930, which led to Iraq's admission to the League of Nations as an independent state in 1932.

Death and legacy

Faisal died of a heart attack in Bern, Switzerland, on 8 September 1933 and was succeeded by his son Ghazi. He is remembered as a unifying, pragmatic statesman who laid the foundations of the modern Iraqi state.
