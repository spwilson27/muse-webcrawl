Overview

The Dunkirk evacuation, codenamed Operation Dynamo, was the evacuation of Allied soldiers during the Second World War from the beaches and harbour of Dunkirk in northern France between 26 May and 4 June 1940. After German forces cut off British, French and Belgian troops in the Battle of France, some 338,000 men were lifted to England by a hastily assembled fleet of over 800 vessels — Royal Navy ships and the famous "little ships", civilian craft that crossed the Channel.

Course and significance

Planners had hoped to save perhaps 45,000 men in two days before the perimeter fell. Fierce rearguard fighting (including the French stand at Lille), Luftwaffe attacks hampered by weather and the RAF, and the calm sea made the "miracle of Dunkirk" possible; about 198,000 British and 140,000 French and Belgian troops escaped, though all heavy equipment was abandoned and some 40,000 French rearguard troops were captured. Churchill hailed the deliverance while warning that "wars are not won by evacuations". The episode became a founding myth of British resilience — the "Dunkirk spirit" — and has been repeatedly retold, including Christopher Nolan's 2017 film Dunkirk.
