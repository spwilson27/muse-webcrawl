Hydrometallurgy

Hydrometallurgy is a branch of extractive metallurgy that uses aqueous chemistry to recover metals from ores, concentrates, and recycled materials. It is contrasted with pyrometallurgy, which uses high temperatures. Hydrometallurgical processes are built from three main stages: leaching (dissolving the valuable metal into an aqueous solution), solution concentration and purification (for example by solvent extraction, ion exchange, or precipitation), and metal recovery (by electrolysis, cementation, or chemical reduction).

Leaching methods

Leaching may be performed in situ, in heap leaching of crushed ore piled on pads, in stirred tanks, or in pressure autoclaves (pressure oxidation for refractory gold ores). Lixiviants include sulfuric acid for copper and uranium oxide ores, cyanide solutions for gold and silver (cyanidation, patented in 1887 by MacArthur and the Forrest brothers), ammonia, and chloride systems. Bioleaching uses bacteria such as Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans to oxidise sulfide minerals, releasing metals at low cost.

Applications

Hydrometallurgy accounts for a large share of world copper (via leach–solvent extraction–electrowinning, SX-EW), zinc, nickel (including laterite high-pressure acid leaching), uranium, and essentially all gold production. It is increasingly important in recycling lithium-ion batteries and electronic waste, where aqueous processing can selectively recover lithium, cobalt, and nickel.
