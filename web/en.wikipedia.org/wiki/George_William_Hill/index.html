George William Hill

George William Hill (March 3, 1838 – April 16, 1914) was an American astronomer and mathematician. Working independently and largely in isolation on his father's farm in West Nyack, New York, he made fundamental contributions to celestial mechanics, in particular to the theory of the Moon's motion. He is regarded as one of the greatest American mathematicians of the nineteenth century.

Early life and education

Hill was born in New York City to the painter John William Hill and Catherine Smith Hill. He graduated from Rutgers College in 1859, where he studied under Theodore Strong. In 1861 he joined the staff of the American Ephemeris and Nautical Almanac office in Cambridge, Massachusetts.

Work on lunar theory

Hill's most famous work concerned the three-body problem and the orbit of the Moon. In his 1877–78 memoir on the part of the motion of the lunar perigee, he introduced the periodic orbit now known as Hill's variational orbit as an intermediate orbit for lunar theory, and derived an infinite determinant to solve for the motion of the perigee. This introduced the infinite determinant (the "Hill determinant") and Hill's differential equation — ÿ + θ(t)y = 0 — into mathematics, laying foundations later developed by Floquet theory and the stability theory of periodic solutions.

Hill sphere

The Hill sphere, the region around a celestial body within which it dominates the attraction of satellites despite the pull of a larger body it orbits, is named for him, based on his work on the restricted three-body problem and the associated zero-velocity curves.

Honours

Hill received the Gold Medal of the Royal Astronomical Society in 1887, the Damoiseau Prize of the Paris Academy of Sciences, and the Copley Medal of the Royal Society in 1909. He served as president of the American Mathematical Society from 1894 to 1896. The crater Hill on the Moon is named after him, as is asteroid 1642 Hill.
