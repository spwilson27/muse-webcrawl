Postcolonialism

Postcolonialism is the critical academic study of the cultural, political, and economic legacies of colonialism and imperialism, and of the continuing effects of colonization on colonized peoples and lands. Emerging in literary studies in the late 1970s and drawing on poststructuralism, Marxism, and decolonization movements, it examines how colonial power produced knowledge about the colonized world and how colonized peoples resisted, negotiated, and remade those representations. Foundational works include Edward Said's Orientalism (1978), Frantz Fanon's The Wretched of the Earth (1961), and the writings of Homi K. Bhabha and Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak.

Origins and development

Anti-colonial thinkers such as Aimé Césaire (Discourse on Colonialism, 1950), Albert Memmi, and Fanon supplied the movement's intellectual roots. Said's Orientalism argued that Western scholarship and art constructed a timeless, exotic, inferior "Orient" that legitimated imperial rule. The Subaltern Studies collective in India, and Spivak's famous 1988 essay questioning whether subaltern voices can be heard within dominant discourse, extended the critique. Bhabha's concepts of hybridity, mimicry, and the "third space" emphasized ambivalence and cultural mixing rather than pure binaries of colonizer and colonized.

Themes

Recurring themes include identity and cultural hybridity; language politics (Ngũgĩ's rejection of English for Gikuyu); nationalism and its limits; Eurocentrism in history-writing; and the persistence of colonial structures after formal independence — the terrain shared with decolonial theory and dependency theory.

Criticism

Critics from Marxist and liberal positions have charged postcolonial theory with textualism and obscurity, neglecting material and class analysis, homogenizing very different colonial histories, and (in Aijaz Ahmad's and Vivek Chibber's critiques) overstating the rupture with Enlightenment universalism.
