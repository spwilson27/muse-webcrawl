American Indian boarding schools, also known as Indian residential schools in the American context, were boarding schools established by the United States government and churches from the mid-19th to the mid-20th centuries to assimilate Native American children into Euro-American culture. Children were often removed from their families by force; at school their hair was cut, their languages and religions suppressed, and many experienced abuse, disease, and death.

History

The model was popularised by Army officer Richard Henry Pratt, who founded the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania in 1879 and summarised his philosophy as "kill the Indian in him, and save the man." Hundreds of schools followed. The 1928 Meriam Report condemned conditions and the policy of forced assimilation, and the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 began a slow reversal. Attendance nevertheless remained widespread into the 1960s and 1970s before most schools closed.

Legacy and investigations

Survivors and descendants describe intergenerational trauma from family separation and cultural loss. In 2021 the U.S. Department of the Interior under Secretary Deb Haaland launched the Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative; its reports (2022 and 2024) identified over 500 schools and nearly 1,000 documented deaths of children at them, and recommended a formal federal apology, which President Joe Biden delivered in October 2024.
