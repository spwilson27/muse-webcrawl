Ecoregions in Australia are geographically distinct plant and animal communities whose boundaries reflect climate, geology and evolutionary history across the Australian continent and Tasmania. Australia forms the bulk of the Australasian biogeographic realm, whose long isolation produced a highly endemic biota dominated by marsupials, eucalypts and acacias.

Classification

Two schemes are widely used. The World Wide Fund for Nature divides Australia into roughly forty terrestrial ecoregions, from the Queensland tropical rainforests and Cape York savannas of the monsoonal north, through the vast central deserts (Great Victoria, Gibson, Great Sandy and Simpson), to the Mediterranean mallee and kwongan shrublands of the southwest — a global biodiversity hotspot — and the temperate forests and alpine heaths of the southeast and Tasmania. For land management, Australian governments instead use the Interim Biogeographic Regionalisation for Australia (IBRA), which defines 89 bioregions subdivided into 419 subregions, and a parallel marine regionalisation (IMCRA).

Conservation

Many Australian ecoregions have been heavily cleared for agriculture, particularly the wheat-sheep belt woodlands and the brigalow lands of Queensland, and face pressure from altered fire regimes, invasive species and climate change. Ecoregion and bioregion frameworks are used to set reserve targets intended to build a comprehensive, adequate and representative national reserve system.
