James–Lange theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The James–Lange theory is a hypothesis on the origin and nature of emotions, developed independently by the American philosopher and psychologist William James and the Danish physiologist Carl Lange in the 1880s. The theory holds that emotions are the mind's perception of physiological changes in the body: an external stimulus first produces bodily responses — changes in heart rate, muscle tension, perspiration and so on — and the feeling of those changes is the emotion.

Statement of the theory

In his 1884 article "What is an Emotion?" and in The Principles of Psychology (1890), James argued against the common-sense sequence in which we see a bear, feel afraid, and then tremble. Instead, he proposed, we see the bear and our body reacts; our feeling of the bodily reaction as it occurs is the fear:

"We feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble, and not that we cry, strike, or tremble because we are sorry, angry, or fearful."

Lange, writing in 1885, developed a similar account emphasising the vasomotor system — changes in blood flow and circulation — as the seat of emotional feeling.

Criticism

The theory was famously criticised by Walter Cannon in the 1920s. Cannon pointed out that bodily changes are too slow and too undifferentiated to account for the speed and variety of emotional experience; that artificially induced arousal (for example by adrenaline injection) does not by itself produce a full emotion; and that animals with severed visceral connections still display emotional behaviour. Cannon and Philip Bard proposed instead that emotional experience and bodily response arise in parallel, in the Cannon–Bard theory.

Legacy

Although superseded in its strong form, the James–Lange theory continues to influence emotion research. The facial-feedback hypothesis, the two-factor theory of Schachter and Singer, and Antonio Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis all revive aspects of James's claim that bodily states contribute constitutively to emotional experience.
