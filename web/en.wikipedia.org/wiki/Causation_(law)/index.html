Causation (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Causation in law connects a defendant's conduct to the harm for which liability is sought. Common-law analysis splits into cause-in-fact and proximate (legal) cause. Cause-in-fact is tested principally by the “but-for” test (the harm would not have occurred but for the conduct), supplemented where multiple causes combine (the substantial-factor test; alternative liability in Summers v. Tice, 1948; and market-share liability in the DES cases, Sindell, 1980).

Proximate cause limits liability to consequences within the scope of the risk that made the conduct wrongful: the foreseeability debate framed by Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad (1928) — Cardozo confining duty to plaintiffs within the foreseeable zone of danger, Andrews arguing duty to the world with proximate cause as policy line-drawing. Civil-law systems developed parallel doctrines (adequacy theory; conditio sine qua non). Criminal law adds the year-and-a-day rule's abolition and intervening-act doctrines (a victim's refusal of treatment, a rescuer's injury — “danger invites rescue”). Causation becomes scientifically hardest in toxic torts and epidemiology-driven litigation, where courts police expert evidence (Daubert, 1993) and general versus specific causation.
