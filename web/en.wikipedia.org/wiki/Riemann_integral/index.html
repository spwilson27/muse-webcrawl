Riemann integral

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the branch of mathematics known as real analysis, the Riemann integral, created by Bernhard Riemann, was the first rigorous definition of the integral of a function on an interval. It was published by Riemann in 1854 in his habilitation thesis and, for many functions and practical applications, remains the integral taught in introductory calculus.

Definition

Let f be a function on [a, b]. Partition the interval into subintervals and, on each, choose a sample point ξi. The Riemann sum is Σ f(ξi) Δxi — the total signed area of rectangles under an approximation of the curve. If these sums converge to a single value as the partitions grow finer (the largest subinterval shrinking to zero), f is called Riemann integrable and that value is its integral, written ∫ab f(x) dx.

An equivalent formulation, due to Darboux, uses upper and lower sums formed from the supremum and infimum of f on each subinterval: f is integrable exactly when the upper and lower integrals coincide.

Which functions are integrable?

Every continuous function on a closed interval is Riemann integrable, as is every monotone function and every function with only finitely many discontinuities, provided it is bounded. Lebesgue's criterion sharpens this: a bounded function is Riemann integrable if and only if its set of discontinuities has Lebesgue measure zero. The Dirichlet function (1 on the rationals, 0 on the irrationals) is the standard example of a bounded function that is not Riemann integrable.

Properties and successors

The Riemann integral is linear, additive over intervals, and satisfies the fundamental theorem of calculus. Its limitations — difficulty with pointwise limits of sequences of functions and with pathological integrands — motivated the Lebesgue integral, which dominates modern analysis and probability, though the Riemann integral remains standard in physics, engineering and elementary teaching. Generalisations include the Riemann–Stieltjes integral and the Henstock–Kurzweil integral.
