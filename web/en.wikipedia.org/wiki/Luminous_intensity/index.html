In photometry, luminous intensity is a measure of the wavelength-weighted power emitted by a light source in a particular direction per unit solid angle, based on the luminosity function, a standardised model of the sensitivity of the human eye. Its SI unit is the candela (cd), one of the seven SI base units.

Definition

The candela is defined by fixing the luminous efficacy of monochromatic radiation of frequency 540×1012 Hz (wavelength about 555 nm) to 683 lm/W. Luminous intensity relates to luminous flux by Iv = Φv/Ω: a uniform point source emitting 1 lumen per steradian has an intensity of 1 candela. A common candle emits roughly one candela in all directions, hence the unit's historical name.

Relation to other quantities

Luminous intensity is the photometric counterpart of radiant intensity (watts per steradian). Illuminance on a surface falls with the square of the distance from a point source (inverse-square law) and with the cosine of the angle of incidence. Luminance — luminous intensity per unit area of a surface — is the related quantity relevant to perceived brightness and display specifications (colloquially, "nits").

Usage

Luminous intensity distributions (polar curves) are used to characterise lamps, LEDs, and luminaires, and are central to lighting design, automotive headlamp regulations, and signal visibility standards.
