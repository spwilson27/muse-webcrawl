Corporate culture

Corporate culture, also known as organizational culture, refers to the shared values, beliefs, assumptions, symbols, and practices that characterize an organization and guide the behavior of its members. It is often summarized as “the way things are done around here.” Culture operates at several levels: visible artifacts and behaviors, stated values and strategies, and deeper underlying assumptions that members take for granted.

Definition and levels

Organizational psychologist Edgar Schein proposed a widely used model in which culture consists of three levels. At the surface are artifacts — office layouts, dress codes, rituals, and language — which are easy to observe but hard to interpret. Beneath these are espoused beliefs and values, the stated goals and philosophies of the organization. At the deepest level are basic underlying assumptions: unconscious, taken-for-granted beliefs that actually determine how members perceive and react to events.

Typologies

Several frameworks classify cultures into types. The Competing Values Framework, developed by Robert Quinn and Kim Cameron, distinguishes clan (collaborative), adhocracy (creative), market (competitive), and hierarchy (controlled) cultures. Charles Handy popularized four types named after Greek gods: Zeus (power), Apollo (role), Athena (task), and Dionysus (person). Geert Hofstede's research, though focused on national cultures, strongly influenced the study of organizational practices and dimensions such as power distance and uncertainty avoidance.

Formation and change

Culture is shaped by founders, leadership behavior, hiring and socialization practices, reward systems, and the organization's responses to crises and critical incidents. Because assumptions become embedded over time, deliberate culture change is difficult and typically requires sustained leadership attention, changes to structures and incentives, and clear communication. Phenomena such as mergers and acquisitions frequently fail, in part, because of clashes between the cultures of the combining organizations.

Effects

A strong culture can improve coordination, employee commitment, and consistency of decision-making, but it can also produce rigidity, groupthink, and resistance to innovation. Research links aspects of culture to job satisfaction, performance, and ethical conduct; notorious cases such as the fraud at Enron are often cited as examples of cultures that rewarded aggressive risk-taking and misconduct.
