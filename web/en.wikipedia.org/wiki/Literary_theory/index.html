Literary theory is the systematic study of the nature of literature and of the methods for literary analysis. It asks what literature is, how texts produce meaning, and what roles authors, readers, language, and society play in that process. Since the 19th century, literary scholarship has split between the historical study of texts and a succession of theoretical "schools" that offer competing accounts of interpretation.

History

Theoretical reflection on literature is ancient: Aristotle's Poetics analysed tragedy, genre, and mimesis, and rhetorical and poetic treatises continued through the classical, medieval, and Renaissance periods. Modern literary theory emerged in the early 20th century with Russian formalism, whose concept of "defamiliarisation" (Viktor Shklovsky) treated literariness as an effect of devices rather than content, and with the New Criticism of the Anglo-American academy, which advocated close reading of the autonomous text.

Major schools

  • Structuralism (1950s–60s) applied Saussurean linguistics and Lévi-Strauss's anthropology to narrative, seeking the underlying codes (narratology, Propp's functions) that make stories intelligible.
  • Post-structuralism and deconstruction: Roland Barthes proclaimed "the death of the author"; Jacques Derrida's deconstruction showed texts undermining their own binary oppositions.
  • Psychoanalytic criticism reads texts through Freud and Lacan.
  • Marxist criticism, from Lukács to Terry Eagleton, relates literature to class, ideology, and modes of production.
  • Feminist and gender theory, including Judith Butler, examines the representation and construction of gender.
  • Postcolonial theory, founded in part by Edward Said's Orientalism (1978), studies literature's entanglement with empire.
  • New historicism (Stephen Greenblatt) and cultural materialism treat texts as embedded in the discourses of their time.
  • Reader-response and reception theory shift attention to the reader's role in producing meaning.

The "theory wars"

From the 1980s, "theory" became a flashpoint in the humanities: critics charged it with jargon, relativism, and politicisation, while defenders argued it exposed hidden assumptions of supposedly neutral reading. Debates over the canon, the Sokal affair, and the value of theory continue in altered form, now alongside newer approaches such as ecocriticism, cognitive poetics, and digital humanities.
