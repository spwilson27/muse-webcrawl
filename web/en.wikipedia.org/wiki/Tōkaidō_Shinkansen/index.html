The Tōkaidō Shinkansen (Japanese: 東海道新幹線) is a high-speed railway line in Japan connecting Tokyo with Shin-Ōsaka station in Osaka, via Nagoya and Kyoto. Opened on 1 October 1964, nine days before the Tokyo Olympic Games, it was the world's first high-speed railway and the original Shinkansen line. Operated since the 1987 privatisation of Japanese National Railways by the Central Japan Railway Company (JR Central), it remains the busiest high-speed line in the world, carrying on the order of 150–160 million passengers a year in recent decades and more than 6 billion passengers cumulatively since opening.

A Tōkaidō Shinkansen train passing Mount Fuji — one of Japan's iconic railway views.

History

By the 1950s the old narrow-gauge Tōkaidō Main Line, serving Japan's densest corridor — the Tōkaidō megalopolis between Tokyo, Nagoya, Kyoto, and Osaka — was saturated. Under JNR president Shinji Sogō and chief engineer Hideo Shima, Japan chose to build an entirely new standard-gauge line rather than widen the existing one. Construction began in 1959, partly financed by an $80 million World Bank loan. The line opened in 1964 with trains travelling at 210 km/h, cutting Tokyo–Osaka journey time from 6 hours 30 minutes to 4 hours (3 hours 10 minutes by 1965).

Services

Three service tiers operate, all stopping patterns using the same tracks:

  • Nozomi — the fastest, stopping only at Tokyo, Shinagawa, Shin-Yokohama, Nagoya, Kyoto, and Shin-Ōsaka; the journey takes about 2 hours 21 minutes. Not valid with the Japan Rail Pass.
  • Hikari — semi-fast, with a few additional stops.
  • Kodama — stops at all 17 stations.

Trains run at up to 285 km/h (since 2015), with extraordinary frequency — as many as 12–13 departures per hour each way at peak — and punctuality measured in average delays well under a minute per train. Rolling stock has evolved from the original 0 Series through the 300, 700, and N700 series; the N700S series entered service in 2020. Despite its speed and volume, the Tōkaidō Shinkansen has never had a fatal passenger accident in more than half a century of operation.

Route and significance

The line takes its name from the Tōkaidō, the historic coastal road of the Edo period, whose post towns (famously depicted in Hiroshige's Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō) the railway largely parallels. Its success demonstrated the viability of dedicated high-speed rail and directly inspired the French TGV and later high-speed networks worldwide. Capacity pressure on the route led JR Central to develop the Chūō Shinkansen maglev, whose first section between Tokyo and Nagoya is under construction.
