Melamine resin

Melamine resin
TypeThermosetting polymer
PrecursorMelamine + formaldehyde
PropertiesHard, durable, heat- and stain-resistant

Melamine resin or melamine formaldehyde (also shortened to melamine) is a resin with melamine rings terminated with multiple hydroxyl groups derived from formaldehyde. This thermosetting plastic material is made from melamine and formaldehyde by polymerization. In its butylated form, it is dissolved in n-butanol and xylene and used in surface coatings.

Melamine formaldehyde was developed in the 1930s following the discovery of melamine's reaction with formaldehyde, and it rapidly found use in moulding compounds, laminates, and adhesives. Because it cures to a hard, glossy, cross-linked network, it resists heat, moisture, scratching, and staining better than many other plastics of its era.

Uses

  • Laminates: decorative high-pressure laminates (such as Formica-type countertops) use melamine-impregnated paper.
  • Moulded goods: durable tableware, buttons, ashtrays, and electrical fittings.
  • Adhesives and resins: plywood glues and particleboard binders, often blended with urea formaldehyde.
  • Melamine foam: an open-cell foam used for insulation and as an abrasive cleaning sponge.
  • Surface coatings and textile finishes: wrinkle-resistant and flame-retardant treatments.

Properties and safety

Cured melamine resin is hard and stable, but it can release small amounts of formaldehyde and melamine, particularly when heated or exposed to acidic foods; regulators set migration limits for food-contact articles, and melamine tableware is generally not recommended for microwave use.
