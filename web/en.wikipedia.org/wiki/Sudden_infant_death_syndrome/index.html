Sudden infant death syndrome

Sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS), sometimes called cot death or crib death, is the unexplained death, usually during sleep, of an apparently healthy baby less than a year old. SIDS remains the leading cause of death in infants between one month and one year of age in developed countries, even though its incidence has fallen dramatically since the 1990s. A diagnosis of SIDS is made only after a thorough investigation — including a complete autopsy, examination of the death scene, and review of the clinical history — fails to reveal another cause.

Definition

SIDS is defined as the sudden unexpected death of an infant under one year of age, with the onset of the fatal episode apparently occurring during sleep, which remains unexplained after a thorough investigation. By definition it is a diagnosis of exclusion. Most cases occur between two and four months of age, and the great majority occur before six months.

Risk factors

Epidemiological studies have identified a consistent set of risk factors. The strongest modifiable factor is sleep position: infants placed to sleep on their stomachs (prone) or sides have a substantially higher risk than those placed on their backs (supine). Other risks include sleeping on a soft surface, bed-sharing (especially on a sofa or with an adult who smokes, has consumed alcohol, or is overtired), overheating, prenatal and postnatal exposure to tobacco smoke, prematurity and low birth weight, and being a boy — SIDS occurs slightly more often in males. Protective associations include breastfeeding, pacifier use at sleep time, room-sharing without bed-sharing, and up-to-date immunization.

Causes

No single cause has been established. The widely accepted "triple-risk model" proposes that SIDS occurs when three conditions coincide: a vulnerable infant (for example, with subtle abnormalities in the brainstem systems that control breathing, arousal, and autonomic function), a critical developmental period in homeostatic control, and an exogenous stressor such as prone sleeping or an upper-respiratory infection. Research has found abnormalities of serotonin signaling in the medulla of many SIDS infants, suggesting a failure of the protective arousal response to rebreathing or airway obstruction during sleep.

Prevention and the "Back to Sleep" campaign

Beginning in the early 1990s, public health campaigns — most famously the American Academy of Pediatrics' "Back to Sleep" campaign (now "Safe to Sleep") — advised caregivers always to place babies on their backs to sleep, on a firm, flat surface free of soft bedding, pillows, and toys. SIDS rates in countries adopting these recommendations fell by more than half within a decade, one of the clearest successes of modern preventive pediatrics. Current guidance also recommends supine positioning for every sleep, a bare crib or bassinet, avoidance of overheating, and smoke-free environments before and after birth.
