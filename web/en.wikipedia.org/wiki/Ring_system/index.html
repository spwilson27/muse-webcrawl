Ring system

A ring system is a disk or torus of dust and other small particles that orbits around an astronomical object, such as a planet, dwarf planet, or even an asteroid. The most famous and spectacular planetary rings are those around Saturn, but all four of the Solar System's giant planets possess ring systems of varying tenuousness.

Composition and structure

Planetary rings are composed largely of water ice particles, ranging in size from micrometres to metres, with smaller amounts of rocky material. Many rings lie within the planet's Roche limit, the distance within which tidal forces would tear a large moon apart — one leading theory holds that rings are the debris of moons or comets disrupted by tides, while another proposes they are leftover material from the formation of the planet that never coalesced.

Rings in the Solar System

Saturn's rings span up to 282,000 km across yet are only about ten metres thick in places. Jupiter's rings are faint dust tori replenished by material knocked off its small inner moons. Uranus has a system of narrow, dark rings discovered in 1977, and Neptune has five principal rings with unusual bright arcs. Beyond the planets, rings have been found around the centaur 10199 Chariklo, the dwarf planet Haumea, and the trans-Neptunian object Quaoar, whose dense ring lies surprisingly far outside its Roche limit.
