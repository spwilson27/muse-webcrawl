Bowel obstruction

SpecialtyGeneral surgery, gastroenterology
SymptomsAbdominal pain, vomiting, bloating, inability to pass gas or stool
CausesAdhesions, hernias, volvulus, tumors, intussusception, strictures
DiagnosisPhysical examination, abdominal X-ray, CT scan
TreatmentFluids, bowel rest, nasogastric decompression, surgery

Bowel obstruction, also known as intestinal obstruction, is a mechanical or functional blockage of the intestines that prevents the normal movement of the products of digestion. Either the small intestine or the large intestine may be affected. Signs and symptoms include abdominal pain, vomiting, bloating, and the inability to pass gas. Onset may be gradual or sudden, and the pain is often colicky — coming in waves as the bowel contracts against the blockage.

Causes

Causes of bowel obstruction include adhesions (bands of scar tissue), hernias, volvulus (twisting of the bowel), intussusception (telescoping of one segment of bowel into another), inflammatory bowel disease, tumors, strictures, foreign bodies, and diverticulitis. In the small intestine, adhesions from previous abdominal surgery are the most common cause, followed by hernias. In the large intestine, the most common cause is colorectal cancer, followed by volvulus and diverticular disease. Obstruction may also be functional rather than mechanical: in a condition called ileus, the bowel stops moving its contents without any physical blockage, typically after surgery or in severe illness.

Pathophysiology

Proximal to the obstruction, the bowel becomes dilated as gas and fluid accumulate. Swallowed air and bacterial fermentation add to the gas, while the bowel wall secretes fluid into the lumen instead of absorbing it. The resulting distension causes pain, vomiting, and large losses of water and electrolytes into the gut and through vomiting, which can lead to dehydration and shock. If the blood supply to a segment of bowel is compromised — strangulation — the wall may become ischemic and necrotic, leading to perforation, peritonitis, sepsis, and death. Strangulation is a surgical emergency.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on the symptoms and physical examination, which may reveal a distended, tympanitic abdomen and high-pitched or tinkling bowel sounds in early obstruction, or absent sounds in late obstruction. Plain abdominal radiographs classically show dilated loops of bowel with multiple air–fluid levels and little gas in the colon. Computed tomography (CT) is the most informative study: it typically identifies the location and often the cause of the obstruction, and shows signs of ischemia or closed-loop obstruction. Blood tests commonly show dehydration and electrolyte disturbances such as hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis from vomiting.

Treatment

Initial treatment is resuscitation with intravenous fluids, correction of electrolyte abnormalities, keeping the patient nil by mouth, and decompression of the stomach with a nasogastric tube. Many partial obstructions, especially those due to adhesions, resolve with this conservative management, sometimes aided by water-soluble contrast, which can be both diagnostic and therapeutic. Complete obstruction, signs of strangulation or perforation, closed-loop obstruction, and obstruction that fails to resolve require surgery, which may involve dividing adhesions, resecting a tumor or dead bowel, reducing a volvulus, or creating a stoma. Large-bowel obstruction from cancer may sometimes be bridged with an endoscopic stent before definitive surgery.

Epidemiology and prognosis

Bowel obstruction is a common cause of emergency hospital admission and abdominal surgery worldwide. Adhesive small-bowel obstruction is a lifelong risk after abdominal operations. Prognosis is good when treatment is prompt, but mortality rises substantially — to a quarter or more of cases in some series — when strangulation and bowel necrosis have occurred, particularly in elderly patients.
