The Wahhabi sack of Karbala occurred on 21 April 1802, when forces of the First Saudi State (the Emirate of Diriyah), led by Saud ibn Abdul-Aziz, attacked the Shia holy city of Karbala in Ottoman Iraq. The raiders plundered and desecrated the shrine of Husayn ibn Ali, grandson of the Prophet Muhammad, killed thousands of the city's inhabitants, and carried off immense plunder. The attack, which took place on the day of Ghadir Khumm commemoration as many residents had left the city, remains one of the most notorious episodes of the early Wahhabi movement's expansion.

Background

The followers of Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab, allied with the House of Saud, regarded Shia practices — pilgrimage to the graves of the Imams, intercession, and shrine veneration — as idolatry (shirk) warranting violent suppression. From their base in Najd, Saudi-Wahhabi raiders had repeatedly attacked the Shia cities of southern Iraq during the 1790s; Karbala, one of the holiest Shia sites and wealthy from pilgrimage, was a prime target, inadequately defended by the Ottoman Mamluk regime in Baghdad.

The attack

A force estimated at around 12,000 Wahhabi horsemen and camel riders approached Karbala and scaled or breached its weak defences. The attackers destroyed the dome and furnishings of Husayn's tomb, seized its treasures and the offerings accumulated there, and massacred inhabitants; casualty estimates in contemporary and later sources range from about 2,000 to 5,000 or more, including members of prominent clerical families. The raiders withdrew to Najd laden with plunder before Ottoman forces could respond.

Consequences

The sack shocked the Muslim world and hardened Shia hostility toward the Wahhabi movement; among Shia Muslims it entered collective memory alongside the original martyrdom of Husayn at Karbala in 680. It also alarmed the Ottoman Empire and Persia. Within a decade, Ottoman-Egyptian forces under Muhammad Ali and his sons invaded Arabia and destroyed the Saudi state at Diriyah in 1818. The shrine of Husayn was subsequently rebuilt with support from the Qajar shahs of Persia.
