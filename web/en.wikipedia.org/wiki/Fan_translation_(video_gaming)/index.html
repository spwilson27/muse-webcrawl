In video gaming, a fan translation is an unofficial translation of a video game by fans, made to render a game playable in a language in which it was never officially released. Fan translations are typically distributed as patches that must be applied to a game ROM image, and they flourished with the rise of console emulators in the late 1990s, when translation groups could extract game scripts, translate them, and re-insert the text using ROM hacking tools.

History

The scene coalesced in the mid-1990s around emulation websites and IRC channels, with groups racing to translate Japanese role-playing games that Western publishers had skipped. Early milestones included the English translations of Final Fantasy V (1997–98, by RPGe), Seiken Densetsu 3 (Trials of Mana, by Neill Corlett and others), and Tales of Phantasia. Translators had to overcome substantial technical obstacles — text encoded in custom formats, tile-based fonts without lowercase letters, limited string space, and Japanese kanji fonts with thousands of characters — leading to the development of tools for script extraction, table files, and assembly-level expansion of ROMs.

Notable projects

The most celebrated fan translation is that of Nintendo's Mother 3 (2008), led by Clyde "Tomato" Mandelin and a team of volunteers; released two years after the Japanese game, the patch was downloaded hundreds of thousands of times and is frequently cited in discussions of games denied official localisation. Other influential projects include Live A Live (later officially remade), Policenauts, Radical Dreamers, Fire Emblem titles, and visual novels such as Fate/stay night. Fan translation also extends to retranslation — redoing officially localised games whose original translations were censored, cut, or considered inaccurate.

Legal status and industry response

Fan translations exist in a legal grey area: patches contain no copyrighted game data and are distributed free, but producing and applying them generally requires copying a ROM, and rightsholders may issue cease-and-desist orders, as has occasionally occurred shortly before official releases. Publishers' responses vary from tacit tolerance to hiring fan translators or using fan work as evidence of demand; several cult games received official Western releases years after fan translations demonstrated an audience. Figures from fan translation have entered the professional localisation industry, and the boundary between fan and professional work has blurred with crowdfunding and official "by fans" localisations.
