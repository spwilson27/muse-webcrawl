The Great Pyrenees, known in France as the Chien de Montagne des Pyrénées or Patou, is a large livestock guardian dog breed from the Pyrenees mountains of France and Spain. Bred for centuries to protect sheep and goats from wolves and bears, it is distinguished by its massive size, thick white double coat, calm and watchful temperament, and double dewclaws on the hind legs.

History

White guardian dogs of this type have worked in the Pyrenees since at least the medieval period, living with the flocks in the high summer pastures and operating largely independently of the shepherd. The breed was adopted as a court dog at the French royal court in the seventeenth century under Louis XIV, who declared it the Royal Dog of France. Numbers declined with the disappearance of large predators in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, but the breed was preserved by fanciers — the first breed standard dates to the 1920s — and it was exported to North America in the 1930s, where it was recognised by the American Kennel Club in 1933. With the return of wolves to the Pyrenees, working Patous have again become common in French flocks.

Characteristics and temperament

Great Pyrenees are strongly built and heavily coated, with a weather-resistant outer coat and dense undercoat. They are typically calm, patient, and gentle with children and livestock, but independent and territorial, with a deep, frequent bark used to deter predators at night — traits of their guardian heritage. As working livestock guardians they are usually raised with stock from puppyhood so they bond to the flock. Health concerns include hip dysplasia, bloat, and bone cancer; typical lifespan is 10–12 years.
