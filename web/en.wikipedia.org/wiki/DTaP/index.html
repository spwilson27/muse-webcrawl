DTaP is a combination vaccine whose full name is diphtheria and tetanus toxoids and acellular pertussis vaccine. It protects children against three bacterial diseases: diphtheria, tetanus, and pertussis (whooping cough). The capital letters denote full-strength doses of the diphtheria (D) and tetanus (T) components intended for young children, while the lowercase "aP" indicates that the pertussis component consists of purified acellular antigens rather than whole inactivated bacteria.

History

The earlier DTP vaccine combined diphtheria and tetanus toxoids with whole-cell pertussis (wP), and was used from the 1940s. Although effective, whole-cell pertussis vaccine was associated with relatively frequent local reactions and fevers, which prompted the development of acellular pertussis (aP) vaccines in Japan in the 1970s and 1980s and their adoption in the United States during the 1990s. DTaP was licensed for the full childhood series in the US in 1996–1997, replacing DTP. Because protection from acellular pertussis wanes faster than from the whole-cell vaccine, adolescent and adult boosters (Tdap) and pertussis vaccination in pregnancy are recommended in many countries.

Schedule

In the United States, the CDC recommends five doses of DTaP for children at 2, 4, 6, and 15–18 months of age, and at 4–6 years, followed by Tdap at 11–12 years. Many other national schedules are similar. DTaP is also marketed in combinations with additional antigens, such as inactivated polio vaccine, hepatitis B, and Haemophilus influenzae type b (for example, hexavalent DTaP-IPV-Hib-HepB vaccines).

Safety and effectiveness

Common reactions are mild: pain and swelling at the injection site, low-grade fever, and fussiness. Serious adverse events are rare. DTaP is highly effective in the first years after vaccination, but immunity against pertussis diminishes over time, contributing to resurgences of whooping cough and to policy debates about booster strategies.
