Bézout's theorem

Bézout's theorem is a statement in algebraic geometry concerning the number of common zeros of two projective plane curves. It asserts that the number of intersection points of two algebraic curves of degrees m and n, counted with multiplicity, over an algebraically closed field, and with no common component, equals the product m·n.

Statement

Let C and D be projective plane curves of degrees m and n with no common component. Then the sum of the intersection multiplicities of C and D at all their common points equals m·n. For example, a line (m = 1) meets a conic (n = 2) in two points counted with multiplicity, and two conics meet in four points.

History

Special cases were known to Isaac Newton and earlier geometers. Étienne Bézout stated the general result in his Théorie générale des équations algébriques (1779), though his proof was incomplete by modern standards; rigorous proofs came with the development of intersection theory in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Generalizations

The theorem generalizes to intersections of hypersurfaces in projective space, where the number of points equals the product of the degrees, and underlies enumerative geometry and intersection theory. In affine space, the count may drop because intersections can occur at infinity, which the projective statement captures exactly.
