Human rights in Eritrea are widely regarded by international observers as among the most restricted in the world. Eritrea is a one-party state ruled by the People's Front for Democracy and Justice (PFDJ) under President Isaias Afwerki since independence from Ethiopia in 1993. No national elections have been held since independence, the 1997 constitution has never been implemented, and independent media have been banned since a government crackdown in September 2001.

Overview

A United Nations Commission of Inquiry reported in 2015 and 2016 that it had found evidence of crimes against humanity in Eritrea since 1991, including enslavement, enforced disappearance, arbitrary detention, torture and murder. The Eritrean government rejects these findings and disputes the mandate of UN special rapporteurs on the country.

National service

All Eritreans are subject to national service, nominally lasting 18 months, which in practice has often been extended indefinitely. The UN Commission of Inquiry concluded that the national service system amounts to enslavement of the population. Indefinite conscription is a major driver of emigration; Eritreans have for years been among the largest groups seeking asylum in Europe and elsewhere relative to the country's size.

Freedom of the press

In 2001 the government closed all independent newspapers and arrested prominent journalists, many of whom have not been seen since; the fate of several, including the Swedish-Eritrean journalist Dawit Isaak, remains unknown. Reporters Without Borders has repeatedly ranked Eritrea at or near the bottom of its World Press Freedom Index.

Freedom of religion

The state recognises four religious groups: the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church, the Roman Catholic Church, the Evangelical Lutheran Church of Eritrea, and Sunni Islam. Members of unregistered denominations, including Jehovah's Witnesses and many Pentecostal and evangelical Christians, face arrest and detention.

Political prisoners

In September 2001, eleven senior officials who had called for democratic reforms — the so-called G-15 — were arrested and have been held incommunicado without trial. Their whereabouts and condition remain unconfirmed.
