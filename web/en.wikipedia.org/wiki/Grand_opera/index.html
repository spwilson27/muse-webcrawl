Grand opera

Grand opera is a genre of 19th-century opera characterised by large-scale casts, orchestras, and staging, spectacular visual effects, and plots drawn from serious — often historical — subjects involving heroic figures and national or religious conflict. The term is most closely associated with the Paris Opéra, where the genre flourished between about 1828 and 1850.

Characteristics

Grand operas are typically in four or five acts, are sung throughout with no spoken dialogue, and include an extended ballet — traditionally placed in the second or third act to accommodate Parisian subscribers who arrived late. They feature massed choruses, crowd scenes, processions, and elaborate stage machinery, and their libretti, often by Eugène Scribe, juxtapose private love stories with public, historical upheaval.

Principal works

The genre was effectively launched by Auber's La muette de Portici (1828) and Rossini's Guillaume Tell (1829), and was perfected by Giacomo Meyerbeer in Robert le diable (1831), Les Huguenots (1836), Le prophète (1849), and the posthumous L'Africaine (1865). Other landmarks include Halévy's La Juive (1835) and Donizetti's later Paris works. Verdi's Les vêpres siciliennes (1855) and Don Carlos (1867) were written to Parisian grand-opera specifications.

Influence

Though grand opera fell from fashion after 1850 and its Meyerbeerian exemplars were long neglected, its emphasis on spectacle, historical pageantry, and the integration of music and stage action profoundly influenced Wagner, Verdi's mature style, and later forms of large-scale music theatre and film.
