In statistics, a simple random sample (or SRS) is a subset of individuals (a sample) chosen from a larger set (a population) in which a subset of individuals is chosen randomly, all with the same probability. In SRS, each subset of k individuals has the same probability of being chosen as any other subset of k individuals. A simple random sample is an unbiased sampling technique, and it is a common method used in data collection.

Definition

Formally, a simple random sample of size n from a finite population of size N is a set of n units selected such that every one of the possible samples of size n has an equal chance of being selected. Sampling may be done with replacement, allowing the same unit to appear more than once, or without replacement, which is the more common practice for finite populations.

P(selecting any particular unit) = n / N

Generating a simple random sample

Simple random samples are generated using random number tables, mechanical devices such as lottery draws, or, most commonly today, computer algorithms based on pseudorandom number generators. Fisher and Yates published widely used tables of random digits in 1938, and the RAND Corporation's A Million Random Digits with 100,000 Normal Deviates (1955) was a landmark resource before computers became ubiquitous.

Advantages and disadvantages

The chief advantage of SRS is that it eliminates selection bias: estimates derived from a simple random sample are unbiased, and the sampling distribution of statistics such as the sample mean is well understood, allowing standard errors and confidence intervals to be computed directly. Disadvantages include the need for a complete sampling frame listing every population member, and the possibility — purely by chance — of a sample that poorly represents important subgroups. For heterogeneous populations, stratified sampling may be more efficient.

Distinction from other methods

Simple random sampling differs from systematic sampling, in which units are selected at a fixed interval from a randomly started list, and from cluster sampling, in which groups rather than individuals are sampled. It also differs from convenience sampling and quota sampling, which are non-probability methods.

Applications

SRS is a foundation of survey methodology, quality control, clinical trials, and audit sampling. Many more complex probability designs can be understood as modifications or combinations of simple random samples drawn within strata or clusters.
