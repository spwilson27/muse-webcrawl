The Global Maritime Distress and Safety System (GMDSS) is an internationally agreed-upon set of procedures, types of equipment, and communication protocols used to increase safety and make it easier to rescue distressed ships, boats, and aircraft. Established under amendments to the SOLAS Convention adopted by the International Maritime Organization in 1988 and fully in force from February 1, 1999, it replaced the old Morse-code-based distress system and ended the requirement for ships to maintain a manual radio watch on 500 kHz.

Components

  • Emergency position-indicating radio beacons (EPIRBs): satellite-detected distress beacons operating through the COSPAS-SARSAT system on 406 MHz.
  • Digital selective calling (DSC): automated alerting on VHF, MF, and HF bands.
  • NAVTEX: automatic reception of maritime safety information and weather warnings.
  • Inmarsat / recognized mobile satellite services: ship-to-shore alerting and communications, including Inmarsat-C and Fleet Safety.
  • Search and rescue transponders (SART) and AIS-SART: locating devices for survival craft.
  • Survival craft radios: portable VHF sets for lifeboats and rafts.

Sea areas

Equipment requirements scale with four defined sea areas: A1 (within VHF coast-station range), A2 (within MF range), A3 (within satellite coverage, roughly between 70°N and 70°S), and A4 (the polar regions, requiring HF). A core principle is functional redundancy: a ship must be able to send a distress alert by at least two separate means, and shore-based rescue coordination centers, rather than nearby ships alone, are responsible for organizing the response.
