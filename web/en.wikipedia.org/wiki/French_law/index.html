French law

France's highest courts sit in Paris.

French law is the legal system of France, a civil law system in the Romano-Germanic tradition in which statute and codified law are the primary sources of law and judicial precedent plays a secondary role. It has been one of the most influential legal systems in the world, serving as a model for much of Europe, Latin America, Africa and parts of Asia, largely through the export of the Napoleonic Code of 1804.

Structure

A defining feature is the dual court system. The judicial courts, headed by the Court of Cassation (Cour de cassation), handle civil and criminal matters, while the administrative courts, headed by the Council of State (Conseil d'État), handle disputes between citizens and public authorities; the Tribunal des conflits resolves jurisdictional clashes between the two orders. Since 1958 the Constitutional Council (Conseil constitutionnel) reviews the constitutionality of legislation.

Codes

French law is organised into codes, including the Civil Code (Code civil), Penal Code, Code of Civil and Criminal Procedure, Commercial Code, Labour Code and Administrative Justice Code. Private law divides broadly into civil and commercial law; criminal law emphasises an inquisitorial procedure in which an investigating judge (juge d'instruction) directs inquiries in serious cases. Since the mid-20th century, European Union law and the European Convention on Human Rights have also become major sources shaping French law.

Legal profession

The profession is divided among advocates (avocats), notaries (notaires), who authenticate legal acts, and a career judiciary trained at the National School for the Judiciary in Bordeaux.
