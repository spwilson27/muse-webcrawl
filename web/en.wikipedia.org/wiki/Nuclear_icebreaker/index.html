Nuclear icebreaker

A nuclear icebreaker is an icebreaker ship powered by nuclear reactors. The significant advantage of nuclear propulsion is that refuelling is only required every few years, giving the vessel effectively unlimited range and sustained full power — valuable for breaking thick Arctic ice far from ports. All nuclear icebreakers built to date have been operated by Russia (and previously the Soviet Union) for service along the Northern Sea Route.

History

The first nuclear icebreaker, Lenin, was launched by the Soviet Union in 1957 and entered service in 1959. It was followed by the Arktika class, the first surface ship to reach the North Pole (Arktika, 1977), and the shallow-draft Taymyr class designed for river mouths. The modern Project 22220 vessels (Arktika, Sibir, Ural, and successors) are the largest and most powerful icebreakers in the world, powered by two RITM-200 reactors.

Design

Nuclear icebreakers typically use two pressurized water reactors driving steam turbo-electric propulsion with three propellers. Their hulls are heavily reinforced with rounded, sloping bows that ride up onto the ice so the ship's weight crushes it. Reactor power output far exceeds that of comparable diesel-electric icebreakers, allowing them to break ice up to about 3 metres thick continuously.

Use

Besides escorting cargo vessels along the Northern Sea Route, Russian nuclear icebreakers have carried tourists to the North Pole, notably the vessel 50 Let Pobedy. Nuclear propulsion has also been applied to the cargo ship Sevmorput.
