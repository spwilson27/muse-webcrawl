The University of Marburg (German: Philipps-Universität Marburg) is a public research university in Marburg, Hesse, Germany. Founded in 1527 by Philip I, Landgrave of Hesse, it is the world's oldest university still operating that was founded as a Protestant institution, established only a decade after the start of the Reformation.

History

Philip of Hesse founded the university in 1527 as part of his campaign to consolidate the Protestant Reformation in his territory. In 1529 the university hosted the Marburg Colloquy, the famous but ultimately unsuccessful debate between Martin Luther and Huldrych Zwingli over the nature of the Eucharist. Unlike most German universities of the era, Marburg was founded without a papal charter, receiving imperial privileges only later from Emperor Maximilian II in 1566.

During the 17th century the university alternated between Lutheran and Calvinist confessions as control of Hesse shifted. It later became part of Prussia after the annexation of Hesse-Kassel in 1866. In the 19th and early 20th centuries Marburg gained distinction in chemistry and medicine; Emil von Behring, the first Nobel laureate in Physiology or Medicine, conducted his work on diphtheria antitoxin while associated with the university.

Academics

The university comprises faculties and departments spanning the humanities, social sciences, law, economics, mathematics and computer science, physics, chemistry, pharmacy, biology, medicine, psychology, geography, theology, and education. It is noted for research in materials science, infection biology, and Middle East studies, and for its historic botanical garden.

Notable people

Alumni and faculty include the Brothers Grimm (Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm), who studied law at Marburg; the philosopher Hannah Arendt; the chemist Robert Bunsen; and the theologian Rudolf Bultmann. The university town of Marburg, dominated by the castle of the landgraves and the Elisabeth Church, retains a strong student character, with students making up a large share of the population.
