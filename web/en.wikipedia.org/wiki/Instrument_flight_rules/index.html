Instrument flight rules

Instrument flight rules (IFR) are a set of regulations under which a pilot operates an aircraft in conditions where flight by outside visual reference is not safe — that is, in instrument meteorological conditions (IMC) such as cloud, fog or heavy rain. Under IFR the pilot navigates and controls the aircraft primarily by reference to flight instruments, and air traffic control provides separation from other traffic and terrain. IFR contrasts with visual flight rules (VFR), where the pilot flies by sight and is personally responsible for seeing and avoiding other aircraft. Virtually all commercial airline flying is conducted under IFR.

Requirements and procedures

To fly IFR, the pilot must hold an instrument rating and maintain currency (recent experience requirements), the aircraft must carry prescribed instruments and radios (and, in most controlled airspace, a transponder and altitude encoder), and the flight must operate on an IFR flight plan with an air traffic control clearance. ATC assigns routes, altitudes and clearances; pilots follow published procedures including standard instrument departures (SIDs), airways and standard terminal arrival routes (STARs).

Approaches to land are flown using published instrument approach procedures — precision approaches such as the instrument landing system (ILS), which provides lateral and vertical guidance to decision heights as low as 200 feet (Category I) or lower, and non-precision approaches using VOR, NDB or GPS. Each approach specifies minima: if the pilot cannot see the required visual references at the decision height or missed approach point, a missed approach must be flown.

Rules and separation

Under IFR in controlled airspace, ATC is responsible for separating IFR traffic from all other traffic; cruising altitudes follow the semicircular (hemispheric) rule based on magnetic course. Pilots must maintain two-way radio communication, comply with clearances, and report specified events such as reaching assigned altitudes or malfunctions affecting IFR capability. In uncontrolled airspace some jurisdictions permit IFR flight without a clearance, with the pilot responsible for terrain clearance via published minimum altitudes.

History

Instrument flying developed in the late 1920s: in 1929 Jimmy Doolittle made the first fully "blind" take-off, flight and landing using the newly developed artificial horizon and directional gyro of Elmer Sperry, and the Link Trainer (1929) made systematic instrument training possible. Scheduled air transport's all-weather reliability — and its safety record — rests largely on the IFR system that matured through the mid-20th century.
