Smoking cessation, usually referred to as quitting smoking or stopping smoking, is the process of discontinuing tobacco smoking. Tobacco smoke contains nicotine, which is addictive and can cause dependence; as a result, nicotine withdrawal makes the process of quitting difficult. Smoking is the leading cause of preventable death and a global public health concern, and cessation substantially reduces the risk of smoking-related diseases such as lung cancer, cardiovascular disease and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Health benefits of quitting

Benefits begin almost immediately. Within 20 minutes of the last cigarette, heart rate and blood pressure drop; within 12 hours, carbon monoxide levels in the blood normalise. Over the following years, circulation and lung function improve, coughing and shortness of breath decrease, and the excess risk of coronary heart disease falls to about half that of a continuing smoker within one to two years. Ten years after quitting, the risk of dying from lung cancer is roughly half that of a person who continues to smoke.

Methods

Unassisted cessation

Most smokers who quit do so without assistance ("cold turkey" or gradual reduction), though unaided attempts have relatively low long-term success rates — roughly 3–5% remain abstinent at one year.

Pharmacotherapy

Nicotine replacement therapy (patches, gum, lozenges, inhalers and nasal spray) supplies nicotine without tobacco smoke and increases quit rates by roughly 50–70%. Varenicline, a partial agonist at nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, approximately doubles the odds of quitting compared with placebo. Bupropion, an atypical antidepressant, and cytisine, a plant alkaloid used for decades in Eastern Europe, are also effective. Combining medication with behavioural support gives the best outcomes.

Behavioural support

Counselling, quitlines, group programmes, text-message services and self-help materials all improve success rates, especially when combined with medication.

Electronic cigarettes

Nicotine-containing e-cigarettes have been found in clinical trials to help some smokers quit combustible cigarettes, and some guidelines regard them as a cessation aid, though long-term safety data are still accumulating and recommendations vary by country.

Withdrawal

Nicotine withdrawal symptoms include irritability, anxiety, difficulty concentrating, increased appetite, restlessness, depressed mood and insomnia. They typically begin within a few hours of the last cigarette, peak during the first week, and largely subside within two to four weeks, although cravings may persist longer.

Epidemiology

In many high-income countries a majority of people who have ever smoked have quit, and "former smoker" prevalence now exceeds current-smoker prevalence in some populations. Public-health measures that promote cessation include taxation, smoke-free laws, advertising bans, graphic warning labels and national quit campaigns.
