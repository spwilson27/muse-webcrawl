A viaduct is a specific type of bridge that consists of a series of multiple spans supported by piers or arches, typically carrying a road or railway across a valley, gorge or other low ground. The word derives from the Latin via (way) by analogy with aqueduct.

Characteristics

Unlike a bridge over water, a viaduct crosses dry land or a depression, and its spans are usually of roughly equal length, carried on tall piers or masonry arches. Viaducts became common with the expansion of railways in the 19th century, when brick and stone arch viaducts — some with dozens of arches — were built across Britain and Europe. Modern examples are typically of concrete or steel, with long approach viaducts used for elevated highways, metro lines and high-speed rail.

Notable examples

The Millau Viaduct in France, a cable-stayed road bridge opened in 2004, is the tallest bridge structure in the world. Scotland's Glenfinnan Viaduct (1901) is famed for its concrete arches and appearances in the Harry Potter films, while Switzerland's Landwasser Viaduct (1902) is a landmark of the Rhaetian Railway. One of the longest viaducts in the world is the Danyang–Kunshan Grand Bridge in China, part of the Beijing–Shanghai high-speed railway.
