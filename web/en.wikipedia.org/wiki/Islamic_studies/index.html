Islamic studies

Islamic studies examines the religion, texts, history, and cultures of the Islamic world.

Islamic studies refers to the academic study of Islam and the Islamic world. It is a multidisciplinary field that draws on history, religious studies, philology, law, philosophy, art history, and the social sciences. Scholars working in the field — Muslim and non-Muslim alike — study primary sources in Arabic, Persian, Turkish, and other languages of Muslim societies.

Within Muslim educational traditions, related disciplines have long been cultivated in madrasas and universities such as al-Azhar in Cairo, covering Qur'anic exegesis (tafsir), hadith studies, jurisprudence (fiqh), theology (kalam), and Arabic grammar. Modern academic Islamic studies developed in European and North American universities partly out of older "Oriental studies," a heritage that has itself become a subject of critical reflection.

Fields of study

  • Qur'anic studies: the text, history, and interpretation of the Qur'an.
  • Hadith studies: traditions of the Prophet Muhammad, their transmission and authentication.
  • Islamic law and jurisprudence: sharia, the schools of law (madhhabs), and legal theory (usul al-fiqh).
  • Islamic theology and philosophy: kalam, falsafa, and debates over reason and revelation.
  • Sufism: the mystical traditions of Islam, their orders, poetry, and practices.
  • History of the Islamic world: from the seventh century to the present, including science, art, and architecture.

History of the discipline

Systematic European study of Islam expanded during the colonial period, producing major reference works such as the Encyclopaedia of Islam. Edward Said's Orientalism (1978) provoked lasting debate about the relationship between Western scholarship and imperial power. Since the late twentieth century the field has broadened to emphasize lived religion, gender, media, and Muslim communities in Europe, the Americas, and Southeast Asia.
