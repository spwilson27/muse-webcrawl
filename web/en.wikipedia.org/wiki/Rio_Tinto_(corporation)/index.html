Rio Tinto Group is an Anglo-Australian multinational mining company and one of the world's largest metals and mining corporations. The company was founded in 1873 when a group of investors purchased from the Spanish government the Rio Tinto mines on the Rio Tinto river in Huelva province, Andalusia, from which the group takes its name. It is dual-listed on the London and Australian stock exchanges.

History

The original Rio Tinto Company was formed in 1873 under the leadership of Hugh Matheson of Matheson & Co. to reopen the ancient mines at Riotinto, worked since the Copper Age and by the Romans. Under Charles Prebble and later the Rothschild family, the company became the world's leading producer of pyrites and a major copper producer, dominating the economy of the Huelva mining district.

In the 1960s the Rio Tinto Company merged with the Australian company Consolidated Zinc to form RTZ (Rio Tinto-Zinc Corporation), and in 1997 RTZ merged with its Australian sister company CRA Limited to form the modern dual-listed Rio Tinto Group. Major acquisitions included the iron ore assets that underpin its Pilbara operations in Western Australia, the Alcan aluminium business in 2007, and diamond mines including Argyle in Australia and Diavik in Canada.

Operations

Rio Tinto's principal product groups are Iron Ore, Aluminium, Copper and Minerals. Its largest operations include the Pilbara iron ore mines, railways and ports in Western Australia; the Escondida copper mine in Chile (a minority stake); the Oyu Tolgoi copper-gold mine in Mongolia; bauxite mines and alumina refineries and aluminium smelters in Australia, Canada and elsewhere; and borates and titanium dioxide operations.

The Argyle diamond mine in Western Australia, once the world's largest source of diamonds by volume and famous for its pink diamonds, closed in 2020 after 37 years of production.

Juukan Gorge

In May 2020 Rio Tinto destroyed two ancient rock shelters at Juukan Gorge in the Pilbara to expand an iron ore mine; the shelters had been occupied for some 46,000 years and were of great significance to the Puutu Kunti Kurrama and Pinikura peoples. The destruction, though legally permitted, provoked widespread outrage, a parliamentary inquiry, and the resignation of chief executive Jean-Sébastien Jacques and other senior executives. The scandal reshaped debate over heritage protection law in Australia.
