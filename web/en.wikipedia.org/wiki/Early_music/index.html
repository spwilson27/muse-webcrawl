Early music generally comprises the music of Western art tradition from the earliest notated works through the Medieval, Renaissance and Baroque periods — roughly up to the middle of the eighteenth century. The term also denotes the modern movement devoted to performing this repertoire in a historically informed manner, using period instruments (or copies), historical tunings and temperaments, and performance practices reconstructed from treatises and other sources.

Repertoire

Medieval music includes Gregorian chant and other plainchant, the polyphony of the Notre-Dame school (Léonin and Pérotin), the ars nova of Machaut, and the songs of troubadours and trouvères. Renaissance music (c. 1400–1600) is dominated by the Franco-Flemish polyphonists such as Josquin des Prez, Palestrina's sacred works, the English and Italian madrigal, and consort music for viols. Baroque music (c. 1600–1750) brought opera, the concerto and the basso continuo, with composers including Monteverdi, Vivaldi, Handel and J. S. Bach.

The revival

Interest in early music revived in the nineteenth century with scholarly editions and performances of Bach and others, but the modern early music movement took shape after the Second World War. Pioneers such as Nikolaus Harnoncourt, Gustav Leonhardt, Christopher Hogwood, Trevor Pinnock and Jordi Savall founded period-instrument ensembles — Concentus Musicus Wien, the Academy of Ancient Music and Hespèrion XX among them — and the debate over "authenticity" reshaped mainstream performance of Baroque and Classical repertoire. Specialist festivals, record labels and academic programmes now support a large professional early-music scene.
