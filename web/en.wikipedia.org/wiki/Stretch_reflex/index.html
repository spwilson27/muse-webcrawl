Stretch reflex

The patellar (knee-jerk) stretch reflex arc

The stretch reflex (myotatic reflex), or more accurately "muscle stretch reflex", is a contraction of a muscle in response to its lengthening, mediated by a monosynaptic reflex arc in the spinal cord. It is the fastest reflex in the body because the sensory neuron synapses directly on the motor neuron without an interposed interneuron (apart from a parallel inhibitory pathway).

Mechanism

Stretch is detected by muscle spindles, encapsulated sensory receptors lying parallel to the extrafusal muscle fibres. Group Ia afferent fibres wrap around the central region of intrafusal fibres and increase their firing rate when the muscle is stretched. In the spinal cord the Ia afferent excites alpha motor neurons supplying the same (homonymous) muscle, causing contraction, and, through a Ia inhibitory interneuron, inhibits motor neurons of antagonist muscles (reciprocal inhibition). Gamma motor neurons adjust spindle sensitivity so the reflex remains useful across muscle lengths.

Clinical significance

The familiar knee-jerk (patellar) reflex is a stretch reflex tested routinely in neurological examination. Reflexes may be brisk (hyperreflexia) in upper motor neuron lesions, diminished or absent (hyporeflexia) in lower motor neuron or peripheral nerve disease, and are graded on a 0–4+ scale. The work of Charles Sherrington on reflex arcs, including decerebrate rigidity, laid foundations for modern neurophysiology.

Function

Functionally, stretch reflexes provide rapid, automatic resistance to unexpected loading and contribute to muscle tone and postural stabilisation, forming the fastest layer of a hierarchy of motor control loops completed by slower transcortical responses.
