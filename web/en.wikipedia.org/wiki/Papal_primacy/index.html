Papal primacy

Papal primacy, also known as the primacy of the bishop of Rome, is an ecclesiological doctrine in the Catholic Church concerning the respect and authority that is due to the pope from other bishops and their episcopal sees. In the 19th century, papal primacy was defined at the First Vatican Council as the doctrine that "the Bishop of Rome has full and supreme power of jurisdiction over the whole Church".

The doctrine is grounded, in Catholic teaching, in Christ's commissioning of St Peter ("You are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church... I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven," Matthew 16:18–19), and in the Petrine ministry of the bishops of Rome as Peter's successors. Its claimed scope developed over the centuries: from a primacy of honour widely acknowledged in the early church, through the medieval assertions of documents such as the Dictatus papae (1075) and the bull Unam sanctam (1302), to the formal definitions of Vatican I (1870) and the collegial balancing of Vatican II's Lumen gentium (1964).

Views of other churches

Eastern Orthodox churches generally accept a primacy of honour for Rome within a pentarchy of patriarchates but reject universal jurisdiction, a dispute at the heart of the East–West Schism of 1054. Protestant traditions generally reject papal primacy altogether, grounding church authority in Scripture. Ecumenical dialogues, including the 2007 Ravenna document, have agreed that Rome held the first place (taxis) in the ancient church while continuing to differ over what that primacy entails.
