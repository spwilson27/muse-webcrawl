Afrikaans

Afrikaans is a West Germanic language spoken mainly in South Africa and Namibia. It developed in the Dutch Cape Colony from the Dutch vernacular spoken by European settlers from the seventeenth century onward, influenced by the languages of enslaved people (Malay, Malagasy, and others), indigenous Khoisan languages, Portuguese, and later English. It is the first language of about 7 million people and is understood by many more; roughly 10 million people speak it in total. Afrikaans is one of South Africa's official languages.

History

Dutch settlement at the Cape began in 1652 under the Dutch East India Company. The settlers' Dutch diverged from the metropolitan language through contact with Khoi and San peoples, enslaved people from Indonesia, Madagascar, and India, and other Europeans. By the nineteenth century this "Cape Dutch" was widely spoken by both white (Afrikaner) and Coloured communities. The Genootskap van Regte Afrikaners (Society of True Afrikaners), founded in 1875, promoted Afrikaans as a written language. Afrikaans replaced Dutch as an official language of South Africa in 1925 and was fully standardised in the following decades, with a Bible translation in 1933.

Under apartheid, the government's policy of imposing Afrikaans as a medium of instruction in Black schools triggered the Soweto uprising of 1976. After 1994, Afrikaans became one of eleven official languages; today the majority of its first-language speakers are Coloured South Africans.

Linguistic features

Afrikaans is notable for its simplified grammar relative to Dutch: verbs do not conjugate for person, grammatical gender has been lost, and negation uses a characteristic double negative (e.g., Ek weet nie — "I don't know"). Its vocabulary is overwhelmingly Dutch-derived, with loanwords from Malay (e.g., piesang, banana), Portuguese, French, German, English, and African languages.

Geographic distribution

Afrikaans is the most geographically widespread and racially diverse of South Africa's official languages. It is dominant in the Western Cape and Northern Cape provinces. Namibia has a significant Afrikaans-speaking population, and emigrant communities exist in the United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand, and Canada.

Literature and media

Afrikaans literature includes writers such as C. J. Langenhoven, N. P. van Wyk Louw, Breyten Breytenbach, André Brink, and Antjie Krog. The language has newspapers, television channels (such as kykNET), and a substantial music culture.
