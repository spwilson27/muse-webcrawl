The Johnstown Flood, sometimes referred to locally as the Great Flood of 1889, occurred on May 31, 1889, after the catastrophic failure of the South Fork Dam, located on the south fork of the Little Conemaugh River, 14 miles (23 km) upstream of the town of Johnstown, Pennsylvania, United States. The dam broke after several days of extraordinary rainfall, unleashing 14.55 million cubic metres of water from Lake Conemaugh. With a death toll of 2,209, it was the deadliest flood in United States history and one of the worst man-made disasters of the nineteenth century.

Background

Johnstown was a booming steel and coal town of some 30,000 people at the confluence of the Little Conemaugh and Stonycreek rivers. The South Fork Dam had been built by Pennsylvania for a canal reservoir, then sold and rebuilt, poorly, by the private South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club, whose members included Andrew Carnegie, Henry Clay Frick and Andrew Mellon. The club's modifications — lowering the dam's crest, removing discharge pipes and clogging the spillway with fish screens — badly weakened it. Engineer Daniel Morrell of the Cambria Iron Company had warned of the danger years before.

The flood

On May 30–31, 1889, a massive storm dumped as much as 10 inches of rain on the region. Lake Conemaugh rose about two feet per hour. Despite frantic efforts and warning telegrams sent down the valley (which were not heeded, earlier false alarms having bred complacency), the dam gave way at about 3:10 p.m. The lake emptied in roughly 36 minutes. The flood wave, carrying trees, houses, locomotives and miles of barbed wire, struck the East Conemaugh viaduct, then Mineral Point, Woodvale, and Johnstown itself at about 4:07 p.m., where a 60-foot wall of debris smashed into the Pennsylvania Railroad's stone bridge and caught fire, trapping and killing hundreds in the burning wreckage.

Aftermath

Bodies were found as far away as Cincinnati, and some were identified only decades later; 99 entire families died, and 396 children under ten were killed. Recovery was one of the first major peacetime relief efforts of the American Red Cross, led by Clara Barton, who spent five months in Johnstown. Survivors' lawsuits against the club's members failed — courts treated the disaster as an act of God and no compensation was ever paid — though club members contributed to public relief funds. The disaster fuelled lasting debate about corporate and private liability for negligence.

Legacy

Johnstown was flooded again, disastrously, in 1936 and 1977. The Johnstown Flood National Memorial preserves the dam site, and the Johnstown Flood Museum occupies the former Cambria Library building, itself a Carnegie-funded replacement. David McCullough's The Johnstown Flood (1968) remains the standard popular account.
