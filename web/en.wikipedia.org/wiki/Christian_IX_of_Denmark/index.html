Christian IX (8 April 1818 – 29 January 1906) was King of Denmark from 1863 until his death in 1906. From 1863 to 1864, he was concurrently Duke of Schleswig, Holstein and Lauenburg. He was nicknamed the "father-in-law of Europe" because of the marriages of his children into royal families across the continent.

Accession and war

A German prince of the House of Glücksburg, a junior branch of the Oldenburgs, Christian was chosen as heir to the Danish throne under the 1852 Treaty of London. His reign began with the Second Schleswig War of 1864, in which Denmark lost the duchies of Schleswig, Holstein and Lauenburg to Prussia and Austria — a national trauma that shaped his conservative politics for decades.

The father-in-law of Europe

Christian's six children married widely: Alexandra became queen consort of Edward VII of the United Kingdom; Georgios I reigned as king of Greece; Marie (Dagmar) became empress of Russia as wife of Alexander III; Thyra married the Crown Prince of Hanover; and Valdemar was offered — but declined — the thrones of Bulgaria and Norway. Annual family gatherings at Fredensborg Palace brought together much of Europe's royalty.

Domestic politics

For most of his reign Christian resisted the growth of parliamentary democracy, appointing conservative governments that ruled against Venstre majorities. In 1901, after a final Venstre election victory, he conceded the "system change" (Systemskiftet), appointing a government based on the Folketing majority and establishing parliamentarism in practice.
