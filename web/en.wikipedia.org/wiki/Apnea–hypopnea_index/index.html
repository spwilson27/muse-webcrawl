Apnea–hypopnea index

The apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) is an index used to indicate the severity of sleep apnea. It is represented by the number of apnea and hypopnea events per hour of sleep. An apnea is a complete cessation of airflow lasting at least ten seconds; a hypopnea is a partial reduction of airflow, conventionally of at least 30%, also lasting at least ten seconds and associated with either an oxygen desaturation or an arousal from sleep.

Calculation

The AHI is calculated by dividing the total number of apnea and hypopnea events recorded during a sleep study (usually polysomnography, or a home sleep apnea test) by the number of hours of sleep. For example, 150 events during five hours of sleep gives an AHI of 30 events per hour.

Severity classification

For adults, commonly used thresholds (following the American Academy of Sleep Medicine) are:

AHI (events/hour)Severity
< 5Normal / none
5 to < 15Mild
15 to < 30Moderate
≥ 30Severe

Different cutoffs apply to children, in whom an AHI above 1–1.5 events per hour is already considered abnormal. Diagnosis of obstructive sleep apnea generally requires an AHI of at least 5 with associated symptoms or comorbidities, or an AHI of at least 15 regardless of symptoms.

Limitations

The AHI depends on the scoring rules used — definitions of hypopnea changed over time (notably in the 2007 and 2012 AASM scoring manuals), so AHIs measured under different criteria are not fully comparable. The index also correlates imperfectly with symptoms and outcomes: it counts events but does not capture their duration, the depth of oxygen desaturation, or sleep fragmentation. Alternative metrics such as the respiratory disturbance index (RDI, which also counts respiratory effort-related arousals) and the oxygen desaturation index (ODI) are often reported alongside it, and researchers have proposed measures of "hypoxic burden" that may better predict cardiovascular risk.

Clinical use

AHI guides treatment decisions, such as prescribing continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy, oral appliance therapy, or surgery, and is used to assess treatment response — a successfully treated patient's AHI typically falls to below 5.
