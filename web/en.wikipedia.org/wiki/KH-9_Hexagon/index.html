The KH-9 (BYEMAN codename HEXAGON), commonly known as Big Bird, was a series of photographic reconnaissance satellites launched by the United States between 1971 and 1986. Operated by the National Reconnaissance Office (NRO), the KH-9 combined broad area-search coverage with high resolution, and it was the last generation of American film-return spy satellites: exposed film was ejected in reentry capsules ("buckets") that were snatched in mid-air by recovery aircraft over the Pacific.

Design and operations

Each satellite was enormous for its era — about 16.5 m (55 ft) long and weighing up to 13 tonnes — and carried two large Perkin-Elmer panoramic cameras that together supplied stereo imagery with a best ground resolution of roughly 0.6 m (2 ft). A lower-resolution mapping camera on later missions supported geodesy and charting. Four film-return capsules (five on the final mission) allowed missions to lengthen over time, from about three weeks early in the programme to as long as 275 days by the 1980s.

Twenty spacecraft were launched on Titan IIID and Titan 34D rockets from Vandenberg Air Force Base; the final launch, in April 1986, was destroyed seconds after liftoff in a booster failure. A single KH-9 mission could photograph tens of millions of square miles, and the fleet collectively imaged much of the Earth's land surface.

Declassification

The programme was officially declassified in September 2011, when an actual KH-9 was publicly displayed at the National Museum of the United States Air Force before going to the Smithsonian's Udvar-Hazy Center. Imagery from KH-9 and earlier Corona satellites has since been used by archaeologists, glaciologists and environmental scientists as a historical record of the Earth's surface.
