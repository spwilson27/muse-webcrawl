MOS Technology SID

The SID (Sound Interface Device) is the built-in programmable sound generator chip of Commodore's CBM-II, Commodore 64, Commodore 128 and Commodore MAX home computers. It was one of the first sound chips of its kind to be included in a home computer prior to the digital sound revolution.

Together with the VIC-II graphics chip, the SID was instrumental in making the Commodore 64 the best-selling home computer in history, and is reputed to be partly responsible for the emergence of the demoscene. The chip was designed by engineer Robert "Bob" Yannes, who aimed to create a true musical-instrument synthesizer on a chip rather than a mere beeper.

Features

  • Three separately programmable independent audio oscillators (voices), each with a range of about eight octaves
  • Four waveforms per oscillator: triangle, sawtooth, variable pulse and noise
  • One multi-mode resonant filter (low-pass, high-pass, band-pass and notch combinations) shared by the voices, with external audio input
  • Three attack/decay/sustain/release (ADSR) envelope generators, one per voice
  • Oscillator synchronisation and ring modulation between voices
  • A pseudo-random number and modulation generator; the output of one oscillator can be read by the CPU
  • Two A/D converters for paddle controllers

Two revisions dominate: the original MOS Technology 6581 and the later MOS Technology 8580 (later 6582), which runs at a lower voltage and has a somewhat cleaner, less distorted filter sound. Because the filter was implemented with imperfect analogue circuitry, every SID chip sounds slightly different, a trait prized by chiptune musicians.

Legacy

The SID's distinctive sound defined the music of thousands of games and demos, and compositions were distributed in the dedicated ".sid" file format, archived by the High Voltage SID Collection. The chip remains in demand for instruments such as the Elektron SidStation, and is widely emulated in software.
