RLC circuit

Placeholder image: a series RLC circuit diagram.

An RLC circuit is an electrical circuit consisting of a resistor (R), an inductor (L), and a capacitor (C), connected in series or in parallel. The name of the circuit is derived from the letters that are used to denote the constituent components of this circuit, where the sequence of the components may vary from RLC.

The circuit forms a harmonic oscillator for current, and resonates in a similar way to an LC circuit. Introducing the resistor increases the decay of these oscillations, which is also known as damping. The resistor also reduces the peak resonant frequency. Under normal conditions with a physical inductor having some resistance, an RLC resonator still results.

Basic concepts

Resonance

The circuit exhibits resonance when driven at its natural frequency, at which the inductive and capacitive reactances cancel. The (angular) resonant frequency is:

ω0 = 1 / √(LC)

At resonance, a series RLC circuit presents minimum impedance (equal to R), while a parallel RLC circuit presents maximum impedance. Resonant circuits are used in radio tuning, filters, and oscillators.

Damping

The damping factor ζ is determined by the values of R, L and C. For a series RLC circuit the attenuation constant is α = R / (2L), and the circuit is underdamped when α < ω0, critically damped when α = ω0, and overdamped when α > ω0. An underdamped circuit oscillates with decaying amplitude; an overdamped circuit returns to equilibrium without oscillating.

Bandwidth and Q factor

The sharpness of resonance is described by the quality factor Q, the ratio of the resonant frequency to the bandwidth over which the power falls to half its peak value. High-Q circuits have narrow bandwidth and are used where selectivity matters, such as in radio receivers.

Applications

RLC circuits are used as band-pass and band-stop filters, in tuning circuits for radio and television receivers, in oscillators, voltage multipliers, and pulse discharge circuits, and as models for many other oscillatory physical systems.
