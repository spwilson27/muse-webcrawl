Sanjo (Korean: 산조, literally "scattered melodies") is a genre of traditional Korean folk music for a solo melodic instrument accompanied by the janggu, an hourglass-shaped drum. Developed in the late 19th century in the southwestern province of Jeolla, sanjo is one of the most highly developed and virtuosic forms of Korean instrumental music and a showcase for improvisation within inherited melodic frameworks.

Structure

A sanjo performance unfolds as a suite of movements, each in a different rhythmic cycle (jangdan) that grows progressively faster and more intense: typically beginning with the slow, spacious jinyang, proceeding through jungmori and jungjungmori, then the quicker jajinmori, and often concluding with the very fast hwimori (and sometimes danmori). Over the full span — a complete performance may last 20 minutes to an hour — the music moves from meditative lyricism to dazzling display, with shifts of mode (jo) providing emotional contrast.

History and schools

The genre is credited to Kim Chang-jo (1856–1919), who organised the scattered melodic materials of shaman ritual music (sinawi) and the narrative song pansori into the first sanjo for the twelve-string zither gayageum. Other instruments soon adopted the form: the six-string geomungo, the bamboo flute daegeum, the two-string fiddle haegeum, the bowed zither ajaeng and the double-reed piri.

Because sanjo was transmitted master-to-disciple, distinct schools (yu-pa) formed around great performers, each school preserving its founder's characteristic melodies, ornaments and pacing. Famous gayageum lineages include those of Kim Chang-jo, Kim Juk-pa and Seong Geum-yeon. Masters of sanjo are designated holders of Important Intangible Cultural Property in South Korea, and the music is a staple of traditional concert programming.
