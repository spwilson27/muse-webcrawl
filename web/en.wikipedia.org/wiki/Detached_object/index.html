Detached object

A detached object is a dynamical class of minor planets in the outer Solar System beyond Neptune. Such objects have perihelia sufficiently distant from Neptune that they are not significantly influenced by Neptune and the other known planets, which makes them appear "detached" from the rest of the Solar System, except for their attraction to the Sun.

Characteristics

Detached objects generally have perihelion distances greater than about 40 astronomical units (AU), so that they never approach Neptune (which orbits at about 30 AU) closely enough to be scattered by it. Their orbits are often highly eccentric and inclined. They are distinguished from the classical Kuiper belt objects, which are in more regular orbits, and from scattered disc objects, whose perihelia remain close enough to Neptune for gravitational interaction.

Because their orbits cannot easily be explained by interactions with the known giant planets, detached objects are considered important evidence in debates about the early evolution of the outer Solar System and about the possible existence of additional planets.

Notable examples

The most famous detached object is Sedna (90377 Sedna), discovered in 2003, with a perihelion of about 76 AU and an orbital period of roughly 11,400 years. Other notable examples include 2012 VP113 (nicknamed "Biden"), which has a perihelion near 80 AU, and the large trans-Neptunian objects Gonggong and Leleākūhonua. Some classifications also place Eris and other distant scattered-disc objects near the boundary of the detached population.

Origins

Proposed explanations for detached orbits include close passes by other stars in the Sun's birth cluster, capture of extrasolar planetesimals, perturbations by a rogue planet ejected from the Solar System, and the hypothesised distant giant planet known as Planet Nine, whose predicted clustering effect on the arguments of perihelion of extreme trans-Neptunian objects remains a subject of active research and debate.
