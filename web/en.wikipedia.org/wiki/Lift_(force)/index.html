Lift (force)

Lift on an airfoil
DefinitionComponent of aerodynamic force perpendicular to the oncoming flow
SI unitNewton
Key variablesAir density, velocity, wing area, lift coefficient

Lift is the component of the aerodynamic force on a body that is perpendicular to the direction of the oncoming fluid flow, as distinct from drag, which acts parallel to the flow. Lift is most familiar as the force generated by an aircraft's wings (aerofoils) that counteracts weight and keeps the aeroplane aloft; it also acts on propellers, sails, helicopter rotors, hydrofoils, and racing-car wings (in the downward direction, as "downforce").

How lift is generated

An airfoil generates lift when it deflects the airflow downward: by Newton's third law, the reaction pushes the wing up. Equivalently, circulation around the wing, together with the Kutta condition at the trailing edge, produces a pressure difference — lower pressure above the wing and higher pressure below — whose integral is the lift. The popular "equal transit time" explanation, which claims air parcels separated at the leading edge must reunite at the trailing edge, is incorrect.

The lift equation

Lift is quantified by L = ½ ρ v² S CL, where ρ is air density, v is true airspeed, S is wing area, and CL is the lift coefficient, which increases with angle of attack up to the critical angle, beyond which the flow separates and the wing stalls. Camber, flaps, and slats raise the maximum lift coefficient for takeoff and landing.

Applications

Beyond aircraft, lift explains the flight of birds and insects, the tacking of sailing boats (the sail and keel act as air- and hydrofoils), and the curve of spinning balls is related to the Magnus effect. Engineers exploit the same principles in wind turbines and compressors.
