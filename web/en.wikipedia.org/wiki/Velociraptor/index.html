Velociraptor

Velociraptor
Temporal range: Late Cretaceous, ~75–71 Ma
Kingdom: Animalia
Order: Saurischia
Family: Dromaeosauridae
Genus: Velociraptor Osborn, 1924
Type species: V. mongoliensis

Velociraptor (meaning "swift seizer" in Latin) is a genus of small dromaeosaurid dinosaur that lived in Asia during the Late Cretaceous epoch, about 75 to 71 million years ago. Two species are currently recognized, V. mongoliensis and V. osmolskae, from fossils found in Mongolia and Inner Mongolia, China.

A modern reconstruction showing Velociraptor with feathers and wing-like arms.

Description

Velociraptor was a small, bipedal carnivore, roughly 1.6–2.1 metres long, about 0.5 metres tall at the hip, and weighing perhaps 15–20 kilograms — about the size of a turkey. Like other dromaeosaurids it bore an enlarged, sickle-shaped claw on the second toe of each foot, held off the ground while walking, and a long, stiffened tail that acted as a counterbalance. Its skull was elongated with a distinctively upturned snout and jaws lined with serrated teeth.

Feathers

In 2007, quill knobs — attachment points for large flight feathers — were reported on a Velociraptor forearm bone, direct evidence that it carried feathers. Being far too small to fly, Velociraptor probably used its plumage for display, insulation or brooding eggs. This contrasts with the scaly portrayal made famous by the film Jurassic Park, whose "raptors" were also much larger than the real animal, being modelled on the bigger relative Deinonychus.

Palaeobiology

The most famous specimen, the "Fighting Dinosaurs" (1971), preserves a Velociraptor locked in combat with a Protoceratops — its sickle claw embedded in the herbivore's throat region while its arm is bitten — both buried alive, probably by a collapsing dune. Feeding traces show Velociraptor also scavenged carcasses of larger dinosaurs. Studies of its inner ear and braincase indicate an agile predator with good senses of smell, hearing and balance.

Discovery

The type specimen was found in 1923 in the Djadochta Formation at the Flaming Cliffs of Mongolia by an American Museum of Natural History expedition, and Henry Fairfield Osborn named the genus in 1924. Fossils of V. mongoliensis come chiefly from the Djadochta Formation; V. osmolskae, named in 2008, is known from the Bayan Mandahu Formation of Inner Mongolia.
