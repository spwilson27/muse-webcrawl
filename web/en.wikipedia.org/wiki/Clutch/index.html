A clutch is a mechanical device that engages and disengages the power transmission between two rotating shafts, typically a driving shaft and a driven shaft. In a motor vehicle with a manual transmission, the clutch connects the engine to the gearbox and is operated by a pedal, allowing the driver to change gear and to bring the vehicle to a stop without stalling the engine.

Friction clutches

The most common automotive clutch is the single-plate friction clutch. A friction disc, splined to the gearbox input shaft, is clamped between the engine's flywheel and a spring-loaded pressure plate. Releasing the clamping force by pressing the pedal lets the disc spin freely. Multi-plate clutches, often running in oil, are used in motorcycles and automatic transmissions. The friction material must tolerate high temperatures and wear.

Other types

Centrifugal clutches engage automatically as engine speed rises, as in chainsaws and mopeds. Dog clutches engage by interlocking teeth and are used in some racing and marine gearboxes. Electromagnetic clutches switch engagement electrically, as in air-conditioning compressors. A freewheel or overrunning clutch transmits torque in one direction only.
