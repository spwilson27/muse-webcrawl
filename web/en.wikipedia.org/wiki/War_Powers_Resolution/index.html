War Powers Resolution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The United States Capitol, where Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over President Nixon's veto in 1973
The United States Capitol, where Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over President Nixon's veto in 1973

The War Powers Resolution of 1973 (also called the War Powers Act) is a United States federal law intended to check the President's power to commit the nation to armed conflict without the consent of Congress. Passed over Richard Nixon's veto in the aftermath of the Vietnam War, it requires the President to notify Congress within 48 hours of deploying forces and limits unauthorised hostilities to 60 days (plus a 30-day withdrawal period).

Provisions

The Resolution (50 U.S.C. §§ 1541–1548) states that the President may introduce forces into hostilities only pursuant to a declaration of war, specific statutory authorisation, or a national emergency created by attack. Section 4 requires written reports; section 5(b) starts the 60-day clock; section 5(c) originally allowed Congress to direct withdrawal by concurrent resolution — a device undermined by the Legislative veto ruling in INS v. Chadha (1983).

Practice and disputes

Presidents of both parties have reported 'consistent with' rather than 'pursuant to' the Resolution and disputed its constitutionality. Episodes testing it include Lebanon (1982–84, where Congress authorised 18 months), the Gulf War (authorised 1991), Kosovo (1999, which passed the 60-day mark), and Libya (2011, where the administration argued the operation did not amount to 'hostilities'). Congress has rarely enforced the clock, and the courts generally treat war-powers disputes as non-justiciable.

Assessment

Supporters credit the Resolution with forcing consultation and reporting; critics call it both unconstitutional in restricting the Commander-in-chief and ineffective — even counterproductive, by implying presidential authority to wage war for 60 days without approval. Post-2001 practice under the AUMF has further shifted the balance toward the executive.
