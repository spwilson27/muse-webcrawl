An artisan (from French: artisan, Italian: artigiano) is a skilled craft worker who makes things by hand, or with hand tools, either wholly or in part. Artisans practise a craft and may through experience and aptitude reach the expressive levels of an artist. Their products — furniture, clothing, jewellery, household items, decorative art, and foodstuffs — are often described as artisanal when produced in small quantities by traditional methods rather than by industrial mass production.

History

During the Middle Ages, artisans in Europe were organized into guilds, associations that regulated entry into a trade, training, and standards of workmanship. The route to mastery typically ran from apprentice, living and working under a master, to journeyman, and finally to master craftsman entitled to open a workshop and take apprentices, after producing a "masterpiece" judged by the guild. Guilds were powerful urban institutions, but their monopolies were gradually dismantled in the early modern period.

The Industrial Revolution displaced much artisan production with factory manufacture, in which tasks were subdivided and mechanized. Reactions such as the Arts and Crafts movement of the late nineteenth century, led by figures including William Morris, sought to restore the dignity and quality of hand craftsmanship.

Today

Contemporary artisans range from traditional tradespeople — potters, blacksmiths, weavers, luthiers, stonemasons — to makers of specialty foods such as artisanal cheese and bread. The term is also widely used in marketing to suggest small-batch, high-quality production. Artisan skills are transmitted through apprenticeships, vocational schools, and increasingly through online marketplaces and maker communities. In some countries the title of master craftsman remains a formal, protected qualification.
