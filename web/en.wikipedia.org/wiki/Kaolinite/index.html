Kaolinite

Kaolinite is a clay mineral with the chemical composition Al2Si2O5(OH)4. It is a layered silicate mineral, with one tetrahedral sheet of silica (SiO4) linked through oxygen atoms to one octahedral sheet of alumina (AlO6). Rocks rich in kaolinite are known as kaolin or china clay, and the mineral is named after Gaoling ("high ridge"), a village near Jingdezhen in Jiangxi province, China, where the clay was mined for porcelain production.

Properties

Kaolinite is a soft, usually white mineral, produced by the chemical weathering of aluminium silicate minerals such as feldspar. It has a low shrink–swell capacity and a low cation-exchange capacity compared with other clay minerals such as montmorillonite. Under the electron microscope, kaolinite crystals appear as hexagonal plates stacked together. Its Mohs hardness is about 2–2.5 and its specific gravity is about 2.6.

Formation and occurrence

Kaolinite forms by hydrothermal alteration and weathering of feldspar-rich rocks such as granite, particularly in warm, moist climates. Major deposits are mined in Georgia and South Carolina in the United States, in Cornwall in England, in Brazil, and in China. In many regions kaolin occurs as sedimentary deposits washed from weathered source rocks.

Uses

The principal use of kaolin is in the manufacture of paper, where it serves as a filler and coating to improve gloss, brightness, and printability. It is the traditional raw material for porcelain and is also used in ceramics, paint, rubber, plastics, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals. Calcined kaolin is used as a functional extender pigment, and kaolinite is a feedstock for producing metakaolin, a pozzolanic material for cement.
