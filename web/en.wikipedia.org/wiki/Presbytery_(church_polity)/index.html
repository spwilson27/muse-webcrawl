Presbytery (church polity)

In presbyterian church government, a presbytery is a governing body of elders representing a regional group of congregations. The word derives from the Greek presbyteros, meaning "elder". The presbytery is the second of the graded courts of presbyterian polity, above the local session and below the synod and general assembly.

Composition and functions

A presbytery consists of all the ministers (teaching elders) within its bounds and an equal number of ruling elders commissioned by each congregation's session. It is responsible for ordaining, installing, and supervising ministers; overseeing congregations and their missions; examining candidates for ministry; and hearing appeals and references from sessions. Its acts are subject to review by the higher courts of synod and general assembly.

History

Presbyterian polity took shape in the Reformation, in the churches of Geneva under John Calvin and Scotland under John Knox, as an alternative both to episcopal government by bishops and to congregational independence. The Westminster Assembly of the 1640s codified the system in documents such as the Form of Presbyterial Church Government. Presbyteries became the characteristic regional units of Reformed churches worldwide, including the Church of Scotland, where presbyteries cover defined geographic areas and, following recent restructuring, have been merged into larger bodies.

Moderator

Each presbytery elects a moderator, usually annually, who presides over its meetings — a role held in parity by ministers and elders, reflecting the presbyterian principle of the equality of elders.
