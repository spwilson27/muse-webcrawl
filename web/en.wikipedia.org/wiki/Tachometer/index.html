A tachometer (from Greek táchos, "speed", and métron, "measure") is an instrument measuring the rotation speed of a shaft or disk, as in a motor or other machine. The device usually displays revolutions per minute (rpm) on a calibrated analogue dial or digital readout. In vehicles, the tachometer indicates engine crankshaft speed and typically marks a redline zone beyond which operation may damage the engine.

Types

Mechanical

Early tachometers were centrifugal: spinning weights moved outward against springs in proportion to speed, driving a needle. In mid-20th-century cars, a flexible cable driven from the engine rotated a magnet inside an aluminium cup; eddy currents dragged the cup against a spring to indicate speed, the same principle as cable-driven speedometers.

Electronic

Modern tachometers count pulses from an ignition system, crankshaft position sensor, alternator, or a dedicated sensor, and compute speed electronically. Many use a microcontroller that can also drive a shift light or communicate with the engine control unit. Optical (laser) handheld tachometers measure speed from reflective tape on a rotating part without contact, and stroboscopic instruments freeze apparent motion to read speed.

Use

Drivers of manual-transmission cars use the tachometer to choose shift points; pilots monitor tachometers for propeller and turbine speeds; and engineers use them to verify machine speeds in manufacturing, medicine (centrifuges) and marine engines. Aircraft tachometers may display limits with coloured arcs, as on other flight instruments.

History

The first mechanical tachometers were adapted from centrifugal governors in the early 19th century and were used on locomotives; by about 1817 the principle had been applied as a dedicated speed-measuring instrument. Bryan Donkin is credited with an early tachometer used on machinery in the 1820s–30s. Tachometers became standard in automobiles during the mid-20th century, initially on performance models.
