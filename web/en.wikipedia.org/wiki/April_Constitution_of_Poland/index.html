The April Constitution (Polish: Konstytucja kwietniowa) was the constitution of the Second Polish Republic adopted on 23 April 1935. Promoted by the Sanation movement of Marshal Józef Piłsudski, it replaced the democratic March Constitution of 1921 and introduced a strongly presidential, authoritarian-leaning system of government, concentrating power in the hands of the President of the Republic, who was declared responsible "only before God and history".

Background

After Piłsudski's May Coup of 1926, the Sanation camp sought to revise the 1921 constitution, which it blamed for parliamentary instability and party fragmentation ("sejmocracy"). A first step was the August Novelization of 1926, which strengthened the executive. Work on a wholly new constitution proceeded in the Sejm through the early 1930s amid boycotts and protests by the opposition. The draft was passed by the Sejm on 26 January 1934 in circumstances the opposition considered irregular — opposition deputies had left the chamber in protest — and, after approval by the Senate, was signed into law by President Ignacy Mościcki on 23 April 1935. Piłsudski, whose personal authority the document was designed to institutionalise, died less than three weeks later, on 12 May 1935, leaving the system to his successors, the "colonels' government".

Provisions

The April Constitution replaced parliamentary sovereignty with the principle of a strong, unified state power headed by the President. Its main features included:

  • The President was the supreme organ of the state, coordinating the other organs; he appointed the government, could dissolve the Sejm and Senate, issue decrees with the force of law in defined circumstances, designate a successor in wartime, and was not politically responsible to parliament.
  • The President was elected for a seven-year term by an Assembly of Electors dominated by the outgoing President's nominees, with a popular vote only between two candidates in specified cases.
  • The Sejm's powers were curtailed: its term was fixed, the Senate was partly appointed by the President, and electoral law (revised in 1935) allowed the authorities to filter candidates through electoral assemblies, effectively excluding much of the opposition.
  • The catalogue of civil rights was retained but made subordinate to the "common good" as defined by the state.

Legacy

The April Constitution remained the formal legal basis of the Polish government-in-exile during and after the Second World War, which invoked it to maintain constitutional continuity when the communist authorities in Poland adopted their own constitutions (1947 and 1952). The legal dispute over succession in exile — including the 1954 split over President August Zaleski's refusal to step down — turned on its provisions. After 1989, the restored independent Poland adopted new constitutions (the Small Constitution of 1992 and the Constitution of 1997), and the April Constitution's role became historical, though the insignia of state were transferred from the last president-in-exile, Ryszard Kaczorowski, to Lech Wałęsa in December 1990 as a symbol of continuity.
