Perceptual constancy

Perceptual constancy, also called object constancy or constancy phenomenon, is the tendency of animals and humans to see familiar objects as having standard shape, size, colour, and location regardless of changes in the angle of perspective, distance, or lighting. The impression tends to conform to the physical object as it actually is rather than to the proximal stimulus (the pattern of light reaching the eye). A white sheet of paper, for example, is perceived as white in both bright sunlight and dim shade, even though the amount of light it reflects to the eye differs enormously.

Types

Size constancy

Size constancy is the perception of an object as having a fixed size despite the fact that its retinal image shrinks as it moves farther away. A person walking away from us is not perceived to shrink, because the visual system takes distance into account (a relationship formalized in Emmert's law). The Ames room illusion exploits failures of size constancy produced by false assumptions about the shape of a room.

Shape constancy

Shape constancy is the perception of an object's true shape despite changes in the shape of its projection. A circular plate seen at an angle projects an ellipse on the retina, yet is perceived as a tilted circle; a door swinging open is perceived as the same rectangular door rather than as a shape cycling through trapezoids.

Colour and lightness constancy

Colour constancy is the tendency for a surface to appear the same colour under different illuminants — a banana looks yellow in daylight and under tungsten light, even though the spectra reaching the eye differ. Lightness constancy concerns perceived reflectance: white paper in shadow may reflect less light than black paper in sunshine, yet each is seen according to its surface. Theories such as Edwin Land's retinex theory and the von Kries adaptation model attempt to explain how the visual system discounts the illuminant.

Location and orientation constancy

Objects are perceived as stationary even though their retinal images move whenever the eyes, head, or body move. The visual system combines retinal signals with information about eye and head movements (efference copy and proprioception) to maintain a stable visual world.

Development and mechanisms

Some constancies are present very early in infancy: experiments with newborns and young infants show sensitivity to size and shape constancy, suggesting a substantial innate basis, though constancies continue to refine with experience. Explanations of constancy appeal both to unconscious inference — the perceptual system "computing" the distal object from cues such as texture gradients, occlusion, and familiar size — and to direct pickup of invariant relationships in the optic array, as proposed in J. J. Gibson's ecological approach to perception.

Failures and illusions

When the assumptions underlying constancy mechanisms are violated, perceptual illusions result. The Müller-Lyer and Ponzo illusions involve misapplied size constancy; the Ames room shows size constancy overridden by assumed distance; and simultaneous-contrast displays show limits of lightness constancy. Adelson's checker-shadow illusion demonstrates that the visual system relies heavily on inferred illumination rather than raw luminance.
