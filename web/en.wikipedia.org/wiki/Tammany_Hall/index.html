Tammany Hall

[Image: the Tammany tiger, from Thomas Nast's 1871 cartoon "The Tammany Tiger Loose" in Harper's Weekly]

Tammany Hall, also known as the Society of St. Tammany, the Sons of St. Tammany, or the Columbian Order, was an American political organisation founded in New York City in 1786. It became the main local political machine of the Democratic Party, controlling nominations and patronage in New York City and often New York State from the 1850s into the 1930s, and it remains the archetype of the corrupt urban political machine in American memory.

History

Founded in 1789 as a patriotic fraternal society (named for the Lenape chief Tamanend, canonised as "St. Tammany"), the organisation was taken over by Aaron Burr's supporters and converted into a political body. Under Fernando Wood in the 1850s it consolidated control of the Democratic Party in the city, naturalising and mobilising immigrants — above all the Irish — in exchange for jobs and assistance.

Tammany's power peaked under William M. "Boss" Tweed (1868–1871), whose ring stole, by modern estimates, tens of millions of dollars through padded contracts, most notoriously the New York County Courthouse. Thomas Nast's cartoons in Harper's Weekly and reporting in The New York Times destroyed Tweed, who died in jail in 1878. The machine recovered under bosses such as Richard Croker and Charles Francis Murphy, adapting to reform eras; it pragmatically advanced measures such as work-safety laws after the Triangle Shirtwaist fire (1911), and launched the careers of governor and presidential candidate Al Smith and Senator Robert F. Wagner.

Decline

Tammany's grip was broken by a combination of the Seabury investigations (1930–32), the mayoralty of Fiorello La Guardia, New Deal welfare provision that replaced the machine's personal favours with state entitlements, and demographic change. Its last boss, Carmine DeSapio, lost power in the 1960s to reform Democrats; the society dwindled and its hall on Union Square became a theatre and landmark.

Assessment

Historians are divided: the machine was unquestionably corrupt, yet it also functioned as a welfare system and escalator for poor immigrants — as the Tammany philosopher George Washington Plunkitt explained in his defence of "honest graft" — a duality central to debates about machine politics generally.
