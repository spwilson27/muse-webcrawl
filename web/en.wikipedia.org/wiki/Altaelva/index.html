Altaelva (English: Alta River; Northern Sami: Álttáeatnu; Kven: Alattionjoki) is a river in Troms og Finnmark county in northern Norway. At about 240 kilometres long, it is the third-longest river in the county. The river flows from the lakes of the Finnmark plateau near the Finnish border, through the municipality of Kautokeino and the Alta canyon, and empties into the Altafjorden at the town of Alta.

Geography

The river rises in the mountains and lakes south-east of Kautokeino, including Reisavannet, and takes the name Altaelva downstream of the junction of the Kautokeinoelva and Mázejohka rivers. In its middle course it cuts the Sautso canyon (Norwegian: Čávžu), approximately 10 km long and 300–420 m deep — one of the largest river canyons in Northern Europe.

Salmon fishing

Altaelva is regarded as one of the finest Atlantic salmon rivers in the world, famous for exceptionally large fish; salmon exceeding 20 kg are caught regularly, and historical catches approached 30 kg. Fishing rights have long been associated with the local commons organisation Alta Laksefiskeri Interessentskap, and the river has hosted celebrated visiting anglers including British nobility and royalty in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Alta controversy

Plans to dam the river for hydroelectric power provoked the Alta controversy (1978–1982), one of the defining events of modern Norwegian politics. Protests by environmentalists and Sami activists — including hunger strikes outside the Storting and a large civil-disobedience camp at Stilla — highlighted Sami indigenous rights. The Alta power station was eventually built with a reduced design: a 150 m-high dam at Virdnejávri feeding a plant producing about 625 GWh per year, completed in 1987. Despite the dam, the salmon fishery survived largely intact, and the conflict is credited with accelerating recognition of Sami rights, including the creation of the Sami Parliament of Norway.
