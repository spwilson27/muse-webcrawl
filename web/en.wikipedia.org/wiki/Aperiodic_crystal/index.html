Aperiodic crystal

Aperiodic crystals are crystals that lack three-dimensional lattice periodicity but nevertheless show long-range order and sharp diffraction patterns. They are described mathematically using superspace crystallography.

An aperiodic crystal is a crystalline material whose atomic arrangement is ordered but not periodic in three dimensions. Although such a structure has no translational symmetry described by a conventional unit cell in three-dimensional space, it produces a diffraction pattern consisting of sharp Bragg peaks, the hallmark of long-range order.

Categories

Aperiodic crystals are generally divided into three classes: incommensurately modulated structures, in which a basically periodic structure is distorted by a modulation whose wavelength is incommensurate with the lattice; incommensurate composite crystals, formed of two or more interpenetrating subsystems with mutually incommensurate periodicities; and quasicrystals, which are ordered structures with rotational symmetries forbidden in classical crystallography, such as fivefold or icosahedral symmetry, discovered in 1982 by Dan Shechtman.

Description

Because their diffraction patterns require more than three indices, aperiodic crystals are formally described in a higher-dimensional "superspace", where they appear as periodic structures. This superspace approach led the International Union of Crystallography in 1991 to redefine a crystal as any solid having an essentially discrete diffraction diagram, a definition that accommodates aperiodic crystals.
