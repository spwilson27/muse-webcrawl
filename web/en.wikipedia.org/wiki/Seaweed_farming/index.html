Seaweed farming

Seaweed farming or kelp farming is the practice of cultivating and harvesting seaweeds. It is a form of aquaculture that has grown rapidly since the mid-20th century, with global production of farmed seaweed exceeding 35 million tonnes annually in the 2020s, overwhelmingly in Asia. China, Indonesia, South Korea, the Philippines, and Japan are the leading producers.

Cultivated species and uses

Major farmed species include Japanese kelp (Saccharina japonica), nori (Pyropia species), wakame (Undaria pinnatifida), and the red algae Kappaphycus and Eucheuma, grown for the hydrocolloids carrageenan and agar. Seaweeds are eaten directly as food, and processed into food additives, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, fertilizers, animal feed, and increasingly bioplastics and biofuels.

Methods

Farmers typically seed juvenile seaweed onto ropes, nets, or lines suspended from buoys or rafts in sheltered coastal waters, or grow plants on the seabed. Because seaweeds absorb dissolved nitrogen, phosphorus, and carbon dioxide as they grow, farms require no fertilizer, freshwater, or land, and can improve water quality — a property exploited in integrated multi-trophic aquaculture, where seaweed is co-cultivated with fish or shellfish.

Environmental and economic aspects

Seaweed farming provides income for coastal communities, particularly in Southeast Asia, and is promoted as a climate-friendly industry because of carbon uptake and potential reduction of ocean acidification locally. Concerns include disease (such as "ice-ice" disease in carrageenophytes), genetic pollution of wild populations, shading of seagrass beds, and marine debris from farm gear. Cultivation is expanding in Europe and North America, where wild kelp forests provide habitat that researchers seek to protect.

History

Systematic cultivation began in Japan in the Edo period; the life cycle of nori was elucidated in 1949 by the British phycologist Kathleen Mary Drew-Baker, whose work enabled modern nori farming and is commemorated in Japan. Chinese scientist C. K. Tseng pioneered large-scale kelp cultivation in China from the 1950s.
