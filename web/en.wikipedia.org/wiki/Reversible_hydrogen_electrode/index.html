Reversible hydrogen electrode

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) is a reference electrode, more precisely a subtype of the standard hydrogen electrode, for electrochemical processes. Unlike the standard hydrogen electrode, however, its measured potential does change with the pH of the solution, in accordance with the Nernst equation. The RHE therefore conveniently reports electrode potentials on a scale that already accounts for the pH dependence of proton-coupled reactions.

Principle

The RHE is a hydrogen electrode operating in the same electrolyte as the working electrode: hydrogen gas is bubbled over a catalytically active electrode, usually platinized platinum, immersed in the solution under study. It equilibrates with the H+/H2 couple at the local pH. Its potential relative to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) is:

E(RHE) = E° − 0.05916 × pH (volts, at 25 °C),

so that a potential measured against RHE relates to the SHE scale by E(vs SHE) = E(vs RHE) − 0.05916 × pH. Potentials of pH-dependent reactions — such as the hydrogen evolution reaction and oxygen evolution reaction — fall at the same value on the RHE scale regardless of pH, which makes comparisons between acidic and alkaline experiments straightforward.

Use

The RHE is widely used in research on water electrolysis, fuel cells, corrosion, and electrocatalysis. In practice it can be realized as an actual electrode in the cell (for example, a platinum wire through which electrolytically generated hydrogen is evolved, as in a "hydroflex"-style probe), or computed from a measurement against a conventional reference electrode such as Ag/AgCl or the saturated calomel electrode after calibrating and correcting for pH. Care is needed because its potential depends on hydrogen partial pressure and temperature, and the platinum surface must remain clean and fully wetted.
