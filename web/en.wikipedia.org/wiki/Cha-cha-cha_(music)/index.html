Cha-cha-cha (music)

Cha-cha-cha is a genre of Cuban music. It has been a popular dance music form which developed from the danzón in the early 1950s. The genre is closely related to the mambo, from which its rhythm section technique derives, and it gave rise to the dance of the same name. The music's name imitates the sound made by the dancers' feet shuffling across the floor in the characteristic triple step.

Origins

The cha-cha-cha was created by the Cuban violinist and composer Enrique Jorrín, a member of Orquesta América, at the beginning of the 1950s. Jorrín had been experimenting with the nuevo ritmo (new rhythm) that pianist-arranger Ninón Mondéjar had introduced into the danzón, and he sought a dance music that all dancers could follow. His composition "La engañadora" (1951) is widely considered the first cha-cha-cha; other early pieces include "Silver Star" and "Bululu".

Jorrín described how he composed melodies with the emphasis placed so that the dancers' foot-shuffle became an integral part of the rhythm: the percussion explicitly marks a syncopated figure that the dancers answer with three quick steps — cha, cha, cha — usually played on the güiro and reflected in the drum patterns.

Characteristics

Cha-cha-cha is written in 4/4 time with about 30 measures per minute, slower than mambo. Its ensemble format is the charanga francesa: European classical instruments (French flute and a violin section) combined with an Afro-Cuban rhythm section of piano, double bass, güiro, timbales and congas. Arrangements typically alternate sung verses — often delivered in unison or with light harmonies — with flute and violin passages. Distinctive hits and unison breaks close the phrases, and the güiro scrape articulates the rhythmic cell from which the dance step is derived.

International popularity

The style spread rapidly through recordings by Orquesta América, Orquesta Aragón, and José Fajardo, and within a few years had become a craze in the United States and Europe. Big bands and popular singers recorded cha-cha-cha numbers, and the craze left a permanent mark on ballroom dancing, where the cha-cha remains one of the five International Latin dances. In Cuba the genre continued to evolve, feeding into later styles such as the pachanga and, through New York recording sessions, Latin boogaloo.
