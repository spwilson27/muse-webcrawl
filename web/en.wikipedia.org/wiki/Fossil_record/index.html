Fossil record

The fossil record is the totality of fossils — the preserved remains, impressions and traces of organisms from past geological ages — and their placement within rock formations and sedimentary layers (strata). Studying the fossil record is the principal means by which scientists reconstruct the history of life on Earth: which organisms lived, how they were related, how they changed over time (evolution), and how environments changed with them. The discipline that studies fossils is palaeontology, which draws on geology, biology and geochemistry.

The fossil record is strongly biased: organisms with hard parts (shells, bones, teeth, wood) that lived in depositional environments are far more likely to be preserved than soft-bodied organisms from upland habitats, and most fossils are marine invertebrates. Exceptional deposits called Lagerstätten preserve soft tissues and give rare windows into whole ecosystems.

Development of the concept

Thinkers from Xenophanes to Leonardo da Vinci recognised fossils as remains of once-living organisms, but the modern science began with Nicolas Steno's stratigraphic principles (1669) and William Smith's recognition, around 1800, that strata are characterised by distinctive fossil assemblages (faunal succession), allowing rocks to be correlated across regions. Georges Cuvier demonstrated extinction, and Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859) made the fossil record central evidence for evolution, while noting its incompleteness — a "history of the world imperfectly kept".

What the record shows

The record documents the origin of life by at least 3.4–3.7 billion years ago, the rise of complex cells, the Cambrian diversification of animal body plans, the conquest of land by plants and tetrapods, the reign and extinction of the dinosaurs, five major mass extinctions, and the recent evolution of humans. Transitional fossils — such as Archaeopteryx (birds/dinosaurs), Tiktaalik (fish/tetrapods) and the whale series from Pakicetus onward — document major evolutionary transitions. Radiometric dating of volcanic layers bracketing fossil beds gives the record an absolute timescale.

Gaps and biases

Fossilisation is rare, and erosion, metamorphism and plate subduction destroy evidence, so the record samples only a small fraction of past species. Taphonomy studies these biases. Nonetheless, quantitative analyses show the record is adequate to reveal large-scale patterns of diversification and extinction, especially when combined with molecular phylogenies.
