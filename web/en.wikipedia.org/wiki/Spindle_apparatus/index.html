Spindle apparatus

In cell biology, the spindle apparatus (or mitotic spindle) is the cytoskeletal structure that separates chromosomes during cell division. It forms during mitosis and meiosis from microtubules nucleated at the two centrosomes (spindle poles) and is responsible for moving sister chromatids to opposite poles of the cell so that each daughter cell receives a complete genome.

Components

The spindle contains three classes of microtubules: kinetochore microtubules, which attach to chromosomes at their kinetochores; polar microtubules, which interdigitate from opposite poles and push the poles apart; and astral microtubules, which anchor the poles to the cell cortex. Motor proteins of the kinesin and dynein families organise and slide these filaments.

Function and checkpoint

During metaphase, chromosomes align at the spindle equator under balanced tension. The spindle assembly checkpoint monitors kinetochore attachment and tension, delaying anaphase until every chromosome is correctly bioriented — a safeguard against aneuploidy. The molecular search-and-capture dynamics of microtubules, driven by their dynamic instability, allow the spindle to assemble within minutes.
