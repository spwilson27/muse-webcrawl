General will

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The general will (volonté générale) is a concept in political philosophy, central to Jean-Jacques Rousseau's The Social Contract (1762), denoting the will of the people as a whole directed at the common good, as distinct from the mere sum of private interests (the "will of all"). For Rousseau, legitimate law is the expression of the general will; citizens, in obeying laws they have prescribed for themselves as members of the sovereign, remain free — while the recalcitrant may, in the notorious phrase, be "forced to be free".

Interpretations and influence

Interpreters divide over whether the general will is a democratic ideal discoverable by deliberation (voters asking what the common good requires, not what they privately want) or a dangerous fiction licensing coercion in the people's name — the reading that made Rousseau a contested father of both participatory democracy and, in Jacob Talmon's thesis, "totalitarian democracy". The concept influenced Kant's public reason, the French Revolution's rhetoric, and modern deliberative-democracy theory; its operational problem (how the general will is known — by vote? by a legislator-prophet? by consensus?) remains the concept's open wound.
