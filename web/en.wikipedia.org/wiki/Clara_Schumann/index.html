Clara Schumann

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Clara Schumann in the 1850s, daguerreotype portrait: the pianist-composer seated, lace collar at her throat]

Clara Josephine Schumann (née Wieck; 13 September 1819 – 20 May 1896) was a German pianist and composer, one of the most celebrated concert pianists of the nineteenth century and, in the modern reassessment, a significant composer whose work her century preferred to applaud her for performing rather than writing. Over a 61-year concert career she reshaped the piano recital — playing from memory, programming serious repertoire — and championed the music of her husband Robert Schumann and their friend Johannes Brahms.

Her own works include the Piano Concerto in A minor (premiered 1835, when she was 16, with Mendelssohn conducting), a piano trio, three Romances for violin and piano (1853), songs, and the piano pieces she played across Europe. Her diaries and her edition of Robert's works are primary documents of Romantic music.

Prodigy and marriage

Raised as a commercial prodigy by her father Friedrich Wieck, Clara debuted at the Leipzig Gewandhaus at nine and toured Paris and Vienna as a girl. Her marriage to Wieck's pupil Robert Schumann in 1840, against her father's furious legal opposition, began a partnership — eight children, joint diaries, mutual premieres — ended by his madness and death in an asylum in 1856. Thereafter she supported the family by the keyboard, in a black dress that became her uniform.

Brahms and legacy

Brahms, who arrived at the Schumanns' door in 1853 aged 20, loved Clara for forty years; their correspondence (much of it destroyed at her wish) and her premieres of his works are central to his career. She taught at Frankfurt's Hoch Conservatory from 1878. Modern recordings and scholarship have restored her compositions — long dismissed, including by herself, as women's work unworthy of ambition — to the repertoire.
