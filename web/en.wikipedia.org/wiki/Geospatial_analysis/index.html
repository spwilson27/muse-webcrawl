Geospatial analysis

Geospatial analysis
FieldGeography, geoinformatics
ToolsGIS, remote sensing, spatial statistics
Core conceptsSpatial autocorrelation, interpolation, topology

Geospatial analysis is an approach to applying statistical analysis and other analytical techniques to data that has a geographic or spatial aspect. It typically involves the use of geographic information systems (GIS), remote sensing imagery, and methods from spatial statistics to uncover patterns, relationships and trends that are not apparent in non-spatial data.

History

The discipline's celebrated founding example is John Snow's 1854 map of cholera deaths around the Broad Street pump in London, which identified contaminated water as the source of the outbreak. Modern geospatial analysis emerged in the 1960s with the first computerised GIS (the Canada Geographic Information System, developed under Roger Tomlinson) and matured with the spread of satellite remote sensing, digital cartography and, from the 2000s, cheap GPS-equipped devices and location data.

Core concepts

Analysis builds on the observation summarised in Tobler's first law of geography — "everything is related to everything else, but near things are more related than distant things" — formalised through measures of spatial autocorrelation such as Moran's I. Key techniques include:

  • Spatial interpolation — estimating values at unsampled locations (inverse distance weighting, kriging)
  • Hot spot and cluster analysis — detecting statistically significant concentrations (e.g. Getis-Ord Gi*)
  • Overlay and suitability analysis — combining layers to site facilities or assess risk
  • Network analysis — routing, service areas and accessibility on transport networks
  • Spatial regression — models that account for spatial dependence and heterogeneity, such as geographically weighted regression
  • Surface and terrain analysis — slope, aspect, watershed and viewshed from elevation models

Applications

Geospatial analysis is used in epidemiology and public health, urban and transport planning, criminology, market and site selection, precision agriculture, environmental monitoring, disaster response, archaeology and natural resource management. The growth of volunteered geographic information, open street data and cloud computing platforms has made spatial analysis a routine part of data science.

Challenges

Well-known pitfalls include the modifiable areal unit problem (results that change with the choice of zones or scale), the ecological fallacy, edge effects, spatial data quality and uncertainty, and the privacy implications of fine-grained location data.
