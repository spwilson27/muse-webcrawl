Dispensationalism

Dispensationalism is a theological framework within evangelical Christianity that views history as divided into distinct periods ("dispensations") in which God interacts with humanity in different ways, and that maintains a sharp distinction between Israel and the Church. It interprets the Bible using a literal hermeneutic, especially regarding prophecy.

Dispensationalism
Origin: 19th century, John Nelson Darby
Key text: Scofield Reference Bible (1909)

History

The system was developed by John Nelson Darby and the Plymouth Brethren in the 1830s, spread through prophecy conferences, and popularised in America by the Scofield Reference Bible (1909), Dallas Theological Seminary, and figures such as Lewis Sperry Chafer. The Left Behind novels brought its pretribulation rapture teaching to a mass audience.

Core beliefs

  • History divided into dispensations — commonly Innocence, Conscience, Human Government, Promise, Law, Grace, and the future Kingdom.
  • A distinction between God's programme for national Israel and for the Church.
  • The rapture of the Church before a seven-year Tribulation, followed by Christ's millennial kingdom centred on Jerusalem.
  • Consistent literal interpretation of Scripture.

Varieties and criticism

Forms range from classical and revised dispensationalism to progressive dispensationalism, which softens the Israel–Church divide. Covenant theologians and other critics dispute its hermeneutics and eschatology, while it remains influential in American evangelicalism and Christian Zionism.
