Overview

A solstice is the moment when the Sun reaches its greatest distance north or south of the celestial equator — the extreme points of its annual journey, caused by Earth's axis being tilted about 23.44° to its orbit. Solstices occur twice a year, around 20–21 June and 21–22 December.

At the June solstice the Sun stands directly overhead at noon on the Tropic of Cancer, the Northern Hemisphere has its longest day, and the North Pole has been in continuous daylight since the equinox. At the December solstice the situation reverses: the Sun is overhead on the Tropic of Capricorn and the south has its longest day.

Name and culture

The word comes from Latin solstitium, 'the Sun stands still', because for a few days around each solstice the Sun's noon altitude and its rising point on the horizon barely change before the trend reverses. Solstices anchor many festivals and monuments: midsummer celebrations cluster on the June solstice, midwinter ones on the December solstice, and structures such as Stonehenge are aligned on solstitial sunrise.
