Marsilio Ficino (Latin name: Marsilius Ficinus; 19 October 1433 – 1 October 1499) was an Italian scholar and Catholic priest who was one of the most influential humanist philosophers of the early Italian Renaissance. An astrologer and a reviver of Neoplatonism in touch with the major academics of his day, he was the first translator of Plato's complete extant works into Latin. His Florentine Academy, an attempt to revive Plato's Academy, influenced the direction and tenor of the Italian Renaissance and the development of European philosophy.

Life and work

Ficino's father was a physician to Cosimo de' Medici, who became the young scholar's patron. Cosimo commissioned Ficino to translate Plato, giving him a villa at Careggi that became the seat of the Platonic Academy. Ficino's Latin Plato appeared in 1484, followed by his translation of Plotinus's Enneads (1492) and renderings of Hermetic, Orphic, and other ancient texts, which he presented as parts of a single "ancient theology" (prisca theologia) harmonizing with Christianity.

His major original work, the Theologia Platonica de immortalitate animorum (Platonic Theology, written 1469–74, published 1482), argues for the immortality of the soul and places the soul at the centre of a fivefold hierarchy of being. His De amore, a commentary on Plato's Symposium, popularized the notion of "Platonic love" across Europe. His Three Books on Life (1489) combined medicine, astrology, and natural magic, advising scholars how to maintain health and draw down beneficial celestial influences. Ficino was ordained a priest in 1473 and wrote extensively in defence of Christianity, notably De Christiana religione. His voluminous letters circulated his ideas among humanists across Europe.
