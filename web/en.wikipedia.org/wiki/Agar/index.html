Agar (also agar-agar) is a jelly-like substance consisting of polysaccharides obtained from the cell walls of some species of red algae, primarily from the genera Gelidium and Gracilaria. It is a mixture of two components: the linear polysaccharide agarose and a heterogeneous mixture of smaller molecules called agaropectin. Agar has been used in East Asian cuisine for centuries and, since the late nineteenth century, as the standard solidifying agent in microbiological culture media.

History

Agar was discovered in Japan in the 1650s, where it is known as kanten. Its introduction to microbiology is credited to Fanny Hesse, who suggested it to her husband Walther Hesse, a collaborator of Robert Koch, as a replacement for gelatin, which melted at incubation temperatures and was digested by many bacteria. Agar melts at about 85 °C and sets near 35–40 °C, a hysteresis ideal for plating cultures incubated at human body temperature.

Microbiological use

An agar plate is a Petri dish containing agar plus nutrients on which microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi are cultured. Selective and differential formulations — such as MacConkey agar and blood agar — allow identification of organisms. Purified agarose is also used in gel electrophoresis to separate DNA and proteins by size.

Culinary use

Agar is used as a vegetarian substitute for gelatin in desserts such as Japanese anmitsu and yōkan, jellies, ice creams, and as a thickener in soups. As a food additive it carries the E number E406.
