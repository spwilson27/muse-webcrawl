Temperature-dependent sex determination

Temperature-dependent sex determination (TSD) is a type of environmental sex determination in which the temperatures experienced during embryonic or larval development determine the sex of the offspring. It is observed in reptiles and in some fish. Unlike the chromosomal systems familiar from mammals and birds, TSD species generally lack sex chromosomes and a genetically triggered master sex switch; instead, incubation temperature during a thermosensitive period of development directs the gonads to develop as testes or ovaries.

Patterns

Three patterns are recognised. In Pattern IA, found in most turtles, males are produced at cooler incubation temperatures and females at warmer ones (MF pattern). In Pattern IB, found in the tuatara and some lizards, females are produced at cooler temperatures and males at warmer ones (FM pattern). In Pattern II, found in crocodilians and some turtles and lizards, females are produced at both low and high temperatures and males at intermediate temperatures (FMF pattern). The temperature at which the sex ratio is balanced is called the pivotal temperature.

Mechanism

The thermosensitive period typically falls in the middle third of development. Temperature is thought to act through temperature-sensitive enzymes and gene regulation: the enzyme aromatase, which converts androgens to oestrogens, is a key target — high aromatase activity at female-producing temperatures drives ovarian development. Epigenetic mechanisms are also involved: in the red-eared slider turtle, the histone demethylase KDM6B regulates the male-pathway gene Dmrt1 in response to temperature, linking temperature directly to chromatin state. Sex steroids can override temperature in many species, showing that the pathways converge.

Evolution and ecology

TSD is thought to be the ancestral condition for amniote reptiles, with genotypic sex determination evolving repeatedly from it. The leading adaptive explanation is the Charnov–Bull hypothesis: TSD is favoured when the developmental temperature differentially affects the fitness of males and females — for example, when warm incubation produces larger hatchlings and large size benefits one sex more than the other.

Climate change

Because sex ratios in TSD species hinge on nest temperature, climate warming can strongly skew populations. In northern Great Barrier Reef green sea turtle populations, warming sands have produced cohorts that are more than 99% female. Such feminisation raises concerns about future breeding potential, although behavioural shifts (nesting earlier, choosing shadier sites) and evolutionary adaptation of pivotal temperatures may partly buffer the effect. In some Australian skinks, warming has instead produced sex-reversed individuals, illustrating varied outcomes.
