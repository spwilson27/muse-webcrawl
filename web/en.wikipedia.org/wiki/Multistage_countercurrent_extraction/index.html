Multistage countercurrent extraction

Multistage countercurrent extraction is a liquid–liquid extraction arrangement in which two immiscible liquid phases flow in opposite directions through a series of contact stages, so that the extracted material is progressively concentrated in one phase while the depleted feed leaves at the opposite end. It is a standard unit operation of chemical engineering, described in classic texts such as Treybal's Liquid Extraction and in Perry's Chemical Engineers' Handbook.

Principle

In a single extraction stage, mixing a feed with a solvent and letting the phases settle achieves only partial transfer of the solute, limited by the distribution coefficient. By arranging stages countercurrently — fresh solvent meets the most depleted raffinate, while nearly saturated extract meets fresh feed — each stage maintains a favourable concentration driving force. This yields far better recovery and a more concentrated product for a given amount of solvent than single-stage or crosscurrent (parallel-batch) extraction, analogous to the advantage of countercurrent heat exchange and absorption columns.

Design

The number of theoretical stages required for a target separation is determined graphically on a ternary phase diagram (for example, with the Hunter–Nash or Maloney–Schubert methods) or by stepwise equilibrium calculations. Equipment implementing the cascade includes trains of mixer-settlers, in which phases are mechanically mixed and then separated by gravity in each stage, and differential contactors such as spray, packed, pulsed, and centrifugal columns, in which the countercurrent flow is continuous and stages are notional.

Applications

Countercurrent extraction is used in hydrometallurgy (solvent extraction of copper, uranium, and rare-earth elements), nuclear fuel reprocessing (the PUREX process), petrochemical separation of aromatics, recovery of antibiotics and other pharmaceuticals, and food processing such as decaffeination and edible-oil refining.
