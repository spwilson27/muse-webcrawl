Debasement

[Image: Roman denarii showing declining silver content over the Imperial period]

Debasement is the practice of lowering the intrinsic value of a coin, especially one made of a precious metal, by reducing the amount of metal it contains while keeping its face value the same. The minting authority profits from the difference between the face value of the new coins and the cost of their reduced metal content—a form of seigniorage.

History

Debasement was common throughout the ancient and medieval world as rulers sought revenue without explicit taxation. The Roman denarius, originally nearly pure silver under Augustus, was progressively reduced until by the mid-3rd century AD the antoninianus contained only a few percent silver, contributing to the inflation of the Crisis of the Third Century. Diocletian and Constantine responded with monetary reforms, introducing the gold solidus, which remained stable for centuries.

In England, the Great Debasement of 1542–1551 under Henry VIII and Edward VI drastically reduced the silver and gold content of the coinage to finance wars with France and Scotland, generating large profits for the Crown but fuelling inflation; Elizabeth I restored the currency in 1560–61. Debasement tends to trigger Gresham's law: older, full-weight coins are hoarded or melted down and disappear from circulation, leaving only the debased currency in use.

Modern usage

In modern commentary the term is extended to fiat currencies, where critics describe large increases in the money supply as a debasement of the currency's purchasing power, though no physical coinage is involved.
