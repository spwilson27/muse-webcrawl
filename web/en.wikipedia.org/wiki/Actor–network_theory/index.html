Actor–network theory

Actor–network theory (ANT) is a theoretical and methodological approach in the social sciences, originating in science and technology studies, which holds that the social and natural worlds exist in constantly shifting networks of relationships. ANT treats human and non-human entities — people, organizations, machines, texts, animals, and natural phenomena — symmetrically as actants capable of acting within a network.

Origins

ANT was developed in the 1980s by scholars including Bruno Latour, Michel Callon, and John Law, centered at the École des Mines in Paris. Influential works include Latour's Laboratory Life (with Steve Woolgar) and Science in Action (1987), Callon's study of scallop fishing in St Brieuc Bay, and Law's work on Portuguese maritime expansion.

Key concepts

  • Generalized symmetry — human and non-human actors are analyzed in the same terms; agency is distributed across the network rather than residing in individuals alone.
  • Actant — any entity, human or not, that does something in a network, borrowed from semiotics.
  • Translation — the process by which actors align others' interests with their own, enrolling them into a network (Callon's moments of problematization, interessement, enrolment, and mobilization).
  • Black-boxing — when a network stabilizes, its internal complexity becomes invisible and it functions as a single unit.
  • Obligatory passage point — a node through which other actors must pass to pursue their goals.

Criticism and influence

ANT has been criticized for downplaying power structures, morality, and human intentionality, and for its descriptive focus. Nevertheless it has been highly influential across sociology, geography, information systems, organization studies, anthropology, and design, and remains a central framework for understanding sociotechnical systems.
