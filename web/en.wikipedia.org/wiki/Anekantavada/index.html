Anekantavada

TraditionJain philosophy
Meaning"Many-sidedness" / non-absolutism
Related doctrinesSyādvāda, Nayavāda

Anekāntavāda (Sanskrit: अनेकान्तवाद, "many-sidedness") is one of the most important and fundamental doctrines of Jainism. It refers to the principle that truth and reality are complex and have multiple aspects, so that no single point of view can claim to express the whole truth. The doctrine is often illustrated by the parable of the blind men and an elephant, each describing only the part he touches.

Doctrine

Anekantavada is closely connected with two further doctrines: syādvāda, the theory of conditioned predication, which prefixes propositions with syāt ("in some respects", "may be"); and nayavāda, the theory of partial standpoints (naya), according to which every judgment reflects one particular viewpoint among many. Combined, these give the sevenfold scheme of predication known as the saptabhaṅgī.

History and influence

The roots of the doctrine lie in the teachings of Mahavira (6th–5th century BCE), who answered questions about metaphysics with qualifications. Jain philosophers such as Samantabhadra, Siddhasena Divakara, Akalanka and Haribhadra systematised the doctrine in debate with Hindu and Buddhist schools, which often accused the Jains of evasiveness; the Jains replied that one-sided (ekānta) assertions are a form of intellectual violence. Mahatma Gandhi was influenced by the concept, which he linked to religious tolerance and non-violence (ahimsa).
