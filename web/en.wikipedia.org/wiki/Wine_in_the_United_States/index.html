Wine in the United States
Global rank: 4th largest producer
Key states: California, Washington, Oregon, New York
Notable regions: Napa Valley, Sonoma, Willamette Valley, Finger Lakes
Wineries: over 10,000

Wine in the United States has been produced for over 400 years, and the country is today the world's fourth-largest wine producer after Italy, France and Spain. California accounts for roughly 80–90% of production, but wine is made in all fifty states, from the Pinot noir of Oregon's Willamette Valley and the Riesling of New York's Finger Lakes to the Bordeaux-style blends of Washington State.

History

European settlers found abundant native grape species such as Vitis labrusca and Vitis riparia, though wines from them differed in flavour from European Vitis vinifera. Repeated attempts to plant vinifera failed in the east because of phylloxera and disease, while Spanish missions successfully established vinifera in California from 1769. The 19th century saw booms in Ohio's Catawba wines, Missouri, and California after the Gold Rush. Prohibition (1920–1933) devastated the industry and shifted production toward jug wines and sacramental use.

Modern era

Recovery came after the Second World War, guided by research at the University of California, Davis. The 1976 Judgment of Paris, in which California wines defeated French wines in blind tastings, established the reputation of Napa Valley internationally. American Viticultural Areas (AVAs) now number in the hundreds, and American wine is regulated by the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau, with labelling rules requiring, for example, 75% of the named grape variety.

Consumption

The United States is one of the largest wine markets in the world by total consumption, and varietally labelled wines — Cabernet Sauvignon, Chardonnay, Merlot, Pinot noir and Zinfandel among the most prominent — dominate its production and marketing.
