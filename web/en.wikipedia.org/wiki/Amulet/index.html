An amulet, also known as a good luck charm or phylactery, is an object believed to confer protection upon its possessor. The word comes from the Latin amuletum, which Pliny the Elder described as "an object that protects a person from trouble." Amulets may be made of gemstones, metal, plants, animal parts, or written words, and are worn (often as jewellery or pendants), carried, or placed in homes and vehicles.

History and examples

Some of the oldest amulets come from ancient Egypt, where scarabs, the ankh, and the Eye of Horus were placed with the dead and worn by the living. In the ancient Near East and Mediterranean, apotropaic images such as the gorgoneion warded off evil; the blue-glass nazar against the evil eye and the hamsa hand remain widespread in the Middle East and North Africa. Religious traditions worldwide use amulets: Catholic sacramentals and crucifixes, Jewish mezuzot and hamsas, Islamic ta'wiz containing Quranic verses, Buddhist votive tablets in Thailand, Taoist fulu talismans in China, and Shinto omamori in Japan.

Amulet and talisman

In common usage "amulet" and "talisman" overlap, but a frequent distinction holds that amulets protect against harm while talismans attract power, luck, or a specific benefit. Belief in amulets spans recorded history and persists in folk practice worldwide, whatever the official stance of religious authorities, which has varied from endorsement to condemnation as superstition.
