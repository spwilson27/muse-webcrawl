Dominance (genetics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In genetics, dominance is the phenomenon of one variant (allele) of a gene on a chromosome masking or overriding the effect of a different variant of the same gene on the other copy of the chromosome. The first variant is termed dominant and the second recessive. This state of having two different variants of the same gene on each chromosome is originally caused by a mutation in one of the genes, either new (de novo) or inherited.

Dominance is a key concept in Mendelian inheritance and classical genetics. The terms autosomal dominant or autosomal recessive are used to describe gene variants on non-sex chromosomes (autosomes) and their associated traits, while those on sex chromosomes are termed X-linked dominant, X-linked recessive or Y-linked.

Background

Diploid organisms have two homologous copies of each chromosome, inherited from each parent, and thus two copies of each gene. If both copies are the same allele, the organism is homozygous at that locus; if they differ, it is heterozygous. In a heterozygote, when one allele completely masks the phenotypic effect of the other, the masking allele is dominant and the masked allele is recessive. For example, in Mendel's peas, the allele for purple flowers is dominant over the allele for white flowers, so heterozygous plants have purple flowers.

Types of dominance

Complete dominance

In complete dominance, the phenotype of the heterozygote is indistinguishable from that of the dominant homozygote. Mendel's pea traits behaved in this way.

Incomplete dominance

In incomplete (partial) dominance, the heterozygote shows an intermediate phenotype. In snapdragons, crossing red-flowered (RR) with white-flowered (rr) plants gives pink-flowered (Rr) offspring.

Codominance

In codominance, both alleles are fully expressed in the heterozygote. The ABO blood group system is a standard example: individuals with genotype IAIB have blood type AB, expressing both A and B antigens.

Multiple alleles

Many genes have more than two alleles in a population, and these alleles may form a dominance hierarchy. The ABO system has three common alleles (IA, IB, i), with IA and IB codominant and both dominant over i.

Molecular basis

At the molecular level, dominance often arises because one functional copy of a gene produces enough gene product for a normal phenotype (haplosufficiency), so a loss-of-function allele is recessive. Conversely, dominant traits may result from gain-of-function mutations, from haploinsufficiency (where one copy is not enough), or from dominant-negative effects in which a mutant protein interferes with the normal one. Dominance is therefore a property of alleles in relation to a particular phenotype, not an intrinsic property of the allele itself; the same allele may be dominant for one trait and recessive for another, as with the sickle-cell allele, which is recessive for sickle-cell disease but codominant at the level of haemoglobin proteins.

Dominance in human disease

Many inherited disorders are classified by their dominance pattern. Autosomal dominant conditions such as Huntington's disease and Marfan syndrome manifest in heterozygotes, while autosomal recessive conditions such as cystic fibrosis and sickle-cell disease require two copies of the disease allele. X-linked recessive conditions, including haemophilia and Duchenne muscular dystrophy, primarily affect males.
