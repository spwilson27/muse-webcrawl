Loa loa is a filarial nematode (roundworm) that parasitises humans and other primates in the rainforest belt of West and Central Africa. It is commonly known as the eye worm, because adult worms may migrate across the subconjunctival tissues of the eye, where they become dramatically visible. The disease it causes is called loiasis, one of the filarial diseases grouped among the neglected tropical diseases. An estimated 12–13 million people are infected.

Life cycle

The parasite is transmitted by the bites of deer flies of the genus Chrysops (principally C. silacea and C. dimidiata), which feed during the day. Infective larvae enter the skin at the bite site and mature over several months into adult worms that wander through subcutaneous tissues. Females measure 40–70 mm in length and release sheathed microfilariae into the blood, which exhibit diurnal periodicity, peaking in the blood around midday when the fly vectors feed. Unlike many other filariae, Loa loa does not harbour the bacterial endosymbiont Wolbachia.

Clinical features

Many infections are asymptomatic. Characteristic manifestations include Calabar swellings — transient, itchy, localised angioedema-like swellings caused by migrating adults — and the passage of a worm across the eye, which is alarming but usually causes little lasting damage. Chronic infection may be associated with eosinophilia, and rarely with kidney, heart or neurological complications.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on identifying microfilariae in a daytime blood sample, detecting the worm in the eye or a biopsy, or serology. Diethylcarbamazine (DEC) is the drug of choice for individual treatment because it kills both microfilariae and adult worms, but in patients with very high microfilarial loads it can provoke severe adverse reactions, including potentially fatal encephalopathy — a complication that seriously complicates mass ivermectin campaigns for onchocerciasis in co-endemic areas, since ivermectin can trigger the same reactions in heavily infected people.
