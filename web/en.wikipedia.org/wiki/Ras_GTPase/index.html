Ras GTPase

Ras GTPases are a family of small GTP-binding proteins that act as molecular switches inside cells, cycling between an active GTP-bound state and an inactive GDP-bound state. They are central regulators of signal transduction pathways controlling cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival.

FamilyRas superfamily of small GTPases
FunctionMolecular switch in signal transduction
ActivatorsGuanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs), e.g. SOS
InactivatorsGTPase-activating proteins (GAPs), e.g. NF1
Key membersHRAS, KRAS, NRAS

Mechanism

Ras proteins are activated when a guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) such as SOS promotes the release of GDP and its replacement with GTP. In the GTP-bound conformation, Ras binds and activates downstream effectors, most famously the RAF kinases, which trigger the MAPK/ERK cascade. Ras is switched off by GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs), which accelerate hydrolysis of GTP to GDP.

Role in cancer

Mutations in Ras genes are among the most common genetic alterations in human cancer. Activating point mutations, frequently at codons 12, 13, or 61, lock Ras in its GTP-bound state and drive uncontrolled cell growth. KRAS mutations are especially prevalent in pancreatic, colorectal, and lung cancers, and Ras was historically considered "undruggable" until covalent KRAS G12C inhibitors such as sotorasib reached clinical use.

Membrane association

Ras proteins are tethered to the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane by lipid modifications, including farnesylation of a C-terminal CAAX motif and, for some isoforms, palmitoylation. This membrane localization is essential for their signalling function.
