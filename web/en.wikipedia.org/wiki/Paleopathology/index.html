Paleopathology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Paleopathology, also spelled palaeopathology, is the study of ancient diseases and injuries in organisms through the examination of fossils, mummified tissue, skeletal remains, and the analysis of coprolites. The discipline draws on archaeology, anthropology, medicine, and veterinary science to reconstruct the health, diet, and living conditions of past populations.

History

Interest in the diseases of the ancient past dates to the nineteenth century, when physicians such as Marc Armand Ruffer (1859–1917), often called the founder of modern paleopathology, examined Egyptian mummies and identified conditions including tuberculosis, schistosomiasis, and arterial disease. In the twentieth century, researchers such as A. T. Sandison, Don Brothwell, and Keith Manchester developed systematic methods for diagnosing disease in skeletal remains.

The field expanded rapidly with the advent of radiography, computed tomography, and ancient DNA analysis, which allow researchers to detect pathogens such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Yersinia pestis directly in archaeological material.

Methods

Paleopathologists examine bones and teeth for lesions characteristic of specific conditions: periosteal reactions may indicate infection; cribra orbitalia and porotic hyperostosis are associated with anemia; dental enamel hypoplasia records episodes of childhood stress or malnutrition. Mummified soft tissue preserves evidence of diseases such as smallpox, atherosclerosis, and cancer that leave no trace on the skeleton.

Because many diseases produce similar skeletal changes, diagnosis is often probabilistic, and researchers speak of the "osteological paradox": a skeleton bearing healed lesions may represent an individual healthy enough to survive a disease episode, while one without lesions may have died before bone changes could develop.

Significant findings

Studies have documented tuberculosis in pre-Columbian America, leprosy in medieval Europe, syphilis-like treponemal disease on several continents, and osteoarthritis and trauma in Neanderthal populations. Egyptian mummies have yielded evidence of cancer, parasites, and dental abscesses. Coprolites (fossilized feces) preserve intestinal parasite eggs that reveal the sanitary conditions of ancient settlements.
