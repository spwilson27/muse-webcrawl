Child-centred education is an approach to schooling that places the child's interests, needs, and developmental stages at the centre of the learning process, rather than a fixed curriculum delivered by an authoritative teacher. In child-centred classrooms, teachers act as facilitators who arrange environments and activities through which children learn by doing, exploring, and playing, at their own pace. The approach underpins kindergarten practice, Montessori and Steiner (Waldorf) schools, and much progressive primary education.

History

The intellectual roots lie in Jean-Jacques Rousseau's Émile (1762), which argued that children have their own ways of seeing and thinking and that education should follow nature rather than impose adult ideas prematurely. Johann Heinrich Pestalozzi and Friedrich Froebel — who founded the kindergarten in 1837 — developed methods of learning through activity, song, and play. In the early 20th century, John Dewey's laboratory school and writings made "learning by doing" and the child's experience the basis of progressive education, while Maria Montessori designed self-directed materials and prepared environments in which children choose their own work. Later, Jean Piaget's account of developmental stages gave the movement a psychological foundation.

Principles and practice

Common features include activity-based and play-based learning, integrated rather than compartmentalised subjects, flexible grouping, observation-based assessment by the teacher, and an emphasis on social development, creativity, and intrinsic motivation. Official endorsements, such as the United Kingdom's Plowden Report of 1967, helped embed child-centred methods in primary schools.

Criticism

Critics argue that purely child-centred approaches can neglect systematic instruction in foundational knowledge — particularly explicit teaching of reading and mathematics — and may disadvantage children from less advantaged homes. Debates between child-centred and knowledge-centred (teacher-directed) models remain a recurring theme in education policy.
