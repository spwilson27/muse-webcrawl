Post-growth

Post-growth
FieldEcological economics, political economy
Key ideasWellbeing economy, steady-state economy, degrowth, doughnut economics
Key figuresHerman Daly, Tim Jackson, Kate Raworth

Post-growth is a stance within economics and social thought that holds that societies should move beyond the pursuit of aggregate economic growth — typically measured as growth of gross domestic product (GDP) — as their central objective, and instead organise economic life around human wellbeing, social equity, and ecological sustainability within planetary boundaries. Post-growth is an umbrella term encompassing related positions such as the steady-state economy, degrowth, agrowth, and the wellbeing economy.

Post-growth thought emphasises wellbeing and ecological limits over GDP growth.

Background

The critique of growth as an overriding goal draws on several strands: the ecological economics of Herman Daly, who argued the economy is a subsystem of a finite ecosphere; the 1972 Club of Rome report The Limits to Growth; and evidence that beyond a certain level of income, further GDP growth correlates weakly with life satisfaction and other measures of wellbeing (the Easterlin paradox). Tim Jackson's Prosperity Without Growth (2009), a report for the UK Sustainable Development Commission, became a landmark text of the movement.

Positions

  • Steady-state economy: stabilising population and material throughput at ecologically sustainable levels.
  • Degrowth: a planned reduction of energy and resource use in wealthy economies to bring throughput within ecological limits while improving wellbeing.
  • Agrowth: agnosticism about GDP outcomes — policy should target social and ecological goals directly, whether GDP rises or falls as a side effect.
  • Doughnut economics: Kate Raworth's framework of a social foundation and ecological ceiling within which economies should operate.

Policy proposals

Common proposals include measuring progress with indicators beyond GDP (such as the Genuine Progress Indicator or wellbeing dashboards), work-time reduction, universal basic services, green investment directed by need rather than growth expectations, and reform of monetary and welfare institutions that depend structurally on growth. New Zealand, Scotland, Wales, Iceland, Finland and the Netherlands have participated in the Wellbeing Economy Governments partnership (WEGo).

Criticism

Critics argue that growth remains essential for poverty alleviation, funding public services and the green transition, and that green growth through decoupling resource use from GDP is achievable. Post-growth proponents reply that absolute decoupling at the required rate and scale lacks empirical support.
