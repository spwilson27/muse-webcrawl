Basmachi movement
Dates1916 – mid-1930s
RegionCentral Asia (Turkestan)
OpponentsRussian Empire, then the Soviet Red Army
Key figuresEnver Pasha, Ibrahim Bek, Junaid Khan

The Basmachi movement (from an Uzbek word for bandits or raiders) was a loosely coordinated uprising of the Muslim peoples of Central Asia against Russian imperial and then Soviet rule. It grew out of the 1916 revolt against Tsarist conscription and erupted fully after the Bolshevik seizure of Tashkent and the destruction of the Kokand autonomy in 1918.

Course of the revolt

Basmachi bands operated across the Fergana Valley, Bukhara, and Khiva under local commanders (kurbashi). The movement briefly gained central leadership under the Ottoman officer Enver Pasha, who arrived in 1921 hoping to build a pan-Turkic state but was killed fighting the Red Army near Baldzhuan in August 1922. Soviet commanders including Mikhail Frunze combined military campaigns with political concessions and amnesties; organised resistance in Uzbekistan and Tajikistan was largely crushed by the mid-1920s, though Ibrahim Bek held out in Tajikistan until 1931 and cross-border raids from Afghanistan continued into the 1930s.

Legacy

Soviet historiography depicted the Basmachi as bandits and reactionaries, while post-Soviet Central Asian states have reassessed them as early national resistance fighters.
