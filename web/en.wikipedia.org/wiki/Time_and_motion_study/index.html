Time and motion study

A time and motion study is a business-efficiency technique that combines the motion study developed by Frank Bunker Gilbreth and Lillian Moller Gilbreth with the time study methods of Frederick Winslow Taylor. It analyses a job by breaking it into its component steps, measuring the time each step takes, and eliminating wasted movement, in order to establish a standard method and a standard time for the task.

Origins

Taylor, the founder of scientific management, used stopwatch time study from the 1880s to set task times and piece rates. The Gilbreths, working in the same years, filmed workers and analysed their movements frame by frame, identifying seventeen basic motions they called therbligs ("Gilbreth" nearly reversed) — such as "reach", "grasp", "transport loaded", and "search" — and sought to eliminate ineffective ones. Frank Gilbreth famously reduced the motions in bricklaying from eighteen to five, tripling productivity. Using light trails attached to workers' hands (chronocyclegraphs), the Gilbreths produced three-dimensional models of motion paths.

Practice

In a combined study, analysts first chart the process, then observe and time repeated cycles, rating the worker's pace against a normal performance, and adding allowances for fatigue and delay to arrive at a standard time. The standard is then used for planning, costing, incentive pay, and line balancing. The field later developed predetermined motion-time systems such as MTM (Methods-Time Measurement, 1948) and MODAPTS, which assign times to basic motions without a stopwatch.

Criticism and legacy

Time and motion study was resisted by workers and unions as a tool of speed-up and surveillance, and critics from Harry Braverman onward argued it "deskills" labour by transferring knowledge of the work from craftsmen to management. Nevertheless, its descendants — ergonomics, work measurement, and modern industrial engineering — remain foundational to manufacturing, logistics, and service operations.
