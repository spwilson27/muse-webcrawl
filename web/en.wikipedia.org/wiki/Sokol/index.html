Sokol

The Sokol movement combined gymnastics with Slavic national and democratic ideals.

Sokol (Czech for "falcon") is a gymnastics and physical-education movement founded in Prague in 1862 by Miroslav Tyrš and Jindřich Fügner. Inspired in part by German Turnen and classical ideals, Sokol promoted physical fitness, discipline, and moral development under the slogan "a sound mind in a sound body," while serving as a vehicle for Czech national revival within the Habsburg monarchy. Its members — addressed as brothers and sisters — combined gymnastics training with lectures, libraries, and cultural activities.

Growth and slets

The movement spread rapidly among Slavic peoples — to Slovenia, Poland, Serbia, Croatia, Bulgaria, and (as Sokil) Ukraine — and to Czech and Slovak emigrant communities in the United States and elsewhere. Its signature events are the slets (from a word for a flocking of birds): mass festivals, held in Prague from 1882 onward, at which thousands of gymnasts perform synchronized exercises before huge crowds. The great Strahov Stadium in Prague was built to host them. Sokol prepared its members physically and organizationally in ways that later supported the Czechoslovak Legions and the independence movement of 1918; many Sokol members joined the resistance in both world wars.

Suppression and revival

Sokol was banned by the Nazi occupation authorities in 1941 and again suppressed after the communist takeover of Czechoslovakia in 1948, when its slets were replaced by state-run Spartakiads. The organization was revived after 1989; the Czech Sokol Community (Česká obec sokolská) once again organizes training and slets, the most recent of which have drawn tens of thousands of participants, and Sokol organizations remain active in Slovakia, the United States, and other countries.
