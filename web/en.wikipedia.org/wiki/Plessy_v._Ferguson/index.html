Plessy v. Ferguson

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Plessy v. Ferguson, 163 U.S. 537 (1896), is a decision of the United States Supreme Court that held racial segregation constitutional under the "separate but equal" doctrine. Homer Plessy, a man of mixed ancestry (described at the time as seven-eighths white), deliberately challenged Louisiana's Separate Car Act by sitting in a whites-only railway car; the Court, 7–1, held that state-mandated separation stamped no badge of inferiority that the law need notice, so long as facilities were nominally equal.

Legacy

Justice John Marshall Harlan's lone dissent — "our Constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens" — became the case's enduring text, cited by the lawyers who dismantled its rule. Plessy licensed the Jim Crow order across the South (and its logic reached far beyond transport) until eroded in graduate-education cases (1950) and overruled in public education by Brown v. Board of Education (1954); later decisions completed the repudiation, and the case now anchors the "anti-canon" of American constitutional law — the small set of decisions regarded as definitively wrong. Strategists of the Comité des Citoyens in New Orleans designed Plessy's test case; historians note both their foresight and the half-century the Court required to vindicate it.
