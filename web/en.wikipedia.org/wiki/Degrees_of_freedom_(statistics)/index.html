Degrees of freedom (statistics)

[Image: Student's t-distribution for various degrees of freedom]
Student's t-distribution curves approach the normal curve as degrees of freedom increase

In statistics, the number of degrees of freedom is the number of values in the final calculation of a statistic that are free to vary. Estimates of statistical parameters can be based upon different amounts of information or data; the number of independent pieces of information that go into the estimate of a parameter is called the degrees of freedom. The concept was introduced by Ronald Fisher in the 1910s and 1920s.

Basic examples

When a sample of n values is drawn and the sample mean is fixed, the deviations from the mean must sum to zero, so only n − 1 deviations are free to vary; hence the sample variance divides the sum of squared deviations by n − 1 (Bessel's correction) and is said to have n − 1 degrees of freedom. Student's t-distribution, the chi-squared distribution and the F-distribution are all indexed by degrees of freedom that arise in this way.

In regression and ANOVA

In linear regression with n observations and p fitted parameters, the residuals have n − p degrees of freedom, used to estimate the error variance. In analysis of variance, degrees of freedom partition among treatment effects, error and total, and the corresponding mean squares are formed by dividing sums of squares by their degrees of freedom. In chi-squared tests of contingency tables with r rows and c columns, the degrees of freedom are (r − 1)(c − 1). More generally, degrees of freedom can be understood geometrically as the dimension of the subspace in which a vector of residuals or contrasts is free to lie, and generalized notions give non-integer "effective degrees of freedom" for smoothers and random-effects models.
