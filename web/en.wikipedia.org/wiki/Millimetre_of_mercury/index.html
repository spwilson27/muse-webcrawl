A millimetre of mercury (symbol: mmHg) is a manometric unit of pressure, defined as the pressure exerted by a column of mercury one millimetre high under standard gravity. It is nearly equal to the torr (1/760 of a standard atmosphere), and 760 mmHg equals one atmosphere. Though not an SI unit, the mmHg remains in everyday use for measuring blood pressure in medicine and for vacuum and gas pressures in various sciences.

Definition and relation to the torr

The unit originates with the mercury barometer invented by Evangelista Torricelli in 1643: atmospheric pressure supports a mercury column about 760 mm high. The torr, named in Torricelli's honour, is defined as exactly 1/760 of an atmosphere (101,325 Pa), i.e. about 133.322368 Pa; the mmHg, defined via the density of mercury at 0 °C and standard gravity, is 133.322387 Pa. The two differ by less than two parts in ten million and are treated as interchangeable in practice.

Applications

Blood pressure is measured in mmHg almost universally — a typical healthy reading is about 120/80 mmHg (systolic/diastolic). The unit is also standard for intraocular pressure, cerebrospinal fluid pressure, meteorology historically (inches of mercury in the US), and vacuum technology, where pressures below one torr are subdivided into millitorr. SI-oriented fields increasingly report pressures in pascals or kilopascals.
