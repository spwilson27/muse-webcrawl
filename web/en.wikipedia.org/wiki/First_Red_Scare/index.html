First Red Scare

The First Red Scare was a period during the early 20th century marked by a widespread fear of Bolshevism and anarchism in the United States, following the Russian Revolution of 1917 and anarchist bombings in 1919. Occurring in the years 1918–1920, it was characterised by labour unrest, race riots, mail bombs, mass arrests and the deportation of suspected radicals, most notoriously in the Palmer Raids organised by Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer.

Origins

The Bolshevik victory in Russia and the founding of the Communist International convinced many Americans that revolution was being exported to the United States. The labour movement's postwar strike wave — including the Seattle general strike of February 1919, the Boston police strike and the great steel strike — was widely portrayed as revolutionary. In April and June 1919 anarchist followers of Luigi Galleani sent a series of mail bombs to prominent officials and businessmen, and one bomb exploded outside Attorney General Palmer's own home in Washington, D.C.

The Palmer Raids

Palmer created within the Department of Justice a General Intelligence Division headed by the young J. Edgar Hoover, which compiled files on hundreds of thousands of radicals. In November 1919 and January 1920 federal agents raided the Union of Russian Workers and communist organisations in dozens of cities, arresting thousands — some 10,000 in the January raids alone — often without warrants and with considerable brutality. In December 1919, 249 resident aliens, including the anarchist Emma Goldman, were deported to Soviet Russia aboard the USAT Buford, dubbed the "Soviet Ark". Thousands more faced deportation proceedings.

Decline

The scare collapsed in 1920 when Palmer's prediction of a massive May Day uprising failed to materialise, costing him credibility and his hopes of the Democratic presidential nomination. Assistant Secretary of Labor Louis Freeland Post cancelled most of the deportation warrants, and a report by twelve prominent lawyers criticised the Justice Department's illegal practices. The Wall Street bombing of September 1920 killed 38 people but did not revive the panic. Nevertheless, the era left lasting marks: the criminal syndicalism laws of many states, the trial of Sacco and Vanzetti, tightened immigration law culminating in the Immigration Act of 1924, and Hoover's intelligence apparatus.
