Inner core

Earth's inner core is a solid sphere of iron–nickel alloy, about 1,220 kilometres in radius, at the centre of the planet. Its existence was demonstrated by the Danish seismologist Inge Lehmann in 1936 from the behaviour of seismic waves. Despite temperatures estimated at 5,000–6,000 °C — comparable to the surface of the Sun — the inner core is solid because of the immense pressure, about 3.6 million atmospheres.

Composition and structure

The inner core consists mainly of iron and nickel with a few percent of light elements such as silicon, oxygen, sulfur, carbon, or hydrogen. It sits within the liquid outer core, whose convective motion generates Earth's magnetic field.

Growth

As Earth slowly cools, iron crystallizes at the inner core boundary, so the inner core grows by roughly a millimetre per year. Estimates of its age range from about 0.5 to 2 billion years. The release of latent heat and light elements during this freezing helps power the geodynamo.

Rotation

Seismic studies have suggested that the inner core may rotate at a slightly different rate than the mantle and crust, and that its structure is anisotropic, with seismic waves travelling faster along the rotation axis.
