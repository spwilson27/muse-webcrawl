Self-similarity

In mathematics, a self-similar object is exactly or approximately similar to a part of itself (i.e., the whole has the same shape as one or more of the parts). Many objects in the real world, such as coastlines, are statistically self-similar: parts of them show the same statistical properties at many scales. Self-similarity is a typical property of fractals.

Definition

A compact topological space X is self-similar if there exists a finite set S of similarity maps (contractions) whose union of images reproduces X. Exact self-similar sets include the Koch snowflake, the Sierpiński triangle and carpet, and the Cantor set; each is composed of copies of itself scaled by a constant factor. The similarity (Hausdorff) dimension is often a non-integer, e.g. log 2 / log 3 for the Cantor set.

Approximate and statistical self-similarity

Natural structures are self-similar only over limited ranges of scale: the branching of trees, lungs and river networks, fern fronds, Romanesco broccoli florets, and the roughness of coastlines — the insight behind Benoit Mandelbrot's 1967 paper "How Long Is the Coast of Britain?". In stochastic processes, self-similarity appears in long-range dependence, 1/f noise and the scaling of financial time series.

Applications

Self-similar structures and processes are modelled in image compression (fractal coding), antenna design (fractal antennas work at multiple frequencies), urban geography, network traffic engineering and turbulence theory (Kolmogorov's cascade).
