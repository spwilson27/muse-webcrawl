Historical Jesus

The historical Jesus is the figure of Jesus of Nazareth as reconstructed by historians using critical methods applied to the earliest sources — chiefly the canonical Gospels, the letters of Paul, and incidental references in Josephus and Tacitus. The endeavour, known as the quest for the historical Jesus, seeks to distinguish what can be known about Jesus as a man of first-century Roman Judea from the theological portrait of Christ in Christian faith.

The quests

The quest is conventionally traced to Hermann Samuel Reimarus, whose posthumous fragments (1770s) first sharply separated the aims of Jesus from the preaching of his followers. Albert Schweitzer's survey The Quest of the Historical Jesus (1906; German title Von Reimarus zu Wrede) showed how nineteenth-century "Lives" remade Jesus in their authors' images and argued that Jesus was an apocalyptic prophet expecting the imminent end of the age. A mid-twentieth-century "new quest" and, from the 1980s, a "third quest" — using social-scientific and archaeological study of Second Temple Judaism — followed; E. P. Sanders's Jesus and Judaism (1985) is a landmark of the latter.

Points of broad agreement

Virtually all scholars of antiquity accept that Jesus existed — the "Christ myth theory" is a fringe position — and a broad consensus holds that he was baptised by John the Baptist, gathered disciples and preached in Galilee about the Kingdom of God (often in parables), created a disturbance at the Temple in Jerusalem, and was crucified on the orders of the Roman prefect Pontius Pilate around AD 30 or 33. Historians assess individual sayings and deeds using criteria such as multiple attestation, embarrassment (material unlikely to be invented, such as the baptism by John or the crucifixion itself) and coherence, though the criteria themselves are debated. Miracles and the resurrection are generally treated as matters of faith lying outside historical method, which proceeds by methodological naturalism.

Sources

Besides Paul's letters (the earliest evidence, from the 50s AD), key non-Christian testimony includes Josephus's Antiquities of the Jews — containing the partially interpolated Testimonium Flavianum and a widely accepted reference to "James, the brother of Jesus who was called Christ" — and Tacitus's note that Christus was executed under Pilate.
