Quickening
Timing:~13–25 weeks of gestation
Field:Obstetrics

In pregnancy terms, quickening is the moment when the pregnant woman first perceives movements of the fetus in the uterus. The word derives from the archaic sense of "quick" meaning alive — the fetus was said to "quicken", or come to life.

Quickening is usually felt between weeks 16 and 25 of pregnancy, though it may be noticed as early as week 13. Women who have previously given birth generally perceive movements earlier than first-time mothers, because they recognise the sensations and because the uterus and abdominal muscles are more relaxed.

Sensation

The earliest movements are often described as flutters, bubbles, or the feeling of gas, and are easily mistaken for intestinal activity. As the fetus grows, movements become stronger and more distinct, progressing to recognisable kicks, rolls, and stretches.

Historical and legal significance

Before modern diagnostic methods, quickening was one of the most reliable confirmations of pregnancy. In English common law and in many other legal traditions, quickening was treated as a significant threshold: it was historically the point at which the fetus was widely considered to be alive, and it influenced the legal treatment of abortion. In common law, abortion after quickening was an offence, while before quickening it generally was not prosecuted.

The concept also appears in theology: some religious traditions associated quickening with ensoulment, the entry of the soul into the body, a view held at various times by figures such as Aristotle (in his theory of successive souls) and Thomas Aquinas.

Modern medicine

Today, quickening is of mainly clinical and experiential interest; fetal viability and gestational age are assessed by ultrasound rather than maternal perception. Nonetheless, the first felt movements remain a meaningful milestone for many pregnant women, and a marked decrease in fetal movement later in pregnancy is a recognised warning sign prompting medical assessment.
