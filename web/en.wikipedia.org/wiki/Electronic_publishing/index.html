Electronic publishing


Reading an electronic publication on a tablet

Electronic publishing (also referred to as e-publishing or digital publishing) includes the digital publication of e-books, digital magazines, and the development of digital libraries and catalogues. It also includes the editing of books, journals, and other content for viewing on screens — from desktop computers to dedicated e-readers and smartphones.

History

Electronic publishing has a long history of development alongside computer technology. In 1971, Michael S. Hart launched Project Gutenberg and digitized the U.S. Declaration of Independence, becoming the inventor of the e-book. Desktop publishing in the 1980s let authors prepare camera-ready text on personal computers, while the World Wide Web in the 1990s enabled journals, newspapers, and independent authors to distribute work instantly at global scale. Commercial e-book markets expanded rapidly after 2007 with dedicated reading devices such as the Amazon Kindle.

Formats

Common formats include EPUB, PDF, and Kindle formats such as AZW and KPF. Digital publications may add hyperlinks, embedded multimedia, adjustable typography, text-to-speech, and search functions unavailable in print.

Business models

Electronic publishing supports traditional sales, subscriptions, open-access publishing in academia, and self-publishing platforms that let authors bypass conventional gatekeepers. Issues in the field include digital rights management, pricing of e-books relative to print, library lending of digital copies, and long-term preservation of digital files.

Academic publishing

Most scholarly journals are now published electronically, often before or instead of print; electronic dissemination has enabled preprint servers, open-access mandates, and new metrics for measuring scholarly impact.
