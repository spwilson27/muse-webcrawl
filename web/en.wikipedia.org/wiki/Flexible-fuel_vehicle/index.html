Flexible-fuel vehicle (FFV)
FuelsGasoline blended with ethanol up to E85, or (in Brazil) any mix up to pure ethanol E100
Key technologyFuel composition sensor / software inference; ethanol-resistant fuel system; adjusted engine management
Largest fleetsBrazil, United States
First modern FFVFord Model T could run on ethanol (1908); modern era from the 1980s–90s

A flexible-fuel vehicle (FFV) or dual-fuel vehicle is an alternative fuel vehicle with a conventional internal combustion engine designed to run on more than one fuel, usually gasoline blended with either ethanol or methanol, all stored in the same common tank. Modern flex-fuel engines are capable of burning any proportion of the resulting blend in the combustion chamber, as fuel composition is detected automatically.


Refuelling an FFV with E85

Technology

Because ethanol contains less energy per litre than gasoline and is corrosive to some materials, FFVs use ethanol-compatible fuel-system components, larger fuel injectors to deliver the greater volume required at high ethanol blends, and engine software that adjusts ignition timing and fuelling — either from a physical fuel-composition sensor or inferred from oxygen-sensor feedback. Ethanol's high octane rating allows higher compression or turbocharging boost, partly offsetting its lower energy content.

Regional adoption

Brazil is the world leader: flex-fuel cars were introduced there in 2003 (the first was the Volkswagen Gol 1.6) and came to dominate new car sales, running on any mixture of gasoline and hydrous ethanol from the country's sugarcane industry. In the United States, millions of E85 flex-fuel vehicles were produced, encouraged by fuel-economy credits, though E85 fuel stations remain comparatively scarce outside the Midwest. Sweden also promoted E85 vehicles in the 2000s before policy shifted toward electrification.
