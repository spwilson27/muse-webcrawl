Cell theory is the foundational scientific theory of biology stating that living organisms are made of cells, that the cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life, and that all cells arise from pre-existing cells. Together with the theory of evolution and the germ theory of disease, it is one of the unifying principles of the life sciences.

Tenets

The classical formulation, as taught today, holds that: (1) all known living things are composed of one or more cells; (2) the cell is the fundamental unit of structure and organisation in organisms; (3) all cells come from pre-existing cells by division; (4) cells carry hereditary information (DNA) passed from cell to cell; and (5) the functions and metabolism of an organism are, at root, the functions of its cells.

History

The theory became possible with the microscope. Robert Hooke coined the term "cell" in 1665 after seeing cork cell walls, and Antonie van Leeuwenhoek observed living microorganisms in the 1670s. In 1838 the botanist Matthias Jakob Schleiden concluded that all plant tissues are composed of cells, and in 1839 Theodor Schwann extended the claim to animals. The question of cell origin was resolved against spontaneous generation when Rudolf Virchow popularised the aphorism omnis cellula e cellula ("every cell from a cell") in 1855, building on the work of Robert Remak, who had observed cell division. Later microscopy, staining techniques and the discovery of chromosomes and DNA filled in the mechanisms behind the theory.

Exceptions and limits

Viruses are not cells and cannot reproduce on their own, so whether they count as "living" falls outside cell theory. Syncytial structures, such as skeletal muscle fibres and some fungal hyphae, contain many nuclei within a shared cytoplasm, stretching the notion of one cell as a unit. The origin of the very first cells remains a separate question of abiogenesis.
