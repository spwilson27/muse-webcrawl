The Danse Macabre (French for "Dance of Death"), also called the Dance of Death or Totentanz in German, is an artistic genre of allegory from the late Middle Ages. It consists of the dead — or a personification of Death — summoning representatives from all walks of life to dance along to the grave, typically with a pope, emperor, king, labourer and child among the participants. The effect is both frantic and frightening, and the genre was intended as a memento mori: a reminder that however different people may be in life, all are equal in death.

Background

The genre emerged in a Europe traumatised by the Black Death, war and famine. The earliest known fully developed example was a mural painted in 1424–25 in the Cemetery of the Holy Innocents in Paris, accompanied by verses spoken alternately by Death and its victims. Though the mural was destroyed in the seventeenth century, its composition survives through woodcut copies, notably those published by Guy Marchant in 1485.

Notable examples

Famous treatments include Hans Holbein the Younger's woodcut series The Dance of Death (published 1538), the frescoes at Clusone and in the church at Hrastovlje, Bernt Notke's paintings in Lübeck and Tallinn, and Michael Wolgemut's Dance of Death in the Nuremberg Chronicle (1493). In music, the theme inspired Franz Liszt's Totentanz and Camille Saint-Saëns' tone poem Danse macabre, Op. 40 (1874), in which Death plays the fiddle at midnight and skeletons dance until dawn.

Themes and interpretation

The Dance of Death is often read as social satire: popes and emperors are dragged away as readily as peasants, and the verses accompanying the images frequently mock the vanity, greed and corruption of the powerful. At the same time the genre offered consolation and instruction, urging viewers to prepare their souls because death could come at any moment.
