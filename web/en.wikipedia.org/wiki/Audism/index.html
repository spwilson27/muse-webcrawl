Audism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Audism is the notion that one is superior based on one's ability to hear or to behave in the manner of one who hears, or that hearing and speech are inherently superior to deafness and signing. The term was coined by Tom Humphries in his 1977 doctoral dissertation to describe discrimination against deaf people.

Sign language is central to Deaf culture, whose advocates use the concept of audism to describe discrimination.

Definition and forms

Audism can be individual, institutional or internalised. Examples cited by scholars and activists include assuming deaf people are less intelligent, forcing oral-only education, discouraging sign language use, and designing workplaces and media that presume hearing. Humphries originally used the term for individual attitudes; later writers, including Harlan Lane, extended it to institutional practices within "the hearing way" of managing deaf people.

History

The concept emerged from the Deaf studies movement of the 1970s, alongside growing pride in sign languages following linguistic research (notably by William Stokoe) demonstrating that sign languages are full natural languages. Debates over oralism and the 1880 Milan Congress resolutions, which banned sign language in deaf education in many countries, are frequently discussed in the audism literature as historical examples.

Criticism

Some critics argue the term is applied too broadly, conflating genuine prejudice with practical communication choices, or that it unfairly characterises hearing people and parents of deaf children. Others note that the term lacks a precise legal or clinical definition.
