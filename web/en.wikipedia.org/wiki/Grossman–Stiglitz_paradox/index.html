The Grossman–Stiglitz paradox is a paradox introduced by Sanford Grossman and Joseph Stiglitz in a 1980 paper, "On the Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets", published in the American Economic Review. It argues that perfectly informationally efficient markets are impossible when information is costly.

The argument

If a market price fully reflects all available information — as in the strong form of the efficient-market hypothesis — then no trader can earn a return from gathering that information, because the information is already in the price and hence cannot be traded on profitably. But gathering information is costly: traders must be compensated for research, data and analysis. If no one is compensated, no one gathers information; and if no one gathers information, there is nothing for the price to reflect. A perfectly efficient market therefore destroys the very incentive required to produce its efficiency — it cannot exist in equilibrium.

Implications

The resolution of the paradox is that markets must be imperfectly efficient: prices must deviate enough from full information — through "noise" from liquidity traders and random supply — to reward informed traders for their costs. Grossman and Stiglitz called this an "equilibrium degree of disequilibrium". The argument became foundational for market microstructure and information economics, and a standard theoretical response to strong claims of market efficiency, explaining why active fund managers and analysts can earn fees at all.
