Hick's law

Hick's law, or the Hick–Hyman law, named after British psychologist William Edmund Hick and American psychologist Ray Hyman, describes the time it takes for a person to make a decision as a result of the possible choices: increasing the number of choices increases the decision time logarithmically.

Background

In 1868, Franciscus Donders reported the relationship between having multiple stimuli and choice reaction time. Hick's 1952 experiments formalized the finding: reaction time RT = a + b log2(n + 1), where n is the number of equally probable alternatives. The measure relates to the information-theoretic entropy of the choice.

Applications

Hick's law is widely applied in human–computer interaction and user interface design — for example, in limiting menu length, simplifying navigation, and designing controls where rapid response matters. It complements Fitts's law, which models pointing time.

Exceptions

The law does not apply well when choices are highly familiar, when options can be searched visually in parallel, or when stimuli differ greatly in probability.
