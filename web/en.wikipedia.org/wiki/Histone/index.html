Histone

Histones are highly basic proteins found in eukaryotic cell nuclei that package and order DNA into structural units called nucleosomes. They are the chief protein components of chromatin, acting as spools around which DNA winds, and they play a central role in gene regulation through post-translational modifications.

Histone
Function: DNA packaging, gene regulation
Core types: H2A, H2B, H3, H4
Linker: H1 / H5
Discovered: Albrecht Kossel, 1884

Structure

A nucleosome consists of about 147 base pairs of DNA wrapped ~1.67 turns around an octamer of two copies each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4. Linker histone H1 binds DNA between nucleosomes, compacting chromatin into higher-order fibres. Histones are among the most conserved proteins; even small changes, especially in H3 and H4, can be lethal.

Variants and modifications

Variants such as H2A.Z, H3.3 and CENP-A (a centromeric H3 variant) mark functional regions. Tails protruding from nucleosomes are acetylated, methylated, phosphorylated, ubiquitinated and more — the "histone code" read by effector proteins. Acetylation by histone acetyltransferases usually loosens chromatin and activates transcription; deacetylation represses it. Methylation can activate or repress depending on the residue, e.g. H3K4me3 (active promoters) vs H3K27me3 (Polycomb repression).

Biological roles and disease

Histone dynamics govern replication, repair, development and epigenetic memory. Mutations in histone genes (oncohistones, e.g. H3K27M in diffuse midline gliomas) and dysregulated modifying enzymes occur in cancers; HDAC inhibitors are used as drugs.
