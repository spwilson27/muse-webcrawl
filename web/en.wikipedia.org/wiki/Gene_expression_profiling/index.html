Gene expression profiling

In the field of molecular biology, gene expression profiling is the measurement of the activity (the expression) of thousands of genes at once, to create a global picture of cellular function. These profiles can, for example, distinguish between cells that are actively dividing, or show how the cells react to a particular treatment. Nearly every cell in an organism contains the same genome, but different cell types express different subsets of genes as messenger RNA (mRNA).

Methods

Many experiments of this class are performed with technologies that quantify the relative abundance of mRNA in a sample. DNA microarrays, widely used from the late 1990s, hybridize labeled cDNA to arrays of probes for known genes. Expressed sequence tag (EST) sequencing and serial analysis of gene expression (SAGE) were earlier sequence-based approaches. Since roughly 2008, RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) has largely supplanted microarrays: it sequences cDNA directly, offering a wider dynamic range, detection of novel transcripts and splice variants, and single-cell resolution in newer protocols.

Analysis

Expression profiling generates large datasets requiring statistical and bioinformatic analysis. Common steps include normalization, differential expression testing between conditions, and pattern discovery through hierarchical clustering, principal component analysis, and gene set enrichment analysis, which detects coordinated shifts in pathways rather than single genes. Public repositories such as the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) archive millions of profiling experiments.

Applications

Applications include classifying cancers into molecular subtypes (for example, breast cancer intrinsic subtypes), identifying prognostic signatures, discovering drug targets, toxicogenomics, and characterizing developmental and immune processes. Expression profiles of tumors guide some clinical tests, such as Oncotype DX and MammaPrint, used in treatment decisions.
