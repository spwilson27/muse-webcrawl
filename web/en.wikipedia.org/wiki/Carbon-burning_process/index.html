Carbon-burning process

A massive star in its late burning stages, with concentric shells of fusion ash. (Image placeholder)

The carbon-burning process or carbon fusion is a set of nuclear fusion reactions that take place in the cores of massive stars (at least 8 solar masses at birth) after they have exhausted the helium in their cores. It requires very high temperatures — around 5×108 to 6×108 kelvin — and high densities. Carbon fusion follows helium fusion and precedes the neon-burning process in the sequence of advanced stellar burning stages.

Reactions

The principal reaction is the fusion of two carbon-12 nuclei, whose products include:

  • 12C + 12C → 20Ne + 4He
  • 12C + 12C → 23Na + 1H
  • 12C + 12C → 24Mg + γ
  • 12C + 12C → 23Mg + n

The protons and alpha particles released are captured by other nuclei, so the net products of carbon burning are chiefly neon, sodium, magnesium and oxygen. Energy losses to neutrinos become dominant at these temperatures, which greatly accelerates the later evolution of the star: carbon burning lasts only a few hundred to a few thousand years, compared with millions of years for hydrogen burning.

Outcome

In stars of roughly 8–10 solar masses, carbon burning produces an oxygen–neon–magnesium core that may eventually collapse in an electron-capture supernova. In more massive stars, the core proceeds through neon, oxygen and silicon burning until an iron core forms and collapses, triggering a core-collapse supernova. Lower-mass stars, like the Sun, never ignite carbon; they end their lives as carbon–oxygen white dwarfs.

In super-AGB stars, carbon may ignite off-centre under partially degenerate conditions, in a process sometimes called a carbon flame, which can convert the core to oxygen and neon without proceeding to further burning stages.
