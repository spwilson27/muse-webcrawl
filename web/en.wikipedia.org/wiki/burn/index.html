Burn

SpecialtyDermatology, emergency medicine, plastic surgery
CausesHeat, electricity, chemicals, radiation, friction
TreatmentCooling, fluids, wound care, skin grafts

A burn is an injury to the skin or other organic tissue primarily caused by heat, but also by electricity, chemicals, friction, radiation (including sunburn and radiation burns) or cold (frostbite). Most burns are due to heat from hot liquids (scalds), hot solids, or fire. Burns are a major global cause of injury; the World Health Organization estimates that burns cause roughly 180,000 deaths per year, overwhelmingly in low- and middle-income countries, and fire-related burns are among the leading causes of disability-adjusted life years lost in those regions.

Classification

Burns are classified by depth. First-degree (superficial) burns affect only the epidermis, causing redness and pain without blistering; sunburn is a common example. Second-degree (partial-thickness) burns extend into the dermis and typically blister; they may be superficial or deep partial-thickness. Third-degree (full-thickness) burns destroy the full thickness of the skin and appear white, brown or charred, and are often painless because nerve endings are destroyed. Fourth-degree burns extend into muscle or bone.

Severity is also judged by the total body surface area (TBSA) affected, commonly estimated with the "rule of nines" in adults, and by the site of the burn, the patient's age, and the presence of inhalation injury.

Treatment

First aid consists of stopping the burning process, cooling the burn with cool running water, and covering it with a clean dressing. Hospital treatment of major burns involves fluid resuscitation (often calculated with the Parkland formula), pain control, wound care, prevention of infection, excision of dead tissue and skin grafting, and long-term rehabilitation to manage scarring and contractures. Specialised burn centres treat the most severe cases.

Prevention

Prevention measures include smoke alarms, safer cooking stoves, regulation of hot water temperatures, flame-resistant clothing, and public education — particularly aimed at children, who are at high risk of scalds.
