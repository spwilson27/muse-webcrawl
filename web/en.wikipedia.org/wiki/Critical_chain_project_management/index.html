Critical chain project management

The critical chain is the longest chain of dependent tasks and resources

Critical chain project management (CCPM) is a method of planning and managing projects that emphasises the resources (people, equipment, physical space) required to execute project tasks. It was developed by Eliyahu M. Goldratt and introduced in his 1997 business novel Critical Chain, applying his Theory of Constraints to project scheduling as an alternative to the critical path method.

Principles

Traditional scheduling builds safety margins into each individual task; CCPM argues these margins are usually wasted through "student syndrome" (starting at the last moment), Parkinson's law (work expanding to fill the time available), and delays passed on — but gains never passed on — between dependent tasks. Critical chain instead uses aggressive, roughly 50%-confidence task estimates and pools the removed safety into explicit buffers: a project buffer at the end of the project, feeding buffers where non-critical chains join the critical chain, and resource buffers ensuring critical resources are ready when needed.

The critical chain

The critical chain is the longest sequence of precedence- and resource-dependent activities, determined after resource levelling — unlike the critical path, it accounts for the fact that the same resource cannot do two tasks at once. In multi-project environments, CCPM also addresses harmful multitasking by staggering projects around the capacity of a constraint ("drum") resource.

Management and criticism

Execution is monitored by tracking buffer consumption against project completion (often with "fever charts"), triggering action only when buffers are significantly penetrated. Supporters report shorter project durations and improved on-time performance in manufacturing, maintenance, engineering, and software settings; critics note the difficulty of obtaining unbiased estimates, the method's dependence on stable resource assumptions, and limited large-scale empirical validation compared with established methods.
