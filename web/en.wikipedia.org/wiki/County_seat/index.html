County seat

A county seat is an administrative center, seat of government, or capital city of a county or civil parish. The term is used principally in the United States and Canada. A county seat is usually the location of the county courthouse, the county jail, and the offices of county government, and historically it was the legal and commercial hub of the surrounding rural area.

United States

Most U.S. counties have a single county seat, though some have two or more — a legacy of the era when travel was slow and distant parts of a large county needed their own courts; examples include a number of counties in Mississippi, Missouri, and Iowa with dual seats. In New England, where county government is weak, the term "shire town" has traditionally been used in Vermont and elsewhere. Equivalent terms elsewhere include "county town" in the United Kingdom and Ireland, and "parish seat" in Louisiana, whose county-level units are parishes. A few notable facts: the county seat is not always the largest community in the county; some county seats are unincorporated (as in several Maryland and Virginia counties, where the seat may also be an independent city legally separate from the county); and Los Angeles, the seat of Los Angeles County, is both the largest city in its county and the county seat, as is common in the West.

County seat wars

In the nineteenth-century American frontier, rival towns frequently fought — commercially, politically, and occasionally violently — over designation as county seat, because the status conferred economic advantages and prestige. These contests, known as "county seat wars", were especially common in Kansas and Nebraska, and some involved ballot fraud and armed confrontation. In modern times, county seats rarely change, and doing so typically requires legislative action or a referendum.
