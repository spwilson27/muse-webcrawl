Foreland basin

A foreland basin is a structural basin that develops adjacent and parallel to a mountain belt. Foreland basins form because the immense mass created by crustal thickening associated with the evolution of a mountain belt causes the lithosphere to bend, by a process known as lithospheric flexure. The width and depth of the foreland basin is determined by the flexural rigidity of the underlying lithosphere and the characteristics of the mountain belt. The foreland basin receives sediment that is eroded off the adjacent mountain belt, filling with thick sedimentary successions that thin away from the mountain belt. Foreland basins represent an endmember type of basin, the other being rift basins. Space for sediments (accommodation space) is provided by loading and downflexure to form foreland basins, in contrast to rift basins, where accommodation space is generated by lithospheric extension.

Foreland basins are divided into two zones: the foredeep, located on the side of the mountain belt and characterised by a complete absence of deformation or mild deformation, and the forebulge and back-bulge, located further from the mountain belt. Most foreland basins form on continental crust and consist of sediment that has accumulated in a low-relief, usually marine-influenced environment.

Formation and structure

Foreland basins form primarily as a result of the flexural response of the lithosphere to loading by mountain belts, which are formed in convergent tectonic settings. Movement on thrust faults loads the lithosphere, causing it to subside and create accommodation space for sediment shed from the growing orogen. The resulting stratigraphy records the history of thrusting and sedimentation, with unconformities marking periods of tectonic activity.

Depositional systems in foreland basins are categorised into four depozones: the wedge-top, foredeep, forebulge, and back-bulge depozones. Sediment is primarily derived from the recycling of thrust belt sedimentary rocks out of the frontal fold and thrust belt, transported into the basin along strike as turbidites and through axial drainage systems, and from erosion of the highlands behind and beyond the fold and thrust belt.

Types

Foreland basins can be divided into two categories:

  • Peripheral (pro) foreland basins, which occur on the plate that is subducted or underthrust during plate collision (i.e. the outer arc of the orogen). Examples include the North Alpine Foreland Basin and the Persian Gulf basin adjacent to the Zagros.
  • Retroarc (retro) foreland basins, which occur on the overriding plate, behind the magmatic arc associated with subduction of oceanic lithosphere. Examples include the Andean foreland basins of South America and the Laramide basins of western North America, such as the Powder River Basin.

Evolution

The evolution of a foreland basin system typically passes through an early underfilled stage, in which deep-marine sedimentation (flysch) predominates, to a filled and finally overfilled stage dominated by shallow-marine and continental deposits (molasse). As thrusting propagates into the basin, previously deposited sediments may be incorporated into the thrust wedge itself (a "piggyback" or wedge-top basin setting).

Economic significance

Foreland basins are among the most important hydrocarbon provinces in the world. The combination of source rocks, reservoir rocks, seals and structural traps generated by thrusting makes systems such as the Persian Gulf basin, the Alberta basin and the Sub-Andean basins prolific petroleum producers. They are also significant hosts for coal and groundwater resources.
