Parenchyma

Parenchyma is a term used in both botany and zoology. In plants, parenchyma is the ground tissue composed of living, thin-walled cells that carry out functions such as photosynthesis, storage and secretion. In animals, the parenchyma of an organ is its functional tissue, as distinct from the stroma, the connective and supporting framework.

Plant parenchyma

Parenchyma cells are the most abundant cell type in plants. They form the cortex and pith of stems and roots, the mesophyll of leaves, and the flesh of fruits. Chlorenchyma is parenchyma containing chloroplasts and specialised for photosynthesis; aerenchyma has large air spaces and aids buoyancy and gas exchange in aquatic plants; storage parenchyma stores starch, water or other reserves.

Animal and medical usage

In medicine, parenchyma refers to the functional cells of an organ — for example, hepatocytes in the liver, nephrons in the kidney, and neurons and glia in the brain. Diseases are often described as affecting the parenchyma of an organ, as in parenchymal lung disease.
