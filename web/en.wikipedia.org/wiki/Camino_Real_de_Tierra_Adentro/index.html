Camino Real de Tierra Adentro

El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro (Spanish for "The Royal Road of the Interior Land"), also known as the Silver Route, was a Spanish colonial silver trade route between Mexico City and San Juan Pueblo (Ohkay Owingeh), New Mexico, in the modern-day United States. The route, some 2,560 kilometres (1,590 miles) long, was used from 1598 to 1882.

History

The trail was established after Juan de Oñate's expedition of 1598 colonized New Mexico. It carried silver from the mines of Zacatecas, Guanajuato, and San Luis Potosí northward, along with mercury needed for silver refining, and returned missionaries, colonists, and supplies to the northern frontier. Missions, presidios, haciendas, and towns grew up along the route, and it also transmitted Spanish culture, livestock, crops, and language into what is now the U.S. Southwest. Trade along the route enriched regions along its path but also exposed Indigenous peoples to disease, forced labour, and revolt, notably the Pueblo Revolt of 1680.

Recognition

The portion of the route in Mexico was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2010, with 59 component sites including bridges, haciendas, churches, and stretches of the road itself. The United States portion is designated the El Camino Real de Tierra Adentro National Historic Trail, administered by the National Park Service and Bureau of Land Management.
