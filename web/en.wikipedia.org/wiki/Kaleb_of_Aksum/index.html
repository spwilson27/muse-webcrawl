Kaleb (Ge'ez: ካሌብ, c. 514–c. 542), also known in Christian tradition as Saint Elesbaan, was a king of Aksum, the ancient empire centred in present-day Ethiopia and Eritrea. He is best known for his military intervention in South Arabia in defence of persecuted Christians.

Invasion of Himyar

Around 523, the Jewish king Dhu Nuwas of Himyar (in modern Yemen) massacred the Christians of Najran. With encouragement from the Byzantine emperor Justin I, Kaleb launched expeditions across the Red Sea — the decisive campaign around 525 — defeated and killed Dhu Nuwas, and installed a Christian client ruler, later replaced by the Aksumite general Abraha. The episode, recorded in Greek, Syriac, and Ethiopian sources, made Aksum temporarily dominant on both shores of the southern Red Sea.

Later reign and legacy

Kaleb maintained close ties with Byzantium, corresponded with Constantinople, and patronised the Church. Ethiopian tradition holds that he abdicated, sent his crown to the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem, and retired to a monastic life. He is venerated as a saint in the Ethiopian Orthodox, Catholic, and Eastern Orthodox traditions, with his feast day on 24 October in the Roman Martyrology. Abraha's subsequent independence marked the decline of Aksumite power in Arabia shortly before the rise of Islam.
