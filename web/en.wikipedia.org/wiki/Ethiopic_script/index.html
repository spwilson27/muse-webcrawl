The Ethiopic script, also called Ge'ez script (ግእዝ, Gəʾəz), is an abugida developed in the Horn of Africa for writing the ancient Ge'ez language of the Kingdom of Aksum. In its primary form it is used today to write Amharic and Tigrinya in Ethiopia and Eritrea, and it remains the liturgical script of the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox churches.

History

The script descends from the ancient South Arabian alphabet, brought across the Red Sea in the first millennium BC. The earliest inscriptions were consonantal (an abjad), written right-to-left or in boustrophedon. Around the 4th century AD, under the Christian king Ezana of Aksum, the script acquired its defining feature: vowels were systematically marked by modifications of each consonant sign, making it a true abugida, and the direction settled as left-to-right. This vocalised Ethiopic is among the earliest scripts in sub-Saharan Africa with a continuous literary tradition.

Structure

The character set, called fidäl, organises each consonant into a series of seven "orders" corresponding to the seven vowels (ä, u, i, a, e, ə, o). The basic inventory has 26 consonants, giving 182 core characters, extended by labialised variants and additional signs used in Amharic and other languages. Words were traditionally separated by a colon-like mark (፡), and the script has its own numerals, derived partly from Greek letters, used in traditional contexts alongside Arabic numerals.

Modern use

Ge'ez itself is no longer spoken but survives as a liturgical language. Amharic, Ethiopia's working language, and Tigrinya, official in Eritrea and Tigray, use extended versions of the script. Ethiopic was included in Unicode from version 3.0, enabling its wide use in digital publishing and signage.
