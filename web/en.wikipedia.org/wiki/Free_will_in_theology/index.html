Free will in theology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Free will in theology concerns the doctrine of how human freedom relates to divine omnipotence, foreknowledge, grace, and providence. Most religious traditions affirm both that God is sovereign and that human beings make genuinely free choices for which they are morally responsible; reconciling the two has been one of the most persistent problems in theology, especially in Christianity, where the debate centres on sin, salvation, and predestination.

Christianity

Early Christian writers such as Origen and the Eastern Fathers generally affirmed free will as essential to moral responsibility. The first great Western controversy opposed Augustine of Hippo to Pelagius: Pelagius taught that humans can choose good without special divine assistance, while the later Augustine held that fallen humanity's will is enslaved to sin and that salvation depends on God's unmerited, predestining grace. Pelagianism was condemned at the Council of Carthage (418) and the semi-Pelagian controversy ended with the Council of Orange (529), which affirmed the necessity of grace while rejecting predestination to evil.

In the Reformation the issue erupted again. Desiderius Erasmus defended a modest free will in De libero arbitrio (1524); Martin Luther replied in De servo arbitrio ("On the Bondage of the Will", 1525) that the will is captive in spiritual matters. John Calvin developed a strong doctrine of predestination, while Jacobus Arminius and his followers (Arminianism) taught that grace enables a free human response. Catholic teaching, restated at the Council of Trent and in the Molinist and Thomist schools, holds that grace and free will cooperate: God moves the will without destroying its freedom. The Jansenist controversy of the seventeenth century replayed these disputes within Catholicism.

Judaism and Islam

Jewish thought generally affirms free will strongly — "all is in the hands of Heaven except the fear of Heaven" (Berakhot 33b) — and Maimonides made free choice a foundation of the Torah in the Mishneh Torah, though medieval philosophers debated its compatibility with divine foreknowledge. In Islam, the early Qadariyya affirmed human freedom against the Jabriyya's determinism; the Ash'ari school developed the doctrine of kasb ("acquisition"), by which God creates human acts and humans "acquire" them, while the Mu'tazila defended robust free will and divine justice.

The problem of evil

Free will is also invoked in theodicy: the free will defence, developed philosophically by Alvin Plantinga, argues that a world containing free creatures who sometimes choose evil may be more valuable than a world of automata, and that God cannot both grant significant freedom and guarantee that creatures never misuse it.
