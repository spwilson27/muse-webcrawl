Intelligence agency

The headquarters of the Central Intelligence Agency in Langley, Virginia.

An intelligence agency is a government agency responsible for the collection, analysis, and exploitation of information in support of law enforcement, national security, military, and foreign policy objectives. The means of information gathering are both overt and covert and may include espionage, communication interception, cryptanalysis, and cooperation with other institutions.

Well-known agencies include the United States Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) and National Security Agency (NSA), the United Kingdom's Secret Intelligence Service (MI6) and GCHQ, Russia's FSB and SVR, and Israel's Mossad.

Functions

Intelligence agencies collect information through human intelligence (HUMINT), signals intelligence (SIGINT), imagery intelligence (IMINT), and open-source intelligence (OSINT). Analysts assess the material and produce estimates for policymakers. Some agencies also engage in counterintelligence, covert action, and, in certain countries, domestic security.

Types

Agencies may be divided into foreign intelligence services, domestic security services, military intelligence organisations, and signals intelligence agencies. Many democracies separate foreign and domestic roles by law and subject agencies to legislative oversight.

History

Modern intelligence organisations took shape during the world wars; Britain's codebreaking at Bletchley Park and the creation of the CIA in 1947 exemplify the institutionalisation of intelligence in the 20th century. Revelations of surveillance programmes in the 21st century renewed debate about oversight and civil liberties.
