The Vatican Bank, formally the Institute for the Works of Religion (Italian: Istituto per le Opere di Religione, IOR), is a financial institution of the Holy See situated in Vatican City. Founded by Pope Pius XII in 1942, it holds and administers assets entrusted to it for religious and charitable works. It is not a central bank and does not serve the general public.

History

The IOR absorbed earlier papal financial bodies, notably the Administration of the Assets of the Apostolic See. During and after the Second World War it became the custodian of funds for religious orders and dioceses worldwide, as well as gold and investments for the Holy See. Its governance rests with a commission of cardinals and a board of lay banking professionals, supervised in recent years by the Vatican's Financial Intelligence Authority (AIF).

Scandals and reform

The bank's secrecy attracted repeated controversy. Its close ties to the Italian Banco Ambrosiano — whose 1982 collapse and whose chairman Roberto Calvi was found hanged in London — and to the banker Michele Sindona, together with the role of Archbishop Paul Marcinkus, its long-serving president, made it a byword for opaque finance; the Holy See paid substantial sums to Ambrosiano creditors while denying legal liability. Later cases, including the 2010 seizure (later released) of IOR funds by Italian prosecutors and the 2012 "Vatileaks" affair, spurred reform: Pope Benedict XVI introduced anti-money-laundering legislation, and Pope Francis commissioned an external review, closed thousands of accounts, published the bank's first annual reports, and restructured its governance. In 2019 and 2022 further statutes tightened its mission to serving Catholic institutions.
