Government budget balance

The government budget balance, also alternatively referred to as general government balance, public budget balance, or public fiscal balance, is the difference between government revenues and spending. For a government that takes in more than it spends, the difference is a budget surplus; in the opposite case, where spending exceeds revenue, the difference is a budget deficit. A budget is prepared for each fiscal year and is usually presented as a percentage of gross domestic product (GDP) for comparability across periods and between countries.

Definitions

The overall balance includes interest payments on government debt; the primary balance excludes them and therefore reflects the current fiscal stance more directly. The structural (or cyclically adjusted) balance removes the effect of economic fluctuations on revenues and spending, such as higher tax receipts and lower welfare payments during booms, to show the underlying position. Economists distinguish deficits arising passively from the business cycle (automatic stabilizers) from discretionary fiscal policy.

Economic debate

Whether and when governments should run deficits is a central controversy in macroeconomics. Keynesian economics holds that deficits are useful to stimulate demand in recessions, and that attempting balance during downturns deepens them; classical and later monetarist views emphasize crowding out of private investment and inflation risks. Persistent deficits add to the stock of government debt, whose sustainability depends on interest rates relative to economic growth (the r − g relationship), currency denomination, and the maturity structure of the debt.

Rules and practice

Many jurisdictions institutionalize limits: the European Union's Stability and Growth Pact caps deficits at 3% of GDP and debt at 60% of GDP (with escape clauses and reforms since), Germany's constitution contains a "debt brake" (Schuldenbremse), and many US states have balanced-budget requirements. Balanced budget amendments are regularly proposed but rarely adopted at the federal level in the United States. In practice most advanced economies have run deficits in most years since the Second World War, with surpluses rare and usually brief — the United States, for example, ran federal surpluses only in 1998–2001.
