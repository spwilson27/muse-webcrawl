Theta criterion

[Hero image placeholder: a syntactic tree diagram showing theta roles assigned to noun phrases.]

The theta criterion (also written θ-criterion) is a constraint on argument structure in generative linguistics, introduced by Noam Chomsky in Lectures on Government and Binding (1981). It is part of theta theory, the module of grammar that governs how predicates assign semantic roles to their arguments.

Statement

The criterion has two parts: (1) each argument must bear one and only one theta role (θ-role), such as agent, theme, goal or experiencer; and (2) each theta role must be assigned to one and only one argument. A derivation that violates the criterion — for example, a verb that requires a theme argument appearing without one, or a noun phrase receiving two roles — is ruled ungrammatical.

Role in theory

The theta criterion links syntactic argument positions to semantic interpretation, works together with the projection principle, and constrains movement: in a passive or raising construction, an argument moving to a new position must not thereby acquire a second theta role. Later developments, including the Uniformity of Theta Assignment Hypothesis (UTAH), refined how roles map onto syntactic configurations.
