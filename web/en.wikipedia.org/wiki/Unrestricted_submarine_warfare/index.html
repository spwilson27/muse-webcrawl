Unrestricted submarine warfare

Unrestricted submarine warfare
PracticeSubmarines sink vessels without warning or regard for crews
Practised byGermany (1915, 1917–18, 1939–45); United States (1941–45)
ContrastPrize rules (cruiser rules)

Unrestricted submarine warfare is a type of naval warfare in which submarines sink merchant ships, passenger liners and other vessels without warning and without provision for the safety of their crews, in defiance of the traditional prize rules requiring a warship to stop, search and safeguard those aboard a merchant vessel before sinking it. Submarines were exceptionally vulnerable on the surface, making prize rules impractical and, in German doctrine, suicidal.

First World War

Imperial Germany proclaimed a war zone around the British Isles in February 1915 and its U-boats began sinking merchantmen without warning. The sinking of the liner RMS Lusitania on 7 May 1915, with 128 Americans among the 1,198 dead, and the later Sussex incident, provoked American protests that forced Germany to restrict its campaign. Under Chancellor Theobald von Bethmann Hollweg's calculations versus the naval staff's promises — Admiral Henning von Holtzendorff pledged Britain would be starved into peace within five months — Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare on 1 February 1917. Together with the Zimmermann Telegram, the decision brought the United States into the war in April 1917. The convoy system and Allied anti-submarine measures ultimately defeated the campaign, which sank some 5,000 ships yet failed to sever British supply lines.

Second World War

Germany's Kriegsmarine under Karl Dönitz waged the Battle of the Atlantic from 1939 with U-boat "wolfpacks," observing prize rules only briefly at the war's outset; the 1942 Laconia incident ended even rescue attempts. In the Pacific, the United States Navy ordered unrestricted air and submarine warfare against Japan on 7 December 1941, hours after Pearl Harbor, and American submarines devastated Japanese merchant shipping. At the Nuremberg trials, Dönitz was not punished for unrestricted submarine warfare after evidence that the Allies had done the same.
