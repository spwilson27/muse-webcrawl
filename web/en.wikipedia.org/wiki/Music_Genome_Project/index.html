Music Genome Project

The Music Genome Project is a music analysis effort created in 2000 by Tim Westergren and Will Glaser to power the Internet radio service Pandora. It attempts to capture the essence of music at the most fundamental level by breaking songs down into hundreds of detailed musical attributes, or "genes," which together form a song's DNA.

Methodology

Trained music analysts listen to each song in the Pandora catalogue and score it against as many as 450 attributes, covering melody, harmony, rhythm, instrumentation, orchestration, arrangement, lyrics, and singing and vocal harmony. Each song is then represented as a vector of numeric values, and Pandora's recommendation technology compares the vectors of different songs to find music with similar characteristics.

Analysing a typical rock or pop song takes roughly 20 to 30 minutes for a single analyst. Analysts undergo months of training, and the project employs dozens of them to keep up with new releases across genres including rock, pop, jazz, classical, hip-hop, and world music.

Application

When a Pandora listener seeds a station with a song or artist, the service uses the Music Genome data to build a playlist of songs with similar musical traits, refining the selection with listener feedback in the form of thumbs-up and thumbs-down ratings. The approach contrasts with collaborative filtering systems used by other services, which recommend music based on the listening behaviour of similar users rather than on the properties of the music itself.

History

The project grew out of the Savage Beast Technologies company founded by Westergren and Jon Kraft in 1999. After early work licensing the technology to other companies, the firm refocused on the consumer-facing Pandora service in 2005. The Music Genome Project's database of analysed tracks has grown to well over a million songs.
