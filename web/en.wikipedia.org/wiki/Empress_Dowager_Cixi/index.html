Born: 29 November 1835
Died: 15 November 1908 (aged 72)
Clan: Yehe Nara
Consort of: Xianfeng Emperor
Regent of China: 1861–1872, 1875–1889, 1898–1908

Empress Dowager Cixi (29 November 1835 – 15 November 1908), of the Manchu Yehe Nara clan, was a Chinese empress dowager and regent who was the de facto ruler of China for almost fifty years, from 1861 until her death in 1908. She was a concubine of the Xianfeng Emperor, and after his death she seized power in a coup with Empress Dowager Ci'an and Prince Gong, becoming co-regent for her young son, the Tongzhi Emperor.

Rise to power

Born into a Manchu family of officials, Cixi was selected as an imperial concubine in 1852 and entered the Forbidden City. She bore the Xianfeng Emperor his only surviving son, Zaichun, in 1856, which raised her status considerably. When the emperor died in 1861 at the Chengde summer palace, the regency was entrusted to a council of eight officials; Cixi, together with the senior empress Ci'an and Prince Gong, overthrew them in the Xinyou Coup and took control of the regency.

Rule

Cixi ruled as regent during the reigns of the Tongzhi and Guangxu emperors. She supported the Self-Strengthening Movement's limited modernisation in technology and the military while resisting fundamental political reform. When the Guangxu Emperor attempted sweeping changes in the Hundred Days' Reform of 1898, Cixi staged a coup, placed him under house arrest, and resumed direct rule.

During the Boxer Rebellion of 1900, Cixi threw her support behind the anti-foreign Boxers, leading to the invasion of Beijing by the Eight-Nation Alliance and her flight to Xi'an. Afterwards, in her final years, she implemented many of the reforms she had once blocked, including the abolition of the imperial examination system and preparations for a constitutional monarchy.

Death and legacy

Cixi died on 15 November 1908, one day after the Guangxu Emperor, having installed the infant Puyi as emperor. Her reputation in Chinese historiography has traditionally been negative — she is often blamed for corruption, extravagance and the decline of the Qing dynasty — though more recent scholarship presents a more nuanced picture of a conservative but capable ruler operating within severe constraints.
