Product–market fit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

FieldEntrepreneurship, marketing, lean startup
Coined byAttributed to Marc Andreessen; concept from Andy Rachleff

Product–market fit, also known as product/market fit, is the degree to which a product satisfies strong market demand. The term was popularised by venture capitalist Marc Andreessen, who described it as "being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market". Andreessen credited the underlying concept to Sequoia Capital founder Don Valentine and Benchmark co-founder Andy Rachleff.

Product–market fit is generally treated as a foundational step in the lean startup methodology: before a company invests heavily in growth and scaling, it should validate that customers value the product enough to retain, recommend, and pay for it. Andreessen argued that a startup's life can be divided into "before product/market fit" and "after product/market fit", and that lack of market is the most common cause of startup failure.

Measurement

Common indicators include organic growth, retention cohorts that flatten above zero, net promoter scores, and survey methods such as Sean Ellis's test, which asks users how they would feel if they could no longer use the product — if at least about 40% answer "very disappointed", fit is likely. Quantitative frameworks by Rahul Vohra (of Superhuman) popularised the Ellis survey combined with analysis of the most disappointed segment.

Criticism

Critics note that product–market fit is a retrospective and somewhat vague concept: it is often only obvious in hindsight, markets change, and fit achieved with one segment may not transfer to others. See also Lean startup, Minimum viable product, and Startup company.
