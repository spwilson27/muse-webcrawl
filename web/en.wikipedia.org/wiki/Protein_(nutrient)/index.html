Protein (nutrient)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Proteins are essential nutrients: large molecules made of amino acids that the body uses to build and repair tissues, make enzymes, hormones and antibodies, and maintain fluid and acid–base balance. Dietary protein comes from animal foods (meat, fish, eggs, dairy) and plant foods (legumes, nuts, seeds, grains); adults need roughly 0.75–0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day as a reference intake, more in growth, pregnancy, illness and intense training.

Amino acids and quality

Of the twenty amino acids in proteins, nine are 'essential' — the body cannot make them, so they must come from food. Animal proteins and soy supply all nine in good proportions ('complete'), while most plant proteins are low in one or another; eating varied plant sources across the day covers requirements, a point of practical importance for vegetarian and vegan diets.

Deficiency and excess

Severe protein deficiency, usually combined with energy deficiency, causes kwashiorkor in children (with swelling and hair and skin changes) and contributes to stunting. Most people in wealthy countries eat more than enough; very high intakes concern mainly people with kidney disease. Protein needs are higher in older adults to counter muscle loss (sarcopenia).
