Trypsin

Trypsin
EC number3.4.21.4
TypeSerine protease
Produced inPancreas (as trypsinogen)
Site of actionSmall intestine (duodenum)
SubstrateProteins; cleaves after Lys and Arg

Trypsin is a serine protease found in the digestive system of many vertebrates, where it hydrolyses proteins during digestion. It is produced in the pancreas as the inactive zymogen trypsinogen, secreted into the duodenum in pancreatic juice, and activated there by the enzyme enteropeptidase, which cleaves off a small peptide. Active trypsin then activates more trypsinogen and other pancreatic zymogens, including chymotrypsinogen.

Function and specificity

Trypsin cleaves peptide chains mainly on the carboxyl side of the amino acids lysine and arginine (except when followed by proline). Its catalytic mechanism uses the classic catalytic triad of serine, histidine and aspartate residues (Ser195, His57, Asp102 in bovine trypsin). It works best at slightly alkaline pH, around 7.5–8.5, matching conditions in the small intestine.

Biological importance

Premature activation of trypsin inside the pancreas contributes to pancreatitis, in which the organ digests itself; the pancreas protects itself with the trypsin inhibitor SPINK1. Mutations in the cationic trypsinogen gene (PRSS1) cause hereditary pancreatitis.

Uses

In the laboratory, trypsin is a workhorse: it is used to detach adherent cells in cell culture (trypsinisation) and, in proteomics, to digest proteins into peptides for mass spectrometry, its predictable lysine/arginine cleavage making peptide fragments easy to interpret. Bovine and porcine pancreatic trypsin are also ingredients in some digestive-enzyme preparations and wound-care products.
