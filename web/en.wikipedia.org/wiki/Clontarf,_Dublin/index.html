Clontarf, Dublin

Clontarf (Irish: Cluain Tarbh, meaning "meadow of the bull") is an affluent coastal suburb on the northside of Dublin, in Ireland. It is known for the Battle of Clontarf in 1014, in which High King Brian Boru defeated a Viking-Irish alliance but was himself killed. Clontarf lies on the northern shore of Dublin Bay, between Fairview and Raheny.

History

The area takes its name from the meadow of the bull, possibly referring to the sound of waves breaking on the mudflats, likened to the bellowing of a bull. On Good Friday, 23 April 1014, the forces of Brian Boru fought the Battle of Clontarf against the Vikings of Dublin and their Leinster allies. Brian's victory broke Viking power in Ireland, but the elderly king was killed in his tent, and the battle became a central episode of Irish national mythology.

Clontarf was later home to a castle, rebuilt several times; the present Clontarf Castle dates to 1837 and now operates as a hotel. In the 19th century the area developed as a residential suburb served by trams and later by the DART railway.

Geography and amenities

Clontarf is bounded by the sea and by the Tolka river estuary. The long seafront promenade is popular for walking and cycling. A wooden bridge links the suburb to Bull Island, an artificial island in Dublin Bay that is a UNESCO biosphere reserve and home to Dollymount Strand and two golf courses, including the Royal Dublin Golf Club.

Notable residents

Clontarf was the birthplace of Bram Stoker, author of Dracula, born at 15 Marino Crescent in 1847. The area is also associated with singer Sinéad O'Connor and broadcaster Gay Byrne, among others.
