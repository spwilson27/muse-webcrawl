Carpal bones

The carpal bones are the eight small bones that make up the wrist (carpus) connecting the hand to the forearm. They are arranged in two rows of four. The proximal row, from lateral (thumb side) to medial, consists of the scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum and pisiform; the distal row consists of the trapezium, trapezoid, capitate and hamate. A common mnemonic is "Some Lovers Try Positions That They Can't Handle."

Structure and function

The carpals are not individually rigidly fixed; together they form a transverse arch — the carpal tunnel on the palmar side — through which the flexor tendons and the median nerve pass. They articulate with the radius proximally and with the five metacarpal bones distally. Rather than acting through attached muscles, most carpals are moved passively by the forces transmitted through them, contributing to the mobility and stability of the wrist joint.

Clinical significance

The scaphoid is the most commonly fractured carpal, typically from a fall on an outstretched hand, and its fracture risks avascular necrosis because of its retrograde blood supply. The lunate is the most commonly dislocated carpal. Compression of the median nerve within the carpal tunnel produces carpal tunnel syndrome.
