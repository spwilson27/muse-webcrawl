Police power (United States constitutional law)

In United States constitutional law, police power is the capacity of the states to regulate behaviour and enforce order within their territory for the betterment of the health, safety, morals, and general welfare of their inhabitants. The term derives not from the police force but from the Greek polis, and it encompasses most of the ordinary regulatory authority exercised by state and local governments: zoning and land use, building and health codes, professional licensing, quarantine, and much of criminal law.

Basis and scope

Unlike the federal government, which possesses only the powers enumerated in the Constitution (plus those necessary and proper to carry them out), the states retain the general police power as an aspect of their sovereignty, preserved by the Tenth Amendment. The federal government has no general police power, though it exercises analogous authority in federal territories and the District of Columbia and through powers such as the Commerce Clause. In The Federalist No. 45, James Madison described the powers reserved to the states as extending to "the lives, liberties, and properties of the people, and the internal order, improvement, and prosperity of the State."

Limits

The police power is broad but not unlimited. State regulation may not violate specific constitutional rights, and under the Fourteenth Amendment it is subject to substantive due process and equal protection review. The Supreme Court's classic formulation in cases such as Mugler v. Kansas (1887) and Lawton v. Steele (1894) asks whether the regulation is reasonably related to a legitimate public purpose and not unduly oppressive; in the Lochner era (c. 1897–1937) the Court struck down economic regulations as infringements of liberty of contract, but since 1937 economic regulation has received highly deferential rational-basis review. Other limits arise from the dormant Commerce Clause, the Takings Clause (a regulation that goes "too far" may require compensation), preemption by federal law, and provisions of state constitutions. Public health measures — vaccination mandates in Jacobson v. Massachusetts (1905), quarantine, and emergency orders — are canonical exercises of the police power.
