Atomium

The Atomium is a landmark modernist building in Brussels, Belgium, originally constructed as the centrepiece of the 1958 Brussels World's Fair (Expo 58). Designed by the engineer André Waterkeyn with architects André and Jean Polak, it stands 102 metres (335 ft) tall and consists of nine 18-metre-diameter (59 ft) stainless steel-clad spheres connected by tubes, forming the shape of a unit cell of an iron crystal magnified 165 billion times. It is now a museum and one of Belgium's most recognisable symbols.

History

The Atomium was intended to be temporary, standing only for the six months of Expo 58, as an emblem of the peaceful uses of atomic energy and of Belgium's engineering and metallurgical industries. Its popularity was such that demolition was repeatedly postponed, and it became permanent. The spheres contain exhibition halls and public spaces; the top sphere houses a restaurant with panoramic views over Brussels, and the central tube contains one of the fastest lifts of its time, reaching the top in 23 seconds at 5 m/s. Escalators in the connecting tubes include some of the longest in Europe.

Renovation

By the early 2000s the original aluminium cladding of the spheres had badly weathered. A major renovation (2004–2006) replaced the aluminium sheets with stainless steel, replaced the windows, renewed the interior, and installed LED lighting that outlines the spheres at night. The building reopened on 18 February 2006.

Use and copyright

Inside, the Atomium hosts permanent exhibitions about Expo 58 and temporary exhibitions on science, design and society, and it has become a standard backdrop for views of the Heysel plateau alongside the nearby King Baudouin Stadium and Mini-Europe. Belgium's strict copyright regime long made reproduction of images of the Atomium a test case for freedom of panorama; in 2016 Belgium introduced a freedom-of-panorama exception covering works of architecture and sculpture in public places.
