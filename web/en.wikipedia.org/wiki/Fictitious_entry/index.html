Fictitious entry

Trap streets and fictitious entries are deliberate errors used to catch copiers of maps and reference works.

Fictitious entries, also known as trap streets (in maps) or mountweazels (in reference works), are deliberately fabricated entries in a map, directory, encyclopedia, or other reference work, inserted as a copyright trap to detect subsequent plagiarism or copying: if the fictitious entry appears in a competitor's work, it is evidence of copying.

Terminology

The term mountweazel derives from Lillian Virginia Mountweazel, a fictitious fountain designer and photographer who appeared in the 1975 New Columbia Encyclopedia. "Trap street" is used for a fake or misdrawn street on a map. Similar devices include paper towns (also called phantom settlements), fictitious telephone directory entries (sometimes called "salting"), and canary traps in documents.

Examples

The paper town of Agloe, New York, created by the General Drafting Company in the 1930s, later appeared on maps by Rand McNally; when the case came to attention, it emerged that a real "Agloe General Store" had since taken the name from the map, making the place real. The 2001 edition of the New Oxford American Dictionary planted the word esquivalience, defined as "the wilful avoidance of one's official responsibilities", to catch copiers.

Legal status

Because facts are generally not copyrightable, fictitious entries are a practical rather than strictly legal remedy: their presence proves that a work was copied rather than independently compiled, even though the copied facts themselves might not infringe. Courts have treated such evidence as relevant in copyright disputes over compilations.
