God in Christianity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In Christianity, God is believed to be the eternal, supreme being who created and preserves all things. Christians believe in a monotheistic conception of God, generally understood through the doctrine of the Trinity: one God existing eternally as three distinct persons — the Father, the Son (Jesus Christ) and the Holy Spirit — who are consubstantial, co-equal and co-eternal. God is described as omnipotent, omniscient, omnipresent and perfectly good, and as both transcendent of and involved with creation.

Attributes

Classical Christian theology, drawing on Scripture and Greek philosophy, attributes to God eternity, immutability, impassibility and simplicity (that God is not composed of parts). God is creator of heaven and earth, sustains all things, and acts in history, supremely — in Christian belief — in the Incarnation, in which the Son became human in Jesus of Nazareth for humanity's salvation.

The Trinity

Formulated against early heresies and defined by the councils of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381), Trinitarian doctrine holds that the three persons are distinct but not separate gods. The New Testament's triadic language — the baptisms "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" — and the divinity of Christ and the Spirit provided the raw material for the doctrine.

Names and conceptions

Christians address God pre-eminently as "Father", following Jesus's own usage, and inherit from Judaism the name revealed in the Hebrew Bible (the Tetragrammaton). Some traditions, such as Oneness Pentecostalism, reject Trinitarian formulation, while Latter-day Saint theology conceives of the Godhead as three distinct beings — significant exceptions to the mainstream doctrine described here.
