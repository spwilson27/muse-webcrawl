Envy-freeness

Envy-freeness, also known as no-envy, is a criterion for fair division. It says that, when resources are allocated among people with equal rights, each person should receive a share that is, in their own mind, at least as good as the share received by any other person. In other words, no person should feel envy towards another person.

History

The criterion was introduced into the fair division literature by George Gamow and Marvin Stern in their 1958 book Puzzle-Math, and independently by Duncan Foley in 1967 in the context of resource allocation in economics. It has since become one of the most studied fairness properties in economics and computer science.

Definitions

An allocation is envy-free if every agent values their own bundle at least as much as any other agent's bundle. Variants include:

  • Envy-freeness up to one good (EF1) — envy can be eliminated by removing at most one good from the envied bundle; an EF1 allocation always exists for goods.
  • Envy-freeness up to any good (EFX) — envy can be eliminated by removing any single good; existence of EFX allocations is a famous open problem for more than three agents.
  • Approximate envy-freeness and epistemic envy-freeness, where agents have partial information about others' bundles.

Relation to other criteria

For two agents, envy-freeness coincides with proportionality. For more agents, an envy-free allocation is always proportional, but a proportional allocation need not be envy-free. Envy-freeness is also closely related to Pareto efficiency in the study of fair and efficient allocations, and to the concept of competitive equilibrium from equal incomes (CEEI), which produces allocations that are both envy-free and efficient under suitable conditions.
