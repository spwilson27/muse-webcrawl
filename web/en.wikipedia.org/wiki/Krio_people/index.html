The Krio people (also called the Creole people) are an ethnic group of Sierra Leone. They are descendants of freed African American, Afro-Caribbean and Liberated African slaves who settled in the Western Area of Sierra Leone between 1787 and about 1885. The colony of Freetown was founded by the Sierra Leone Company as a settlement for former enslaved people, with the support of British abolitionists.

Origins

The Krio descend from several settler groups: the "Black Poor" of London (1787), Black Loyalists from Nova Scotia (1792) who had fought for the British in the American Revolutionary War, Jamaican Maroons deported after the Second Maroon War (1800), and, after 1808, tens of thousands of "Liberated Africans" or "recaptives" freed from slave ships by the Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron. These culturally diverse groups intermarried and fused into a single community distinguished by its English-based creole language, Krio, and by a largely Christian, Western-oriented culture.

Culture

Krio culture blends African heritage with British and American influences. Distinctive features include the Krio language — now the lingua franca of Sierra Leone as a whole, spoken by the great majority of the population — traditional wooden board houses of the Freetown Peninsula modelled on Caribbean and American styles, cuisine combining West African and European elements, and secret societies such as the Bondo and the hunting societies. Krio Christians historically filled positions in the colonial administration, education, the church and commerce throughout British West Africa; Fourah Bay College (founded 1827) made Freetown a centre of education nicknamed the "Athens of West Africa".

Demographics and status

Although the Krio constitute only a small percentage — around 1–3% — of Sierra Leone's population, their language and culture have been disproportionately influential. Since independence in 1961, political power has largely been held by the larger ethnic groups of the interior, and tensions between the Krio and other groups, as well as internal debates about Krio identity and land rights in the Western Area, remain features of national politics.
