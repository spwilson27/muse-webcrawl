Passion (music)

In music, a Passion is a setting of the Passion of Christ — the Gospel accounts of the suffering and crucifixion of Jesus — for voices and often instruments, traditionally performed during Holy Week. The Passion is a major genre of Christian sacred music, with roots in liturgical chant and the medieval practice of singing the Gospel narrative to plainsong tones.

History

Origins

From the early Middle Ages, the Passion narratives from the four Gospels were chanted during Holy Week, with different singers or pitch levels distinguishing the narrator (Evangelist), Christ, and the crowd (turba). By the Renaissance, composers such as Orlando di Lasso and Tomás Luis de Victoria wrote polyphonic settings of the turba choruses while the rest remained chant (the so-called responsorial Passion), or set the entire text polyphonically (motet Passion).

Baroque Passions

The oratorio Passion, developed in Lutheran Germany, combined the Gospel narrative with newly written reflective poetry in recitatives, arias, and congregational chorales. Heinrich Schütz wrote restrained a cappella Passions; the genre reached its summit in Johann Sebastian Bach's St John Passion (1724) and St Matthew Passion (1727), alongside a lost St Mark Passion. Georg Philipp Telemann composed numerous Passions for Hamburg, and Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach continued the tradition.

Later works

Interest in the genre waned in the Classical era but revived later: notable 20th- and 21st-century Passions include Krzysztof Penderecki's St Luke Passion (1966), Arvo Pärt's Passio (1982), Sofia Gubaidulina's St John Passion (2000), and Osvaldo Golijov's La Pasión según San Marcos (2000), commissioned for the Passion 2000 project in Stuttgart along with works by Tan Dun and Wolfgang Rihm.

Dramatis personae

Typical solo roles in a Passion setting include the Evangelist (narrator), Jesus, Pontius Pilate, Peter, and other minor figures, with the choir taking the parts of the crowd, soldiers, priests and disciples, as well as singing reflective chorales.
