The Treaty of Picquigny was a peace treaty negotiated on 29 August 1475 between the Kingdom of England and the Kingdom of France. It followed an invasion of France by Edward IV of England in alliance with Burgundy and Brittany, and ended that campaign without a major battle: Louis XI of France effectively paid the English army to go home.

Background

Edward IV, restored to the throne after the Wars of the Roses, landed at Calais in the summer of 1475 with a large and well-equipped army, renewing the English claim to the French crown. His allies disappointed him: Charles the Bold of Burgundy arrived late and departed to besiege Neuss, and Breton support was minimal. Louis XI, preferring money to battle, opened negotiations. The two kings met on a specially built bridge over the Somme at Picquigny, separated by a wooden grille so that they could speak but not reach one another.

Terms

• An immediate payment of 75,000 crowns to Edward, and an annual pension of 50,000 crowns.
• Pensions for many of Edward's leading courtiers, which Louis used as a tool of influence.
• A truce of seven years.
• The ransom of Margaret of Anjou, the former queen held in England, for 50,000 crowns; she returned to France and renounced her claims.
• A marriage agreement between the Dauphin Charles and Edward's daughter Elizabeth of York (never carried out).

Aftermath

The treaty was humiliating in the eyes of some contemporaries—the chronicler Philippe de Commynes famously described Louis buying off the English—but it freed Louis to deal with Burgundy, culminating in Charles the Bold's death at Nancy in 1477. In England, the French pension bolstered Edward's finances and his independence from Parliament for the rest of his reign. The annual payment continued until Edward's death in 1483.
