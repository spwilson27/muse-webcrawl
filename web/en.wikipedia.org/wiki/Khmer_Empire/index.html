Khmer Empire

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Angkor Wat's five lotus-bud towers reflected in the moat at sunrise]

The Khmer Empire (802–1431) was a powerful Hindu-Buddhist empire in Southeast Asia, centred in what is now Cambodia and at its height ruling much of mainland Southeast Asia — modern Laos, Thailand and southern Vietnam. Founded, by tradition, when Jayavarman II proclaimed himself universal monarch on Mount Mahendra in 802, its kings built the temple-city of Angkor, the largest pre-industrial urban complex in the world, including Angkor Wat under Suryavarman II and the Buddhist capital Angkor Thom under Jayavarman VII. Hydraulic engineering — vast reservoirs (barays) and canals — underpinned its rice wealth and its monuments.

Angkor

Successive capitals at Angkor (Yasodharapura) accumulated temples conceived as models of the Hindu cosmos: the five-towered Angkor Wat (early 12th century), the world's largest religious monument, originally dedicated to Vishnu; and under Jayavarman VII (r. 1181–c. 1218) the walled city of Angkor Thom with the Bayon's serene giant faces, plus hospitals, resthouses and roads across the empire. A Chinese envoy, Zhou Daguan (1296–97), left a vivid account of the court's splendour and daily life.

Decline

From the 13th century Theravada Buddhism transformed Khmer society from below, the rising Thai kingdoms of Sukhothai and Ayutthaya pressed from the west (sacking Angkor in 1431), and the immense water system may have become a liability amid 14th–15th-century droughts and floods documented in tree rings. The court moved south to the Phnom Penh region; Angkor Wat, never wholly abandoned, survived as a Buddhist shrine in the forest.
