Conflagration

A conflagration is a large fire that threatens human life, animal life, health and property.

A conflagration is a large and destructive fire that threatens human life, animal life, health and/or property. It may also be described as a blaze or simply a fire. A conflagration can begin accidentally, be naturally caused (by lightning), or intentionally created (arson). A very large fire that is wind-driven and moves quickly is often called a firestorm when it creates and sustains its own wind system.

Conflagrations have historically devastated cities built largely of wood, where closely packed combustible structures allowed fire to spread from building to building faster than bucket brigades or early fire engines could contain it. Famous examples include the Great Fire of London (1666), the Great Chicago Fire (1871), the fire that followed the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, and the firebombing raids of the Second World War, which produced urban firestorms in Hamburg, Dresden and Tokyo.

Causes and behaviour

For a conflagration to develop, a fire requires fuel, oxygen and heat, together with conditions that allow rapid spread: combustible construction, strong winds, low humidity and failure of early suppression. In wildland areas, a conflagration may take the form of a wildfire or forest fire. Urban conflagrations are fought by fire services using water, firebreaks and controlled demolition to remove fuel from the fire's path.

Prevention

Modern building codes, fire-resistant materials, compartmentation, sprinkler systems and municipal fire departments have greatly reduced the frequency of urban conflagrations in developed countries, although large wildland–urban interface fires remain a serious hazard.
