Victory Stele of Naram-Sin

The Victory Stele of Naram-Sin, now in the Louvre.

Createdc. 2254–2218 BC
MaterialPink limestone
Size200 cm × 150 cm
Present locationLouvre Museum, Paris

The Victory Stele of Naram-Sin is a stele that dates to approximately 2254–2218 BC, in the time of the Akkadian Empire, and is now displayed in the Louvre Museum in Paris. The relief commemorates the victory of Naram-Sin, king of Akkad, over the Lullubi, a mountain people of the Zagros. Carved in pink limestone, about two metres tall, it is considered one of the masterpieces of ancient Near Eastern art.

Description

Naram-Sin is shown much larger than his soldiers, ascending a mountain peak beneath astral symbols of the gods. He wears the horned helmet of divinity — among the earliest depictions of a Mesopotamian king presenting himself as a god. His troops march in orderly files behind and below him, while defeated Lullubi tumble, plead, and fall beneath his feet. The composition abandons traditional registers in favour of a unified diagonal landscape, an innovation for its time.

History

The stele was originally erected in Sippar (or possibly in the conquered territory itself). Some twelve centuries later the Elamite king Shutruk-Nakhunte carried it off as booty to Susa, where he added an inscription boasting of the capture; the original Akkadian inscription, in the name of Naram-Sin, is partly effaced. French excavators under Jacques de Morgan recovered the monument at Susa in 1898.
