Tertiary education, also known as higher education or post-secondary education, is the level of education following secondary schooling, provided by universities, colleges and other institutions that award academic degrees or higher-level vocational qualifications. The World Bank defines tertiary education broadly to include universities as well as polytechnics and post-secondary technical and professional training.

Structure

Under UNESCO's ISCED classification, tertiary education spans short-cycle programmes (level 5), bachelor's degrees (level 6), master's degrees (level 7) and doctorates (level 8). In Europe, the Bologna Process has harmonised these cycles across dozens of countries to ease recognition and mobility. Admission normally requires completion of secondary education, and provision mixes public and private institutions with widely varying funding models — from tuition-free systems in much of northern Europe to high-fee systems in the United States.

Expansion

The sociologist Martin Trow described the transition from elite (under 15% participation) to mass (15–50%) and then universal (over 50%) higher education; global gross enrolment has risen from under 20% in 2000 to around 40% today, with women now outnumbering men in most regions. Expansion has brought debates about graduate employment, student debt, quality assurance, academic freedom and the role of rankings.

Functions

Beyond teaching, tertiary institutions conduct most basic research, train professionals such as doctors, engineers and teachers, and serve as employers and cultural anchors in their regions. Distance and online provision — including MOOCs — has broadened access further.
