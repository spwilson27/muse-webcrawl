The Great Buddha of Tōdai-ji (Japanese: 奈良大仏, Nara Daibutsu) is a monumental bronze statue of the Buddha Vairocana housed in the Daibutsuden (Great Buddha Hall) of Tōdai-ji temple in Nara, Japan. Commissioned by Emperor Shōmu as the centrepiece of a nationwide network of provincial temples (kokubunji), the statue was cast beginning in 747 and dedicated in a famous eye-opening ceremony in 752 attended by thousands of monks, including priests from China and India. At about 15 metres tall and weighing around 500 tonnes, it remains one of the largest bronze Buddha statues in the world.

History

The project consumed most of Japan's bronze production for years and was cast in eight stages after early attempts failed. The statue has been damaged repeatedly: an earthquake in 855 caused the head to fall, and both the statue and its hall suffered in fires during the wars of 1180 (the siege of Nara by Taira no Shigehira) and 1567. The present head dates from the Edo-period recasting, and much of the body was repaired under the direction of the monk Kōkei in the 1180s–90s; the current Great Buddha Hall, rebuilt in 1709, is the largest wooden building in the world under one roof yet is only about two-thirds the width of the original.

Features

Behind the statue is a pillar with a hole the size of the Buddha's nostril ("the Pillar of Light"); visitors who crawl through it are said to gain enlightenment. The hall is part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site "Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara". The Great Buddha is flanked by statues of Kannon and Kokūzō bodhisattvas and guarded by the Four Heavenly Kings.
