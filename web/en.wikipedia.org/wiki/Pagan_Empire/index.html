The Pagan Empire (Burmese: ပုဂံအင်ပါယာ) was the first kingdom to unify the regions that constitute modern Myanmar. Centred on the city of Pagan (present-day Bagan) on the Irrawaddy River, the empire flourished from the 11th to the late 13th century. Its 250 years of rule laid the foundations of Burmese culture, language, and Theravada Buddhism, and saw the construction of thousands of Buddhist temples on the Pagan plain.

History

According to the Burmese chronicles, Pagan was founded in 849, but its rise to empire is traditionally dated to the accession of Anawrahta in 1044. Anawrahta (r. 1044–1077) subdued the Mon kingdom of Thaton in 1057 — an event chroniclers credit with bringing Theravada scriptures and relics to Pagan — and extended his authority over the Irrawaddy valley, the Shan hills, and parts of Arakan and Tenasserim. His successors, notably Kyansittha, consolidated the realm and its administration.

At its height in the 12th and 13th centuries the empire administered its core dry-zone territory directly and held frontier regions through tributary and marriage ties. Irrigation works around Kyaukse and Minbu made the state rich in rice, funding an enormous programme of temple construction encouraged by the merit-making ethos of Theravada devotion; chronicles attribute some 10,000 religious monuments to the Pagan plain, of which roughly 2,000–3,000 survive today in some form.

Religion and culture

Pagan's court patronised Theravada Buddhism with strong links to Sri Lanka, while Mahayana Buddhism, tantric cults, Hindu deities, and the indigenous nat spirit worship coexisted in its art and ritual. The Burmese script, adapted from Mon script, was standardised in this period, and the four-language Myazedi inscription (c. 1113), in Pali, Pyu, Mon, and Burmese, is a key monument of the kingdom's epigraphy. Pagan-period art fused Mon, Pyu, and Indian influences into a distinct Burmese style.

Decline and fall

The vast donations of tax-exempt land to the sangha steadily eroded the crown's revenue base, weakening the state. After rejecting Mongol demands for tribute, the empire faced Yuan dynasty invasions in 1277 (battle of Ngasaunggyan), 1283, and 1287; King Narathihapate fled the capital, earning the chronicle epithet "the king who fled from the Chinese". Pagan survived as a diminished city-state, but central authority fragmented, giving way to the warring successor kingdoms of the 14th century.

Legacy

The Pagan period is regarded in Myanmar as a golden age: it established the Burmese language in written form, Theravada Buddhism as the dominant faith, and a repertoire of architecture — stupas such as the Shwezigon and temples such as the Ananda — that remains central to Burmese identity. The Bagan Archaeological Zone was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2019.
