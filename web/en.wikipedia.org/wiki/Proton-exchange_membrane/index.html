Proton-exchange membrane

Common material: Nafion (sulfonated tetrafluoroethylene copolymer)
Function: Conducts protons, blocks electrons and gases
Applications: Fuel cells, electrolysers, flow batteries

A proton-exchange membrane, or polymer-electrolyte membrane (PEM), is a semipermeable membrane generally made from an ionomer and designed to conduct protons while acting as an electronic insulator and a barrier to reactant gases such as hydrogen and oxygen. It is the heart of proton-exchange membrane fuel cells and PEM electrolysers.

Properties

The best-known PEM material is Nafion, a sulfonated fluoropolymer whose sulfonic-acid groups form water-filled channels through which hydrated protons move. Conductivity depends strongly on hydration and temperature, so fuel-cell systems carefully manage humidification. Membranes are typically tens of micrometres thick.

Applications

Beyond vehicle and stationary fuel cells, PEMs are used in water electrolysis for green hydrogen, in chlor-alkali production, in vanadium redox flow batteries, and in sensors and electrodialysis. Research seeks cheaper hydrocarbon membranes, higher-temperature operation, and greater resistance to chemical degradation.
