Kenshō

Kenshō
Japanese見性 (kenshō)
Literal meaning"Seeing one's nature"
TraditionZen Buddhism (esp. Rinzai)
Related termSatori (deeper or final awakening)

Kenshō (Japanese: 見性, "seeing [one's true] nature") is a term in Zen Buddhism for an initial insight or awakening experience in which the practitioner directly perceives the true nature of reality and of the self. Kenshō is often described as seeing into one's own Buddha-nature and realising the emptiness and interdependence of all things.

Meaning and usage

Kenshō is most emphasised in the Rinzai school of Japanese Zen, where it is a central goal of kōan practice and is tested and acknowledged by the teacher (rōshi) in private interview (dokusan). It is generally distinguished from satori, which in common usage denotes a deeper, more complete or final enlightenment, although the terms are sometimes used interchangeably. In the Sōtō school, which emphasises shikantaza ("just sitting") and the identity of practice and realisation, sudden awakening experiences are given less prominence, though the concept is not rejected.

After kenshō

Zen teachers traditionally stress that kenshō is a beginning, not an end: the insight must be deepened and integrated through continued practice, sometimes described as "polishing" the realisation in everyday activity. Traditions such as Hakuin Ekaku's system placed kenshō within a structured curriculum of kōan study followed by post-satori training. Accounts of kenshō experiences typically are brief and non-conceptual, and Zen literature warns against attachment to the experience itself (makyō).
