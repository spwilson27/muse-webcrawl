Country

[Image: a political world map with each country coloured distinctly, in an atlas style]

A country is a distinct part of the world, such as a state, a nation or other political entity. The term is used loosely and may refer to a sovereign state, a constituent country of a sovereign state (such as England, Scotland and Wales within the United Kingdom), a dependent territory, or a geographic and cultural region associated with a particular people.

There are 193 member states of the United Nations, plus two observer states (the Holy See and Palestine), commonly taken as the modern reference list of sovereign countries, though several other territories and partially recognised states complicate any exact count.

States, nations and countries

In political science, a state is a political organisation with centralised government and sovereignty over a territory and population; a nation is a community of people sharing identity, language, culture or history. A nation state is one in which the two roughly coincide — an ideal real countries approximate to different degrees.

The modern state system is conventionally dated from the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which entrenched the sovereignty of territorial rulers; decolonisation in the twentieth century multiplied the number of independent countries from about fifty in 1900 to around two hundred today.

Recognition and lists

Whether a polity counts as a country depends on recognition by other states and membership of international bodies, so entities such as Taiwan, Kosovo, Somaliland and Northern Cyprus sit in a grey zone: functioning states with limited recognition. Lists also include dependent territories (Greenland, Puerto Rico) in many statistical contexts without implying sovereignty.

Countries differ enormously in scale — from Russia, the largest by area, to Vatican City and Monaco, smaller than many farms; and from India and China, each with over 1.4 billion people, to states with populations in the tens of thousands.
