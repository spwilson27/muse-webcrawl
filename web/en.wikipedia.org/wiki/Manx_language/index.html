Manx (Manx: Gaelg or Gailck) is a Goidelic Celtic language native to the Isle of Man, closely related to Irish and Scottish Gaelic. It is famous as a rare documented case of language revival: the last native speaker raised speaking Manx in a traditional community, Ned Maddrell, died in 1974, yet a community of second-language speakers had already formed around the language, and today children are again being raised with Manx as a first language.

History

Manx began to diverge from Irish and Scottish Gaelic around the 13th century and was first written down in the early 17th century, using an orthography based on English (hence spellings quite unlike its sister languages: e.g. Gaelg for "Gaelic"). The Book of Common Prayer and the Bible were translated into Manx in the 18th century, giving it a substantial literature. Through the 19th and early 20th centuries, economic pressure and stigma drove a steep decline from tens of thousands of speakers to a handful of elderly natives by the 1960s, whose speech was recorded by the Irish Folklore Commission and the Manx Museum.

Revival

The revival gathered pace from the 1970s with learners' organisations such as Yn Çheshaght Ghailckagh (the Manx Language Society, founded 1899), Manx-medium playgroups (Mooinjer Veggey), and from 2001 the Bunscoill Ghaelgagh, a primary school teaching entirely through Manx. The 2021 census recorded 2,023 people able to speak, read, or write Manx. The language appears on public signage across the island, in Tynwald proceedings, and in a growing body of new literature and media; UNESCO's classification of Manx as "extinct" was famously corrected after schoolchildren wrote to ask which language their letters were written in.

Features

Like other Celtic languages, Manx has initial consonant mutations and VSO-influenced syntax, though centuries of English contact have reshaped its grammar toward analytic forms, including a periphrastic perfect and loss of many older inflections.
