Firebox

A firebox is the chamber in which fuel is burned in a furnace, boiler, or steam locomotive. The term is most strongly associated with the firebox of a steam locomotive, where it is the part of the boiler in which the fire is maintained and where heat is transferred most intensely to the boiler water.

Steam locomotive firebox

In a locomotive, the firebox consists of an inner firebox, typically of copper or steel, surrounded by an outer wrapper, with a water space between the two held apart by staybolts. The fire burns on a grate at the bottom; ash falls into an ashpan below. Heat passes from the firebox into the boiler barrel through fire tubes and flues. The fireman shovels coal through a firehole door, and the size and shape of the firebox largely determine the locomotive's steaming capacity and the fuels it can burn.

Other uses

In domestic and industrial boilers and furnaces, the firebox (or combustion chamber) is the refractory-lined space where oil, gas, wood, or coal is burned. In wood stoves it is the lined box where logs burn. The refractory lining — often fire brick — protects steel surfaces from the highest temperatures.
