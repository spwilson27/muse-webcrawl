Isaac ben Solomon Luria Ashkenazi (1534 – 1572), commonly known as Ha'ARI (the Lion) or Arizal, was a leading rabbi and Jewish mystic in Safed, in the Ottoman Land of Israel. He is considered the father of contemporary Kabbalah, whose teachings — Lurianic Kabbalah — reshaped Jewish mysticism and spirituality.

Life

Luria was born in Jerusalem and raised in Egypt after his father's death, studying Talmud in Cairo and living for some years in seclusion on an island in the Nile, absorbed in the Zohar. In 1570 he settled in Safed, then the foremost centre of Jewish mysticism, where he gathered a close circle of disciples — among them Hayyim Vital, who recorded his teachings. Luria himself wrote almost nothing; he died at 38 in an epidemic, after barely two years of teaching, yet his influence proved immense.

Teachings

Lurianic Kabbalah describes creation through tzimtzum — the self-contraction of the infinite God (Ein Sof) to make space for the world — followed by shevirat ha-kelim, the "breaking of the vessels", in which divine light shattered the vessels meant to contain it, scattering sparks of holiness among the husks of evil (kelipot). Humanity's task is tikkun, the restoration and repair of the world, achieved through prayer with mystical intentions (kavanot) and observance of the commandments, which liberate the trapped sparks. The system also includes doctrines of reincarnation (gilgul) and the faces (partzufim) of the divine. Spread by disciples and popularised across the Jewish world, Lurianic ideas prepared the ground for later movements including Sabbatianism and Hasidism.
