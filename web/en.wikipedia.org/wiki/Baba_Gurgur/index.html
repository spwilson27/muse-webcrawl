Baba Gurgur (Kurdish: بابا گورگور) is a large oil field near the city of Kirkuk in northern Iraq. It is famous historically as the site of the discovery well that opened Iraq's petroleum era and, in local tradition, for its "eternal fire" — a natural gas seep that has burned for thousands of years.

The eternal fire

Long before drilling, the Baba Gurgur site was known for flames issuing from the ground where natural gas escaped through fissures in the rock. The "burning mountain" is mentioned in ancient accounts of the region and is sometimes associated in folk tradition with the fiery furnace described in the Book of Daniel. The flames have drawn pilgrims and curiosity for centuries.

Discovery

The Turkish Petroleum Company (later the Iraq Petroleum Company) spudded the Baba Gurgur No. 1 well in 1927. On 15 October 1927 the well struck oil and blew out, flowing uncontrolled at an estimated rate of about 95,000 barrels per day for nine days before it was capped. The gusher confirmed one of the largest oil accumulations then known and transformed the economics and politics of the region.

The field

Baba Gurgur forms the principal dome of the greater Kirkuk structure, an elongated anticline roughly 100 km long. Oil is produced mainly from fractured Cretaceous and Tertiary limestones. The field was a cornerstone of Iraqi production for decades and remains strategically and politically significant, lying in territory disputed between the Iraqi federal government and the Kurdistan Regional Government.
