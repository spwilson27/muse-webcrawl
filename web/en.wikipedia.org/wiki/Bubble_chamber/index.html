Bubble chamber

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Famous bubble chamber photograph: spiralling tracks of particles in liquid hydrogen, with a neutrino interaction vertex]
Tracks in the Big European Bubble Chamber.

A bubble chamber is a particle detector filled with a superheated transparent liquid (usually liquid hydrogen) in which ionising particles nucleate trails of vapour bubbles along their paths, photographed for analysis. Invented by Donald Glaser in 1952 (Nobel Prize 1960), it is the superheated mirror-image of the Cloud chamber — liquid boiling along tracks instead of vapour condensing on them.

Bubble chambers dominated particle physics in the 1950s–70s: Luis Alvarez's Berkeley programme scaled them to metres of liquid hydrogen with automated film measurement, discovering a flood of resonances (Nobel 1968) that provoked the quark model. CERN's Gargamelle chamber found weak neutral currents (1973) — pivotal evidence for the Electroweak interaction — and the Big European Bubble Chamber (BEBC) served into the 1980s. Electronic detectors, faster and selective, replaced them; their photographs remain particle physics' iconic art, and their data archives are still re-analysed.

Principle

The liquid is held just above its boiling point by pressure, then suddenly expanded: for a few milliseconds it is superheated, and only the local energy deposition of a particle track can trigger boiling — bubbles grow to photographable size in time with the beam pulse, after which recompression resets the chamber. Sensitivity to the beam's timing, and the liquid's double duty as target and detector, were the chamber's genius.
