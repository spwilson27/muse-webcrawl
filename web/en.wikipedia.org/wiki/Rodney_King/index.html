Rodney Glen King (April 2, 1965 – June 17, 2012) was an American man who was a victim of police brutality. On March 3, 1991, he was beaten by Los Angeles Police Department (LAPD) officers during his arrest, after a high-speed chase, for driving while intoxicated on the I-210 freeway. A bystander, George Holliday, filmed the incident from his nearby balcony and sent the footage to a local news station; the video became a worldwide sensation and a landmark in public scrutiny of policing.

The beating and trials

King, on parole for robbery, led police on a high-speed chase before stopping in Lake View Terrace. Officers struck him repeatedly with batons and kicked him while he was on the ground; he suffered a fractured skull, broken bones and teeth, and lasting brain damage. Four officers were charged with assault and use of excessive force. On April 29, 1992, a jury in Simi Valley acquitted three of them and failed to reach a verdict on one charge against the fourth.

The 1992 riots

The acquittals sparked the 1992 Los Angeles riots, six days of unrest in which 63 people were killed, thousands injured and roughly $1 billion in property damage done. During the rioting, King made a televised appeal for calm, asking, "Can we all get along?" In a subsequent federal trial for violating King's civil rights, officers Stacey Koon and Laurence Powell were convicted and sentenced to 30 months in prison. King was awarded $3.8 million in a civil suit against the City of Los Angeles.

Later life

King struggled for years with alcoholism and further arrests, appeared on the reality series Celebrity Rehab, and in 2012 published his memoir The Riot Within: My Journey from Rebellion to Redemption. On June 17, 2012, he was found dead in the swimming pool of his home in Rialto, California; the coroner ruled the death an accidental drowning with heart disease and drug and alcohol use as contributing factors.

Legacy

The Holliday video is regarded as a precursor of the citizen-journalism era of police accountability, and King's case remains a central reference point in debates over race, policing and video evidence in the United States.
