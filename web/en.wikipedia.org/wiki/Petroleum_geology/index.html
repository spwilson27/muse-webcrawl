Petroleum geology

Petroleum geology is the study of the origin, occurrence, movement and accumulation of hydrocarbon fuels — principally oil and natural gas — and of the geological methods used to find and evaluate them. It combines sedimentology, stratigraphy, structural geology, geochemistry and geophysics.

The petroleum system

A working petroleum system requires five elements. A source rock, typically an organic-rich shale, generates hydrocarbons as burial heating converts its kerogen to oil and gas within the "oil window" (roughly 60–120 °C). Expelled hydrocarbons migrate buoyantly through permeable strata until they accumulate in a reservoir rock — usually porous sandstone or fractured/porous carbonate — sealed above by an impermeable cap rock such as shale or salt, arranged in a trap: structural traps include anticlines and fault closures, while stratigraphic traps arise from pinch-outs, reefs and unconformities. Timing is critical: the trap must exist before migration ends.

Exploration methods

Exploration geologists map surface geology, then image the subsurface with seismic reflection surveys (two- and three-dimensional), gravity and magnetic data. Prospects are ranked by estimated reserves and geological chance of success and tested by exploratory ("wildcat") wells; well logging, core analysis and drill-stem tests evaluate discoveries, and reservoir simulation guides development and production. Many basins yield only one commercial discovery for every ten or more wildcats, though seismic advances have steadily improved success rates.

Scope

Beyond conventional accumulations, petroleum geology now encompasses unconventional resources — shale oil and gas, tight sands and oil sands — where the source rock is itself the reservoir and production depends on horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing. The discipline also informs carbon capture and storage site selection and the assessment of subsurface hydrogen storage.
