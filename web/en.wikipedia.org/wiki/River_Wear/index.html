River Wear

SourceWearhead, County Durham
MouthNorth Sea at Sunderland
LengthAbout 97 km (60 mi)
BasinNorth East England
TownsBishop Auckland, Durham, Chester-le-Street, Sunderland

The River Wear in Northern England rises in the Pennines in County Durham and flows eastwards, for about 97 kilometres (60 miles), to the North Sea at Sunderland. It is notable for the dramatic meander — the Wear incised meanders — with which it loops around the rocky peninsula occupied by Durham Cathedral and Castle, and for the coal trade, shipbuilding and glassmaking that once lined its lower reaches.

Course

The river rises at Wearhead in the North Pennines, where the Burnhope Burn and Killhope Burn meet, and flows down Weardale past Stanhope and Wolsingham to Bishop Auckland. At Durham the river winds in a deep, wooded gorge around the cathedral peninsula before passing Chester-le-Street and Washington. At Sunderland it is crossed by the Wearmouth bridges and reaches the sea between the piers at Roker.

History

Coal from the Durham coalfield was shipped from the lower Wear for centuries, and Sunderland grew into one of the largest shipbuilding towns in the world, launching wooden and later iron and steel vessels from yards along the banks. The river also supported glassmaking, pottery, chemicals and marine engineering. As these industries declined in the late twentieth century, the riverside was redeveloped for housing, business and the University of Sunderland.

Geology and wildlife

In Weardale the river cuts through Carboniferous limestones, sandstones and shales of the Pennines; near Durham it crosses the Permian Magnesian Limestone. The Wear is an important salmon and sea trout river, a recovery from the serious pollution of the industrial era, and its valley supports otters, dippers and grey wagtails. Herrington Country Park and other riverside sites have reclaimed former colliery land along its course.

Sport

Sunderland A.F.C. takes the nickname "the Black Cats" and its stadium, the Stadium of Light, stands on the north bank of the Wear on the site of Monkwearmouth Colliery. Durham's stretch of river hosts rowing regattas, including Durham Regatta, held since the nineteenth century. The name of the region, Wearside, and of Sunderland's port, Wearmouth, derive from the river.
