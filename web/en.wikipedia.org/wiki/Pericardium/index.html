Pericardium

The pericardium (pl. pericardia), also called the pericardial sac, is a double-walled sac containing the heart and the roots of the great vessels. It has two layers, an outer fibrous layer and an inner serous layer, and encloses the pericardial cavity, which contains a small amount of lubricating fluid. The pericardium fixes the heart's position within the mediastinum, limits its distension, and protects it from infection and friction.

Structure

The tough fibrous pericardium is continuous with the central tendon of the diaphragm below and the adventitia of the great vessels above. The serous pericardium consists of a parietal layer lining the fibrous sac and a visceral layer (the epicardium) covering the heart itself. Reflections of the serous layer form two recesses, the transverse and oblique pericardial sinuses, landmarks in cardiac surgery.

Function

The pericardium anchors the heart, prevents over-expansion during sudden volume increases, provides a low-friction environment via its fluid, and forms a barrier against infection spreading from adjacent structures such as the lungs.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the pericardium is pericarditis, which causes characteristic sharp chest pain and may produce a friction rub. Accumulation of excess fluid (pericardial effusion) can compress the heart — cardiac tamponade — a life-threatening emergency treated by pericardiocentesis. Chronic inflammation can scar and stiffen the sac (constrictive pericarditis). Congenital absence of part of the pericardium is rare and usually benign.
