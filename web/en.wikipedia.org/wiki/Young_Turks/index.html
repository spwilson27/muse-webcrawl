Young Turks

The Young Turks (Turkish: Jön Türkler or Genç Türkler) were a coalition of reformist and nationalist groups in the late Ottoman Empire that advocated constitutional government and modernisation, and ultimately seized power in the early 20th century. The term has since entered general usage in many languages to describe any young, rebellious faction challenging an established order within an organisation.

Origins

The movement emerged among Ottoman students, military cadets, and exiles in the late 19th century, opposed to the autocratic rule of Sultan Abdul Hamid II. Opposition groups coalesced around the Committee of Union and Progress (CUP), founded in 1889 by medical students in Constantinople and influenced by exiled intellectuals in Paris such as Ahmed Rıza.

Revolution of 1908

In July 1908, Young Turk officers in Macedonia, including Enver Pasha and Ahmed Niyazi, rose in revolt, forcing Abdul Hamid II to restore the Ottoman constitution of 1876 and reconvene parliament — the start of the Second Constitutional Era. A counter-coup in 1909 (the 31 March Incident) was suppressed, and the Sultan was deposed.

Rule and the First World War

After the 1913 coup, the CUP ruled the empire effectively as a triumvirate of Enver Pasha, Talaat Pasha, and Djemal Pasha. The regime pursued centralisation and Turkification, entered the First World War on the side of the Central Powers, and presided over the empire's defeat and dissolution. CUP leaders bore responsibility for the wartime deportations and massacres of Armenians, Assyrians, and Greeks, including the Armenian genocide of 1915. The leading Young Turks fled the country in November 1918.

Legacy

Many Young Turk cadres later served in the Turkish national movement under Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, himself a former CUP member, and carried its centralising, secularising ethos into the Republic of Turkey founded in 1923.
