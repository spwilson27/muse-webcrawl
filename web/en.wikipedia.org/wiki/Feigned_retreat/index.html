Feigned retreat

A feigned retreat is a military tactic in which soldiers pretend to flee the battlefield in order to draw the enemy into a disadvantageous position or an ambush. It is considered one of the most difficult manoeuvres to execute, since undisciplined troops may turn a pretended flight into a real rout; it therefore requires highly trained, well-led forces.

Technique

The retreating force withdraws in apparent disorder, tempting the enemy to pursue. Pursuers typically lose formation and cohesion, exhaust their horses or men, and are drawn onto ground chosen by the defender — often into a prepared position, a concealed reserve, or an encirclement. At a signal, the "fleeing" troops turn and counter-attack, sometimes combined with flanking forces falling on the pursuers.

Historical examples

Steppe peoples — Scythians, Parthians, Turks and Mongols — were masters of the tactic; the Parthian shot at the Battle of Carrhae (53 BC) accompanied a feigned withdrawal that destroyed Crassus's army. Norman cavalry used feigned retreats at the Battle of Hastings (1066) to break the English shield wall. Mongol armies employed multi-day feigned flights, as before the Battle of Mohi (1241) and at the Kalka River (1223). Steppe tactics also appear at Manzikert (1071) and in numerous Crusades-era engagements.

Because the tactic was so associated with steppe warfare, European chroniclers often reported real retreats as feigned ones, and historians still debate whether some famous cases, including Hastings, were genuinely premeditated.
