Self-publishing

Self-publishing is the publication of a book or other media by its author without the involvement of an established publisher. The author bears responsibility — and usually the cost — for editing, design, production, and marketing, in exchange for retaining rights and a much larger share of revenue than traditional royalty contracts offer.

History

Authors have financed their own works for centuries: John Milton, William Blake, Virginia Woolf (through the Hogarth Press), and Marcel Proust all self-published some work, and in the twentieth century "vanity presses" offered fee-based publication of variable reputation. The modern era began with desktop publishing and print-on-demand in the 1990s and 2000s, which removed the need to print and warehouse large runs. The launch of Amazon's Kindle Direct Publishing (2007) and other ebook platforms such as Smashwords, Kobo Writing Life, and Apple Books, together with print-on-demand services like IngramSpark, made publication effectively free at the point of upload and gave self-published books global distribution.

Economics and reception

Platform royalties are commonly up to 70% of an ebook's list price, compared with 10–15% of print price in conventional contracts. Self-published authors range from hobbyists to full-time professionals earning substantial incomes, particularly in genre fiction such as romance, thriller, and science fiction; notable crossover successes include Andy Weir's The Martian, E. L. James's Fifty Shades of Grey, and Hugh Howey's Wool, which began as self-published works. Criticism has focused on uneven quality in the absence of gatekeeping, discoverability amid millions of titles, and services that exploit aspiring authors. Hybrid arrangements, in which authors pay curated publishers for selected services, blur the boundary with traditional publishing.
