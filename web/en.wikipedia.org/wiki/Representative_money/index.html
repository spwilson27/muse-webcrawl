Representative money

Representative money is any medium of exchange that represents something of value but has little or no value of its own (intrinsic value). Unlike commodity money — such as gold or silver coins, whose material is itself valuable — representative money takes the form of paper certificates or tokens that can be exchanged for a fixed quantity of a commodity, historically gold or silver held in reserve by the issuer.

History

Representative money developed as goldsmiths' receipts in 17th-century England: depositors left gold with goldsmiths and received receipts that began to circulate as money, since they could be redeemed for the metal on demand. Banknotes issued by central banks under the gold standard were representative money, as were United States gold certificates and silver certificates.

Distinction from fiat money

The defining feature of representative money is convertibility: the holder has a legal claim on the underlying commodity. When convertibility is suspended permanently, the money becomes fiat money, whose value rests on government decree and public confidence rather than redemption. Most modern currencies became fiat in the 20th century, a process completed internationally when the United States ended the dollar's convertibility into gold in 1971.

Advantages and disadvantages

Representative money is lighter and cheaper to produce and transport than commodity money and can be issued in convenient denominations. Its disadvantages include the costs of storing and guarding reserves, the risk of over-issue beyond reserves, and vulnerability to "runs" when holders doubt convertibility.
