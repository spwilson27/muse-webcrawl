Pilot light

A pilot light is a small gas flame, usually natural gas or liquefied petroleum gas, which serves as an ignition source for a more powerful gas burner. Before the development of reliable electrical ignition, pilot lights were standard features of gas appliances such as furnaces, water heaters, stoves, and space heaters.

Operation

When the appliance's thermostat or control calls for heat, a gas valve opens to the main burner, whose gas is ignited by the pilot flame. Because a constantly burning pilot wastes fuel, many modern appliances instead use electronic ignition — either intermittent pilot systems, which light the pilot only when needed, or hot-surface or spark ignition of the main burner directly.

Safety

A pilot assembly normally includes a thermocouple or thermopile heated by the flame. The small electrical output of the thermocouple holds the gas valve open; if the pilot goes out, the thermocouple cools and the valve closes, preventing unburned gas from escaping into the room. Older appliances without such protection were a recognised hazard.

Fuel waste

A standing pilot light typically consumes a few hundred watts of gas continuously — a substantial fraction of a water heater's total fuel use. This inefficiency, and tightening energy standards, have led to the near-disappearance of standing pilots from new furnaces and water heaters in many markets.

Other uses

The term "pilot light" is sometimes used more generally for any small indicator lamp showing that equipment is powered on; electric pilot lamps (often neon or LED) are fitted to switches and appliances for this purpose.
