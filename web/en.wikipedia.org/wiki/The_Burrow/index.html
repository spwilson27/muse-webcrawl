The Burrow (short story)

"The Burrow" (German: Der Bau) is an unfinished short story by Franz Kafka, written during the last six months of his life and published posthumously in the magazine Witiko in 1931. It is narrated in the first person by an unnamed burrowing animal who describes, in obsessive detail, the elaborate underground dwelling it has constructed and its perpetual anxiety about defending the burrow against imagined enemies.

Plot

The narrator, a creature resembling a large badger or mole, has spent its life building a vast subterranean structure of tunnels, chambers and a central "Castle Keep" where it stores food. The story consists largely of the creature's monologue about the architecture of the burrow: its entrance, concealed beneath moss; its labyrinth of passages designed to confuse intruders; and the constant vigilance required to maintain its security. The creature alternates between pride in its construction and paranoia that some predator — a barely audible whistling or hissing sound haunts the later part of the text — is approaching. The manuscript breaks off mid-sentence, with the creature still listening for the enemy.

Themes

Scholars read "The Burrow" as an allegory of anxiety, artistic creation, and the futility of seeking absolute security. The burrow has been interpreted as a metaphor for the human mind, for Kafka's own writing, and for the defensive structures of the self. The unresolved ending and the creature's inability ever to feel safe are characteristic of Kafka's late work.

Publication history

Kafka wrote the story in Berlin in late 1923 and early 1924 while living with Dora Diamant. Max Brod, Kafka's literary executor, published it after Kafka's death, first in 1931 and then in the collection Beim Bau der Chinesischen Mauer (The Great Wall of China). English translations followed, notably by Willa and Edwin Muir.
