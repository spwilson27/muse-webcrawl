Goodhart's law

Goodhart's law is an adage often stated as, "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." It is named after British economist Charles Goodhart, who advanced the idea in a 1975 article on monetary policy in the United Kingdom:

"Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes."

Interpretation

The law describes how optimising for a proxy metric breaks the relationship between the proxy and the underlying goal. When a government targets a money-supply measure, banks find ways to supply money outside the measure; when schools are judged by test scores, teaching narrows to the test; when researchers are judged by citation counts, citations get gamed. Related formulations include Campbell's law, from social scientist Donald T. Campbell, and the cobra effect, in which an incentive produces the opposite of the intended result.

Applications

Goodhart's law is invoked in economics, education policy, software engineering metrics, machine learning (where reward hacking in reinforcement learning is a close analogue), and management. Proposed mitigations include using several metrics at once, keeping some measures untargeted, and periodically changing what is measured.
