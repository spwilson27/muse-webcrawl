Visual system

The visual system is the physiological basis of visual perception, comprising the eyes, the optic nerves and tracts, and the visual processing areas of the brain, chiefly the visual cortex of the occipital lobe. It transduces light into neural signals and constructs representations of form, colour, depth and motion.

Structure

Light enters the eye through the cornea and pupil, is focused by the lens onto the retina, where photoreceptors — about 120 million rods for low-light vision and some 6–7 million cones for colour — convert photons into electrochemical signals. Retinal ganglion cells carry the signals via the optic nerve. At the optic chiasm, fibres from the nasal halves of each retina cross, so each hemisphere processes the opposite visual field. Most fibres relay in the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus and project through the optic radiation to the primary visual cortex (V1).

Processing streams

From V1, information flows along two broad pathways: the ventral stream ("what" pathway) toward the temporal lobe, supporting object and colour recognition, and the dorsal stream ("where/how" pathway) toward the parietal lobe, supporting spatial location and visually guided action. Specialised areas respond preferentially to faces, places, motion and colour. Other projections reach the superior colliculus (eye movements) and the suprachiasmatic nucleus (circadian rhythm).

Properties

The system achieves colour constancy, depth perception from binocular disparity and motion cues, and rapid saccadic sampling of scenes. Its quirks produce optical illusions, and damage causes deficits ranging from blindness to specific agnosias such as prosopagnosia (face blindness) and akinetopsia (motion blindness).
