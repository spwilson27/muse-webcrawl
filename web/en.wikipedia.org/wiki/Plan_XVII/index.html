Plan XVII (pronounced "Plan seventeen") was the name of a "scheme of mobilization and concentration" adopted by the French General Staff in 1913 for the case of a war between France and the German Empire. It was implemented in August 1914 at the start of the First World War.

Content of the plan

Strictly, Plan XVII was a deployment and concentration plan rather than a detailed operational strategy: it set out how five French armies would mobilise, move by rail, and assemble along the eastern frontier from Belfort to the Ardennes. Once deployed, the commander-in-chief, General Joseph Joffre, intended offensives into Alsace and Lorraine (lost to Germany in 1871) and possibly through the Ardennes, in keeping with the pre-war French doctrine of the offensive — the offensive à outrance. The plan left Joffre considerable discretion over the precise operations once concentration was complete.

The plan in action

In August 1914 France executed the plan in the Battle of the Frontiers. The attacks into Lorraine and the Ardennes ran into prepared German defences and suffered enormous losses — 22 August 1914 remains the deadliest day in French military history. Meanwhile the main German effort, the right wing of the Schlieffen–Moltke plan, swept through Belgium far to the north-west, largely outside Plan XVII's assumptions, which had reckoned mainly with a German advance through the Ardennes and Lorraine. Joffre improvised effectively, shifting forces westward to form new armies, and the Allied counterstroke at the First Battle of the Marne in September 1914 halted the German advance on Paris.

Assessment

Historians note that Plan XVII is often criticised as if it were a rigid battle plan, whereas it was primarily a mobilisation timetable that performed its logistical function well; the failures of August 1914 owed more to doctrine, intelligence, and the underestimation of German strength in the north than to the concentration scheme itself.
