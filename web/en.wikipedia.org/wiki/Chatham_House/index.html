Chatham House, formally the Royal Institute of International Affairs, is a British think tank based in London that analyzes international issues and current affairs. Founded in 1920, following discussions at the Paris Peace Conference of 1919, it is consistently ranked among the world's most influential think tanks. Its headquarters at 10 St James's Square — once the home of William Pitt the Elder, Earl of Chatham, from whom it takes its name — is a Grade I listed building given to the institute in 1923.

History

The institute was established as the British Institute of International Affairs, with Lionel Curtis among its founders, and received its royal charter in 1926. In its early decades its members and publications were closely involved in debates over appeasement, empire, and the League of Nations; its study groups produced influential reports on subjects from Palestine to the gold standard. During and after the Cold War it became a central venue for discussion of British foreign policy, European integration, and transatlantic relations.

The Chatham House Rule

The institute gives its name to the Chatham House Rule, devised in 1927 and refined since, which states: "When a meeting, or part thereof, is held under the Chatham House Rule, participants are free to use the information received, but neither the identity nor the affiliation of the speaker(s), nor that of any other participant, may be revealed." The rule is used worldwide to encourage frank discussion among officials, experts, and journalists.

Activities

Chatham House publishes the journals International Affairs (founded 1922) and The World Today, runs research programmes on global health, energy and climate, international security, and the global economy, and awards the annual Chatham House Prize to a figure deemed to have made a significant contribution to international relations. Its director in the 2020s is Bronwen Maddox, formerly of the Institute for Government.
