Hemolytic disease of the newborn (HDN), also called hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn, is an alloimmune condition in which a mother's antibodies cross the placenta and destroy the red blood cells of her fetus or newborn, causing anaemia, jaundice, and in severe cases hydrops fetalis and stillbirth. It arises when mother and fetus have incompatible blood-group antigens.

Causes

The classic cause is RhD incompatibility: an Rh-negative mother exposed to Rh-positive fetal blood — typically at delivery of a first Rh-positive baby — becomes sensitised, and in later pregnancies her IgG anti-D attacks the fetus. ABO incompatibility (usually a group O mother with a group A or B infant) is more common but generally milder and can affect first pregnancies. Antibodies against Kell and other antigens can also cause severe disease; anti-Kell additionally suppresses fetal red-cell production.

Diagnosis and treatment

Antenatal monitoring uses maternal antibody titres and middle-cerebral-artery Doppler ultrasound to detect fetal anaemia. Severely affected fetuses receive intrauterine transfusion; after birth, treatment includes phototherapy for hyperbilirubinaemia, exchange transfusion, and intravenous immunoglobulin. Untreated extreme hyperbilirubinaemia risks kernicterus, permanent brain damage.

Prevention

Routine antenatal and postnatal anti-D immunoglobulin (commercially RhoGAM, introduced in 1968) given to unsensitised Rh-negative mothers has made RhD disease rare in countries with good prophylaxis coverage — one of the great preventive successes of transfusion medicine.
