Ecology

[Image: a forest ecosystem scene — deer at a stream beneath tall trees, with sunlight through the canopy]

Ecology is the natural science of the relationships among living organisms, including humans, and their physical environment. It studies how organisms interact with each other and with the non-living (abiotic) components of their surroundings, at scales from individual organisms through populations and communities to ecosystems and the entire biosphere.

The word was coined by Ernst Haeckel in 1866 from the Greek oikos, 'household'. Ecology is a branch of biology closely connected with evolution, genetics, ethology and Earth science, and distinct from (though foundational to) environmentalism as a political movement.

Concepts

Core concepts include the food web and trophic levels, energy flow and nutrient cycling, carrying capacity, competition, predation, symbiosis, ecological niches, succession after disturbance, and biodiversity — the variety of life that underpins ecosystem stability and services such as pollination, water purification and carbon storage.

Population ecology models growth and interactions mathematically; community ecology studies assemblages of species; landscape and global ecology confront patterns visible only at continental and planetary scales.

Ecology and the environment

Applied ecology informs conservation biology, restoration, fisheries and wildlife management, and agriculture. Human impacts — habitat destruction, invasive species, pollution and climate change — have made ecology central to policy, including assessments that around a million species face extinction risk.

Famous frameworks include island biogeography (MacArthur and Wilson, 1967), which predicts species numbers from island size and isolation, and the study of keystone species, whose effects on their ecosystems are disproportionate to their numbers.
