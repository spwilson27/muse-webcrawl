Long-distance running

Long-distance running, or endurance running, is a form of continuous running over distances of at least 3 kilometres (1.9 miles). Physiologically, it is largely an aerobic activity and requires stamina as well as mental strength. Standard long-distance events include the 3000 metres, 5000 metres and 10,000 metres on the track, and road races such as the 10K, half marathon and marathon.

History

Endurance running has roots in ancient messenger traditions, most famously the legend of Pheidippides, the Greek messenger said to have run from Marathon to Athens. Modern competitive long-distance running developed in the 19th and early 20th centuries, and the marathon was included in the first modern Olympic Games in 1896.

Physiology

Long-distance performance depends on maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max), lactate threshold and running economy. Training typically combines long slow runs, tempo runs and interval work, with most elite runners covering well over 100 kilometres per week. East African runners, particularly from Kenya and Ethiopia, have dominated elite long-distance events since the late 20th century.

Popular races

The World Marathon Majors series comprises the Tokyo, Boston, London, Berlin, Chicago and New York City marathons. Mass-participation road races and ultramarathons — races longer than the marathon distance — have grown greatly in popularity since the running boom of the 1970s.
