Dormant account

A dormant account is a bank or investment account that has had no activity — deposits, withdrawals or other customer-initiated transactions — for an extended period of time. After a statutory period of inactivity, which varies by jurisdiction, the funds in a dormant account may be treated as unclaimed property and transferred to the state under escheat laws, although the owner or their heirs usually retain the right to reclaim them.

Definitions and periods

Financial institutions typically classify an account as dormant after a period of inactivity ranging from one to five years, depending on local law and the type of account. In the United States, state unclaimed-property statutes commonly set dormancy periods of three to five years for bank accounts. In the United Kingdom, the Dormant Bank and Building Society Accounts Act 2008 defines dormancy for participating institutions as 15 years without customer-initiated transactions.

Escheat and reclaim schemes

Under escheat, dormant funds are remitted to the government, which holds them in trust. In the UK, money from dormant accounts is transferred to Reclaim Fund Ltd, and surplus funds are distributed to good causes through the National Lottery Community Fund and social investment bodies, while customers retain a perpetual right to reclaim their money in full. Similar reclaim funds operate in other countries, including Japan and Ireland.

Fees and prevention

Some institutions charge dormancy or inactivity fees, which can erode small balances. Account holders can prevent dormancy by making periodic transactions, keeping contact details current, and responding to notices from their bank.
