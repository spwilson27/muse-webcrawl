Paradox of thrift

The paradox of thrift (or paradox of saving) is a paradox of economics. The paradox states that an increase in autonomous saving leads to a decrease in aggregate demand and thus a decrease in gross output which will in turn lower total saving. The paradox is, narrowly speaking, that total saving may fall because of individuals' attempts to increase their saving, and, broadly speaking, that the increase in autonomous saving may be harmful to an economy.

The idea was popularised by John Maynard Keynes in The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), though versions appear earlier in Bernard Mandeville's Fable of the Bees (1714) and in the work of underconsumptionist writers. In Keynes's account, if everyone saves more, consumption falls, firms' revenues decline, employment and incomes drop, and the economy settles at a lower level of output with saving—constrained by investment—no higher than before: what is virtuous for the individual household is damaging when practised by all, an instance of the fallacy of composition.

Debate

Classical and neoclassical critics respond that increased saving lowers interest rates and thereby stimulates investment, offsetting the fall in consumption—unless the economy is at the zero lower bound or in a liquidity trap, conditions under which most mainstream models concede the paradox can hold. The paradox has been invoked in debates over austerity during recessions, notably after the 2008 financial crisis.
