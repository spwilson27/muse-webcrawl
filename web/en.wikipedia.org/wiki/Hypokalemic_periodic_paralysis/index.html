Hypokalemic periodic paralysis

SpecialtyNeurology
SymptomsEpisodes of muscle weakness with low serum potassium
Usual onsetAdolescence

Hypokalemic periodic paralysis (HypoKPP) is a rare inherited channelopathy characterised by episodes of muscle weakness or paralysis accompanied by a fall in blood potassium levels. It is the most common form of primary periodic paralysis.

Signs and symptoms

Attacks typically begin in adolescence and involve weakness of the limbs and trunk, often on waking or after rest following vigorous exercise. Triggers include carbohydrate-rich meals, stress, cold and rest after exertion. During an attack serum potassium is low, reflexes are diminished, and the muscles of respiration and swallowing are usually spared. Attacks last hours to a day or two. With age, some patients develop a fixed proximal myopathy.

Genetics

HypoKPP is usually inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. Most cases are caused by mutations in CACNA1S, encoding the skeletal muscle calcium channel Cav1.1, and a minority by mutations in SCN4A, encoding the sodium channel Nav1.4. The mutations create an anomalous gating-pore current that renders fibres inexcitable when potassium falls. A secondary form is associated with thyrotoxicosis, particularly in Asian men (thyrotoxic periodic paralysis).

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on the history, documented low potassium during an attack, family history and genetic testing. Acute attacks are treated with oral potassium chloride. Prevention may involve a low-carbohydrate, low-salt diet, potassium supplementation and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors such as acetazolamide, or potassium-sparing diuretics.
