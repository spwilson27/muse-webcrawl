Pastiche

Pastiche blends elements borrowed from multiple earlier works or styles.

A pastiche is a work of visual art, literature, theatre, music, or architecture that imitates the style or character of the work of one or more other artists. Unlike parody, pastiche pays homage to the work it imitates rather than mocking it. The word comes through French from Italian pasticcio, a kind of pie or pâté made of mixed ingredients — a metaphor for the mixture of styles in the resulting work.

In literature

Literary pastiche is common in detective fiction written in the manner of Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes stories, and in works that continue or imitate authors such as Jane Austen or P. G. Wodehouse. Writers may adopt the voice, vocabulary, and narrative habits of an earlier author either as an affectionate tribute or as a vehicle for exploring familiar characters in new situations. The practice sits between homage and fan fiction, and published pastiches are often authorised sequels commissioned by an author's estate.

In music and film

In music, pastiche ranges from medleys and "in the style of" compositions to the work of bands that recreate the sound of an earlier era. The Italian pasticcio of the 18th century also denoted an opera assembled from arias by several composers. In film and television, genre pastiches — such as neo-noir or affectionate recreations of 1950s science fiction — signal their sources through costume, music, and cinematography while telling original stories.

Pastiche and postmodernism

The theorist Fredric Jameson famously described postmodern pastiche as "blank parody" — imitation emptied of parody's satirical impulse, in a culture saturated with recycled styles. Other critics, including Linda Hutcheon and Richard Dyer, have defended pastiche as a legitimate and often affectionate mode of creativity that keeps older styles alive and in dialogue with the present.
