Telecommuting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Telecommuting, also called telework, remote work, or working from home (WFH), is a work arrangement in which employees perform their job duties from a location outside the traditional office—usually their home—using telecommunications technology such as the internet, email, and videoconferencing.

History

The term "telecommuting" was coined in 1973 by Jack Nilles, who envisioned it as a way to reduce traffic congestion and energy use during the oil crisis era. Adoption grew slowly with personal computers and broadband, then expanded dramatically during the COVID-19 pandemic of 2020, when lockdowns forced large-scale remote work worldwide.

Advantages and disadvantages

Benefits commonly cited include eliminated commute time, greater flexibility, lower office costs, and access to wider talent pools. Drawbacks include social isolation, blurred work–life boundaries, management and collaboration challenges, and uneven suitability across occupations—many jobs in manufacturing, healthcare, and retail cannot be done remotely.

Trends

After the pandemic, many employers adopted hybrid models combining office and home days. Research on productivity effects is mixed, with outcomes depending on job type, management practices, and employee preferences.
