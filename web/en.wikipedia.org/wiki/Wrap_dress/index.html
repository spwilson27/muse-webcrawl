The wrap dress fastens by wrapping and tying at the waist, with no buttons or zips.

A wrap dress is a dress with a front closure formed by wrapping one side of the garment across the other and knotting the attached ties around the waist or back, creating a V-neckline and an adjustable, figure-flattering fit without buttons or zippers. The design became an enduring fashion classic in the 1970s through the jersey wrap dress created by the Belgian-American designer Diane von Fürstenberg.

History

Wrap-over garments are ancient — the wrapped kimono and the draped clothing of classical antiquity are distant ancestors — and wrap-front dresses appeared in Western fashion in the 1930s and 1940s. American designer Claire McCardell's 1942 "popover" dress, a wrap design marketed to wartime homemakers, was a notable precursor. In the early 1970s von Fürstenberg, inspired by a wrap top and matching skirt ensemble, combined them into a single dress in printed Italian silk jersey. Launched in 1974, it was an immediate success: within two years millions had been sold, and in 1976 von Fürstenberg appeared on the cover of Newsweek, which called her the most marketable woman in fashion since Coco Chanel.

Cultural significance

The wrap dress arrived as large numbers of women entered professional life, and it was embraced as a symbol of female independence and effortless sensuality — easy to wear, flattering across body types, and suited to both office and evening. Von Fürstenberg's slogan "Feel like a woman, wear a dress!" became famous. Sales collapsed with market saturation by the end of the 1970s, but she relaunched the design in 1997 to a new generation; the dress has remained in production in constantly changing prints ever since.

Recognition

An original 1970s von Fürstenberg wrap dress is held by the Metropolitan Museum of Art's Costume Institute as an iconic work of twentieth-century design, and the dress featured prominently in the 2014 exhibition Journey of a Dress marking its fortieth anniversary. The style continues to be widely imitated, and "faux wrap" dresses reproduce the look with a fixed closure.
