The provisional government of Israel (Hebrew: ממשלת ישראל הזמנית, Mem'shelet Yisra'el HaZmanit) governed the newly declared State of Israel from the Declaration of Independence on 14 May 1948 until 10 March 1949, when the first government formed after elections to the Constituent Assembly (the first Knesset) took office. It consisted of two bodies drawn from the institutions of the pre-state Jewish community (the Yishuv): the People's Administration (Minhelet HaAm), a thirteen-member executive headed by David Ben-Gurion, which became the provisional cabinet; and the People's Council (Moetzet HaAm), a 37-member body representing the spectrum of Zionist and communal parties, which became the Provisional State Council and acted as the legislature, with Chaim Weizmann as its chairman—effectively the state's first head of state.

Background and formation

Following the United Nations Partition Plan of November 1947 and the civil war that erupted in Mandatory Palestine, the Jewish Agency and the Va'ad Leumi (National Council) prepared the machinery of a state-in-waiting. In April 1948 they created the People's Council and its executive, the People's Administration, deliberately broadening the coalition to include parties from the socialist Mapai to the Revisionists (who joined through their associates), the religious parties, the General Zionists, Mapam, the Communists, and representatives of the Sephardi communities, women and the Yemenite association; the ultra-Orthodox Agudat Yisrael also participated. On the afternoon of Friday, 14 May 1948—hours before the British Mandate expired at midnight—the People's Council met in the Tel Aviv Museum (now Independence Hall) and approved the Declaration of the Establishment of the State of Israel, read by Ben-Gurion. The People's Administration thereupon became the Provisional Government and the Council the Provisional State Council.

Government in wartime

The provisional government governed through the 1948 Arab–Israeli War, conducting Israel's defence, foreign relations and the absorption of mass immigration simultaneously. Its achievements included obtaining rapid de facto recognition from the United States and de jure recognition from the Soviet Union within days; creating the Israel Defense Forces by ordinance of 26 May 1948, dissolving the separate armed organisations into a single army (a unity enforced, painfully, in the Altalena affair of June 1948, when the government ordered the shelling of an Irgun arms ship); establishing the Bank of Israel's precursor currency arrangements and issuing the first Israeli postage stamps and banknotes; and passing the Law of Return's predecessors in open-immigration policy, which doubled the Jewish population within three years. The cabinet met frequently, often in Tel Aviv (the "Kirya", the former German Templer colony of Sarona), because Jerusalem was besieged and then divided. In September 1948 it appointed a five-member committee under the finance minister to prepare elections, repeatedly postponed by the war.

The Provisional State Council legislated by ordinance, laying down the framework of the state: it adopted the body of Mandate law insofar as it did not conflict with new ordinances, established the court system, created the office of State Comptroller, set the flag and emblem of Israel, and in February 1949 passed the Transition Law after the Constituent Assembly convened. Chaim Weizmann, chairman of the Council, was elected President of Israel by the Constituent Assembly on 16 February 1949.

End of the provisional period

Elections for the Constituent Assembly were held on 25 January 1949, the first national elections in Israel, with a turnout of about 87 percent; Mapai won 46 of 120 seats. The Assembly opened on 14 February 1949, renamed itself the First Knesset, elected Weizmann president, and on 10 March 1949 Ben-Gurion presented the first duly elected government, ending the provisional period after just under ten months.

Members

The provisional cabinet's thirteen portfolios were held by David Ben-Gurion (prime minister and defence), Eliezer Kaplan (finance), Moshe Sharett (foreign affairs, as Moshe Shertok), Golda Meir (labour, as Golda Meyerson, from later in 1948), Yitzhak Gruenbaum (interior), Moshe Shapira (immigration and health), Yehuda Leib Maimon (religion and war victims), Pinhas Rosen (justice, as Felix Rosenblüth), David Remez (transport), Peretz Bernstein (trade and industry), Bechor-Shalom Sheetrit (police and minorities—the cabinet's only Arab-Israeli-era Sephardi/Mizrahi and the only minister to sign the Declaration who served continuously until 1966), Fritz Peretz Naphtali and others in agriculture and welfare portfolios. Of the cabinet members, twelve signed the Declaration of Independence; Meir and Ben-Gurion were among the 25 signatories present in Tel Aviv, others being besieged in Jerusalem or abroad.
