Davies sisters

Gwendoline and Margaret Davies, Welsh art collectors and philanthropists

Gwendoline Elizabeth Davies (1882–1951) and Margaret Sidney Davies (1884–1963), known collectively as the Davies sisters, were Welsh philanthropists and art collectors from Llandinam in Montgomeryshire. Heirs to the industrial and shipping fortune of their grandfather David Davies of Llandinam, they assembled one of the great British collections of French Impressionist and Post-Impressionist painting, which they bequeathed to the National Museum of Wales in Cardiff, transforming the nation's art holdings.

Background

The sisters were the granddaughters of David Davies (1818–1890), the railway contractor and coal magnate who built much of the rail network of mid-Wales and developed the port of Barry. On the death of their father Edward Davies, and during their minority, they inherited a substantial fortune, administered initially through trustees. Both sisters were devout Calvinistic Methodists, teetotallers, and committed pacifists; during the First World War they worked with refugees and supported relief efforts in France.

The collection

Advised initially by the critic Hugh Blaker, the sisters began collecting in earnest around 1908–1913, buying boldly at a time when modern French art was still controversial in Britain. Their collection came to include multiple works by Monet (including views of Venice and water lilies), Cézanne, Renoir, Van Gogh, Pissarro, Manet, Daumier, Rodin bronzes, and paintings by Welsh artists such as Augustus John and Kyffin Williams. They bought on an unusually large scale for private collectors, and always with a view to public benefit rather than decoration of their homes.

Gregynog

In 1919 the sisters bought Gregynog Hall near Newtown, Montgomeryshire, which they developed into a centre for music, arts and crafts, and scholarship. Gregynog hosted festivals of music and drama, a fine-print press (the Gregynog Press, founded 1922), and conferences promoting international peace through the League of Nations. The hall and estate later passed to the University of Wales. The sisters were also major benefactors of the National Museum of Wales and of Welsh cultural institutions generally.

Legacy

The Davies bequests of 1951 and 1963 gave the National Museum of Wales the core of what is now the Davies Gallery collection, one of the finest groups of Impressionist paintings in Britain, notable for the sisters' preference — unusual for their era — for artists who were then little appreciated in the United Kingdom. Their example is frequently credited with raising the international standing of Welsh cultural life in the twentieth century.
