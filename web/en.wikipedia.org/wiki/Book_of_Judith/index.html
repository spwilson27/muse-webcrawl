Judith beheading Holofernes was a favourite subject of Renaissance and Baroque art, painted by Caravaggio, Artemisia Gentileschi, and many others.

The Book of Judith is a deuterocanonical book included in the Septuagint and in the canon of the Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches, but excluded from the Hebrew Bible and from Protestant canons, which classify it among the Apocrypha. It tells the story of Judith, a beautiful and pious widow, who saves her city of Bethulia from the invading Assyrian army by seducing and beheading its general, Holofernes.

The narrative is generally regarded by scholars as a work of historical fiction rather than a factual account: its opening verses combine figures and places from different eras (Nebuchadnezzar is called king of the Assyrians ruling from Nineveh, though he was in fact king of Babylon after Nineveh's fall). The book is usually dated to the Hasmonean period, the 2nd or early 1st century BCE, and may have been intended to encourage resistance during the Maccabean Revolt or a later persecution.

Summary

Nebuchadnezzar, king of the Assyrians, sends his general Holofernes to subdue the nations of the west that refused to join his war against Arphaxad. Holofernes lays siege to the mountain city of Bethulia, cutting off its water supply. As the inhabitants press their leaders to surrender, the widow Judith reproaches the elders for testing God, then prays, adorns herself, and enters the Assyrian camp with her maid, claiming she will reveal how the city can be taken.

Holofernes, captivated by her beauty and supposed wisdom, invites her to a banquet where he drinks heavily. Once he has fallen into a drunken stupor, Judith takes his own sword and beheads him, carrying the head back to Bethulia in a food bag. The Assyrians, discovering their leader's body and seeing his head displayed on the city wall, flee in panic and are routed by the Israelites. Judith leads a hymn of thanksgiving, remains unmarried for the rest of her life, and frees her maid.

Historicity and dating

Numerous anachronisms and geographical errors indicate that the author did not intend a strictly historical record, or freely combined traditions: Nineveh had been destroyed before Nebuchadnezzar's reign; the return from the Babylonian exile is treated as recent, yet a functioning high priesthood in Jerusalem is also assumed. The city of Bethulia itself is unknown from other sources and may be fictional. Most scholars date composition to the Hellenistic period, around the late 2nd century BCE.

Themes

The book explores the reversal of power: God delivers Israel not through an army but through the hand of a woman (cf. Judith 16:5), a theme also present in the stories of Deborah and Jael. Other themes include the power of prayer and fasting, strict observance of dietary laws even in the enemy camp, the danger of female beauty, and confidence in God's help without presumption.

In art and literature

The beheading scene became one of the most depicted biblical episodes in Western art, treated by Donatello, Botticelli, Mantegna, Caravaggio, Artemisia Gentileschi (whose two versions are especially famous), Rubens, and Klimt, whose Judith I (1901) reimagined her as a femme fatale. The story was also dramatised by the medieval poet of the Old English Judith, by Handel in an oratorio, and in numerous plays and films.
