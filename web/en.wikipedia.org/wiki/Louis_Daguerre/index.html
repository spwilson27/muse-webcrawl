Louis Daguerre

Louis-Jacques-Mandé Daguerre (18 November 1787 – 10 July 1851) was a French artist and photographer, recognised for his invention of the daguerreotype process of photography. He is considered one of the fathers of photography.

Early career and the diorama

Daguerre was born in Cormeilles-en-Parisis. Trained as an architect and stage designer, he became a painter of theatrical scenery and panoramic backgrounds. In 1822 he opened the Diorama in Paris, a popular entertainment in which huge translucent paintings, lit from changing angles, created the illusion of movement and shifting scenes.

The daguerreotype

Around 1829 Daguerre formed a partnership with Nicéphore Niépce, who had produced the earliest surviving camera photograph. After Niépce's death in 1833, Daguerre continued experimenting and discovered that an invisible "latent" image on a silvered copper plate sensitised with iodine vapour could be developed with mercury vapour and fixed with salt, later sodium thiosulfate. The process was announced to the French Academy of Sciences in January 1839 and its details published that August, after the French government bought the rights and gave them "free to the world" — except in England, where Daguerre patented it. Daguerre's 1838 photograph Boulevard du Temple is among the earliest images showing a recognisable human figure. He died in 1851 at Bry-sur-Marne.
