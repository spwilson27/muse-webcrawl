Sámi people

Regions: Sápmi (northern Norway, Sweden, Finland, Russia's Kola Peninsula)
Population: approx. 80,000–100,000
Languages: Sámi languages, Norwegian, Swedish, Finnish, Russian
Traditional livelihood: Reindeer herding, fishing, hunting

The Sámi (also spelled Saami or Sami; in their own languages sámit or sápmelaččat) are the Indigenous people inhabiting Sápmi, their traditional homeland stretching across the northern parts of Norway, Sweden, and Finland and Russia's Kola Peninsula. They are the only Indigenous people recognised as such in the European Union.

Culture

Sámi culture is closely associated with semi-nomadic reindeer herding, though historically most Sámi lived by fishing, hunting, and small-scale farming, and only a minority herd reindeer today. Distinctive cultural elements include the colourful traditional dress (gákti), the joik (a form of song), handicrafts (duodji), and the lavvu tent. There are several Sámi languages, of which Northern Sámi is by far the most widely spoken; several others are severely endangered.

History

The Sámi have lived in Fennoscandia since prehistoric times. From the Middle Ages they were taxed by neighbouring kingdoms, and from the 18th and 19th centuries they faced intensifying colonisation, Christian missions, and later assimilation policies—most notoriously Norway's "Norwegianisation" era, during which Sámi language and culture were suppressed in schools. Land and water rights have been contested in disputes such as the Alta controversy of 1979–81, which became a turning point for Sámi political mobilisation.

Political organisation

Sámi parliaments established in Finland (1973), Norway (1989), and Sweden (1993) act as elected representative bodies, consulted on matters affecting Sámi interests. The Sámi flag, adopted in 1986, features the colours of the gákti and a circle symbolising the sun and moon. The Sámi National Day is celebrated on 6 February.
