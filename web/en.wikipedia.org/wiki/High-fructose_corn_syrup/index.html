High-fructose corn syrup (HFCS), also known as glucose–fructose syrup in some countries and isoglucose in the European Union, is a sweetener made from corn starch. Starch is hydrolysed to glucose syrup, and enzymes then convert part of the glucose into fructose to produce a syrup whose sweetness and properties resemble those of sucrose (table sugar).

Production

Corn is wet-milled to separate the starch, which is liquefied and saccharified with enzymes (alpha-amylase and glucoamylase) to yield a glucose syrup. The enzyme glucose isomerase then converts a fraction of the glucose to fructose. The standard product contains about 42 percent fructose (HFCS 42); further enrichment by chromatographic separation produces HFCS 90, which is blended back to make HFCS 55, the grade most used in soft drinks. The commercial process was developed in the late 1950s and 1960s, notably by Clinton Corn Processing and Japanese researchers, and the product entered wide use in the United States in the 1970s.

Uses

HFCS is used in soft drinks, baked goods, yoghurts, sauces, cereals and many other processed foods. In the United States it largely displaced sucrose in beverages during the 1980s, aided by corn subsidies, sugar import quotas that kept domestic sugar prices high, its stability in acidic drinks, and the ease of transporting and blending a liquid sweetener. Its use is much lower in Europe, where production quotas historically limited isoglucose, and in Mexico, where cane sugar remained standard in many products.

Health debate

HFCS became a focus of public controversy in the 2000s after researchers noted that the rise in its consumption paralleled the rise in obesity. Some studies suggested fructose is metabolised differently from glucose, with possible effects on satiety hormones and liver fat. Major reviews and medical bodies have generally concluded that, calorie for calorie, HFCS and sucrose are nutritionally similar, and that excess consumption of added sugars in any form is the primary concern; sucrose, after digestion, yields a comparable mixture of glucose and fructose. Per-capita HFCS consumption in the United States peaked around 1999 and has declined since, while obesity rates continued to climb, complicating simple causal claims.

Naming

In 2010 the Corn Refiners Association petitioned the U.S. Food and Drug Administration to allow the name "corn sugar"; the FDA denied the petition in 2012. Labels in the United Kingdom and EU typically list the ingredient as glucose–fructose syrup.
