Sphere eversion

In differential topology, sphere eversion is the process of turning a sphere inside out in three-dimensional space by a continuous deformation (an immersion) that allows the surface to pass through itself but forbids creasing, tearing or pinching. Remarkably, such an eversion is possible, although it seems counter-intuitive — the result is known as Smale's paradox.

History

Stephen Smale proved in 1958 that an eversion exists, as a corollary of his work on immersions, but his proof was non-constructive and gave no explicit deformation. The first explicit methods were produced later: Bernard Morin, who was blind, contributed key insights to visualising the process, and the "Morin surface" is a famous halfway model of an eversion. Charles Pugh produced physical wire-mesh models, and in the 1990s William Thurston's "corrugations" method was popularised in the video Outside In produced by the Geometry Center.

Key idea

The essential trick is to introduce corrugations or waves into the sphere so that it can fold through itself without sharp creases. During the deformation the sphere is always an immersed surface — its derivative never vanishes — even though it intersects itself. At the midpoint, an eversion by the Morin method passes through a surface with fourfold symmetry.
