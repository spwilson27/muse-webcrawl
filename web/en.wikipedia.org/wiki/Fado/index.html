Fado (Portuguese for "destiny" or "fate") is a music genre that can be traced to the 1820s in Lisbon, Portugal, though it probably has much earlier origins. Fado historian Rui Vieira Nery notes that "the only reliable information on the history of fado was orally transmitted and goes back to the 1820s and 1830s at best." The music is usually performed by a solo singer, the fadista, traditionally accompanied by a wire-strung acoustic guitar and the Portuguese guitarra, a pear-shaped lute with twelve strings.

Characteristics

Fado songs are typically lyrical and melancholic, expressing the Portuguese sentiment of saudade — a deep longing for something lost or unattainable. Common themes include fate, lost love, the sea, poverty, and the hardships of everyday life. Performances in Lisbon's traditional fado houses observe a ritual silence, announced by the cry "Silêncio, que se vai cantar o fado!" ("Silence, for the fado is about to be sung!").

Styles

Two main centres are recognised: Lisbon fado, born in the taverns of working-class neighbourhoods such as Alfama, Mouraria and Bairro Alto, and Coimbra fado, associated with the University of Coimbra, where it is sung by male students and academics in academic dress, often as a serenade. Coimbra fado tends toward a more lyrical, less improvisatory style.

Notable performers

The most celebrated fadista was Amália Rodrigues (1920–1999), known as the "Queen of Fado", who carried the genre to international audiences. Later generations include Carlos do Carmo, Mariza, Camané, Ana Moura and Cristina Branco, who have blended fado with contemporary influences.

Recognition

In 2011, fado was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The Museu do Fado in Lisbon's Alfama district documents the genre's history and its role in Portuguese cultural identity.
