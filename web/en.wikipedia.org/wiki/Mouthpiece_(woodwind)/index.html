Mouthpiece (woodwind)

Woodwind mouthpiece
InstrumentsClarinet, saxophone (single-reed); flute head joint; recorder beak
MaterialsEbonite (hard rubber), plastic, metal, glass, wood
ComponentsTip rail, facing curve, table, chamber, bore

The mouthpiece of a woodwind instrument is the part placed partly inside the player's mouth, against which the lips and — on single-reed instruments — the reed act to generate vibration. On clarinets and saxophones it is a separate, detachable component; on recorders and flageolets it is the carved "beak" at the top of the instrument.

Single-reed mouthpieces

A clarinet or saxophone mouthpiece is a wedge-shaped piece, usually of ebonite (hard rubber), though metal, plastic, glass and crystal examples exist. Its flat underside, the table, holds a cane or synthetic reed fastened by a ligature. The reed lies against a precisely machined curve, the facing, which rises to the tip rail; the minute gap between reed and tip is the tip opening. Air pressure and embouchure set the reed beating against the facing, chopping the airstream and exciting the air column.

Key dimensions — tip opening, facing length and curve, chamber size and shape, and baffle profile — determine response, resistance and tone colour. Classical clarinet mouthpieces have small tip openings and long facings; jazz saxophone mouthpieces often have larger openings and high baffles that produce a brighter, louder sound. Makers such as Vandoren, Selmer and Otto Link number their models by these dimensions, and players select reed strengths to match.

Other woodwind mouthpieces

Double-reed instruments (oboe, bassoon) dispense with a mouthpiece proper: the two reeds vibrate against each other, held directly between the lips. Transverse flutes have no mouthpiece but a head joint with an embouchure hole; the player's lips form the vibrating edge. Recorders and tin whistles channel air through a windway cut into the beak against a sharp labium edge. In each case the geometry of the mouth-end component governs articulation, tuning and timbre.
