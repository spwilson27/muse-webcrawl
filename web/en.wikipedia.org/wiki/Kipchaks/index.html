The Kipchaks (also spelled Qipchaq) were a Turkic nomadic people and confederation that, together with the closely related Cumans, dominated the Eurasian steppe from the 11th to the 13th century. In the sources of Kievan Rus' they are the Polovtsy; in Latin sources the western Kipchaks appear as Cumans or Comani; and the whole steppe zone they ruled was known to Muslim geographers as Dasht-i Qipchaq, the Kipchak Steppe.

History

Originating among the Turkic peoples of the Irtysh region, the Kipchaks expanded westward after the collapse of the Kimek Khanate in the 11th century, displacing the Pechenegs and Oghuz and reaching the Black Sea steppe and the Danube. For two centuries they were the principal military power confronting the Rus' principalities (memorialised in The Tale of Igor's Campaign), Georgia, Hungary and the Byzantine Empire, alternating raids with trade and dynastic marriages — Rus' princes and Georgian kings alike married Kipchak princesses. Kipchak mercenaries and slave-soldiers were prized as far away as Egypt, where the Mamluk Sultanate's first rulers, including Sultan Baybars, were themselves of Kipchak origin.

Mongol conquest and legacy

The Mongol invasions of 1223 (Kalka River) and 1236–1242 destroyed the confederation; many Kipchaks fled west — some 40,000 families were admitted to Hungary, where "Kunság" (Cumania) remains a regional name — while those who stayed were absorbed into the Golden Horde, whose population and Turkic language became predominantly Kipchak. The Kipchak branch of the Turkic languages survives in Kazakh, Tatar, Bashkir, Nogai, Karakalpak, Kumyk and others. The Codex Cumanicus, a 14th-century phrasebook compiled for missionaries and merchants, is the principal record of their language, and their carved stone warrior statues (balbals or "stone babas") still dot the steppe.
