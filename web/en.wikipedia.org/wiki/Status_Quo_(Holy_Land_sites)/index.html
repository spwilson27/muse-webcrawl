Status Quo (Holy Land sites)

The Church of the Holy Sepulchre, Jerusalem.

The Status Quo is an understanding among the religious communities in the Holy Land governing ownership and usage rights at certain Christian, Jewish, and Muslim holy sites. The term derives from the phrase status quo ante bellum in the Treaty of Paris (1857), which ended the Crimean War and guaranteed that the arrangements then in force at the holy places would be preserved unchanged.

Origins

Disputes among Christian denominations over the shrines of Palestine intensified in the 1840s and 1850s, and conflicts between the Roman Catholic Church (backed by France) and the Greek Orthodox Church (backed by Russia) over the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem and the Church of the Nativity in Bethlehem formed one of the pretexts for the Crimean War. In 1852, before the war, the Ottoman Sultan Abdülmecid I had issued a firman (decree) codifying the rights of the various communities as they stood in 1757. The 1856 and 1857 Paris settlements and the 1878 Treaty of Berlin (Article 62) internationalized that firman, and the British Mandate and later authorities maintained it.

Scope

The Status Quo applies to nine sites, including:

  • The Church of the Holy Sepulchre, shared by the Greek Orthodox, Roman Catholic (Franciscan Custody), and Armenian Apostolic churches, with smaller rights held by the Coptic, Syriac, and Ethiopian churches
  • The Church of the Nativity in Bethlehem
  • The Tomb of the Virgin Mary in the Kidron Valley
  • The Chapel of the Ascension on the Mount of Olives
  • The Western Wall and Rachel's Tomb (also affected by Muslim–Jewish arrangements)

Under the arrangement, no changes — even cleaning or small repairs — may be made to shared areas without the consent of all communities concerned. A famous symbol of the system is the "immovable ladder," a wooden ladder resting above the entrance of the Holy Sepulchre since at least the 18th century, which cannot be moved because the ledge is controlled by one church and the wall below by another. A Muslim family, the Joudeh Al-Goudia, has held the key to the church since the time of Saladin, and the Nuseibeh family opens and closes its doors daily.
