Algebraic cycle

In mathematics, an algebraic cycle on an algebraic variety is a formal linear combination of subvarieties of the variety. Algebraic cycles are central objects in algebraic geometry and in the modern theory of motives, because quotienting cycles by suitable equivalence relations produces the groups — such as Chow groups — that capture the geometry and cohomology of the variety.

Definition

Let X be a variety over a field. An algebraic cycle of codimension p (or of dimension k) on X is an element of the free abelian group generated by the closed irreducible subvarieties of X of that codimension (respectively dimension). Thus a cycle is a finite sum of subvarieties with integer coefficients, which may be positive or negative. The group of cycles of codimension p is usually denoted Zp(X).

Equivalence relations

Cycles are rarely studied on their own; instead one quotients by an equivalence relation. The finest commonly used relation is rational equivalence, generated by cycles arising as the zeros and poles of rational functions on subvarieties one dimension larger; the quotient groups are the Chow groups of X. Coharser relations include algebraic equivalence, homological equivalence and numerical equivalence. For cycles on a smooth projective variety over the complex numbers, there is a cycle class map to singular cohomology, and the Hodge conjecture asks which rational cohomology classes arise from algebraic cycles — one of the Millennium Prize Problems.

Operations

Cycles admit operations such as pullback under morphisms, pushforward under proper morphisms, and an intersection product (when cycles can be moved to intersect properly, or via intersection theory). The Chow ring of a variety, formed by its Chow groups with the intersection product, generalises the cohomology ring and the classical enumerative geometry of Schubert calculus.
