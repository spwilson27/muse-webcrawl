Marina Ivanovna Tsvetaeva (Russian: Марина Ивановна Цветаева; 8 October 1892 – 31 August 1941) was a Russian poet. She is considered one of the great poets of 20th-century Russian literature. Her work is marked by intense, passionate lyricism, daring rhythmical and metrical experimentation, and a distinctive, often elliptical syntax.

Tsvetaeva's life was scarred by the Russian Revolution and the Moscow famine, emigration to Paris, poverty, the arrest of her husband and daughter by the NKVD, and her return to the Soviet Union in 1939. She died by suicide in Yelabuga in 1941.

Early life

Tsvetaeva was born in Moscow, the daughter of Ivan Vladimirovich Tsvetaev, a professor of fine art at Moscow University and founder of the Alexander III Museum of Fine Arts (now the Pushkin Museum), and Maria Meyn, a concert pianist of Polish-German descent. She published her first book of poems, Evening Album (Vecherny Albom), at her own expense in 1910; it attracted the favourable attention of poets such as Maximilian Voloshin, who became her mentor and friend.

Revolution and emigration

In 1912 she married Sergei Efron, a cadet who later served as an officer in the White Army. During the Civil War she remained in Moscow with her two daughters, suffering extreme deprivation; her younger daughter Irina died of starvation in 1920. Her cycles of poems from these years include The Tsar-Maiden and The Swans' Encampment, the latter sympathetic to the White cause.

In 1922 Tsvetaeva emigrated, rejoining Efron in Berlin and then Prague, where she wrote some of her finest long poems, including Poem of the Mountain and Poem of the End (1924). In 1925 the family moved to Paris, where they lived for fourteen years in deepening poverty and isolation. Tsvetaeva's mature verse — among it The Pied Piper (1925) and the cycle Poems to Czechoslovakia (1938–39), written in response to the Munich Agreement — went largely unrecognised by the émigré literary establishment, and she turned increasingly to prose, including the memoir essays My Pushkin.

Return and death

Efron, who had become involved with Soviet intelligence and was implicated in an assassination, fled to the USSR in 1937; their daughter Ariadna returned the same year. Tsvetaeva followed with her son Georgy ("Mur") in June 1939. Within months Ariadna was arrested (she spent years in the gulag and exile), and Efron was arrested and shot in 1941. Evacuated to Yelabuga in Tatarstan after the German invasion, unable to find work or support, Tsvetaeva hanged herself on 31 August 1941.

Legacy

Long largely unpublished in her homeland, Tsvetaeva's poetry was gradually restored to print in the Soviet Union from the 1960s. She is now recognised, alongside Anna Akhmatova, Osip Mandelstam and Boris Pasternak (with whom she sustained a famous epistolary relationship), as one of the defining Russian poets of the century. Her work has been widely translated, notably into English by Elaine Feinstein and others.
