Depositional environment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In geology, a depositional environment or sedimentary environment describes the combination of physical, chemical and biological processes associated with the deposition of a particular type of sediment and, eventually, the sedimentary rocks formed when those sediments are lithified. Each environment leaves a distinctive record — characteristic grain sizes, sedimentary structures, fossils and geometry — called its sedimentary facies, allowing geologists to reconstruct ancient landscapes from the rock record.

Main categories

Continental (terrestrial)

  • Alluvial fans and fluvial (river) systems
  • Lacustrine (lake) environments
  • Aeolian (desert dune) environments
  • Glacial environments
  • Swamps and coal-forming mires

Transitional (coastal)

  • Deltas
  • Beaches and barrier islands
  • Estuaries, lagoons and tidal flats

Marine

  • Continental shelf (shallow marine)
  • Reefs and carbonate platforms
  • Continental slope and deep marine environments, including turbidite systems and abyssal plains

Recognition

Sedimentary environments are identified in ancient rocks from their lithology, grain-size trends, sedimentary structures (such as cross-bedding, ripple marks and mud cracks), trace fossils and body fossils. Walther's law relates the vertical succession of facies to the lateral migration of environments. Understanding depositional environments is essential to petroleum geology, because reservoir rocks, source rocks and seals are all products of particular sedimentary settings.
