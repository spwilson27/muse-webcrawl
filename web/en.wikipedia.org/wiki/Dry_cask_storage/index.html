Dry cask storage

Dry cask storage is a method of storing high-level radioactive waste, principally spent nuclear fuel, in sealed containers surrounded by inert gas and shielded by steel and concrete, after the fuel has first been cooled for several years in a spent fuel pool. Unlike pool storage, dry casks rely on passive air circulation for cooling and require no active systems or water.

Design

A typical system consists of a welded stainless-steel canister holding the fuel assemblies, placed inside a massive overpack of reinforced concrete and steel that provides radiation shielding and physical protection. Vents allow outside air to circulate around the canister, removing decay heat by natural convection. Casks are designed to withstand earthquakes, tornadoes, floods, fires, and impacts.

Use

Dry cask storage was first licensed in the United States in the 1980s, at the Surry Nuclear Power Plant in Virginia, and has since been adopted at most US reactor sites as pools have filled. Similar systems are used in Canada, Germany (where the CASTOR casks are well known), Switzerland, and other countries with nuclear power programmes. In the US, casks are typically licensed as "independent spent fuel storage installations" (ISFSIs) adjacent to reactor sites, pending the availability of a permanent geological repository.

Safety debate

Supporters argue that passive dry storage is safer and cheaper than continued pool storage, particularly after the Fukushima accident raised concerns about pool fires. Critics focus on long-term corrosion, monitoring, security against attack, and the indefinite siting of waste at reactor sites, many of which were never intended as waste repositories.
