S corporation
Legal basis: Internal Revenue Code, Subchapter S
Jurisdiction: United States
Taxation: Pass-through
Shareholders: ≤ 100, US persons only

An S corporation, for United States federal income tax purposes, is a closely held corporation (or similar entity) that makes a valid election to be taxed under Subchapter S of Chapter 1 of the Internal Revenue Code. In general, S corporations do not pay income tax at the corporate level; instead, income, deductions and credits pass through to shareholders, who report them on their personal tax returns, avoiding the double taxation of C corporations.

Eligibility

To qualify, a corporation must be a domestic corporation with only one class of stock, no more than 100 shareholders, and shareholders who are individuals (US citizens or residents), certain trusts or estates — not partnerships, corporations or non-resident aliens. The election is made by filing Form 2553 with the IRS.

Advantages and limits

Beyond pass-through taxation, S corporation status can let owner-employees reduce self-employment taxes by splitting income between salary and distributions, though the IRS requires "reasonable compensation" be paid as wages. Restrictions on ownership and stock classes make S corporations unsuitable for venture-backed companies, and some states tax or recognise S corporations differently.
