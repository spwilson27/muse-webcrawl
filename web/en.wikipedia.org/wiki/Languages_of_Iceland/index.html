The languages of Iceland are dominated by Icelandic, the North Germanic language spoken by virtually the entire population and the country's sole official language. Iceland is among the most linguistically homogeneous countries in the world, though immigration in the 21st century has increased diversity.

Icelandic

Icelandic descends from Old Norse brought by Norse settlers in the 9th and 10th centuries and has changed relatively little in written form, so Icelanders can still read the medieval sagas. The language is famous for linguistic purism: new concepts are usually named by coining words from native roots (for example tölva, "computer," from words for number and prophetess) rather than borrowing. An Act of 2011 confirmed Icelandic as the official language and the language of government business.

Other languages

Icelandic Sign Language was officially recognised in 2011 as the first language of the deaf community. English is very widely understood and spoken, given tourism, media, and its place in schooling, and Danish (or another Scandinavian language) is a compulsory school subject, a legacy of Iceland's centuries under the Danish crown. The largest immigrant language is Polish, reflecting the biggest immigrant community; other significant languages among residents include Lithuanian, English, Filipino, Latvian, and Ukrainian. Historically, Basque whalers in the 17th century produced a Basque–Icelandic pidgin, and French was once used by French fishermen operating off Iceland.

Language policy

Iceland maintains language planning bodies (the Árni Magnússon Institute for Icelandic Studies and the Icelandic Language Council) that promote terminology creation and correct usage. Concern has been expressed that English-language digital media and voice technology may weaken Icelandic, and the government has funded language-technology programmes to ensure Icelandic is usable in artificial intelligence and computing contexts.
