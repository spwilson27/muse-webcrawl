The Nahe (German pronunciation: [ˈnaːə]) is a river in Rhineland-Palatinate and Saarland, Germany, a left tributary to the Rhine. It gives its name to the wine region Nahe situated around it.

The name Nahe is derived from the Latin word Nava, which is based on the Celtic word for "wild river" — the Nahe was Roman Nava, and the area was home to the Celtic Treveri and later Vangiones tribes.

Course

The Nahe rises in the district of Sankt Wendel in the Saarland, in the Nohfelden municipality near Selbach. It flows northeast through Idar-Oberstein, Kirn, Bad Sobernheim, and Bad Kreuznach, and joins the Rhine at Bingen am Rhein after about 125 kilometres. Its principal tributaries are the Glan and the Alsenz from the south, and the Hahnenbach and Simmerbach from the Hunsrück in the north.

The valley is known for its dramatic geology: steep porphyry cliffs near Bad Münster am Stein-Ebernburg, including the Rotenfels, the highest rock face north of the Alps in Germany at some 200 metres, and the Rheingrafenstein formation.

Wine region

The Nahe wine region, established as one of Germany's 13 designated wine regions (Anbaugebiete), covers roughly 4,000 hectares along the lower Nahe. Despite its small size, the region has an extraordinary diversity of soils — volcanic porphyry and melaphyre, Rotliegend sandstone, slate, loess, and clay in close succession — which produces Riesling wines of distinctive character, along with Müller-Thurgau, Silvaner, and Pinot varieties. Famous estates include those of Schlossgut Diel, Dönnhoff, and Emrich-Schönleber.

History

The Nahe valley was settled early; the area around Bad Kreuznach has Roman villas and baths, and the town was a spa (Bad) famous for brine springs. Idar-Oberstein became a centre of gemstone cutting using local agate and jasper deposits. The railway along the Nahe (Nahe Valley Railway) opened in stages between 1858 and 1860 and remains an important regional line.
