Hops are the female flower clusters, or seed cones, of the hop plant Humulus lupulus, a climbing perennial in the Cannabaceae family. They are used primarily as a bittering, flavouring, and stability agent in beer, to which they impart bitter, zesty, or citric flavours. The hop plant is dioecious: male and female flowers grow on separate plants, and only the female cones are used in brewing, because the lupulin glands within them contain the resins and essential oils brewers prize.

Hop cones ripening on trained bines.

Cultivation

Hops grow best in temperate latitudes between roughly 35° and 55°, where long summer days trigger flowering. The climbing stems, called bines, are trained up strings or wires on tall trellises in fields known as hop gardens or hop yards. Major production regions include the Hallertau in Bavaria — the world's largest contiguous hop-growing area — and the Yakima Valley in Washington State. Plants are propagated from rhizome cuttings so that each field is a single variety.

Use in brewing

In brewing, hops provide bitterness from alpha acids isomerised during boiling, aroma from essential oils, and antimicrobial compounds that historically helped preserve beer — a property that contributed to the keeping qualities of heavily hopped styles such as India pale ale. Varieties are broadly classed as bittering, aroma, or dual-purpose. Modern craft brewing has driven demand for intensely aromatic varieties such as Citra, Mosaic, and Galaxy.

History

Hop cultivation is documented in Europe from the early Middle Ages, and hopped beer gradually replaced ale flavoured with gruit across northern Europe between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries. In England, hops were at first controversial — Henry VIII is said to have restricted their use — but by the seventeenth century hopped beer had become standard. See also history of beer.

Other uses

Young hop shoots are eaten as a vegetable in some regions, and hop extracts appear in herbal medicine as a mild sedative, though clinical evidence is limited. The fibres of the stems have been used for cloth and paper.
