An anchorite (female: anchoress) is a person who, for religious reasons, withdraws from secular society into strict enclosure — in medieval Christianity, typically a small cell, called an anchorhold, attached to the wall of a church, where the recluse vowed to remain for life. Unlike hermits, who might wander or live alone in the wilderness, anchorites were permanently enclosed: the rite of enclosure sometimes included elements of the funeral service, symbolising that the anchorite was dead to the world.

Life in the cell

The cell usually had a small window (a squint or hagioscope) opening into the church so the anchorite could follow Mass and receive communion, and another window to the outside through which food was passed, servants attended, and visitors sought counsel. Anchorites devoted themselves to prayer and contemplation, supported by patrons and alms. The early 13th-century guide Ancrene Wisse ("Guide for Anchoresses") regulated their daily life in detail.

History

Enclosed solitaries are attested from the early centuries of Christianity in the East, but the anchoritic life reached its height in medieval England, where hundreds of anchorites — the majority of them women — are recorded between the 12th and 15th centuries. The best known is Julian of Norwich (c. 1342 – after 1416), whose Revelations of Divine Love, reflecting on visions she received, is the earliest surviving book in English known to have been written by a woman. Other noted English anchoresses include Eve of Wilton and Margaret Kirkby. The dissolution of the monasteries under Henry VIII ended the institution in England.
