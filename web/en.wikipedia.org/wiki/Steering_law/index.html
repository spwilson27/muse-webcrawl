Steering law

Steering tasks include dragging a cursor through tunnels, as in hierarchical menus.

The steering law in human–computer interaction and human factors is a predictive model of human movement, describing how long it takes a user to steer a pointer (or a vehicle, hand, or other controlled object) along a constrained path or "tunnel" of a given length and width. It was formulated by Johnny Accot and Shumin Zhai in 1997 as a generalisation of Fitts's law from aimed pointing to constrained trajectory-based movement.

Formulation

For a straight tunnel of length A and width W, the predicted movement time is:

T = a + b · (A / W)

where a and b are empirically fitted constants. The term A/W is the index of difficulty of the steering task. For curved or varying-width paths, the model generalises to an integral along the path:

T = a + b · ∫ ds / W(s)

Applications

The steering law is used to evaluate and design interface elements that require path-following: cascading (hierarchical) menus, where the pointer must be steered along a submenu without slipping off; scrollbars and sliders; drawing and tracing tasks; and crossing-based interfaces. It complements Fitts's law models used in pointing-device evaluation standards, and has been applied beyond HCI to contexts such as driving through constrained roadways and teleoperation.
