Totten v. United States, 92 U.S. 105 (1876), is a United States Supreme Court case that established that courts may not entertain lawsuits whose very subject matter is a secret — specifically, it barred a suit to enforce an alleged secret contract for espionage services. The decision is the foundation of the so-called Totten bar, closely related to the state secrets privilege in American law.

Background

William A. Lloyd claimed that President Abraham Lincoln had hired him in 1861, during the American Civil War, to spy on Confederate forces, promising compensation of $200 per month. Lloyd's administrator (Totten) sued the United States in the Court of Claims to recover unpaid fees, alleging Lloyd had received only part of the promised amount. The Court of Claims dismissed the claim, and the case reached the Supreme Court.

Decision

Justice Stephen Johnson Field, writing for a unanimous Court, held that the suit could not be maintained. Because the alleged contract was for secret services, secrecy was itself part of the agreement: both parties understood the contract could never be publicly disclosed. To litigate the claim would inevitably reveal matters that national security required to remain concealed. The Court reasoned that such contracts are unenforceable in court precisely because the secrecy they require cannot survive a public trial.

Legacy

Totten is cited as the origin of the rule that claims are barred at the threshold when the very subject matter of the action is a state secret. The Supreme Court reaffirmed and applied the doctrine in Tenet v. Doe (2005), dismissing a suit by alleged Cold War spies, and Totten arguments have been raised in modern litigation involving intelligence contractors and surveillance. Commentators debate the proper boundary between the categorical Totten bar and the narrower, evidentiary state secrets privilege of United States v. Reynolds (1953).
