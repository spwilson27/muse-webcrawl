PEGylation

PEGylation (or pegylation) is the process of both covalent and non-covalent attachment or amalgamation of polyethylene glycol (PEG) polymer chains to molecules and macrostructures, such as a drug, therapeutic protein or vesicle, which is then described as PEGylated.

Purpose

PEGylation increases the hydrodynamic size of a molecule, shielding it from proteolytic enzymes and the immune system, reducing renal clearance, and thereby prolonging its circulation time in the body. It is routinely achieved by incubation of a reactive derivative of PEG with the target molecule, commonly reacting with amino groups (lysine residues or the N-terminus) on proteins.

Applications

PEGylated drugs include peginterferon alfa (for hepatitis C), pegfilgrastim (Neulasta, to reduce infection risk during chemotherapy), pegaspargase, and pegloticase. PEG is also a component of the lipid nanoparticles used in mRNA COVID-19 vaccines. PEGylation was pioneered in the 1970s by Frank F. Davis and colleagues at Rutgers University.

Limitations

A growing number of people have pre-existing anti-PEG antibodies, which can accelerate clearance of some PEGylated drugs or provoke hypersensitivity in rare cases, prompting research into alternative polymers.
