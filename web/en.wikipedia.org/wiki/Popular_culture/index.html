Popular culture

Popular culture (also called mass culture or pop culture) is generally recognized by members of a society as a set of practices, beliefs, artistic output and objects that are dominant or prevalent in a society at a given point in time. Popular culture also encompasses the activities and feelings produced as a result of interaction with these dominant objects. Heavily influenced in modern times by mass media, this collection of ideas permeates the everyday lives of people in a given society.

Definitions

The term "popular culture" emerged in the 19th century in contrast to "high culture", the culture of the educated elite: opera, classical music, fine art and literary fiction. Popular culture was associated with the tastes of the working and middle classes — music hall, popular song, the novel, and later film and broadcasting. Scholars such as John Storey have catalogued multiple competing definitions: culture that is widely liked, culture left over after high culture is defined, mass-produced commercial culture, culture originating from "the people", and culture understood through Antonio Gramsci's concept of hegemony.

History

Cheap printed matter — chapbooks, broadsides and penny dreadfuls — spread shared stories through early modern Europe. The Industrial Revolution created urban working-class audiences for music hall, professional sport and the popular press. In the 20th century, cinema, radio and television created a genuinely mass culture, while the post-war rise of the teenager as a consumer group produced rock and roll, youth fashion and fandom. The Frankfurt School theorists Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer criticised the "culture industry" for standardising taste, while later cultural studies scholars such as Stuart Hall emphasised that audiences actively decode and resist media messages.

Forms

  • Film and television — blockbuster franchises, streaming series and reality television.
  • Popular music — pop, rock, hip hop and electronic dance music distributed globally.
  • Print and comics — bestsellers, manga, superhero comics and graphic novels.
  • Video games — among the largest entertainment industries by revenue.
  • Internet culture — memes, viral video, social media trends and influencer culture.
  • Sport and fashion — globally televised leagues and fast-moving style cycles.

Folklore and distinction from folk culture

Popular culture overlaps with, but is distinct from, folklore and traditional folk culture, which are transmitted informally within communities rather than produced commercially. In practice the boundary blurs: folk tales are repackaged as blockbuster films, and fan communities generate their own traditions around commercial properties.

Criticism

Critics argue that popular culture encourages passive consumption, homogenises local cultures under global (often American) commercial influence, and trivialises public discourse. Defenders reply that popular culture is a site of creativity, identity and democratic participation, and that judgements of taste often encode class prejudice.
