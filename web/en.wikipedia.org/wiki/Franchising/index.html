Franchising

Franchising is a business model in which a franchisor licenses its brand, operating system and know-how to independent operators (franchisees), who run their own outlets under the franchisor's name in exchange for initial fees and ongoing royalties. It is most common in fast food, retail, hospitality and services.

History

Early forms appeared with Singer sewing machine agents in the 1850s and automobile dealerships; the model expanded rapidly after the Second World War with chains such as McDonald's — franchising since 1955 under Ray Kroc — and Holiday Inn.

Agreements and regulation

The franchise agreement sets territory, standards, supply, training and termination terms. In the United States, the FTC Franchise Rule requires a Franchise Disclosure Document; many jurisdictions regulate disclosure and good-faith dealing because of the power imbalance between franchisor and franchisee.

Advantages and criticisms

Franchising lets brands expand rapidly with franchisee capital, while franchisees gain a proven concept and marketing. Criticisms include high fees, restrictive controls, encroachment and disputes over renewal and transfer.
