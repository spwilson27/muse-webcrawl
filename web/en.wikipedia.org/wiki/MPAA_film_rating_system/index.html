Motion Picture Association film rating system

The current MPA film ratings

The Motion Picture Association film rating system is used in the United States and its territories to rate a motion picture's suitability for certain audiences based on its content. Administered by the Motion Picture Association (MPA, formerly MPAA) through the Classification and Rating Administration (CARA), the voluntary system was introduced on November 1, 1968, replacing the restrictive Hays Code.

The ratings

  • G — General audiences; all ages admitted.
  • PG — Parental guidance suggested; some material may not be suitable for children.
  • PG-13 — Parents strongly cautioned; some material may be inappropriate for children under 13. Introduced in 1984, following controversy over films such as Gremlins and Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom.
  • R — Restricted; viewers under 17 require an accompanying parent or adult guardian.
  • NC-17 — Adults only; no one 17 and under admitted. Introduced in 1990 to replace the X rating.

Process and criticism

Films are rated by a board of parents employed by CARA, and producers may edit a film or appeal to seek a different rating. Ratings also include brief descriptors of the content prompting the rating. The system has been criticized for inconsistency, secrecy, being stricter about sex than violence, and for the commercial stigma of the NC-17 rating, which many theaters and retailers refuse to carry — issues explored in the 2006 documentary This Film Is Not Yet Rated.
