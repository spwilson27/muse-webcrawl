Māori culture

Māori culture (Māori: tikanga Māori) is the customs, cultural practices and beliefs of the Māori, the indigenous Polynesian people of mainland New Zealand. It originated from, and is still part of, Eastern Polynesian culture. Māori culture forms a distinctive part of New Zealand culture and, due to a large diaspora and the incorporation of Māori motifs into popular culture, is found throughout the world.

Origins and history

Māori ancestors voyaged from Hawaiki, in eastern Polynesia, reaching New Zealand in successive waka (canoe) migrations conventionally dated to about 1250–1300 CE. Isolated in a temperate land much colder than their tropical homeland, they developed a distinct culture: fortified pā, large carved war canoes, and complex art forms. From the late 18th century, contact with Europeans brought trade, muskets, Christianity and literacy; the Treaty of Waitangi (1840) made New Zealand a British colony, and the New Zealand Wars and land confiscations of the 19th century were followed by steep population and cultural decline. A Māori cultural renaissance from the 1970s onward revived the language and arts and reshaped national life.

Social organisation

Traditional society is organised by whakapapa (genealogy) into whānau (extended families), hapū (subtribes) and iwi (tribes). The marae — a complex centred on a carved meeting house (wharenui) — remains the focus of community life, hosting gatherings, funerals (tangihanga) and formal welcomes (pōwhiri). Core values include mana (prestige and authority), tapu (sacredness and restriction), utu (reciprocity and balance) and kaitiakitanga (guardianship of the natural world).

Arts and performance

Māori visual arts include whakairo (wood carving), tā moko (tattooing), raranga (weaving, especially of flax) and tukutuku (lattice panels). Kapa haka, the performing-arts ensemble of song, dance and chant, includes the haka — a posture dance made world-famous by the All Blacks rugby team. Oral traditions preserve migration narratives, cosmology (such as the separation of Ranginui the sky father and Papatūānuku the earth mother), and the exploits of the demigod Māui.

Language and revival

Te reo Māori declined sharply in the 20th century but was revitalised through kōhanga reo ("language nest") preschools founded from 1982, kura kaupapa immersion schools, and official status under the Māori Language Act 1987. Māori Television, iwi radio and events such as Te Wiki o te Reo Māori (Māori Language Week) support the language, now spoken by a growing share of New Zealanders.
