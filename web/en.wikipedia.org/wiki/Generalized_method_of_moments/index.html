Generalized method of moments

In econometrics and statistics, the generalized method of moments (GMM) is a generic method for estimating parameters in statistical models. It is a generalisation of the classical method of moments. GMM is usually applied when a likelihood function is difficult or impossible to specify, but moment conditions — statements that certain functions of the parameters and data have zero expected value — can be derived from modelling assumptions. The method was introduced by Lars Peter Hansen in 1982.

GMM estimation chooses parameters to make the sample moments as close as possible to zero.

Method

Suppose the model implies moment conditions E[g(X, θ₀)] = 0 at the true parameter value θ₀. The GMM estimator replaces the population moments by their sample averages and chooses the parameter estimate that minimises a quadratic form in those averages, weighted by a positive-definite weighting matrix W. When the number of moment conditions exceeds the number of parameters, the model is overidentified and the conditions cannot all be satisfied exactly; the choice of W then affects the estimator. The efficient two-step procedure first estimates with a simple weighting matrix, then re-estimates using the inverse of the covariance matrix of the moments as the optimal weight.

Properties and testing

Under regularity conditions the GMM estimator is consistent and asymptotically normal, and with the optimal weighting matrix it is the most efficient estimator using those moment conditions. The minimised criterion, scaled by sample size, gives Hansen's J statistic, which tests the validity of the overidentifying restrictions. GMM encompasses many standard estimators: ordinary least squares, instrumental variables/two-stage least squares, and maximum likelihood can all be written as special cases.

Applications

GMM is foundational in empirical macroeconomics and finance, where it was used to estimate Euler-equation asset pricing models and consumption-based models without fully specifying the distribution of shocks. In panel data econometrics, the Arellano–Bond estimator uses lagged values as instruments within a GMM framework to estimate dynamic models with fixed effects.
