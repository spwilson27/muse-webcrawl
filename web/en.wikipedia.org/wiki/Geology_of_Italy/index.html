The geology of Italy is dominated by the collision between the African Plate and the Eurasian Plate, which built the Alps and the Apennines, drives continuing seismicity across the peninsula, and feeds some of Europe's most famous volcanoes, including Etna, Stromboli and Vesuvius.

Tectonic framework

Italy sits within the complex Mediterranean collision zone. The small Adriatic (Apulian) microplate, a promontory of the African Plate, indents northward into Europe, sustaining deformation in the Alps and the Dinarides. Meanwhile the Tyrrhenian Sea is a young back-arc basin that opened behind the migrating Apennine–Calabrian subduction system, where Ionian oceanic lithosphere sinks beneath Calabria.

The Alps

The Italian Alps expose a stacked nappe pile of European, Penninic and Austroalpine units, including ophiolites that record the Jurassic Piedmont–Liguria Ocean, and high-pressure metamorphic rocks exhumed from subduction depths. Glacial erosion during the Pleistocene carved the deep valleys and lakes, such as Garda and Como, along the range front.

The Apennines and foreland

The Apennines, running the length of the peninsula, are a fold-and-thrust belt of Mesozoic–Cenozoic sedimentary rocks scraped off the subducting Adriatic slab. East of the divide, the foreland deepens into the Po Basin and the Adriatic, where thick Plio-Quaternary sediments host many of Italy's gas fields.

Volcanism

Subduction-related and intraplate volcanism has produced the Roman and Campanian volcanic provinces, the Aeolian Islands, and Sicily's Mount Etna, one of the world's most active volcanoes. Vesuvius, overlooking the Bay of Naples, famously destroyed Pompeii and Herculaneum in 79 AD.

Seismicity

Active faulting in the Apennines and the Calabrian arc generates destructive earthquakes, such as those at Messina (1908), Irpinia (1980), L'Aquila (2009) and Amatrice (2016).
