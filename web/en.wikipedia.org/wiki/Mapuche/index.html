Mapuche

The Mapuche (Mapudungun: "people of the land") are a group of indigenous peoples of south-central Chile and southwestern Argentina, including parts of Patagonia. They number roughly 1.5–2 million in Chile, where they are the largest indigenous group, and over 200,000 in Argentina. Their language is Mapudungun (Mapuche), spoken by a minority today, and their traditional homeland is Wallmapu.

History

The Mapuche successfully resisted Inca expansion and then fought the Spanish Empire for more than 300 years in the Arauco War, beginning in the 16th century; figures such as the toqui Lautaro and Caupolicán became cultural heroes, celebrated in the epic poem La Araucana. Treaties such as the Parliament of Quillín (1641) recognised a frontier along the Biobío River. Mapuche autonomy ended only in the later 19th century, with Chile's "Pacification of Araucanía" (1861–1883) and Argentina's "Conquest of the Desert", after which their lands were drastically reduced.

Culture

Traditional Mapuche society was organised in local communities (lof) led by lonko (chiefs), with spiritual life guided by machi (shamans, usually women) and centred on ceremonies such as the nguillatún prayer gathering and We Tripantu, the new year at the winter solstice. Textiles, silverwork, wood carving and the kultrun drum are notable arts.

Contemporary issues

Since Chile's return to democracy, Mapuche organisations have campaigned for land restitution, language revitalisation and constitutional recognition. Land disputes with forestry companies and the Chilean state, especially in the Araucanía and Biobío regions, remain a major political issue, sometimes marked by violence and contested application of anti-terrorism laws.
