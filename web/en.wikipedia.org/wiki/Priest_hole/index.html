Priest hole

Priest hole
TypeConcealed hiding place
PeriodLate 16th–17th century
CountryEngland
PurposeHiding Catholic priests from state pursuivants

A priest hole is a hiding place for a priest built into many of the principal Catholic houses of England, Wales, and Scotland during the period when Catholics were persecuted by law in England. When Queen Elizabeth I came to the throne in 1558, Catholic priests — especially Jesuits and seminary priests trained abroad — were viewed as traitors and agents of foreign powers. Repeated searches by "pursuivants" put priests and the families sheltering them in mortal danger: under the Jesuits, etc. Act 1584, being a Catholic priest ordained abroad and present in England was itself high treason.

Construction

Priest holes were fitted into fireplaces, chimneys, staircases, panelling, attics, and even moats and drains, often several to a house as decoys and reserves. The most ingenious were built by Nicholas Owen, a Jesuit lay brother and master carpenter who constructed hides at houses including Harvington Hall and Hindlip Hall; Owen was starved out of hiding at Hindlip in 1606 and tortured to death in the Tower of London, and was later canonized among the Forty Martyrs of England and Wales. A good hide had to be discoverable neither by eye nor by measurement, knocking, or prolonged search; pursuivants might occupy a house for a week or more, sometimes pulling down wainscoting and sounding floors, while the priest crouched without food, light, or room to move.

Use and survival

Priest holes were used intensively from the 1580s through the era of the Gunpowder Plot (1605) — the plot's surviving conspirators sheltered in houses such as Hindlip — and beyond, into the 1680s. Many survive in English country houses and are shown to visitors today; celebrated examples include Harvington Hall in Worcestershire, Baddesley Clinton, Moseley Old Hall (where Charles II also hid after the Battle of Worcester in 1651), and Oxburgh Hall.
