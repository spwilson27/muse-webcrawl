Information society

An information society is a society in which the creation, distribution, use, integration and manipulation of information is a significant economic, political and cultural activity. The concept emerged as scholars described a shift from industrial economies based on manufacturing to post-industrial economies in which information and knowledge workers predominate.

Origins

Fritz Machlup measured the size of the "knowledge industry" in the United States in 1962; economist Marc Porat extended this analysis in the 1970s. Daniel Bell's The Coming of Post-Industrial Society (1973) argued that theoretical knowledge had become the axial principle of society. Manuel Castells later described the "network society", in which social and economic organisation is structured around information networks.

Characteristics

Indicators of an information society include the share of the workforce in information occupations, the contribution of information industries to GDP, ubiquitous computing and connectivity, and the centrality of digital media to everyday life. Critics question whether the quantitative growth of information marks a qualitative break with industrial capitalism, noting the persistence of material production and new forms of inequality — the "digital divide".

Policy

The United Nations' World Summit on the Information Society (Geneva 2003, Tunis 2005) addressed global governance of the internet and access gaps between developed and developing countries.
