A domain as a partially ordered set

Domain theory

Domain theory is a branch of mathematics and theoretical computer science that studies special kinds of partially ordered sets (posets) commonly called domains. It was originated in the late 1960s by Dana Scott, who sought a mathematical model for the lambda calculus and for denotational semantics of programming languages, in which computations are modelled as continuous functions between domains.

Basic ideas

Elements of a domain represent pieces of information or partial states of a computation, ordered by an information ordering: x ≤ y means y contains at least as much information as x. A least element (bottom, ⊥) represents no information or a non-terminating computation. Computable functions are modelled as Scott-continuous functions — monotone functions that preserve suprema of directed sets — and recursion is interpreted via least fixed points.

Formal structures

Common classes of domains include directed-complete partial orders (dcpos), complete partial orders with bottom, Scott domains, algebraic and continuous lattices, and bifinite (SFP) domains used in stable and event-structure semantics. The theory connects order theory, topology (the Scott topology), logic and category theory.

Applications

Domain theory underlies denotational semantics for imperative and functional languages, models of concurrency, and the semantics of probabilistic and quantum computation.
