A DMCA takedown is a procedure under the United States Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) of 1998 by which a copyright holder (or their agent) asks an online service provider to remove or disable access to material alleged to infringe copyright. In return for complying expeditiously, providers receive "safe harbor" protection from liability for their users' infringement.

Procedure

A valid takedown notice must identify the copyrighted work, the infringing material and its location, the sender's contact information, and statements of good-faith belief and accuracy made under penalty of perjury. The provider removes the material and notifies the uploader, who may file a counter-notice claiming the removal was mistaken or that the use is lawful (for example, fair use). If the copyright holder does not sue within 10 to 14 business days, the provider may restore the content.

Scale and automation

Major platforms receive takedown requests on an enormous scale — Google alone has processed requests covering billions of URLs — and much of the process is automated by rights holders and by anti-piracy vendors. Platforms such as YouTube supplement the statutory system with private tools such as Content ID.

Criticism

The notice-and-takedown system is criticised both by rights holders, who argue infringing copies reappear quickly ("whack-a-mole"), and by users and free-expression advocates, who document erroneous and abusive notices targeting criticism, journalism and fair use. Section 512(f) allows damages for knowing material misrepresentations, and in Lenz v. Universal Music Corp. the Ninth Circuit held that copyright holders must consider fair use before sending a takedown notice.
