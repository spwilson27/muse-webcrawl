Chinook wind

A Chinook wind (or simply Chinook) is a warm, dry foehn wind that blows down the eastern slopes of the Rocky Mountains in North America, particularly in Alberta, Canada, and the northwestern United States. Chinooks can raise temperatures dramatically — sometimes by more than 20 °C (36 °F) within hours — and rapidly melt snow, earning them the nickname "snow eaters".

Mechanism

Moist Pacific air forced up the western slopes of the Rockies loses moisture as precipitation, then descends the lee side, warming by adiabatic compression at the dry adiabatic lapse rate. The resulting warm wind is often accompanied by a distinctive stationary band of cloud, the "Chinook arch", over the foothills.

Records and effects

One of the most extreme temperature swings was recorded at Spearfish, South Dakota, in January 1943, when the temperature rose from −20 °C to 7 °C in two minutes. In Pincher Creek, Alberta, the temperature rose 41 °C in one hour in 1962. Chinooks provide welcome mid-winter relief and forage access for livestock, but the rapid pressure and temperature changes are popularly blamed for migraines and irritability.
