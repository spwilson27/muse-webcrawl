Francisque Poulbot (6 February 1879 – 16 September 1946) was a French poster artist, draughtsman, and illustrator. He is best known for his affectionate drawings of Parisian street urchins — the gavroches of Montmartre — which became so popular that such children are still called poulbots in French.

Life and work

Born in Saint-Denis into a family of teachers, Poulbot was a gifted draughtsman from childhood but a reluctant schoolboy. He began publishing drawings in the Parisian illustrated press while still a teenager and settled in Montmartre in 1900, becoming a fixture of the neighborhood's artistic life. From 1900–1901 onward he contributed regularly to humor magazines, and during the First World War his posters and postcards of brave, patriotic children — drawn despite his own discharge from military service on health grounds — made him famous throughout France. He was made a chevalier of the Legion of Honour in 1920.

Deeply attached to Montmartre and its children, Poulbot campaigned for the preservation of the neighborhood, helped organize the "République de Montmartre" charitable association in 1921, and used his fame to raise funds for needy children of the district. His house on the avenue Junot, decorated with his own work, stood opposite the famous cabaret district he chronicled.

Legacy

The word poulbot entered everyday French as a generic term for a Paris street kid, and his images of rosy-cheeked urchins in oversized shoes remain staples of Parisian souvenir art. Poulbot is buried in Montmartre, and a street and a square in the 18th arrondissement bear his name.
