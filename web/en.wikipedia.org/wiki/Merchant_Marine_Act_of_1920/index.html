Merchant Marine Act of 1920

The Merchant Marine Act of 1920, commonly known as the Jones Act, is a United States federal statute regulating maritime commerce in U.S. waters and between U.S. ports. It was sponsored by Senator Wesley L. Jones of Washington and enacted in the aftermath of World War I to support a strong American merchant marine considered essential to national defense.

Cabotage provisions

Section 27 of the Act—the provision most often meant by "the Jones Act" today—requires that all goods transported by water between U.S. ports be carried on ships that are built in the United States, owned by U.S. citizens, registered in the United States, and crewed predominantly by U.S. citizens and permanent residents. Waivers may be granted in limited circumstances in the interest of national defense.

Seamen's protections

Other sections of the Act govern the rights of seamen. Section 33 amended earlier law to allow injured seamen to bring negligence actions against their employers — the "Jones Act" also denotes this personal-injury remedy in maritime law — and the Act addressed seamen's wages, working conditions, and desertion penalties.

Debate

Supporters argue the Act sustains the U.S. shipbuilding industry, mariner workforce, and sealift capacity needed in wartime. Critics contend it raises shipping costs, particularly to Puerto Rico, Hawaii, Alaska, and Guam, and reduces competition; it is frequently debated after natural disasters when temporary waivers are issued for relief shipments. Reform and repeal proposals are regularly introduced in Congress but have not been enacted.
