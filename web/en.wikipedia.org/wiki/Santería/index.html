Santería (Spanish pronunciation: [santeˈɾi.a]) is an Afro-Cuban religion that developed in Cuba from the traditions of the Yoruba people of West Africa, syncretised with Roman Catholicism and Spiritism under conditions of slavery. Its formal name is the Regla de Ocha, and it is also called La Regla de Lucumí, from the Lucumí (Yoruba) liturgical language preserved in its songs, prayers and divination verses.

Beliefs

Practitioners believe in a remote supreme creator, Olodumare (Olofi), and venerate intermediary deities called orishas (orichas), each governing aspects of nature and human life. Because enslaved Yoruba in Cuba outwardly identified their orishas with Catholic saints, each orisha is paired with a saint whose iconography or legend resembles theirs: Changó (thunder) with Saint Barbara; Yemayá (the sea) with Our Lady of Regla; Oshún (rivers, love) with Our Lady of Charity of El Cobre, Cuba's patroness; Babalú-Ayé (disease and healing) with Saint Lazarus. This correspondence gives the religion its popular name — "the way of the saints".

Practice

Worship centres on altars in homes and temple-houses (casas de santos), where consecrated stones embodying the orishas are kept in decorated tureens and fed with offerings of food, herbs and — in many lineages — animal sacrifice, a practice whose legality in the United States was affirmed by the Supreme Court in Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah (1993). Music and dance with consecrated batá drums are used to invite orishas to possess their initiates at festivals (bembés).

Divination is central: priests (santeros) and priestesses (santeras) consult the dilogún (cowrie-shell oracle), while high priests of Ifá, called babalawos, practise the more senior Ifá divination of the related Regla de Ifá. Initiation is a week-long process in which the novice is "crowned" with their tutelary orisha, followed by a year of restrictions wearing white (iyaworaje).

History and spread

Santería crystallised in 19th-century Cuba, spread after the Cuban Revolution (1959) with emigration to Miami, New York, Puerto Rico, Venezuela, Mexico and Spain, and now counts millions of adherents and sympathisers across the Americas and Europe, though secrecy and syncretism make numbers difficult to estimate. In Cuba it coexists and mixes with Palo (of Kongo origin) and the Abakuá mutual-aid societies. It has strongly influenced Cuban music, dance and visual art.
