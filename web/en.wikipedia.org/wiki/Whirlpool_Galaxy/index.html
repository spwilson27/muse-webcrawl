Whirlpool Galaxy

The Whirlpool Galaxy, also known as Messier 51a, M51a or NGC 5194, is an interacting grand-design spiral galaxy in the constellation Canes Venatici, about 23 million light-years from Earth. It is one of the most photographed galaxies in the sky and a classic example of spiral structure.

Structure

The galaxy's two sweeping spiral arms are unusually well defined, which is why it is called a "grand-design" spiral. The arms are sites of intense star formation, traced by bright pink hydrogen clouds and blue clusters of young stars. At its centre lies a supermassive black hole, surrounded by a yellowish bulge of older stars.

The Whirlpool is interacting with a smaller companion galaxy, NGC 5195, which is passing behind one of its arms. The companion's gravity is thought to have amplified the spiral pattern and triggered the bursts of star formation along the arms.

Observation history

The galaxy was discovered by Charles Messier in 1773. In 1845, Lord Rosse, using the 72-inch Leviathan telescope at Birr Castle, became the first to recognise its spiral form — the first spiral structure ever identified in a "nebula". This observation was a milestone in the realisation that many nebulae are distant galaxies like our own Milky Way.
