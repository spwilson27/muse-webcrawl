Evolution of sexual reproduction
FieldEvolutionary biology
Key puzzleThe "twofold cost of sex"
Main hypothesesRed Queen, DNA repair, mutational deterministic, lottery models

The evolution of sexual reproduction describes how sexually reproducing animals, plants, fungi, and protists evolved from a common ancestor that was a single-celled eukaryotic species. Sexual reproduction is widespread among eukaryotes, yet its origin and persistence pose a major puzzle for evolutionary biology, because asexual reproduction appears more efficient: an asexual female passes on twice as many of her genes per offspring as a sexual female, and does not need to invest in producing males — the so-called twofold cost of sex.

Origin

Sex is thought to have originated in early eukaryotes more than a billion years ago, possibly as a by-product of mechanisms for DNA repair and genetic exchange related to those still seen in bacteria (transformation, conjugation, and transduction). The cell fusion and meiosis at the heart of sexual reproduction may have evolved from processes by which early eukaryotes repaired damaged DNA using a homologous template from another cell.

Explanations for the maintenance of sex

Numerous hypotheses seek to explain why sex persists despite its costs. The Red Queen hypothesis proposes that genetic recombination continually generates novel resistance genotypes in an arms race against parasites. Mutational hypotheses, such as Muller's ratchet and the mutational deterministic hypothesis, argue that sex purges deleterious mutations that would otherwise accumulate irreversibly in asexual lineages. Lottery and tangled bank models emphasise that genetically varied offspring are advantageous in unpredictable or heterogeneous environments. The DNA repair hypothesis holds that meiosis is maintained chiefly because it repairs DNA damage.

Evidence

Comparative studies show that obligately asexual lineages are usually short-lived "twigs" on the tree of life, suggesting they arise often but rarely persist — consistent with long-term advantages of sex. Experiments with yeast, rotifers, and water fleas have demonstrated faster adaptation and parasite resistance in sexual populations under some conditions. Many biologists now regard plural explanations as likely: different benefits may dominate in different organisms and environments.
