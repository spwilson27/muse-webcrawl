The Deer Hunter is a 1978 American epic war drama film co-written and directed by Michael Cimino, about a trio of Russian-American steelworkers from a small town in Pennsylvania whose lives are upended by the Vietnam War. The film stars Robert De Niro, Christopher Walken, and John Savage as the three friends, with John Cazale (in his final film role) and Meryl Streep in an early breakthrough performance. It won five Academy Awards, including Best Picture and Best Director, and is regarded as one of the greatest American films ever made.

Plot

The film unfolds in three movements. In the first, Michael (De Niro), Nick (Walken), and Steven (Savage) work in a steel mill in Clairton, Pennsylvania. The community celebrates Steven's Orthodox wedding, and the friends take a final deer-hunting trip to the mountains, where Michael espouses his "one shot" philosophy of clean killing. In the second movement, the three serve together in Vietnam and are captured by the Viet Cong, who force their prisoners to play Russian roulette against one another. Michael engineers an escape, but the men are separated; Steven loses both legs, and Nick, traumatised, stays behind in Saigon, becoming a performer in a Russian roulette gambling den. In the final movement, Michael returns home unable to readjust, learns of Nick's fate, and travels back to Saigon as the city falls, confronting Nick in a last game in which Nick, recognising his friend too late, kills himself. The film closes with the surviving friends gathered after Nick's funeral, singing "God Bless America" around a kitchen table.

Production

The screenplay originated in a story by Louis Garfinkle and Quinn K. Redeker about Russian roulette players in Las Vegas, extensively rewritten by Cimino and Deric Washburn. Principal photography took place in Ohio, Pennsylvania, West Virginia, and Washington state, with the Vietnam sequences shot in Thailand. The wedding sequence runs nearly an hour of screen time and was shot in a real Russian Orthodox cathedral in Cleveland. The Russian roulette scenes, though powerful, have no documented historical basis as a practice inflicted on American prisoners, a point of lasting controversy.

Reception and legacy

The film was a critical and commercial success and a lightning rod in the late-1970s reckoning with Vietnam. At the 51st Academy Awards it won Best Picture, Best Director, Best Supporting Actor (Walken), Best Film Editing, and Best Sound. Some critics and veterans criticised its depiction of the Vietnamese and the roulette device as sensationalised, while others praised its portrayal of working-class friendship and trauma. It was selected for preservation in the US National Film Registry in 1996, and appears on the American Film Institute's list of the 100 greatest American films. The film's success gave Cimino the leverage to make Heaven's Gate (1980), whose failure became a famous counterpoint in Hollywood history.
