Heat shock protein

Heat shock proteins (HSPs) are a family of proteins produced by cells in response to exposure to stressful conditions. They were first described in 1962 by Ferruccio Ritossa, who observed new puffs on the chromosomes of fruit fly (Drosophila) salivary glands after accidental heat shock. HSPs are found in virtually all living organisms, from bacteria to humans, and many act as molecular chaperones, helping other proteins fold correctly, refold after damage, or be transported and disposed of.

Discovery and induction

The heat shock response is among the most conserved genetic systems known. When cells are exposed to elevated temperature, heavy metals, toxins, infection, inflammation or oxidative stress, transcription of heat shock genes rises sharply. The master regulator in eukaryotes is heat shock factor 1 (HSF1), which trimerises under stress and binds heat shock elements in gene promoters.

Classification

HSPs are named by their approximate molecular weight in kilodaltons. Major families include:

  • Hsp70 — the most abundant and best studied; assists folding of new and misfolded proteins using ATP.
  • Hsp90 — stabilises and activates many signalling proteins and steroid hormone receptors; an important drug target in cancer.
  • Hsp60 (chaperonins) — barrel-shaped complexes (such as bacterial GroEL) that provide an isolated chamber for folding.
  • Small HSPs (e.g. Hsp27, αB-crystallin) — ATP-independent "holdases" that prevent aggregation of damaged proteins.
  • Hsp100/Clp family — disaggregases that can pull proteins out of aggregates.

Functions

Beyond stress protection, chaperones are essential in unstressed cells: they fold a large fraction of newly synthesised proteins, assemble complexes, escort proteins across membranes, and target irreparably damaged proteins for degradation. This quality-control network is called proteostasis.

Medical significance

Failure of chaperone systems contributes to neurodegenerative diseases in which proteins aggregate, including Alzheimer's, Parkinson's and Huntington's diseases. Tumour cells often overexpress Hsp90 and Hsp70 to survive their stressful microenvironment, making HSP inhibitors a focus of oncology research. Mutations in small HSP genes cause certain inherited neuropathies and cataracts. Extracellular HSPs can also act as danger signals, stimulating the immune system.
