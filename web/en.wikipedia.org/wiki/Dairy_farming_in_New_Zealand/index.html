Dairy farming in New Zealand

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dairy farming in New Zealand is one of the country's largest industries and its biggest export earner. New Zealand is among the world's leading exporters of dairy products, and the co-operative Fonterra—owned by roughly ten thousand farmer shareholders—collects the great majority of the country's milk and is one of the largest dairy companies in the world by volume. Dairy products account for roughly a quarter to a third of New Zealand's merchandise export earnings in many years.

History

Commercial dairying began in the nineteenth century and expanded rapidly after the introduction of refrigerated shipping in 1882, which allowed butter and cheese to be exported to Britain. Through the twentieth century the industry was organised around farmer-owned co-operatives and export marketing boards. The merger that created Fonterra in 2001 consolidated most of the industry under the Dairy Industry Restructuring Act.

Production system

New Zealand dairying is based on a low-cost, pasture-based system: cows graze outdoors year-round on ryegrass–clover pastures, with calving concentrated in late winter and spring to match grass growth. The national herd numbers around five million cows, milked on some eleven to twelve thousand farms. The main dairying regions are Waikato, Taranaki, Canterbury, Southland and Manawatū-Whanganui. Typical herds milk once or twice daily through herringbone or rotary sheds, and the predominant breeds are Holstein-Friesian, Jersey and their cross (KiwiCross).

A distinctive labour institution is sharemilking, under which a sharemilker owns the herd and machinery and milks on another person's land for an agreed share of the milk income—a traditional pathway to farm ownership.

Environmental issues

The expansion and intensification of dairying has raised concerns over water quality (nitrate and pathogen contamination of rivers and lakes), greenhouse-gas emissions—agriculture produces about half of New Zealand's emissions—and animal welfare. Regulatory responses have included freshwater reforms, limits on synthetic nitrogen fertiliser, and work toward pricing agricultural emissions.
