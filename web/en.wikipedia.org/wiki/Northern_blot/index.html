A Northern blot is a laboratory technique used in molecular biology to study gene expression by detecting a specific RNA molecule in a sample. The method takes its name from its analogy to the Southern blot — named after its inventor, biologist Edwin Southern, who developed it for DNA — making "Northern" a geographer's pun rather than an eponym.

Procedure

  1. RNA is extracted from cells or tissue, keeping it intact with RNase-free technique and denaturants such as formamide or glyoxal.
  2. The RNA is separated by size using gel electrophoresis (typically agarose gel under denaturing conditions).
  3. The separated RNA is transferred ("blotted") to a nylon or nitrocellulose membrane by capillary action and fixed by UV crosslinking or baking.
  4. The membrane is hybridized with a labelled probe — DNA, RNA or oligonucleotide complementary to the target sequence, tagged radioactively (historically 32P) or with chemiluminescent/fluorescent markers.
  5. After washing away unbound probe, the signal is detected on film or a digital imager; band intensity reflects the abundance of the target RNA, and band position reveals its size.

Applications

Northern blotting measures the amount and size of a specific mRNA across tissues, developmental stages or experimental treatments — for example, showing that a gene is switched on only in liver, or is induced after hormone treatment. Because it reveals transcript size, it can detect alternative splicing and processing products that PCR alone might miss. Applications include validating gene-expression changes first seen in microarray or RNA-seq screening.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include direct measurement of transcript size, detection of splice variants, and robustness in experienced hands. Disadvantages are the need for relatively large amounts of intact RNA, low throughput, low sensitivity compared with RT-PCR, laborious protocol, and (in classic form) radioisotope hazards. Reverse Northern blots reverse the format: labelled RNA probes are hybridized to cDNA targets immobilized on the membrane.
