Von Neumann conjecture

The von Neumann conjecture was a statement in the mathematical field of geometric group theory. It proposed that a group is non-amenable if and only if it contains a subgroup that is a free group on two generators. The "if" direction was classical; the conjectured "only if" direction stood for roughly fifty years before being disproved.

Background

John von Neumann introduced the notion of amenability in 1929 while analysing the Banach–Tarski paradox: he showed that the paradoxical decompositions behind it were possible because the group of rotations of the sphere contains a free subgroup on two generators, and that amenable groups admit no such paradoxical decomposition. It was natural to ask whether containing a free subgroup was the only obstruction to amenability — this is the statement later named after him, although von Neumann himself may never have stated it as a conjecture.

Disproof

In 1980 Alexander Olshansky proved the conjecture false by constructing finitely generated non-amenable groups — "Tarski monster" type examples — in which every proper subgroup is cyclic, so no free subgroup on two generators can exist. In 1985 Sergei Adian showed that the free Burnside groups B(m, n) for odd n ≥ 665 are further counterexamples: they are non-amenable yet every subgroup is finite or cyclic in structure and they contain no free subgroup of rank two. Later work by Olshansky and Mark Sapir produced even finitely presented counterexamples.

Legacy

The failure of the conjecture shaped modern group theory: it showed that non-amenability is a subtler property than containing free subgroups, and helped motivate related notions such as property (T), sofic groups, and the study of elementary amenable groups. A related statement often confused with it — the von Neumann–Day problem, concerning elementary amenable groups — was disproved separately by Olshansky (1980) and Grigorchuk's group (1984).
