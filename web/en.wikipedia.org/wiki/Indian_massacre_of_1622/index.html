Indian massacre of 1622


A depiction of the 1622 attack

The Indian massacre of 1622, also known as the Jamestown massacre, was a coordinated attack by the Powhatan Confederacy against the English colony of Virginia on 22 March 1622. Led by Chief Opechancanough, Powhatan warriors attacked dozens of English settlements along the James River, killing 347 colonists — about a quarter of the colony's population — including settlers at Jamestown's outlying plantations, Henricus, and Martin's Hundred.

Background

Relations between the colonists and Powhatan had deteriorated as English tobacco plantations expanded onto Indigenous land and after the death of Pocahontas in 1617 and of her father, Chief Powhatan, in 1618. Opechancanough, who succeeded as paramount chief, planned the attack to drive out or contain the invaders. On the morning of the assault, warriors entered settlements unarmed as if to trade, then seized tools and weapons and attacked.

Aftermath

Jamestown itself was warned by an Indigenous convert named Chanco and survived. The colony retaliated with years of punitive warfare, destroying Powhatan crops and villages, and the Virginia Company of London — already financially troubled — lost its charter in 1624, making Virginia a royal colony. A second major Powhatan offensive in 1644, also led by the elderly Opechancanough, failed, and by 1646 the Confederacy was effectively destroyed as an independent power. The events are often cited by historians as a turning point that hardened English attitudes toward Native peoples and accelerated the colony's reliance on enslaved African labor.
