The Tunisia campaign (also known as the Battle of Tunisia) was a series of battles in French Tunisia during the North African campaign of the Second World War, between Axis forces under Hans-Jürgen von Arnim (and Erwin Rommel) and Allied forces under Dwight D. Eisenhower, Harold Alexander, and Bernard Montgomery. It began with Operation Torch landings in November 1942 and ended with the surrender of some 230,000–275,000 Axis troops in May 1943 — a defeat sometimes called "Tunisgrad".

Background

After the Second Battle of El Alamein (October–November 1942), Axis forces retreated westward through Libya, pursued by the British Eighth Army. On 8 November 1942, Anglo-American forces landed in Morocco and Algeria under Operation Torch. Rather than retreat to Sicily, Hitler ordered reinforcements rushed to Tunisia, establishing a bridgehead around Tunis and Bizerte.

Race for Tunis

Allied forces advanced east hoping to seize Tunis before the Axis could consolidate, but by December 1942 mud, supply shortages, and Axis air superiority stalled the offensive. Rommel's Afrika Korps, retreating from Libya, meanwhile dug in along the Mareth Line in southern Tunisia facing Montgomery.

Kasserine Pass

In February 1943 Rommel struck westward against inexperienced American troops, inflicting a sharp defeat at the Battle of Kasserine Pass (19–24 February). The reverse prompted reforms in American command, with George S. Patton taking over II Corps, and closer Anglo-American coordination under Alexander's 18th Army Group.

Final offensive

In March, Montgomery broke the Mareth Line with a flanking move through the Tebaga Gap. In early May 1943 the final Allied offensive (Operation Vulcan and Strike) shattered the Axis position; Tunis and Bizerte fell on 7 May, and the remaining Axis forces in the Cap Bon peninsula surrendered by 13 May 1943. The victory cleared North Africa, secured the Mediterranean sea lanes, and set up the invasion of Sicily.
