Inattentional blindness

Inattentional blindness is the failure to notice a fully visible but unexpected object or event because attention is engaged elsewhere. It is not a defect of vision: the eyes may fixate the object, yet it never reaches conscious awareness. The phenomenon demonstrates that perception depends on attention — we see far less of a scene than we intuitively believe.

In the famous "invisible gorilla" experiment, about half of observers missed the gorilla.

The invisible gorilla experiment

In a celebrated 1999 study, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris asked participants to count the passes made by a basketball team wearing white shirts. Midway through the video, a person in a gorilla suit walks into the scene, beats its chest, and leaves. Roughly half of the participants, absorbed in counting, failed to notice the gorilla at all — and were astonished when shown the replay. Earlier work by Ulric Neisser and by Arien Mack and Irvin Rock, who coined the term "inattentional blindness", had shown similar effects with superimposed scenes and unexpected shapes.

Factors and implications

Blindness increases with the difficulty and attentional load of the primary task, and decreases when the unexpected item resembles what is being attended to. It has practical consequences: drivers looking at a junction may fail to see a motorcyclist ("looked but failed to see" accidents); phone use worsens the effect; and studies have found that even expert radiologists screening lung scans can miss a gorilla image embedded in the slides. The phenomenon is closely related to change blindness, in which large changes to a scene go unnoticed.
