Public bathing

Public bathing is the practice of bathing in facilities shared by members of the public, originating from a time when few households had private plumbing. Public baths have served purposes of hygiene, health, ritual purification, and social life in many cultures.

History

Some of the earliest public baths date to the Indus Valley Civilization, including the Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro (c. 2500 BCE). Roman thermae were vast complexes combining hot, warm, and cold baths with exercise grounds and libraries, central to Roman social life. Bathing cultures developed independently around the world: the Islamic hammam, Japanese sentō and onsen, Korean jjimjilbang, Russian banya, Finnish sauna (often private but also communal), and Turkish baths exported widely in the 19th century.

Modern era

In industrial Europe and America, municipal public baths expanded in the 19th century as sanitation reform, providing baths and wash-houses ("baths and washhouses" in Britain) to working-class districts. Most declined as indoor plumbing spread in the 20th century, though public bathing persists for leisure, tradition, and tourism in many countries — for example, Japan's sentō, Hungary's thermal baths, and communal saunas in Finland and Russia.

Cultural role

Beyond cleanliness, public baths function as social spaces and, in some traditions, sites of religious ritual such as mikveh in Judaism and ghusl in Islam. Customs regarding nudity, mixed or single-sex use, and etiquette vary widely by culture.
