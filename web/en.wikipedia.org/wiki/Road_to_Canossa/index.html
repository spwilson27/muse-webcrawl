The Road to Canossa (German: Gang nach Canossa; Italian: l'umiliazione di Canossa, "the humiliation of Canossa") was the journey of the Holy Roman Emperor Henry IV to Canossa Castle in northern Italy in January 1077, where he stood as a penitent before Pope Gregory VII to seek absolution and the lifting of his excommunication. It is one of the most famous episodes of the Investiture Controversy between Empire and Papacy.

Background

Gregory VII's reform programme asserted papal authority over the investiture of bishops, which Henry regarded as a royal prerogative. After Henry's supporters declared the pope deposed at the Synod of Worms (1076), Gregory excommunicated the emperor and released his subjects from their oaths of allegiance. Facing revolt by the German princes, who threatened to elect a new king if he remained excommunicate, Henry crossed the Alps via the Mont Cenis pass in mid-winter to intercept the pope, who was travelling north and took refuge at Canossa, the castle of Matilda of Tuscany.

The penance

According to the traditional account, Henry stood for three days (25–27 January 1077) outside the castle gates in the snow, barefoot and clad in a hair shirt, fasting and pleading for admission. On 28 January, after intercession by Matilda and Abbot Hugh of Cluny, Gregory absolved him and celebrated Mass with him. Historians note that the dramatic details were shaped by partisan chroniclers such as Lampert of Hersfeld, but the core event — a ritual public penance and reconciliation — is not in doubt.

Aftermath and significance

The absolution undercut the rebellious princes, though they elected an anti-king, Rudolf of Rheinfelden, anyway. Conflict resumed: Henry was excommunicated a second time in 1080, marched on Rome, and installed an antipope, while Gregory died in exile at Salerno in 1085. Tactically astute, the penance nonetheless became a lasting symbol of the submission of secular power to spiritual authority — and, conversely, of papal overreach. In 1872 Chancellor Otto von Bismarck told the Reichstag during the Kulturkampf, "We will not go to Canossa", making the phrase a byword for refusing humiliating submission.
