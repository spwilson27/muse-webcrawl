The field-effect transistor (FET) is a type of transistor that uses an electric field to control the flow of current in a semiconductor. FETs have three terminals — source, gate and drain. Applying a voltage to the gate varies the conductivity of a channel between source and drain, and thereby the current flowing through it. Because the gate draws almost no current, FETs have very high input impedance.

History

The field-effect principle was patented by Julius Edgar Lilienfeld in 1925 and by Oskar Heil in 1934, but practical devices had to wait for advances in semiconductor processing. The first working FETs were the junction FET (JFET) in the early 1950s; the metal–oxide–semiconductor FET (MOSFET), demonstrated by Mohamed Atalla and Dawon Kahng at Bell Labs in 1959, became the most widely used transistor in history and the building block of modern integrated circuits.

Types

  • MOSFET — insulated-gate FET; n-channel or p-channel, enhancement or depletion mode. Dominant in digital logic (CMOS) and power electronics.
  • JFET — gate formed by a reverse-biased p–n junction.
  • MESFET — Schottky-barrier gate, common in gallium arsenide devices.
  • HEMT — high-electron-mobility transistor for very high frequencies.

Operation

For an enhancement-mode n-channel MOSFET, a positive gate voltage attracts electrons into the channel beneath the gate oxide, allowing current to flow from drain to source once the gate voltage exceeds the threshold voltage. FETs operate in cutoff, triode (linear) and saturation regions; in saturation the drain current is approximately proportional to the square of the gate overdrive voltage.
