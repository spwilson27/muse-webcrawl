Software engineer

A software engineer is a person who applies the principles of software engineering to design, develop, maintain, test, and evaluate computer software. The term programmer is sometimes used as a synonym, but may emphasize implementation over design and can imply a lack of engineering disciplines. Engineering techniques are used to inform the software development process, which involves the definition, implementation, assessment, measurement, management, change, and improvement of the software life cycle process itself.

History of the term

Margaret Hamilton is often credited with popularizing the term "software engineering" during her work on the Apollo program at MIT in the 1960s, when she led the team that developed the on-board flight software for the Apollo Guidance Computer. The term gained formal standing at the NATO Software Engineering Conferences of 1968 and 1969, which framed a "software crisis" of projects delivered late, over budget, and unreliable. Before the title became widespread, practitioners were usually called programmers or software developers; today usage varies by country and company, and in some jurisdictions (notably parts of Canada and the United States), the title "engineer" is regulated by professional engineering bodies.

Work

Software engineers analyse requirements; design systems and architectures; write and review code; construct tests; debug and maintain systems; and collaborate with product managers, designers, and other engineers. Specializations include front-end and back-end development, mobile applications, embedded systems, data engineering, machine learning, site reliability, security, and quality assurance. Work commonly follows processes such as agile development, continuous integration, and code review.

Education and skills

Many software engineers hold degrees in computer science, software engineering, or related fields, though self-taught and bootcamp-trained engineers are also common in the industry. Core skills include proficiency in one or more programming languages (such as Python, Java, JavaScript, C++, or Rust), data structures and algorithms, version control, testing, and system design. Professional certification and licensing exist in some regions but are not generally required for employment.

Employment outlook

Software development is among the fastest-growing occupations in many economies; the US Bureau of Labor Statistics has repeatedly projected much-faster-than-average growth for software developers, quality assurance analysts, and testers, driven by demand for applications, systems software, and cybersecurity. The field has also been reshaped by remote work, open-source collaboration, and, since the early 2020s, AI-assisted coding tools.
