Brain Trust

[Image: Franklin D. Roosevelt with advisers, 1930s]

The Brain Trust is a term for a group of close advisers to a political candidate or incumbent, prized for their academic expertise in fields such as economics, law, and public policy. The term is most closely associated with the group of Columbia University academics who advised Franklin D. Roosevelt during his 1932 presidential campaign and the early years of his presidency, helping to design important parts of the New Deal.

The original Brain Trust

The phrase was popularised by journalist John F. Kieran of The New York Times and is a pun on the era's business "trusts". The core members assembled by Roosevelt's aide Samuel Rosenman in 1932 were Raymond Moley, Rexford Guy Tugwell, and Adolf A. Berle Jr. — professors of government, economics, and law respectively at Columbia. Later additions to the wider circle included Felix Frankfurter, Donald Richberg, and others who supplied ideas, speeches, and policy drafts.

Influence

The Brains Trust shaped the programme of the First New Deal: banking reform, agricultural adjustment, and industrial recovery planning debated between Moley's and Tugwell's competing visions of regulation and planning. Moley broke with Roosevelt in 1933, but the model — a president drawing systematically on university experts — endured. The term has since been applied generically to any politician's circle of expert advisers, sometimes approvingly and sometimes as a populist gibe at unelected intellectuals.
