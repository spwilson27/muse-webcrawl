Dur-Sharrukin

Dur-Sharrukin (Akkadian: Dūr-Šarru-kīn, "Fortress of Sargon"), in present-day Khorsabad in northern Iraq, was the capital of the Neo-Assyrian Empire during the reign of Sargon II (r. 722–705 BC). The city was built almost from scratch on a previously sparsely occupied site near the Tigris, beginning around 717 BC, and was inaugurated in 706 BC. After Sargon's death in battle in 705 BC, his son Sennacherib abandoned the city and moved the capital to Nineveh.

Layout and excavation

Dur-Sharrukin was laid out as a near-square walled city of about 3 square kilometres, with seven gates named for Assyrian gods. In its northwest stood the citadel containing Sargon's palace — a vast complex of more than 200 rooms and courtyards decorated with carved stone reliefs and colossal human-headed winged bulls (lamassu) — along with temples and a ziggurat. The palace reliefs, depicting Sargon's campaigns and court life, are now among the treasures of the Louvre, the Oriental Institute in Chicago, and the Iraq Museum.

The site was first excavated by the French consul Paul-Émile Botta in 1843, who initially believed he had found Nineveh. One shipment of antiquities from the site was lost when the raft carrying it sank in the Tigris in 1855. In modern times the site has suffered looting and damage, including during the conflicts in Iraq in the 2010s.
