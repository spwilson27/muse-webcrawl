Political capital

Political capital is the goodwill, trust and influence that a politician earns or builds through public approval, successful policies, personal reputation and relationships with other political actors. Like financial capital, political capital can be accumulated, invested and spent: a politician "spends" political capital by pursuing controversial policies or expending goodwill on unpopular decisions, and builds it by winning elections, delivering popular results or handling crises effectively.

Concept

The term borrows from economics and from sociological theories of capital, notably Pierre Bourdieu's forms of capital, in which social, cultural and symbolic capital can be converted into power. In political commentary, political capital is often treated as a depletable resource: leaders typically have the most of it immediately after an election victory — during a "honeymoon period" — and lose it over time through setbacks, scandals and the compromises of governing.

Usage

The phrase entered wide circulation in American political journalism. After his re-election in 2004, United States President George W. Bush famously declared: "I earned capital in the campaign, political capital, and now I intend to spend it." Commentators likewise assess whether leaders use moments of high approval to pass ambitious legislation or conserve their capital for future battles.

Accumulation and loss

Political capital grows through electoral mandates, high approval ratings, successful crisis management, and coalition-building. It is depleted by policy failures, broken promises, corruption scandals, and association with unpopular causes. Because it rests on perception as much as on formal power, political capital can shift rapidly after major events.
