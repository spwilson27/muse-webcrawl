Francisella tularensis

Francisella tularensis is a pathogenic species of Gram-negative coccobacillus, an aerobic and facultative intracellular bacterium that causes the zoonotic disease tularemia. It is a highly infectious organism: as few as ten bacteria can cause disease in humans, and it is classified as a Tier 1 select agent in the United States.

Subspecies

Four subspecies are recognised: F. t. tularensis (type A), found mainly in North America and the most virulent; F. t. holarctica (type B), found across the Northern Hemisphere; F. t. mediasiatica, from Central Asia; and F. t. novicida, of low virulence in humans.

Transmission and disease

The bacterium circulates in lagomorphs (rabbits and hares), rodents and other small mammals, and is transmitted to humans by tick and deer-fly bites, contact with infected animals, contaminated water, or inhalation of aerosols. Clinical forms of tularemia include ulceroglandular, glandular, oculoglandular, oropharyngeal, pneumonic and typhoidal disease. Treatment is with antibiotics such as streptomycin, gentamicin, doxycycline or ciprofloxacin.

History

The organism was first isolated in 1911 by George Walter McCoy from ground squirrels in Tulare County, California, and was studied extensively by Edward Francis, for whom the genus is named.
