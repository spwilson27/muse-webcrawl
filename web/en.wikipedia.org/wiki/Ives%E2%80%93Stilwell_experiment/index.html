Ives–Stilwell experiment

The Ives–Stilwell experiment, first performed by Herbert E. Ives and G. R. Stilwell in 1938, tested the contribution of the transverse Doppler effect — the time dilation of special relativity — to the Doppler shift of light emitted by moving atoms. It was the first direct, quantitative confirmation of time dilation and remains a classic test of special relativity; modern repetitions are among the most precise tests of the relativistic Doppler formula.

Purpose

In special relativity, the observed frequency of light from a moving source combines the classical (longitudinal) Doppler effect with a second-order redshift caused by time dilation in the source's rest frame. Measuring the small second-order shift separately from the much larger first-order shift offered a direct way to verify the Lorentz transformations and Einstein's clock hypothesis. Ironically, Ives himself opposed relativity and interpreted the result within a Lorentzian ether framework, but the measurements matched Einstein's predictions.

Original experiment

Ives and Stilwell used beams of hydrogen canal rays (ions) accelerated through known voltages to speeds of up to roughly 0.01c. They spectroscopically compared light emitted in the forward and backward directions along the beam (the Hβ Balmer line). The average — or "centre of gravity" — of the two shifted lines was displaced from the rest wavelength by exactly the second-order amount predicted by relativity, γ ≈ 1 + v²/2c². A refined version followed in 1941.

Modern versions

Later experiments used Mössbauer rotors, fast atomic beams, and two-photon spectroscopy of stored ion beams. In experiments at the Heidelberg Test Storage Ring, lithium ions moving at 0.03c and 0.064c were probed with lasers; the results verified the relativistic Doppler formula to precisions approaching one part in 109. These tests constrain possible deviations from Lorentz invariance in test theories such as the Robertson–Mansouri–Sexl framework.
