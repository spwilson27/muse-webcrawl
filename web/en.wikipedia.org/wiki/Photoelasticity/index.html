Photoelasticity

Photoelasticity describes changes in the optical properties of a material under mechanical deformation, and is used as an experimental method of stress and strain analysis. Certain transparent materials become birefringent when stressed: the refractive index varies with the local principal stresses, so that when a loaded model is viewed between crossed polarising filters, colourful interference fringes appear. The fringe patterns reveal the distribution of stress, with fringe order proportional to the difference of principal stresses (the stress-optic law).

History

The phenomenon was described by David Brewster in 1815–16, who observed that glass became birefringent under load. In the early twentieth century, engineers such as E. G. Coker and L. N. G. Filon developed photoelasticity into a practical method; with the advent of transparent plastics (Bakelite, then epoxy resins) it became a standard tool for analysing stress concentrations around holes, notches, and other features before the rise of computer-based finite element analysis.

Method

In transmission photoelasticity, a scale model of a part is made from a photoelastic polymer, loaded in a polariscope, and the resulting isochromatic fringes (loci of constant principal-stress difference) and isoclinic fringes are photographed and interpreted. In reflection photoelasticity, a photoelastic coating is bonded to a real component, allowing stresses on the actual structure to be measured. Photoelasticity is also used to inspect residual stresses in glass and transparent plastics, where stress patterns can be seen directly between polarisers.
