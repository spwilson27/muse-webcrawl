Sumer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Great Ziggurat of Ur, a stepped mudbrick temple tower, as it appears today]

Sumer was one of the earliest civilisations, centred in southern Mesopotamia (modern south-east Iraq) from about 4500 to 1900 BC. The Sumerians built the world's first cities — Uruk, Ur, Eridu, Lagash, Nippur and Kish among them — invented cuneiform writing, the wheel and the plough, divided time into units of sixty, and composed the earliest surviving literature, including the Epic of Gilgamesh. Their language, a language isolate unrelated to any known tongue, was written for two thousand years after it ceased to be spoken.

City-states

Sumer was never a single state for long: rival city-states, each ruled by an ensi or lugal under the patronage of a city god, fought over land and water, building stepped temple-towers (ziggurats) such as the Great Ziggurat of Ur. Around 2334 BC Sargon of Akkad conquered the cities and created the Akkadian Empire; after its fall, Ur-Namma of Ur (r. c. 2112–2095 BC) founded the Third Dynasty of Ur and issued one of the earliest law codes.

Achievements and legacy

Sumerian scribes recorded accounts, contracts, hymns, proverbs and epics on clay tablets, millions of which survive. Their sexagesimal mathematics underlies our 60-minute hour and 360-degree circle; their astronomy, irrigation law and literary themes passed into Babylonian, Hebrew and Greek culture. Sumerian identity faded as Akkadian speech prevailed, and by about 1900 BC Sumer had been absorbed into Babylonia.
