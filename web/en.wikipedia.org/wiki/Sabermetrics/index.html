Sabermetrics

Sabermetrics, also originally called SABRmetrics, is the empirical analysis of baseball, especially baseball statistics that measure in-game activity. Sabermetricians collect and summarize the relevant statistics which traditionally comprise baseball; they synthesize this data with various statistical tools to form, validate, and refine hypotheses about the sport and the players.

The term is derived from the acronym SABR, which stands for the Society for American Baseball Research, founded in 1971. The term was coined by Bill James, who cofounded SABR, and became one of its most prominent proponents.

History

Statistical analysis of baseball has roots in the work of Henry Chadwick in the 19th century and in Branch Rickey's hiring of statistician Allan Roth in the 1940s. Earnshaw Cook's Percentage Baseball (1964) anticipated later work. The modern movement coalesced around James, whose self-published Baseball Abstract appeared annually from 1977, and around SABR's publications. Pete Palmer and John Thorn's The Hidden Game of Baseball (1984) introduced the linear weights system.

The approach entered the baseball mainstream through the Oakland Athletics under general manager Billy Beane, whose low-payroll teams of the early 2000s exploited undervalued skills such as on-base percentage — a story popularised by Michael Lewis's book Moneyball (2003) and its 2011 film adaptation. Major league front offices subsequently institutionalised analytics departments, and new measurement systems such as PITCHf/x and Statcast supplied ever richer data.

Key concepts

  • On-base plus slugging (OPS) and its scaled form OPS+ as measures of offensive value.
  • Wins above replacement (WAR), an attempt to summarise a player's total contribution in a single number.
  • Defensive independent pitching statistics (DIPS), including FIP, which evaluate pitchers on outcomes they control directly.
