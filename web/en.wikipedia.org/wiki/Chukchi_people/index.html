The Chukchi are an Indigenous people inhabiting the Chukchi Peninsula, the shores of the Chukchi Sea and the Bering Sea region of the Arctic Ocean within the Russian Far East. Their ethnonym in their own language means "rich in reindeer", reflecting the centrality of reindeer herding to traditional life. The population numbers roughly sixteen thousand, concentrated in Chukotka Autonomous Okrug.

Traditional life

Chukchi society was historically divided between two economic groups. The inland Reindeer Chukchi were nomadic herders who lived in portable conical tents (yaranga) covered with reindeer hides and followed their herds across the tundra. The coastal Maritime Chukchi lived in semi-permanent settlements and hunted sea mammals — whales, walrus, and seals — from skin boats (umiaks), trading marine products with the herders for reindeer meat and hides. Dog sleds were a principal means of transport, and the Chukchi dog is regarded as an ancestor of the Siberian Husky.

History

The Chukchi fiercely resisted Russian expansion in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Unlike most Siberian peoples, they were never fully subdued by force; the Russo-Chukchi wars ended with a negotiated peace in the late eighteenth century, after which trade fairs and gradual integration followed. During the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, American traders were also active on the Chukchi coast. In the Soviet period, herding was collectivised, settlements were consolidated, and many Chukchi were educated in Russian boarding schools, transforming traditional society while reindeer herding continued as the economic base.

Culture

Traditional Chukchi religion was animistic and shamanistic, centred on reverence for natural forces, the sea, and ancestral spirits, with rituals surrounding hunting and herding success. Chukchi artisans are known for carving in walrus ivory and bone, and for a distinctive graphic art tradition. The writer Yuri Rytkheu, a Chukchi, brought Chukchi life to international attention through novels written in Russian and Chukchi.
