Quiver

A quiver is a container for holding arrows, bolts, or darts. It can be carried on an archer's body, on the archer's horse, or mounted on the ground, and historically was often made of leather, wood, fur, or woven materials. Quivers appear in cultures across the world, from ancient Egypt and Assyria to medieval Europe, Central Asia, Japan, and the Indigenous peoples of the Americas.

Types

  • Back quiver: slung over the shoulder and worn on the back, common in European tradition and modern target archery; arrows are drawn over the shoulder.
  • Belt (hip) quiver: hung from the belt at the waist, common among modern target archers for easy access.
  • Bow quiver: attached directly to the limbs or riser of the bow, popular with modern hunters.
  • Horse quiver: carried on the saddle; steppe peoples such as the Mongols and Turks often paired a large quiver with a separate bow case.
  • Ground quiver: a stand or hoops set in the ground at the shooting line.

Historical use

Reliefs from ancient Egypt and Assyria show archers and charioteers with quivers mounted on chariots and shoulders. Ötzi, the Neolithic "Iceman", was found with a quiver containing 14 arrows—two finished and the rest unfinished—dating to about 3300 BC. In Japan, distinctive quivers (ebira, yazutsu) protected war arrows, while Turkish and Persian quivers were often richly decorated. The combined bow-case and quiver worn by horse archers influenced equipment across Eurasia.

In language and culture

The word "quiver" comes through Old French cuivre from a Germanic source (compare Old English cocor). Surviving idioms include "an arrow in the quiver", meaning a resource held in reserve. In heraldry, quivers appear as charges; in the Bible, children are compared to arrows in a quiver ("Happy is the man that hath his quiver full of them", Psalm 127), a phrase behind the "quiverfull" movement's name. In mathematics, a quiver is a directed graph used in representation theory, named by analogy with a bundle of arrows.
