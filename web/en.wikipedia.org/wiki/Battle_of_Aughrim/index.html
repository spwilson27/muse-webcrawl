The Battle of Aughrim was the decisive battle of the Williamite War in Ireland, fought on 12 July 1691 (Old Style) near the village of Aughrim, County Galway. It was the bloodiest battle ever fought on Irish soil, with roughly 7,000 men killed. The Williamite army under Godert de Ginkel defeated the Jacobite army of James II, commanded by the French general the Marquis de Saint-Ruth.

The battle

The Jacobites occupied a strong defensive position along Kilcommodan Hill, protected by bogs. After hours of fighting, Williamite cavalry forced a crossing of the causeway through the bog on the Jacobite right. Saint-Ruth was decapitated by a cannonball at the critical moment, and the leaderless Jacobite army collapsed. The defeat destroyed the Jacobite field army; Galway and then Limerick surrendered later that year, ending the war with the Treaty of Limerick.

Legacy

Aughrim, rather than the more famous Battle of the Boyne, effectively decided the war. The battle is commemorated in Irish folk memory in the lament tradition and, for unionists, as part of the "Twelfth" of July commemorations (though these centre on the Boyne's calendar-adjusted date). An interpretive centre at Aughrim village tells its story.
