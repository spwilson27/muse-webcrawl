Varenicline, sold under the brand names Chantix (United States) and Champix (Europe and elsewhere), is a prescription medication used to help people stop smoking tobacco. Developed by Pfizer, it is considered among the most effective single pharmacotherapies for smoking cessation.

Mechanism

Varenicline is a partial agonist of the α4β2 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, the receptor subtype principally mediating nicotine dependence. By partially stimulating the receptor it eases craving and withdrawal, while simultaneously blocking nicotine from binding, which blunts the reward of smoking a cigarette. The drug was designed from the plant alkaloid cytisine, long used in eastern Europe as a quitting aid.

Use and effectiveness

Treatment typically begins one to two weeks before a target quit date, with dose escalation over the first week to 1 mg twice daily, for a course of 12 weeks, sometimes extended by a further 12 weeks. Clinical trials and the large EAGLES study found varenicline roughly doubles to triples the odds of quitting compared with placebo, and outperforms single-form nicotine replacement therapy and bupropion.

Side effects and safety history

The commonest adverse effect is nausea; vivid dreams, insomnia and headache also occur. Post-marketing reports of neuropsychiatric symptoms led the US FDA to add a boxed warning in 2009, but the EAGLES trial (2016) found no significant increase in serious neuropsychiatric events, and the boxed warning was removed later that year. In 2021 Pfizer paused distribution and recalled lots of Chantix after finding nitrosamine impurities above acceptable limits; generic versions subsequently became widely available.
