Pareidolia

FieldPsychology, perception
DefinitionPerceiving a meaningful pattern (especially a face) in random or ambiguous stimuli
Famous examplesMan in the Moon, Face on Mars, religious images in food and stains
Related phenomenaApophenia, Rorschach test

Pareidolia is the tendency for perception to impose a meaningful interpretation on a nebulous stimulus, usually visual, so that one detects an object, pattern, or meaning where there is none. The most common form is facial pareidolia — seeing faces in inanimate objects such as electrical outlets, the fronts of cars, cloud formations, or the craters of the Moon. The word derives from the Greek para ("beside, instead of") and eidolon ("image, form").

Facial pareidolia: a rock formation that appears to have facial features.

Explanations

Humans are highly specialised for face recognition; brain regions such as the fusiform face area respond preferentially to face-like configurations, and infants orient toward face-like patterns from birth. Evolutionarily, rapid face detection is advantageous for social interaction and for spotting predators or conspecifics, so the detection system is biased toward false positives — it is safer to see a face that is not there than to miss one that is. Pareidolia is thus considered a by-product of the brain's pattern-recognition and agency-detection systems.

Examples

The "Man in the Moon" is a cross-cultural example. The "Face on Mars," a mesa in the Cydonia region photographed by Viking 1 in 1976 that appeared face-like under certain lighting, became famous in popular culture; later higher-resolution images from Mars Global Surveyor showed a natural landform. Religious pareidolia includes reported images of the Virgin Mary or Jesus in tortillas, windows, tree bark, and water stains, which have attracted pilgrims. Auditory pareidolia includes hearing hidden messages in music played backward and perceiving voices in random noise.

Clinical and scientific use

The Rorschach inkblot test exploits pareidolia, asking subjects what they see in ambiguous blots. Excessive pareidolia and apophenia are associated with some psychotic conditions, but ordinary pareidolia is a normal feature of human perception. Scientists use pareidolia as a cautionary example in evaluating anomalous claims, from canals on Mars to faces in satellite imagery.
