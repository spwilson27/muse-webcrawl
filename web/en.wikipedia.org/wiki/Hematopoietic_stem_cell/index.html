Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) are the stem cells that give rise to other blood cells. This process is called haematopoiesis. In vertebrates, the very first definitive HSCs arise from the ventral endothelial wall of the embryonic aorta during mid-gestational development, and then colonize the fetal liver. In adult humans, haematopoiesis occurs in the red bone marrow found in the core of most bones, including the pelvis, vertebrae, sternum, ribs, and skull.

The blood cells are divided into three lineages. The erythroid cells are the oxygen-carrying red blood cells. Both the red and white blood cells are made in the bone marrow from HSCs. The lymphoid cells, made from the progeny of HSCs, are the immune cells: lymphocytes (T cells and B cells), and natural killer cells.

Properties

HSCs are a rare population, with estimates in humans of roughly one HSC per 10,000 to 100,000 bone marrow cells. Two defining properties set them apart from their progeny:

  • Self-renewal — the capacity to divide and produce at least one daughter cell that remains an HSC, maintaining the stem-cell pool for life.
  • Multipotency — the capacity to differentiate into every mature blood cell lineage.

Most HSCs in the steady state are quiescent, dividing rarely; they are activated to proliferate in response to blood loss, infection, or chemotherapy. Experiments by Till and McCulloch in 1961, demonstrating spleen colony-forming units in irradiated mice, established the existence of multipotent blood-forming stem cells and founded the field of stem cell biology.

Identification and isolation

HSCs can be identified and enriched using flow cytometry and combinations of cell-surface markers. In humans, long-term HSCs are typically characterized as CD34+, CD38−, CD45RA−, CD90+, and lineage-marker negative (Lin−). In mice, the classic phenotype is Lin−, Sca-1+, c-Kit+ (LSK), with further refinement using the SLAM family markers CD150 and CD48. The functional gold standard remains transplantation: a single mouse HSC can reconstitute the entire blood system of a lethally irradiated recipient.

Clinical use

Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), commonly called bone marrow transplantation, is the most established stem-cell therapy in medicine. HSCs can be harvested from bone marrow, mobilized peripheral blood, or umbilical cord blood. Transplantation is used in the treatment of leukemias, lymphomas, multiple myeloma, myelodysplastic syndromes, aplastic anemia, and certain inherited immune and metabolic disorders. Gene therapy approaches that correct a patient's own HSCs ex vivo are now approved treatments for conditions such as sickle cell disease and beta-thalassemia.

Niche

HSCs reside in specialized microenvironments called niches, where stromal cells, endothelial cells, osteoblasts, and sympathetic nerves provide signals — including stem cell factor (SCF), CXCL12, and thrombopoietin — that regulate quiescence, self-renewal, and differentiation. Understanding the niche has led to strategies for expanding HSCs outside the body and improving transplant outcomes.
