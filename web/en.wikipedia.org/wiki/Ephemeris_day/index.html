Ephemeris day

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An ephemeris day is a unit of time defined as exactly 86,400 ephemeris seconds. The ephemeris second, adopted in the 1950s as the fundamental unit of ephemeris time, was defined as a specified fraction (1/31,556,925.9747) of the tropical year for 1900 January 0 at 12 hours ephemeris time — that is, it was based on the Earth's orbital motion around the Sun rather than on its rotation.

Ephemeris time was defined from the Earth's orbital motion rather than its irregular rotation.

Background

By the early twentieth century astronomers had established that the Earth's rotation is not perfectly uniform: tidal friction gradually lengthens the day, and there are smaller seasonal and irregular variations. A time scale based on the rotation — Universal Time — therefore made a poor independent variable for the equations of celestial mechanics. In 1952 the International Astronomical Union adopted ephemeris time, whose unit was derived from Simon Newcomb's tables of the Sun, effectively defining time by the observed motions of the Solar System bodies.

The ephemeris second was taken into the International System of Units in 1960 as the definition of the second, before being replaced in 1967 by the atomic definition based on the caesium-133 hyperfine transition. Ephemeris time itself was superseded in 1984 by Terrestrial Time (TT) and Barycentric Dynamical Time (TDB).

Relation to other units

An ephemeris day is not the same as a mean solar day. Because the Earth's rotation is slowing, the mean solar day is now slightly longer than 86,400 SI seconds, and the difference between rotation-based time (UT1) and uniform time scales such as TT accumulates: the accumulated difference ΔT amounts to roughly 69 seconds in the early twenty-first century, growing by roughly 2.3 milliseconds per day each century. The ephemeris day survives implicitly in modern practice, since Terrestrial Time is defined so as to be continuous with ephemeris time, and TT days are reckoned as exactly 86,400 SI seconds.
