Hate crime

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hate crime
Also known asBias-motivated crime
Legal formPenalty enhancement or standalone offence, varies by jurisdiction

A hate crime (also known as a bias-motivated crime) is a crime committed against a person or property motivated in whole or in part by the offender's bias against a race, religion, disability, sexual orientation, ethnicity, gender, or gender identity, among other protected characteristics. The underlying act — assault, vandalism, murder — is a crime in itself; hate crime laws typically enhance the penalty because the bias motive is regarded as harming not only the immediate victim but the wider community to which the victim belongs.

Legal frameworks

In the United States, the FBI has collected hate crime statistics since the Hate Crime Statistics Act of 1990. Federal law was substantially expanded by the Matthew Shepard and James Byrd Jr. Hate Crimes Prevention Act of 2009, which covered crimes motivated by gender, sexual orientation, gender identity, or disability and removed the requirement that the victim be engaged in a federally protected activity. Most US states have their own penalty-enhancement statutes with varying lists of protected classes.

In the United Kingdom, offences such as assault, criminal damage, and harassment can be charged as racially or religiously aggravated under the Crime and Disorder Act 1998, attracting higher sentences; sentencing law also requires courts to treat hostility based on disability, sexual orientation, or transgender identity as an aggravating factor. Many European states have analogous provisions, and the OSCE's Office for Democratic Institutions and Human Rights collects hate crime data across its participating states.

Statistics and underreporting

Official statistics substantially understate incidence because many victims do not report offences. The US Bureau of Justice Statistics' victimisation surveys have consistently estimated far more hate crime victimisations per year (often over 200,000) than the roughly 8,000–11,000 incidents annually reported to the FBI by police agencies. The most commonly reported motivations in the United States and Europe are race/ethnicity and religion, including antisemitic and anti-Muslim offences.

Debate

Supporters argue that hate crimes inflict greater psychological harm and risk retaliatory escalation, justifying stronger punishment, and that such laws affirm societal commitment to equality. Critics argue variously that penalty enhancement punishes motive or speech rather than conduct alone, that the laws are applied inconsistently, or that they create unequal categories of victims. Constitutional challenges in the US have largely failed where statutes target conduct rather than expression (Wisconsin v. Mitchell, 1993).
