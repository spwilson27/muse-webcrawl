Delphi method

The Delphi method is a structured forecasting and decision technique based on several rounds of anonymous questionnaires to a panel of experts, with controlled feedback of the group's responses between rounds, aiming to converge toward a consensus or a well-characterised range of views. It is named after the Oracle of Delphi.

Delphi method
Developed: 1950s–60s, RAND Corporation
Key figures: Olaf Helmer, Norman Dalkey
Features: Anonymity, iteration, controlled feedback, statistical aggregation

Method

A facilitator selects experts, who answer questionnaires in two or more rounds. After each round, participants receive an anonymised summary (often medians and quartiles, plus reasons for outlier views) and may revise their estimates. The process stops after a predefined number of rounds or when consensus/stability is reached. Anonymity reduces dominance, authority and bandwagon effects of face-to-face groups.

History and applications

Developed at RAND during the Cold War (Project Delphi) for technological and military forecasting, it is now used in business forecasting, health research (developing clinical guidelines and core outcome sets), education, policy and risk assessment. Variants include the policy Delphi (which maps disagreement rather than forcing consensus) and real-time / roundless Delphi.

Criticism

Critics note sensitivity to panel selection and question design, attrition between rounds, and that consensus is not correctness; accuracy versus other structured judgement methods is debated.
