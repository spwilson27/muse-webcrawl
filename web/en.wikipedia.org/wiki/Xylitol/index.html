Xylitol is a chemical compound with the formula C5H12O5, a sugar alcohol used as a sugar substitute in foods, chewing gum, oral-care products and pharmaceuticals. It occurs naturally in small amounts in many fruits and vegetables and is produced commercially chiefly from xylan-rich plant material such as birch bark and corn cobs.

Properties and production

Xylitol was first derived from birch chips by the German chemist Emil Fischer and his student Rudolf Stahel in 1890. Industrially it is made by hydrolysing xylan to the sugar xylose, which is then hydrogenated over a catalyst to xylitol. It is about as sweet as sucrose but contains roughly 40% fewer calories, and it dissolves with a notable cooling sensation.

Dental and medical uses

Unlike sugars, xylitol is not fermented by oral bacteria such as Streptococcus mutans, and regular use in gum and toothpaste is associated with reduced dental caries. Because its absorption is slow and only partly insulin-dependent, it has a low glycaemic index and is used in diabetic foods. Excessive consumption can have a laxative effect.

Toxicity to dogs

Xylitol is highly toxic to dogs: ingestion triggers a rapid release of insulin, causing potentially fatal hypoglycaemia, and can lead to liver failure. It is increasingly listed on product labels under the name "birch sugar."
