William F. Albright

William Foxwell Albright (May 24, 1891 – September 19, 1971) was an American archaeologist, biblical scholar, and linguist. Regarded as the founder of biblical archaeology as a modern discipline, he directed excavations in the Levant, developed the standard chronology of Palestinian pottery, and argued that archaeological evidence broadly supported the historicity of many biblical traditions.

Life and career

Albright was born in Chile to American Methodist missionary parents and developed an early fascination with the ancient Near East. He earned his doctorate at Johns Hopkins University in 1916 under Paul Haupt. From 1919 he was based in Jerusalem, serving as director of the American School of Oriental Research (1920–1929), where he excavated sites including Tell el-Ful and Tell Beit Mirsim, whose stratified pottery sequence became a benchmark for dating Levantine sites.

In 1929 Albright returned to Johns Hopkins as professor of Semitic languages, a chair he held until 1958. He was a master of dozens of ancient and modern languages and contributed to the decipherment and study of inscriptions, including early alphabetic Proto-Sinaitic texts. He was among the first scholars to authenticate the Dead Sea Scrolls in 1948, recognising the great Isaiah Scroll's antiquity. His major works include From the Stone Age to Christianity (1940), Archaeology and the Religion of Israel (1942), and Yahweh and the Gods of Canaan (1968).

Influence

Albright trained a generation of scholars and dominated American biblical archaeology for decades. His confident synthesis of archaeology and biblical narrative was later challenged by younger scholars, but his methodological contributions — particularly ceramic typology and stratigraphy — remain foundational.
