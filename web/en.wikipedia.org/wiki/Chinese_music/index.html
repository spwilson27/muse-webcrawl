Chinese music

Traditional instruments of a Chinese orchestra

Chinese music is the music of the Han Chinese people and the ethnic minorities of China. It encompasses a vast history spanning more than three thousand years, from court music (yayue), ritual music and literati solo repertoire to regional opera, folk song, religious music, and in the 20th and 21st centuries Mandarin and Cantonese popular music.

Traditional music

China's earliest surviving instruments include bone flutes from Jiahu (c. 7000 BCE) and the bronze chime bells (bianzhong) of the Zenghouyi tomb (433 BCE), whose set of 65 bells spans five octaves. Recorded theory held music to be a mirror of cosmic order; the pentatonic scale was foundational, and Confucian ritual music governed court ceremony for millennia.

Key traditional instruments include the guqin (seven-string zither), pipa (lute), erhu (two-string fiddle), dizi (bamboo flute), sheng (mouth organ), and a large family of gongs, drums and bells. Regional genres include Jiangnan sizhu (silk-and-bamboo ensemble music of the south), Cantonese music, Xi'an drum music, and the narrative song and opera forms described at Chinese opera. The modern Chinese orchestra, formed on the model of the Western symphony but using Chinese instruments, was established in the 20th century.

Modern music

In the 20th century Chinese composers and conservatories absorbed Western classical music, while popular songs in Shanghai jazz (shidaiqu) emerged in the 1920s–40s. From the 1980s Cantopop from Hong Kong and Mandopop from Taiwan and the mainland became global industries; Chinese rock and independent music developed alongside state-supported patriotic and folk revival repertoires. Composers such as Tan Dun and Lang Lang achieved international fame in classical music.
