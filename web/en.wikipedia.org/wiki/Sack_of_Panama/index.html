Sack of Panama (1671)

Date: 28 January 1671
Location: Panamá Viejo, Panama
Attackers: Buccaneers under Henry Morgan
Defenders: Spanish colonial forces under Juan Pérez de Guzmán

The sack of Panama was the capture and destruction of the Spanish colonial city of Panama (now Panamá Viejo) by English privateers and buccaneers under Henry Morgan on 28 January 1671. It was one of the most famous buccaneering exploits of the 17th century.

Panama was the Pacific terminus of Spain's American treasure system: silver from Peru was shipped to the city and carried across the isthmus by mule train to be loaded onto the treasure fleets for Spain. Morgan, commissioned by the governor of Jamaica to strike Spanish targets, crossed the isthmus with roughly 1,400 men after storming Fort San Lorenzo at the mouth of the Chagres River.

The battle and fire

On the plain before the city, Morgan's men routed a larger Spanish force of infantry and cavalry under the governor, Juan Pérez de Guzmán. As the buccaneers entered Panama, a great fire consumed much of the city; who set it remains disputed — the Spanish governor ordered powder stores blown up, while Spanish accounts blamed the invaders. The city was thoroughly plundered over the following weeks, though much of its movable treasure had been evacuated by ship.

Aftermath

Panama was never rebuilt on the same site; a new city (the present-day Casco Viejo of Panama City) was founded in 1673 on a more defensible peninsula nearby. Morgan returned to Jamaica to find that England and Spain had signed the Treaty of Madrid, making his raid illegal; he was sent to London in arrest but was never tried, and was later knighted and made lieutenant governor of Jamaica. The ruins of Panamá Viejo are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
