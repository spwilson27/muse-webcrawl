Fort Laramie was a significant 19th-century trading post, diplomatic site and military fort in the valley of the North Platte River in what is now eastern Wyoming, United States. Founded in 1834 as a private fur-trading post (originally Fort William), it became one of the most important staging points on the Oregon, California and Mormon emigrant trails, and was purchased by the US Army in 1849 to protect travellers on the trails.

History

The post was rebuilt as an adobe fort in 1841 and renamed for the trapper Jacques La Ramée. As emigration westward surged in the 1840s, Fort Laramie served as a place to rest, resupply and repair wagons. The Army era saw the fort host major treaty councils: the Treaty of Fort Laramie (1851) with the Plains tribes, and the Treaty of Fort Laramie (1868) with the Sioux, Cheyenne and others, which established the Great Sioux Reservation and closed the Bozeman Trail forts. During the Indian Wars the fort was a major supply and command centre; it was finally abandoned in 1890.

Preservation

The site is preserved as the Fort Laramie National Historic Site, administered by the National Park Service, with numerous restored buildings — including the 1849 cavalry barracks and "Old Bedlam", the oldest surviving military structure in Wyoming — interpreting the fur trade, emigration and frontier Army eras.
