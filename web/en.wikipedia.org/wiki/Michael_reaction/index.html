Michael reaction

Placeholder image: General scheme of the Michael addition

The Michael reaction or Michael 1,4-addition is the nucleophilic addition of a carbanion or another nucleophile (the Michael donor, typically an enolate) to an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound (the Michael acceptor) at the β-carbon. It belongs to the class of conjugate additions and is one of the most important carbon–carbon bond-forming reactions in organic synthesis. It was defined by Arthur Michael in 1887 in work with ethyl acetoacetate and diethyl 2-bromomalonate derivatives.

Mechanism

Under basic conditions, a base (often catalytic, such as sodium ethoxide) deprotonates the donor to give a resonance-stabilised enolate. The enolate attacks the β-carbon of the α,β-unsaturated carbonyl conjugately, forming a new enolate at the carbonyl oxygen; protonation of this enolate yields the 1,4-addition product and regenerates the base. Because the addition is reversible in some systems, the thermodynamic product generally predominates.

Scope and variants

Common donors include malonic esters, β-keto esters, nitro compounds, and nitriles; common acceptors include enones, enals, α,β-unsaturated esters, amides, and nitroalkenes. Heteroatom nucleophiles give related reactions: the addition of amines is often called the aza-Michael reaction, sulfur nucleophiles give the thia-Michael, and phosphorus nucleophiles the phospha-Michael. Enantioselective (asymmetric) Michael additions have been developed using chiral organocatalysts and metal complexes. The Michael addition is the key first step of the Robinson annulation (with methyl vinyl ketone), a classic route to fused six-membered rings such as the Wieland–Miescher ketone.

Applications

The reaction is used industrially and in total synthesis; a famous industrial example is the addition chemistry in the manufacture of pharmaceuticals and fine chemicals, and the Mukaiyama–Michael reaction extends the method to silyl enol ethers under Lewis acid catalysis.
