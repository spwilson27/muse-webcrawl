Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios, Inc.

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios, Inc., 464 U.S. 417 (1984), known as the Betamax case, is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States ruling that making individual copies of complete television shows for later viewing ("time-shifting") constitutes fair use under copyright law, and that manufacturers of home video recording devices cannot be held liable for contributory copyright infringement merely because the devices are capable of infringing uses, provided they are capable of substantial noninfringing uses.

Background

Universal Studios and Walt Disney Productions sued Sony in 1976 over its Betamax videotape recorder, alleging that home taping infringed their copyrights and that Sony was liable as a contributory infringer. The district court ruled for Sony in 1979, finding time-shifting a fair use; the Ninth Circuit reversed in 1981, holding Sony liable and threatening the future of the VCR market. The Supreme Court heard the case twice before deciding it.

Decision

By a 5–4 majority, in an opinion by Justice John Paul Stevens, the Court held for Sony. Borrowing from patent law's staple article of commerce doctrine, the Court held that a product capable of substantial noninfringing uses cannot be banned merely because it may also be used to infringe. The Court further held that private, noncommercial time-shifting is fair use: it is a legitimate, widely accepted practice, and the studios had failed to show a meaningful likelihood of harm to the market for their programmes. Justice Harry Blackmun dissented, joined by three colleagues, arguing that the Copyright Act did not permit wholesale copying of programmes for home retention.

Significance

The decision removed the legal threat hanging over the VCR just as the home video market exploded, enabling the rental and sell-through industries that soon exceeded box-office revenues. Its "substantial noninfringing use" standard became central to later technology cases, including debates over file sharing in MGM Studios v. Grokster (2005), and remains a foundation of US law on consumer copying technologies.
