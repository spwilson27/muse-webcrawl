Greater sage-grouse

The greater sage-grouse (Centrocercus urophasianus) is the largest grouse in North America. It lives in the sagebrush steppe of the western United States and southern Canada, where it depends on big sagebrush for food and cover throughout the year.

Description

Adults have long pointed tails and feathered legs. Males are much larger than females and are distinguished by a black throat and head markings, a white breast, and two yellowish air sacs on the neck. During the spring breeding season, males gather on traditional display grounds called leks, where they strut with tails fanned and inflate their air sacs to produce a remarkable series of swishing, popping, and whistling sounds. Females visit the lek to mate, then nest and rear the chicks alone.

Habitat and diet

Sage-grouse eat sagebrush leaves almost exclusively in winter; in spring and summer they take forbs and insects, which are especially important for growing chicks. Birds move seasonally between breeding, brood-rearing, and wintering areas, sometimes over considerable distances.

Conservation

Populations have declined substantially from historical levels owing to habitat loss and fragmentation from energy development, agriculture, invasive annual grasses and altered fire regimes, fences, and West Nile virus. After a long-running controversy, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service concluded in 2015 that listing under the Endangered Species Act was not warranted, relying instead on federal and state conservation plans covering millions of acres; the decision has remained politically contested. In Canada the species is listed as endangered, and a distinct population in the Gunnison Basin is recognised as a separate species, the Gunnison sage-grouse.
