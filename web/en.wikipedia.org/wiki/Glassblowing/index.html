Glassblowing

Glassblowing is a glass forming technique that involves inflating molten glass into a bubble (or parison) with the aid of a blowpipe (or blow tube). A person who blows glass is called a glassblower, glassmith, or gaffer. The technique was invented in the Syro-Palestinian region in the mid-first century BC and quickly spread throughout the Roman world, revolutionizing glass production by making vessels cheap and widely available.

Process

The glassblower gathers molten glass from a furnace (typically held at about 1,100 °C) on the end of the blowpipe, rolling it on a steel table called a marver to shape and cool the skin. Air blown through the pipe creates a bubble; the gather may be built up in layers, shaped with wet newspaper pads, wooden blocks, and tools such as jacks and shears, and reheated in a second furnace called the glory hole to keep it workable. Finished pieces are transferred to a punty rod, detached, and placed in an annealing oven (lehr) to cool slowly and relieve internal stress.

Techniques and traditions

Glass can be worked free-blown or blown into moulds to impart pattern and form. Famous traditions include the glasshouses of Murano near Venice, Swedish glass from Småland, and the American studio glass movement founded in the 1960s by Harvey Littleton and Dominick Labino, which took glassblowing out of factories and into artists' studios. Related lampworking techniques shape glass in the flame of a torch rather than at a furnace.
