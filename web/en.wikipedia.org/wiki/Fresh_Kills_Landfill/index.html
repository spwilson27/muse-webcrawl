The Fresh Kills Landfill was a landfill covering 2,200 acres (890 ha) in the borough of Staten Island in New York City. Opened in 1948, it grew to become the largest landfill in the world, at its peak receiving some 29,000 tons of residential waste per day, and its mounds rising higher than the Statue of Liberty.

History

The site took its name from Fresh Kills, the tidal creek (from the Dutch kill, a channel) running through the salt marshes on Staten Island's western shore. City planner Robert Moses proposed the landfill as a temporary fill that would prepare the land for later development. Instead it operated for over half a century as New York City's principal refuse facility, becoming a symbol of the city's waste problems and a source of odours and pollution complaints from Staten Island residents.

Closure and reuse

Under political pressure, the city agreed to close the landfill, and the last barge of household waste arrived on 22 March 2001. The site was temporarily reopened after the September 11 attacks to receive and process debris from the World Trade Center, which was sifted for human remains and evidence. Since closure the landfill has been capped and is being transformed into Freshkills Park, a large public park with hills, waterways and recreation areas, scheduled to open in phases.
