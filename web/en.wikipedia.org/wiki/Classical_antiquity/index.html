Classical antiquity

Classical antiquity, also the classical era, classical period, classical age, or simply antiquity, is the period of cultural European history between the 8th century BC and the 5th century AD comprising the interwoven civilisations of ancient Greece and ancient Rome, known together as the Greco-Roman world, which played a major role in shaping the Mediterranean world, having deeply influenced language, politics, law, education, philosophy, science, warfare, poetry, architecture and art across Europe and beyond.

Periods

The era is conventionally dated from the earliest recorded Greek poetry of Homer (8th–7th century BC) and the founding of Rome (traditionally 753 BC) to the fall of the Western Roman Empire in AD 476, or, in a broader treatment, into late antiquity (to c. AD 600). It is usually subdivided as follows:

  • Archaic Greece (c. 800–480 BC) — the rise of the polis, Greek colonisation, lyric poetry, and early philosophy.
  • Classical Greece (c. 480–323 BC) — Athenian democracy, the Persian and Peloponnesian Wars, the tragedians, Plato and Aristotle.
  • The Hellenistic period (323–31 BC) — the kingdoms founded by Alexander the Great's successors, Alexandria as a centre of learning, and the spread of Greek culture across the Near East.
  • The Roman Republic (509–27 BC) — Rome's conquest of Italy and then of the Mediterranean world.
  • The Roman Empire (27 BC – AD 476 in the West) — the Principate and Dominate, Roman law, engineering, and the gradual Christianisation of the empire.

Legacy

The literature, philosophy, political concepts, legal codes, and artistic canons of classical antiquity were preserved and transmitted by the Byzantine Empire, the Islamic world, and the monasteries of the Latin West, and were revived self-consciously in the Renaissance. The term "classical" itself reflects the Renaissance judgment that Greek and Roman culture set the standard (Latin classicus, "of the highest class") for subsequent achievement. Modern fields from classics and archaeology to political theory and architecture continue to engage with its texts, languages (Greek and Latin), and material remains.
