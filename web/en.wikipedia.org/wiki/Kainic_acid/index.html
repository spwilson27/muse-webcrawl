Kainic acid, or kainate, is an acid found naturally in some seaweed. It is a potent neuroexcitatory amino acid agonist that acts by activating receptors for glutamate, the principal excitatory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system. Kainate receptors are so called because kainic acid is their selective agonist.

History and source

Kainic acid was originally isolated from the red alga Digenea simplex (in Japanese, kaininsō), which had been used in Japan as an anthelmintic—a folk remedy for intestinal worms, particularly ascariasis—for over a thousand years. The compound was characterised in the 1950s.

Pharmacology

Kainic acid is a rigid structural analogue of glutamate. It activates kainate-type ionotropic glutamate receptors (GluK1–5) and, at higher concentrations, AMPA receptors. Excessive activation produces excitotoxicity: seizures and selective neuronal death, particularly in the hippocampus.

Research use

In neuroscience, kainic acid is widely used experimentally: systemic or intracerebral injection in rodents produces status epilepticus and hippocampal damage used as a model of temporal lobe epilepsy, and focal injection is used to produce excitotoxic lesions to study neuronal death. Together with domoic acid (a related kainoid), it helped define the kainate receptor class.

Uses

Kainic acid is no longer used clinically as an anthelmintic in most countries, owing to its neurotoxicity, but remains an important pharmacological tool.
