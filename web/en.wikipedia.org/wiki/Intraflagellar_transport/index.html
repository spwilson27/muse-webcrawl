Intraflagellar transport


IFT trains carry cargo along the microtubules of the cilium.

Intraflagellar transport (IFT) is the bidirectional movement of protein complexes — called IFT particles or trains — along the axonemal microtubules of cilia and flagella. It is essential for the assembly and maintenance of these organelles, because cilia cannot synthesise proteins and must import all their components from the cell body.

Mechanism

Anterograde transport, from the base to the tip, is driven by the kinesin-2 motor and carries IFT complex B proteins together with axonemal precursors and signalling molecules. Retrograde transport, returning material to the base, is driven by cytoplasmic dynein-2 and involves IFT complex A. At the tip the trains are remodelled and change direction.

Discovery

The process was first observed in the flagella of the green alga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii by Joel Rosenbaum and colleagues in the early 1990s, using video-enhanced differential interference contrast microscopy, and was subsequently characterised genetically through temperature-sensitive mutants with short flagella.

Clinical significance

Mutations in IFT genes cause a group of disorders known as ciliopathies, including Bardet–Biedl syndrome, polycystic kidney disease, retinitis pigmentosa and skeletal dysplasias. IFT is also required for Hedgehog signalling in vertebrates, which depends on primary cilia.
