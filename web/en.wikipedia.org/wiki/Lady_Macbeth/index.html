Lady Macbeth

Lady Macbeth is a leading character in William Shakespeare's tragedy Macbeth (c. 1603–1607). The wife of the play's protagonist, Macbeth, a Scottish nobleman, she goads her husband into murdering King Duncan to seize the throne — famously questioning his manhood when he hesitates — and later descends into guilt, madness, and death. She is one of Shakespeare's most celebrated female roles.

Role in the play

Reading Macbeth's letter about the witches' prophecy, Lady Macbeth resolves that Duncan must die and calls on spirits to "unsex me here" and fill her with cruelty. When Macbeth wavers, her taunts drive him to the murder; afterwards she returns the daggers he has brought away and smears the grooms with blood. In Act 3 she presides uneasily as queen while Macbeth spirals into tyranny without her involvement. In Act 5 she appears sleepwalking, compulsively rubbing her hands — "Out, damned spot!" — and confessing fragments of the crimes before dying offstage, probably by suicide. Malcolm's closing epithet calls her Macbeth's "fiend-like queen."

Historical basis and analysis

The historical Macbeth's wife was Gruoch ingen Boite, a descendant of Kenneth III; little is known of her, and Shakespeare's ruthless queen derives largely from Holinshed's Chronicles. Critics have debated her character for centuries: Sarah Siddons made the role famous in the 18th century, and feminist readings variously interpret her as a monster of ambition, a figure crushed by the patriarchal bargain she strikes, or the play's true tragic consciousness. Her line about having "given suck" has fueled speculation about the Macbeths' lost children.

In other works

The archetype echoes in works such as Nikolai Leskov's novella Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk District (1865), adapted by Shostakovich into an opera, and in the phrase's modern use for a ruthless, ambitious woman.
