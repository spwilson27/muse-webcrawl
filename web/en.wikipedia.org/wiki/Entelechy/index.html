Entelechy

Entelechy (Greek entelécheia) is a central concept in the philosophy of Aristotle, denoting the realisation or full actuality of what was present only potentially — a thing's having its end (telos) within itself. Aristotle coined the word by fusing en (in), telos (end, completion) and echein (to have). Entelechy is closely paired with his concept of energeia (actuality, being-at-work) in contrast to dynamis (potentiality).

Aristotle

In the Physics and Metaphysics, Aristotle explains change as the actualisation of potential: an acorn becomes an oak, a block of marble a statue. In De Anima he defines the soul (psyche) as "the first entelechy of a natural body that has life potentially in it" — that is, the soul is the form and actuality of a living body, as sight is the actuality of the eye. "First" entelechy is the possessed capacity (as knowledge one is not currently using), while "second" entelechy is its active exercise.

Later uses

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz adopted the term for his monads, calling each soul-like monad an entelechy because it unfolds its states from an internal principle. In the early twentieth century the embryologist Hans Driesch revived "entelechy" as the name of a non-material, goal-directed life force that he believed guided development, making the word a flagship term of vitalism. Modern biology rejected Driesch's vital force, but "entelechy" survives in philosophy of biology discussions of teleology and in literary criticism, where it can describe a work's achieved, realised form.
