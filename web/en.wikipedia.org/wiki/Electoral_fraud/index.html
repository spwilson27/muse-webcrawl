Electoral fraud

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Electoral fraud, sometimes referred to as election manipulation, voter fraud or vote rigging, involves illegal interference with the process of an election, either by increasing the vote share of a favoured candidate, depressing the turnout or vote share of rivals, or both. It differs from electoral malpractice and from legal (if sharp-elbowed) manipulation such as gerrymandering in that it violates election law.

Methods

Catalogued techniques include voter impersonation and multiple voting; ballot stuffing and ballot-box swapping; falsified counts and protocols; vote buying and treating; misuse of state resources, media and administrative machinery ("administrative resource"); suppression of opposition voters through registration obstacles, purges, intimidation or violence; manipulation of postal and proxy votes; and, increasingly alleged, cyber interference with registration systems and information environments. Countermeasures include voter identification debates, indelible ink, transparent counting with party agents and observers, parallel vote tabulation, election courts and international monitoring missions (OSCE/ODIHR, EU, Carter Center), whose reports are the standard evidence in disputes.

Scale and politics

Fraud's prevalence varies enormously; authoritarian "elections by acclamation" industrialise it (implausible turnouts and margins in some post-Soviet and other states are statistical signatures), while in established democracies proven in-person fraud is rare and litigation over rules — not ballot-stuffing — dominates, though disputed elections (the US in 2000 and 2020, Kenya 2007, many others) show allegations themselves can destabilise politics regardless of scale. The scientific literature studies fraud forensically (digit tests, turnout distributions) and institutionally (independent electoral commissions as prevention).
