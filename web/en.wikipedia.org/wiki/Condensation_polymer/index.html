Condensation polymer

A condensation polymer is a polymer formed by a condensation reaction between monomer units, in which a small molecule — most commonly water, but also methanol, hydrogen chloride, or other simple compounds — is eliminated as the monomers join together. This contrasts with addition polymers, which form without the loss of any atoms from the reacting monomers.

Characteristics

Condensation polymerization is typically a step-growth polymerization: chains of any length can react with one another, and high molecular weights are achieved only at very high conversions, as described by the Carothers equation. Because a byproduct is expelled at each linkage, the repeat unit of the polymer contains fewer atoms than the sum of the monomers that formed it. Most condensation polymers are built from bifunctional monomers bearing groups such as carboxylic acids, alcohols, or amines; using monomers with functionality greater than two produces branched or cross-linked networks, as in Bakelite and other thermosetting resins.

Examples

Important classes of condensation polymers include:

  • Polyamides such as nylon, formed from diamines and dicarboxylic acids (e.g. nylon 6,6 from hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid, with loss of water).
  • Polyesters such as polyethylene terephthalate (PET), formed from terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol.
  • Polycarbonates, formed from bisphenol A and phosgene or diphenyl carbonate.
  • Polyurethanes, formed from diisocyanates and polyols (technically an addition of functional groups, but classed historically with condensation polymers because no small molecule is lost yet the mechanism is step-growth).
  • Phenol–formaldehyde resins and urea–formaldehyde resins, among the earliest synthetic plastics.
  • Natural polymers such as polysaccharides, which form by condensation of sugars with loss of water at each glycosidic bond, and polypeptides, which form similarly at each peptide bond.
