New Public Management

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

New Public Management (NPM) is a management approach used by governments from the 1980s to modernise the public sector. Associated with the reforms of Thatcher's Britain, New Zealand's state-sector reforms and the US “reinventing government” movement (Osborne and Gaebler, 1992), it imports private-sector techniques into administration: separating policy-making from service delivery (executive agencies), competition and contracting-out, performance targets and measurement, output budgeting, customer charters, and managing public services at arm's length from political control.

Christopher Hood's classic 1991 analysis codified NPM's doctrines (hands-on professional management; explicit standards; output controls; disaggregation; competition; private-sector styles; discipline in resource use). The results are contested: efficiency gains and clearer accountability in some services; target-gaming, fragmentation, loss of institutional capacity and blurred accountability in others. Post-NPM agendas (joined-up government, digital-era governance, public value) keep many of its instruments while trying to restore coordination and capacity — and the debate framed modern civil-service reform across the OECD.
