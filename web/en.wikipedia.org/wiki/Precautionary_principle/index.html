Precautionary principle

The precautionary principle (or precautionary approach) is a strategy for approaching issues of potential harm when extensive scientific knowledge is lacking. It emphasises caution, pausing and review before leaping into innovations that may prove disastrous. Critics argue that it is vague, self-cancelling, unscientific and an obstacle to progress.

Formulation

The best-known statement of the principle appears in Principle 15 of the 1992 Rio Declaration on Environment and Development:

"In order to protect the environment, the precautionary approach shall be widely applied by States according to their capabilities. Where there are threats of serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason for postponing cost-effective measures to prevent environmental degradation."

Stronger and weaker versions exist. Weak versions permit preventive action under uncertainty when costs are reasonable; strong versions shift the burden of proof so that proponents of a new technology or activity must demonstrate its safety before proceeding. The principle has roots in the German environmental policy concept of Vorsorgeprinzip ("foresight principle") developed in the 1970s.

Applications

The precautionary principle is embedded in European Union law — the Maastricht Treaty adopted it for environmental policy and it underlies EU food-safety regulation (including the creation of the European Food Safety Authority after the BSE crisis), chemicals regulation (REACH), and pesticide policy. It also appears in international agreements such as the Montreal Protocol, the Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants, and the Cartagena Protocol on Biosafety.

Criticism

Critics, including the legal scholar Cass Sunstein, contend that strong forms of the principle are incoherent or paralysing: because every course of action — including inaction — carries risks, the principle taken literally forbids everything, including the precautions themselves. Others note that it can be invoked selectively to justify trade protectionism or to block beneficial technologies such as genetically modified crops or nuclear power, and that it diverts attention from comparing actual risks and benefits. Defenders respond that carefully designed versions, requiring plausible threats of serious harm and proportionate responses, avoid these problems.
