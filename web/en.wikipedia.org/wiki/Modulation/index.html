Modulation

Modulation
FieldTelecommunications, signal processing
Main typesAmplitude (AM), frequency (FM), phase (PM); digital: ASK, FSK, PSK, QAM
Inverse processDemodulation

In electronics and telecommunications, modulation is the process of varying one or more properties of a periodic waveform, called the carrier signal, with a separate signal called the modulation signal that typically contains information to be transmitted. For example, the modulation signal might be an audio signal representing sound from a microphone, a video signal representing moving images from a camera, or a digital signal representing a sequence of binary digits. The receiver recovers the original information by demodulation; a device that performs both functions is a modem.

Why modulate?

Modulation allows signals to be transmitted efficiently over a medium. Antennas radiate efficiently only when their size is comparable to the wavelength of the signal, so low-frequency information (such as voice) is shifted onto a high-frequency carrier. Modulation also enables frequency-division multiplexing — many signals sharing one channel at different carrier frequencies — and lets engineers trade bandwidth for noise immunity.

Analog modulation

In amplitude modulation (AM), the amplitude of the carrier varies with the message; AM is used in medium-wave broadcasting and aviation radio. In frequency modulation (FM), the carrier's instantaneous frequency varies; developed by Edwin Armstrong in the 1930s, FM offers much better noise performance and is used for high-fidelity broadcasting. Phase modulation (PM) varies the carrier's phase and is closely related to FM. Single-sideband (SSB) modulation, a refinement of AM, transmits only one sideband and is used in amateur radio and military communications.

Digital modulation

Digital systems encode bits onto a carrier using schemes such as amplitude-shift keying (ASK), frequency-shift keying (FSK), phase-shift keying (PSK) and quadrature amplitude modulation (QAM), in which both amplitude and phase carry information. Constellation diagrams represent the symbol states of these schemes. Higher-order QAM (e.g. 256-QAM, 1024-QAM) underlies cable television, Wi-Fi and 4G/5G mobile networks, while orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) — used in Wi-Fi, LTE, 5G and digital broadcasting — splits data across many narrowband subcarriers, each modulated separately.
