Women's colleges

Women's colleges are institutions of higher education whose student body is composed primarily or exclusively of women. They emerged in the 19th century in the United States, Britain, and elsewhere at a time when established universities excluded women entirely, and they played a central role in the expansion of women's education and professional life.

History

Early American women's colleges included Mount Holyoke (founded 1837 as a seminary, later a college), Vassar (1861), Wellesley (1870), Smith (1871), and Bryn Mawr (1885); together with Barnard and Radcliffe, several of these formed the informal group known as the "Seven Sisters", the women's counterparts of the Ivy League. In England, Girton (1869) and Newnham (1871) pioneered women's admission to Cambridge-level study, while Oxford's first women's colleges, Lady Margaret Hall and Somerville, opened in 1879. In Japan, institutions such as Tsuda University (1900) played a similar pioneering role.

Decline and persistence

As formerly male universities became coeducational from the 1960s onward, the number of women's colleges fell sharply — in the United States from more than 200 in the 1960s to only a few dozen today. Supporters argue that women's colleges still produce disproportionate numbers of women leaders, scientists, and PhD recipients, and offer leadership opportunities not diluted by mixed settings. Several institutions, notably Mills College and others, have closed or merged in the 21st century, while others have debated admitting transgender students and adopted inclusive admission policies.
