The Charter of Medina (Arabic: صحيفة المدينة, Ṣaḥīfat al-Madīna), also known as the Constitution of Medina, was a document drawn up after Muhammad's arrival in Medina (the Hijrah, 622 CE) that established the basis of the first Muslim community (ummah) and regulated relations among Medina's tribes, including its Jewish clans.

Content

Preserved in the biography of Muhammad by Ibn Ishaq (via Ibn Hisham) and in Abu Ubayd's Kitab al-Amwal, the charter declares the signatories "one community" and sets out mutual obligations: collective payment of blood money, ransoming of prisoners, joint defence of Medina, freedom of religion for the Jewish tribes as allies of the believers, and Muhammad as arbiter of disputes. Many scholars regard it as one of the earliest written constitutions, though debates continue over whether it is a single document or a composite of agreements from different years.

Significance

The charter is frequently cited in discussions of religious pluralism, minority rights, and constitutionalism in early Islam.
