The Polish–Soviet War (1919–1921) was an armed conflict between Poland, seeking to establish its eastern borders and restore its influence in Ukraine and Belarus, and Soviet Russia, whose Red Army sought to carry revolution westward into Europe. The war's decisive moment, the Polish victory at the Battle of Warsaw in August 1920 — the "Miracle on the Vistula" — halted the Soviet advance and secured Polish independence for a generation.

Background

Poland regained independence in November 1918 after 123 years of partition. Its leader, Józef Piłsudski, envisioned a federation (Międzymorze) of states between the Baltic and Black Seas as a bulwark against Russia. As German forces withdrew from the east at the end of the First World War, Polish and Soviet troops moved into the vacuum and clashed from early 1919.

Course of the war

In April 1920 Poland, allied with Symon Petliura's Ukrainian People's Republic, launched the Kiev offensive and briefly took the Ukrainian capital. The Soviet counteroffensive under Mikhail Tukhachevsky drove the Red Army to the gates of Warsaw by August. Piłsudski's risky counterstroke from the Wieprz River on 16 August struck the Soviet flank and rear, turning the advance into a rout; further Polish victories at Komarów and the Niemen followed. An armistice was signed in October 1920.

Treaty of Riga and legacy

The Treaty of Riga (18 March 1921) divided Ukraine and Belarus between Poland and the Soviet republics, leaving millions of Ukrainians and Belarusians inside Poland — and Piłsudski's federation project unrealized. The war fixed Poland's eastern frontier until 1939 and left a legacy of mutual hostility that shadowed Polish–Soviet relations through the Second World War, including the fate of Polish prisoners in 1920 and the Katyń massacre of 1940.
