Dynamical billiards
FieldDynamical systems; ergodic theory
Key figuresGeorge Birkhoff; Yakov Sinai; Leonid Bunimovich
Famous examplesSinai billiard, stadium billiard, elliptical billiard

A dynamical billiard is a dynamical system in which a particle moves freely in straight lines inside a bounded region and reflects elastically off the boundary, with the angle of incidence equal to the angle of reflection. Billiards are a central model system in ergodic theory because simple changes to the shape of the table produce behaviour ranging from complete integrability to strong chaos.

Examples

Billiards in a rectangle or an ellipse are integrable: in the ellipse, trajectories remain tangent to a fixed confocal conic, and the motion is regular. Birkhoff introduced the mathematical study of billiards in the 1920s. Yakov Sinai showed in 1970 that a billiard with convex obstacles (the Sinai billiard, or Lorentz gas) is ergodic and strongly chaotic, because collisions with convex walls disperse nearby trajectories. Leonid Bunimovich proved that the "stadium" — a rectangle capped by semicircles — is also ergodic despite having focusing boundary parts, owing to the defocusing mechanism.

Mathematical theory

The dynamics are usually studied through the billiard map, which records the position and outgoing angle at each collision. Questions include ergodicity, mixing, periodic orbits, and the spectral theory of the corresponding quantum billiards, where the shape of a domain governs the eigenvalues of the Laplacian — the setting of the famous question "Can one hear the shape of a drum?"

Applications

Billiard models appear in statistical mechanics (the Lorentz gas model of diffusion), optics and acoustics, mesoscopic physics, and the study of microwave cavities, where chaotic versus integrable shapes produce measurably different spectra.
