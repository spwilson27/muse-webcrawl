The 1936–1939 Arab revolt in Palestine was a popular uprising by Palestinian Arabs in Mandatory Palestine against British colonial rule and mass Jewish immigration. The revolt, the largest of its kind during the Mandate period, began with a six-month general strike in 1936 and evolved into an armed insurgency that at its height in 1938 controlled much of the rural countryside.

Causes

The revolt was driven by Arab opposition to Jewish immigration, which had surged following the Nazi rise to power in Germany — over 60,000 Jewish immigrants arrived in 1935 alone — and to land purchases that displaced Arab tenant farmers. The death of the guerrilla preacher Izz ad-Din al-Qassam at the hands of British police in November 1935 made him a martyr and helped galvanise unrest. In April 1936 violence erupted in Jaffa and elsewhere, and the newly formed Arab Higher Committee under Haj Amin al-Husseini called a general strike demanding an end to Jewish immigration, a ban on land sales to Jews, and the establishment of a national government.

Course of the revolt

The first phase (1936) centred on the strike and urban unrest; Britain responded by appointing the Peel Commission, which in 1937 recommended partitioning Palestine into Jewish and Arab states — the first official partition proposal. The revolt resumed violently in autumn 1937 after Britain moved to deport the Arab Higher Committee's leaders and al-Husseini fled to Lebanon. During 1938 rebel bands seized towns such as Beersheba and briefly the Old City of Jerusalem. Britain deployed more than 20,000 troops, imposed martial law, built the Tegart frontier wall and forts, and used air power; Jewish auxiliary forces, including Orde Wingate's Special Night Squads, aided the counterinsurgency.

Outcome

By 1939 the revolt had been militarily crushed: an estimated 5,000 Arabs were killed and many more wounded, imprisoned, or exiled, and Palestinian society was left leaderless and weakened on the eve of the 1948 war. Politically, however, Britain issued the White Paper of 1939, which sharply limited Jewish immigration and land purchases and envisioned an independent, majority-Arab Palestinian state within ten years — a policy adopted partly to secure Arab goodwill as war with Germany loomed.
