Open-question argument

The open-question argument is a philosophical argument advanced by the British philosopher G. E. Moore in his 1903 work Principia Ethica. It is intended to show that "good" cannot be defined in terms of any natural property — and, by extension, to challenge ethical naturalism.

The argument

Moore proposed that for any proposed definition of good — say, identifying good with pleasure, or with what we desire to desire — one can always intelligibly ask of something possessing that property, "It has that property, but is it good?" The question remains significantly open, whereas if the definition were correct, asking it would be like asking "It is good, but is it good?" Moore concluded that good is a simple, indefinable, non-natural property, and that attempts to define it commit what he called the naturalistic fallacy.

Criticism and influence

The argument has been enormously influential in metaethics but widely criticized. Critics observe that a question can seem open even when an identity is true — a point illustrated by the "paradox of analysis" — as with questions about whether water is H2O before one knows the relevant facts. Defenders of naturalism argue that moral identities could be synthetic rather than analytic truths, so Moore's test would not refute them. Frankena and others questioned whether the naturalistic fallacy is really a fallacy at all.

Despite these objections, the open-question argument set the agenda for twentieth-century metaethics and remains a starting point for debates between naturalists, non-naturalists, and non-cognitivists.
