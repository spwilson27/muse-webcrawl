The Sacred Band (Greek: Ἱερὸς Λόχος, Ieros Lochos) of 1821 was a military corps of Greek volunteer students and expatriates formed during the opening phase of the Greek War of Independence. Organised under Alexander Ypsilantis, the leader of the secret revolutionary society Filiki Eteria, it was modelled on the famous Sacred Band of Thebes of antiquity — a fact consciously evoked in its name.

Formation

When Ypsilantis crossed the Prut River from Russia into Moldavia in February–March 1821 to launch the Greek uprising in the Danubian Principalities, he raised the Sacred Band from young Greeks studying in Odessa, Vienna, Paris and other European cities — sons of merchants and scholars who volunteered as an elite, idealistic corps. The men wore black uniforms (said to symbolise mourning for enslaved Greece) with a cap bearing a skull-and-crossbones over the motto "Freedom or Death". Their banner bore, on one side, the phoenix rising from ashes with the words "From my ashes I am reborn", and on the other a cross with a laurel wreath and the inscription "In this sign conquer".

Destruction at Dragashani

Ypsilantis's campaign faltered as the expected Russian support failed to materialise and local support was limited. On 19 June 1821, at the Battle of Dragashani in Wallachia, the Sacred Band — fighting virtually unsupported after allied cavalry fled — made a heroic stand against overwhelming Ottoman cavalry. Roughly 375 of its members were killed; a remnant was saved by the intervention of a detachment under Georgakis Olympios. The corps ceased to exist as a fighting unit, but its sacrifice became one of the founding heroic myths of the Greek revolution.

Legacy

The members of the Sacred Band are commemorated in Greece as the "Hierolochites", symbols of youthful self-sacrifice for national liberation. Their stand at Dragashani entered Greek national memory alongside the parallel sacrifice of Athanasios Diakos and the Souliot women at Zalongo.
