Galician-Portuguese

Galician-Portuguese (Galician-Portuguese: galego-portugués), also known as Old Portuguese or Medieval Galician, was the common Romance language of the northwest of the Iberian Peninsula during the Middle Ages. It is the common ancestor of modern Galician and Portuguese.

Origins

The language evolved from the Vulgar Latin spoken in the Roman province of Gallaecia, roughly the territory of modern Galicia, northern Portugal and adjacent areas. By the 9th century it had acquired distinctive features separating it from neighbouring Leonese and Castilian varieties, including the loss of intervocalic -n- and -l- (Latin luna > lua, "moon") and the development of nasal vowels.

The lyric tradition

Between the late 12th and the mid-14th centuries Galician-Portuguese was the prestige language of lyric poetry throughout Christian Iberia — even the Castilian king Alfonso X composed his Cantigas de Santa Maria in it. Some 1,680 secular songs (cantigas) survive, in the genres of cantigas de amor, cantigas de amigo and cantigas de escarnio e maldizer, preserved in songbooks such as the Cancioneiro da Ajuda.

Divergence

From the 15th century onward the varieties north and south of the Minho river began to diverge. In Galicia the language fell under Castilian influence during the so-called Séculos Escuros ("Dark Centuries"), while in independent Portugal the language was standardised, expanded overseas and eventually became modern Portuguese. The Fala language of the Jálama valley in Extremadura is a surviving descendant of Galician-Portuguese.

Reintegrationism

Today, the reintegrationist movement in Galicia argues that Galician and Portuguese remain one language and advocates convergence of Galician orthography and usage with Portuguese.
