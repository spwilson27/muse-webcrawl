Delta blues is one of the earliest recorded styles of blues music. It originated among African-American communities in the Mississippi Delta — the flat alluvial plain between the Mississippi and Yazoo rivers, in the northwest of the state of Mississippi — in the early twentieth century. The style is characterised by intense solo performance: a singer accompanying himself on acoustic guitar (often using a bottleneck slide and open tunings) and harmonica, with rhythmically free phrasing, stark lyrics and a driving, percussive guitar style.

Delta blues exerted an outsized influence on later popular music. Its records were comparatively scarce in its own time, but through the Great Migration its players carried the music to Chicago, where it electrified into Chicago blues, and from there shaped rhythm and blues and rock and roll.

Origins

The Delta in the post-Reconstruction era was a cotton economy of plantations and sharecropping, with a Black majority population and notorious prison farms such as Parchman. Music grew out of field hollers, work songs, spirituals and dance music played at juke joints and house parties. Guitarists such as Charley Patton, based around Dockery Plantation, synthesised these sources into a recognisable style by the 1910s–20s.

Style

Delta performances typically use a twelve-bar blues structure loosely — singers stretch or drop bars to follow the vocal line. Open tunings (such as open G, "Spanish", and open D, "Vestapol") facilitate the bottleneck slide, played with a knife, bottleneck or metal tube, producing sustained, vocal-like lines. Lyrics dwell on travel, trains, love and betrayal, hard labour, prison, death and the supernatural — most famously in Robert Johnson's crossroads myth, elaborated from his songs "Cross Road Blues" and "Me and the Devil Blues".

Principal artists

  • Charley Patton (c. 1891–1934) — "Founder of the Delta Blues"; recorded from 1929 for Paramount.
  • Son House (1902–1988) — raw, emotional slide playing; a mentor to Robert Johnson and Muddy Waters.
  • Robert Johnson (1911–1938) — recorded only 29 songs (1936–37); canonised by the 1961 reissue King of the Delta Blues Singers.
  • Tommy Johnson, Skip James (whose eerie falsetto and minor-key guitar came from Bentonia), Bukka White, Fred McDowell and Mississippi John Hurt (from the hill country nearby) round out the classic roster.

Recording and rediscovery

Talent scouts for labels such as Paramount, Vocalion and Victor recorded Delta musicians from the late 1920s until the Depression collapsed the market. Alan Lomax's Library of Congress field trips in 1941–42 recorded a young McKinley Morganfield — soon to be Muddy Waters — at Stovall Plantation. In the 1960s folk-blues revival, researchers relocated Son House, Skip James and Mississippi John Hurt, who performed at Newport and on college circuits; John Fahey, Dick Waterman and Gayle Dean Wardlow were among the key "blues detectives".

Legacy

Muddy Waters and Howlin' Wolf transplanted the Delta sound to Chicago and electrified it; from there it fed directly into the Rolling Stones, Led Zeppelin, Eric Clapton and much of rock music. The Mississippi Blues Trail now marks sites across the Delta, and towns such as Clarksdale have built a music-heritage economy around the style.
