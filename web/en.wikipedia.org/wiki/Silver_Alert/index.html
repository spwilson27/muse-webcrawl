A Silver Alert is a public notification system used in many U.S. states to broadcast information about missing persons — especially seniors with Alzheimer's disease, dementia or other mental disabilities — in order to aid their safe recovery. Modelled on the AMBER Alert system for abducted children, Silver Alerts typically use highway dynamic message signs, broadcast media and wireless alerts to enlist the public's help when an elderly person, often driving, goes missing and is believed to be in danger.

Silver Alerts are commonly displayed on electronic highway signs, especially where the missing senior is believed to be driving.

History and criteria

The first programmes appeared in the mid-2000s; Oklahoma's is often cited among the earliest, and Texas enacted its Silver Alert in 2007. Because the systems are created by state law, the name, eligible ages (commonly 65, sometimes 55 or 60), required medical conditions and activation criteria vary considerably between states. Some states use other names, such as "Missing Endangered Person" advisories, or extend alerts to adults of any age with cognitive disabilities. Typically a local law-enforcement agency must confirm that the person is missing, has an irreversible cognitive impairment, and that sufficient identifying information (often including a vehicle description) exists to make an alert useful.

Related systems

Silver Alerts belong to a family of targeted alert programmes that grew out of the AMBER Alert, including Blue Alerts (for suspects who have injured or killed officers), Ashanti Alerts (for missing adults 18–64), and state-level "missing Indigenous person" alerts. Advocates credit the alerts with many safe recoveries; critics note the risk of alert fatigue and, since there is no single national Silver Alert statute in the United States, coverage gaps between states.
