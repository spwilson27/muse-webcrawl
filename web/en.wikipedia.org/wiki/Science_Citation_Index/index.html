Science Citation Index

The Science Citation Index (SCI) is a citation index originally produced by the Institute for Scientific Information (ISI) and created by Eugene Garfield. It was officially launched in 1964 and is now owned by Clarivate (previously the Intellectual Property and Science business of Thomson Reuters). The larger version, the Science Citation Index Expanded (SCIE), covers more than 9,200 notable and significant journals, across 178 disciplines, from 1900 to the present. These are alternatively described as the world's leading journals of science and technology, because of a rigorous selection process.

History

Eugene Garfield founded the Institute for Scientific Information in 1960. The Science Citation Index was first published in 1964, covering the literature of 1963 across some 700 journals. Garfield's idea, first outlined in a 1955 paper in Science, was that by tracking citations — the references authors make to earlier work — researchers could navigate the scientific literature by association, moving both forward and backward in time from any given paper.

The index grew steadily: by the 1980s the SCI covered several thousand journals, and it became a central tool in academic libraries. The companion product, the Journal Citation Reports, used SCI data to compute journal impact factors, which became widely used (and widely debated) in academic evaluation.

Coverage and use

The SCIE is available online through the Web of Science platform. It indexes articles, reviews, editorial material and other items from selected peer-reviewed journals in the natural sciences, engineering and medicine. Selection is based on criteria including peer review, editorial quality, timeliness and citation impact.

Researchers use the index to find related literature, trace the development of ideas, and identify who has cited a particular work. Bibliometricians use its citation data for scientometric studies of scientific output, collaboration and influence.

Criticism

The index has been criticised for its bias toward English-language journals, for the regional imbalance of its coverage, and for the distorting effects of impact-factor-based evaluation on scientific publishing practices.
