Standing (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In law, standing or locus standi is a condition that a particular plaintiff must satisfy to bring a lawsuit — a sufficient connection to, and harm from, the matter challenged. In US federal courts, Article III standing requires (1) an injury in fact that is concrete, particularised and actual or imminent; (2) causation traceable to the defendant; and (3) redressability by a favourable decision (Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 1992) — plus prudential and statutory layers (zone of interests, no generalised grievances, third-party-standing limits).

Function and variation

Standing doctrines ration access to courts and, in constitutional systems, allocate power: denying standing for generalised environmental or taxpayer grievances keeps policy in branches answerable to elections — while critics call the doctrine a manipulable merits-avoidance device (compare Massachusetts v. EPA's "special solicitude" for states, 2007). Other systems swing wider: India's public interest litigation virtually abolished standing barriers for rights claims (with docket consequences); the UK's "sufficient interest" test liberalised progressively; EU law's restrictive Plaumann standing for challenging Union acts is the counter-model, critiqued by its own courts' advocates general. Climate litigation worldwide is, in large part, a standing fight — who may sue, for whose future injury, against whom.
