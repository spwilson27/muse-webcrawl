Music of Tunisia

The music of Tunisia is dominated by ma'luf (or malouf), the Tunisian branch of the Andalusi classical tradition inherited from Muslim Spain, alongside folk, Sufi, and modern popular styles.

Ma'luf

Tunisian ma'luf consists of suites called nuba (Tunisian: nauba), each built on a melodic mode (tab') and combining vocal and instrumental movements. The tradition was codified and preserved by the Rashidiyya Institute, founded in 1934, named after the composer Mohamed Rachid Bey. The national La Rachidia ensemble remains its principal guardian. Instruments include oud, rabab, nay, qanun, violin, and darbuka.

Folk and Sufi music

Folk music of the countryside includes Bedouin styles accompanied by the gasba flute and bendir frame drum. Stambeli is an Afro-Tunisian healing and trance ritual music of the Gnawa-related tradition centred on the guembri lute and qraqeb metal castanets, honoured at the Dar Barnu shrine in Tunis. Sufi brotherhoods perform devotional dhikr chanting.

Popular music

The 20th century produced stars such as singer Saliha, Jewish-Tunisian singer Habiba Msika, and Cheikh El Afrit. The mezoued, a bagpipe-driven popular genre, produced stars like Hedi Habbouba. Modern Tunisian artists range from oud virtuoso and composer Anouar Brahem, whose jazz-inflected work is internationally acclaimed, to singer Emel Mathlouthi, whose song "Kelmti Horra" became an anthem of the 2011 Tunisian revolution.
