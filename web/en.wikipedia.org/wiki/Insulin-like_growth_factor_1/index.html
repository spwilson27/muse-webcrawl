Insulin-like growth factor 1

Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), also called somatomedin C, is a hormone similar in molecular structure to insulin which plays an important role in childhood growth and has anabolic effects in adults. It is a protein encoded in humans by the IGF1 gene on chromosome 12. IGF-1 consists of 70 amino acids in a single chain with three intramolecular disulfide bridges and a molecular weight of about 7,649 daltons. It is produced primarily by the liver in response to growth hormone (GH) and is a primary mediator of GH's effects on growth.

Function and regulation

Growth hormone released from the pituitary gland stimulates the liver (and many other tissues) to synthesise and secrete IGF-1. IGF-1 then acts in endocrine, paracrine and autocrine fashion, binding the IGF-1 receptor — a tyrosine kinase receptor similar to the insulin receptor — to activate PI3K/AKT and MAPK signalling, promoting cell growth, proliferation and survival and inhibiting apoptosis. In the circulation more than 99% of IGF-1 is bound to IGF-binding proteins (especially IGFBP-3 with an acid-labile subunit), which prolong its half-life and regulate its availability. IGF-1 exerts negative feedback on GH secretion. Levels rise through childhood, peak at puberty, and decline with age; they also depend strongly on nutrition.

Clinical significance

Deficiency of IGF-1 causes growth failure: in Laron syndrome, GH receptor defects make the body unresponsive to growth hormone, producing severe short stature despite high GH levels. Recombinant IGF-1 (mecasermin) is approved for treating severe primary IGF-1 deficiency. Conversely, excess GH/IGF-1 causes gigantism in children and acromegaly in adults, and serum IGF-1 is the standard screening test for acromegaly. Epidemiological studies have associated high-normal IGF-1 levels with increased risk of some cancers (including prostate, breast and colorectal), though causation is debated. IGF-1 is also misused as a performance-enhancing drug and is prohibited in sport. Research in model organisms links reduced IGF-1 signalling to extended lifespan, a finding whose relevance to human ageing remains contested.

History

In 1957 Salmon and Daughaday showed that GH's growth effects were mediated by a serum "sulphation factor"; renamed somatomedin, it was purified and sequenced in the 1970s by Rinderknecht and Humbel, who recognised its homology with proinsulin and coined "insulin-like growth factor".
