Recursion theory

Recursion theory, also called computability theory, is a branch of mathematical logic, computer science and philosophy originating in the 1930s, studying computable functions and Turing degrees — the classification of problems by relative computability.

Recursion theory
Field: Mathematical logic, theoretical CS
Founders: Gödel, Church, Turing, Kleene, Post
Key concepts: Recursive functions, halting problem, Turing degrees, arithmetical hierarchy

Origins

Hilbert's Entscheidungsproblem prompted formal models of computation: Alonzo Church's lambda calculus, Alan Turing's machines (1936), Gödel–Herbrand general recursive functions, and Emil Post's systems — all proving equivalent, supporting the Church–Turing thesis. Turing showed the halting problem is undecidable.

Areas

Topics include recursive and recursively enumerable sets, the arithmetical and analytical hierarchies, degree structures (Post's problem, priority arguments), Kolmogorov complexity, effective descriptive set theory, and higher recursion theory. Applications span logic, complexity and philosophy of mind.

Terminology

"Recursive" (older) has largely been replaced by "computable" in modern usage, but the field's name persists.
