Liber Studiorum

The Liber Studiorum (Latin for "Book of Studies") is a collection of prints by the English artist J. M. W. Turner, produced between 1807 and 1819, intended to demonstrate the range and principles of landscape painting. It consisted of 71 prints in the original series, divided into fourteen parts, each containing five plates, together with a frontispiece. Turner etched the outlines of the compositions himself and employed professional engravers, including William Say, Charles Turner, and Robert Dunkarton, to work up the plates in mezzotint and aquatint.

Background and purpose

The project was conceived partly in response to the Liber Veritatis ("Book of Truth") of the seventeenth-century French landscape painter Claude Lorrain, who recorded his compositions in drawings to authenticate his works. Turner, who became Professor of Perspective at the Royal Academy in 1807, intended his book as an educational statement of the types and principles of landscape art.

Categories

The prints were organised into categories, indicated by letters on each plate: P for Pastoral, M for Mountainous, H for Historical, A for Architectural, EP for Elevated Pastoral, and Marine. Many of the images were based on Turner's own paintings and watercolours, while others were drawn from his tours in Britain and on the Continent.

Later history

Publication ceased in 1819, though Turner continued to make plates that were never published in his lifetime. Later editions were printed from the worn plates in the later nineteenth century, notably under the direction of Sir Frank Short in the 1940s. The Liber Studiorum is regarded as one of the most important print series in British art, and complete sets are held by major museums, including Tate Britain.
