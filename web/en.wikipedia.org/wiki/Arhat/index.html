Arhat

In Buddhism, an arhat (Sanskrit: अर्हत् arhat; Pali: arahant) is one who has gained insight into the true nature of existence and has achieved nirvana and liberation from the endless cycle of rebirth (saṃsāra). The arhat, having freed himself from the bonds of desire, will not be reborn. The term is translated as "worthy one" or "perfected one".

A luohan (arhat) statue, one of the Eighteen Arhats venerated in Chinese Buddhism.

In early Buddhism and Theravada

In the Pali Canon, the arahant is the final stage of the four stages of awakening. An arahant has eradicated the ten fetters and the defilements (greed, hatred, and delusion), and upon death attains parinibbana, final nirvana, without further rebirth. The Buddha himself is often described in the early texts as an arahant, as are his enlightened disciples. In Theravada Buddhism, arahantship remains the stated goal of monastic practice.

In Mahayana Buddhism

Mahayana traditions honour the arhat but regard the bodhisattva ideal — remaining in the world to liberate all beings — as a higher aspiration; arhats are sometimes criticised in Mahayana sutras for a supposedly self-regarding liberation. Nevertheless, groups of arhats, particularly the Sixteen Arhats (and in China, the Eighteen Arhats or Luohans), became popular subjects of devotion and art, believed to remain in the world to protect the Dharma until the coming of Maitreya.

In Jainism

The term arihant is also central to Jainism, where it denotes a soul that has conquered inner passions such as anger, pride, deceit, and greed and attained omniscience (kevala jnana) while still embodied. The Namokar Mantra begins with salutations to the arihants.
