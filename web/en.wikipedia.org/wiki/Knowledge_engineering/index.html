Knowledge engineering

Knowledge engineers elicit expertise from human specialists and encode it in a knowledge base.

Knowledge engineering is a field of artificial intelligence (AI) that creates and maintains knowledge-based systems, including the processes of eliciting knowledge from human experts, representing it in formal structures, and engineering the reasoning mechanisms that use it. The term was coined around 1977 by Edward Feigenbaum, a pioneer of expert systems. Its best-known products were the expert systems of the 1970s and 1980s, such as MYCIN (medical diagnosis) and DENDRAL (chemical analysis), which encoded specialists' rules of thumb as IF–THEN rules.

Process

Classical knowledge engineering involves: identifying a suitable problem; knowledge acquisition (interviews, protocol analysis, and observation of experts); conceptualisation and formalisation into a representation such as rules, frames, semantic networks, or an ontology; implementation in a shell or language (e.g. CLIPS, Prolog); and testing and refinement. Because experts often cannot articulate their tacit skill directly — the "knowledge acquisition bottleneck" — elicitation is the hardest and most time-consuming stage.

Methodologies

Structured methodologies such as CommonKADS organise knowledge engineering around models of expertise, distinguishing task, inference, and domain knowledge and separating problem-solving methods from domain content. Ontology engineering — the design of shared conceptualisations, using languages such as OWL — grew out of this tradition and underpins the Semantic Web and modern knowledge graphs.

Relation to modern AI

The expert-systems boom faded in the late 1980s (the "AI winter") as maintenance costs and brittleness became apparent, and machine learning shifted emphasis from hand-coded knowledge to data-driven models. Knowledge engineering persists in ontology design, knowledge graphs, decision-support systems, and neuro-symbolic AI, and in curation of structured resources such as Wikidata.
