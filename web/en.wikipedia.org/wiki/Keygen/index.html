Keygen

A key generator, often shortened to keygen, is a computer program that generates a valid product key or serial number for a piece of software, allowing it to be activated without a legitimate licence. Keygens are created by cracking groups who reverse-engineer the key-validation algorithm of the target program. Their use to activate software without payment is illegal in most jurisdictions as a circumvention of copy protection and a violation of software licensing terms.

How keygens work

Offline activation schemes check a user-supplied key against an internal algorithm — for example, a checksum, a mathematical relationship between characters, or a cryptographic verification using an embedded public key. Cracking groups disassemble the software, recover the algorithm, and write a small generator that produces keys the program will accept. Simple algorithms can be replicated exactly; stronger schemes may require extracting secrets embedded in the program.

History and culture

Keygens emerged with commercial software serial numbers in the 1980s and flourished in the 1990s and 2000s within the warez and demoscene subcultures. Many keygens were themselves miniature artworks, featuring pixel graphics, scrolling text and chiptune music, produced by groups such as Razor 1911 and PARADOX. Because keygens are distributed through untrusted channels, they have frequently been used as a lure for distributing malware.

Countermeasures

Software vendors have responded with online activation, hardware binding, account-based licensing and server-side verification, which make purely offline key generation ineffective. Legal regimes such as the US Digital Millennium Copyright Act prohibit the distribution of circumvention tools, and vendors pursue keygen sites through takedown notices. Nevertheless, mass-generated or leaked volume licence keys ("keygen keys" shared online) remain a persistent problem addressed by key blacklisting.
