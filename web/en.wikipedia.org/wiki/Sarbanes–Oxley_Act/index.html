The Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002, often shortened to SOX, is a United States federal law that sets enhanced standards for the boards, management and public accounting firms of all U.S. public companies. It was enacted in response to a series of major corporate accounting scandals, most prominently those at Enron and WorldCom, which destroyed billions of dollars of investor value.

President George W. Bush signing the Sarbanes–Oxley Act on July 30, 2002.

Major provisions

The Act created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB) to oversee the audits of public companies. It strengthened auditor independence rules, barred accounting firms from providing many non-audit services to audit clients, and required chief executives and chief financial officers to personally certify the accuracy of financial reports (Section 302). Its best-known requirement, Section 404, obliges management and external auditors to assess and report on the effectiveness of a company's internal controls over financial reporting. The Act also imposed stiffer criminal penalties for securities fraud and obstruction of investigations, and protections for whistleblowers.

Debate and impact

Supporters credit SOX with restoring investor confidence and improving the quality of financial reporting after the scandals of 2001–2002. Critics argue that its compliance costs, particularly under Section 404, fall disproportionately on smaller public companies and may discourage firms from listing in the United States. Some requirements were later softened for smaller issuers by the JOBS Act of 2012 and subsequent regulatory relief.
