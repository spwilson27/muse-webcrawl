Michel Foucault (1926–1984) was a French philosopher, historian of ideas and social theorist whose work examined the relationships between power, knowledge and the formation of subjects. He held the chair in the History of Systems of Thought at the Collège de France from 1970 until his death.

Work

Foucault's early "archaeologies" — Madness and Civilization (1961), The Birth of the Clinic (1963) and The Order of Things (1966) — studied how madness, medicine and the human sciences were constituted in particular historical periods ("epistemes"). His later "genealogies", above all Discipline and Punish (1975), analysed modern power as disciplinary: exercised through prisons, schools, hospitals and surveillance — the panopticon serving as emblem — rather than merely repressive. The History of Sexuality (first volume 1976) argued against the "repressive hypothesis" and treated sexuality as produced by discourse; later volumes turned to ancient Greek and Roman ethics and the "care of the self".

Influence and criticism

Foucault's concepts — biopower, governmentality, power/knowledge, the author-function — permeate history, sociology, literary studies, gender studies and critical theory. Critics have challenged his historical accuracy, his apparent relativism about truth, and the political implications of his account of power. He was also a public activist, co-founding the Groupe d'information sur les prisons in 1971. He died in Paris in 1984 of complications from AIDS, among the first prominent French figures whose death was associated with the disease.
