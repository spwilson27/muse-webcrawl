Rocky Mountain Fur Company

Trappers at a Rocky Mountain rendezvous

The Rocky Mountain Fur Company, sometimes called Ashley's Hundred, was an American fur trading company organized in St. Louis, Missouri in 1822 by William Henry Ashley and Andrew Henry. They placed a famous advertisement in St. Louis newspapers seeking "enterprising young men" to ascend the Missouri River to its source and trap beaver in the Rocky Mountains — a venture that launched the career of the Rocky Mountain mountain man.

History

The company's early expeditions met disaster: in 1823 the Arikara attacked Ashley's party on the Missouri, killing several men and forcing a military response in the Arikara War. In response, the company shifted strategy, sending trappers overland and relying on independent mountain men rather than large river expeditions. In 1824 a party under Jedediah Smith rediscovered South Pass, the broad, easy crossing of the Continental Divide that would later carry the Oregon Trail.

The rendezvous system

In 1825 Ashley instituted the rendezvous system: instead of maintaining fixed posts, the company held an annual summer gathering at a prearranged site in the mountains, where trappers exchanged a year's furs for supplies brought out from St. Louis. That year Ashley sold his interest to Jedediah Smith, David Jackson, and William Sublette. The reorganized firm of Smith, Jackson & Sublette operated until 1830, when it was sold to Thomas Fitzpatrick, Milton Sublette, Jim Bridger, Henry Fraeb, and Jean Baptiste Gervais, who continued under the Rocky Mountain Fur Company name until the partnership dissolved in 1834 amid competition from John Jacob Astor's American Fur Company.

Legacy

The company's trappers — among them Jedediah Smith, Jim Bridger, Thomas Fitzpatrick, Hugh Glass, and James Beckwourth — explored much of the American West, opening routes later used by emigrants and contributing greatly to geographic knowledge of the Rockies, the Great Basin and California.
