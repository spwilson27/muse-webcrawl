Tilapia

Tilapia is the common name for nearly a hundred species of cichlid fish belonging to the genera Oreochromis, Sarotherodon, Tilapia, and related groups. Native primarily to freshwater habitats in Africa and the Levant, tilapias have been introduced around the world and have become among the most important fish in aquaculture and inland fisheries.

Characteristics

Tilapias are laterally compressed, deep-bodied fish with long dorsal fins. Most species are herbivorous or omnivorous, feeding on algae, detritus, and plankton, which makes them cheap to feed in captivity. Many are mouthbrooders: the female (in Oreochromis) carries the eggs and fry in her mouth until they are large enough to survive independently. They tolerate poor water quality, crowding, and a wide range of temperatures, though they cannot survive prolonged cold below about 10 °C.

Aquaculture and cuisine

The Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) accounts for the great majority of farmed production, led by China, Egypt, Indonesia, the Philippines, and Bangladesh. Because tilapia is inexpensive, mild-flavoured, and easy to fillet, consumption grew sharply in the United States and Europe from the 1990s onward; it is sometimes marketed as "aquatic chicken". Ancient Egyptians raised tilapia in ponds over four thousand years ago, and the fish appears in Egyptian tomb art as a symbol of rebirth.

Invasive species

Escaped or deliberately released tilapia have established feral populations in warm waters on every continent except Antarctica. In Australia, Mozambique tilapia is a declared pest blamed for displacing native fish; in Lake Victoria and elsewhere introduced species have contributed to major ecological changes. Their hardiness and rapid breeding make control difficult once established.
