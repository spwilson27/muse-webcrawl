Diorite is an intrusive igneous rock formed by the slow cooling underground of magma (molten rock) rich in silica and low in alkali metal oxides. It has a coarse, grainy structure and is intermediate in composition between mafic gabbro and felsic granite. Chemically and mineralogically, diorite corresponds roughly to the volcanic rock andesite.

Composition

Diorite consists principally of plagioclase feldspar (typically oligoclase or andesine), hornblende, and biotite, with smaller amounts of pyroxene and little or no quartz. Its speckled black-and-white appearance gives it a "salt and pepper" look. Rocks with more than about 20% quartz are classified as quartz diorite or tonalite; varieties rich in mafic minerals grade into gabbro.

Occurrence and use

Diorite forms in plutons, dikes, and stocks above subduction zones, and occurs in many mountain belts including the Andes, the Scottish Highlands, and parts of New England. Because it is hard and durable, ancient peoples — notably the Egyptians — carved it into statues and vessels; the famous Code of Hammurabi stele is carved from diorite (often described as such, though sometimes identified as basalt). Today diorite is used as crushed stone, paving, and ornamental "black granite" in commerce.
