Grand piano

A grand piano is a type of piano in which the frame and strings are horizontal, with the strings extending away from the keyboard. It is distinguished from the upright piano, in which the strings are vertical. Grand pianos range in length from about 1.5 metres (baby grands) to concert grands of around 3 metres (9 feet 10 inches), such as the Steinway Model D.

Construction

The grand piano's wing-shaped case houses a cast-iron frame over which steel strings are stretched at high tension; bass strings are wound with copper. The soundboard, usually spruce, amplifies the strings' vibration. The lid can be propped open to project the sound toward an audience. The action, refined from Bartolomeo Cristofori's original design of around 1700 and by the double-escapement action of Sébastien Érard (1821), allows rapid repetition of notes. Most grands have three pedals: damper (sustain), sostenuto, and soft (una corda).

History

Cristofori built the first pianos in Florence around 1700. Through the nineteenth century, makers including Steinway & Sons, Bösendorfer, Bechstein, and Blüthner developed the modern concert grand, with a full iron frame and cross-stringing enabling greater volume and brilliance. Grand pianos are the standard instrument for concert performance and advanced study, while upright pianos are more common in homes.
