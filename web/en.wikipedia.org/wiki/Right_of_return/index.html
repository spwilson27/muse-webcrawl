Right of return

Right of return
FieldInternational human rights law; nationality law
Key instrumentsUniversal Declaration of Human Rights, Art. 13(2); ICCPR, Art. 12(4)
Related rightsFreedom of movement; right to a nationality

The right of return is a principle in international law that guarantees everyone's right of voluntary return to, or re-entry to, their country of origin or of nationality. It is closely related to the constitutional prohibition of exile and is enshrined in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (Article 13: "Everyone has the right to leave any country, including his own, and to return to his country") and in Article 12(4) of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which provides that no one shall be arbitrarily deprived of the right to enter his own country.

Legal foundations

The right of return is recognised in regional instruments (for example, the Fourth Protocol to the European Convention on Human Rights and the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights) and in the nationality laws of many states, which typically make a citizen's right of entry absolute and non-derogable. It may also arise for non-citizens with strong ties to a country, and in specific historical claims, most prominently the Palestinian right of return asserted by Palestinian refugees and their descendants since 1948, in reference to United Nations General Assembly Resolution 194. Other notable cases include the jus sanguinis-based return laws for ethnic diasporas (such as Germany's former Aussiedler provisions, Greece, Armenia, and South Korea) and Israel's Law of Return (1950), which grants Jews worldwide the right to immigrate and acquire citizenship.

Scope and disputes

Debates over the right of return concern its beneficiaries (original refugees only, or also descendants), its application to property restitution versus compensation, and its interplay with the right of self-determination of existing populations. Post-conflict settlements — e.g., Dayton's Annex 7 for Bosnia and Herzegovina — have operationalised return as a peace-building measure, with varying success. The right of return is distinct from, though sometimes overlaps with, asylum and the principle of non-refoulement.
