Tholos tomb

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The corbelled dome of a tholos tomb

A tholos tomb (plural tholoi; from Greek θόλος, "round building") is a burial structure characterised by a circular chamber roofed with a corbelled vault, giving it a beehive shape. The type is best known from the Mycenaean world of Late Bronze Age Greece, though related forms occur elsewhere in the Mediterranean.

Anatomy

A typical Mycenaean tholos consists of the round burial chamber itself, approached by a long open passage (dromos) cut into a hillside, with a formal doorway (stomion) often framed by dressed stone and capped by a massive lintel. The vault was built of rings of stone, each course projecting slightly inward, and the whole structure was covered by an earthen mound.

Examples

The grandest example is the Treasury of Atreus at Mycenae (c. 1250 BC), also called the Tomb of Agamemnon: its chamber is about 14.5 metres in diameter and 13 metres high, and it remained the largest dome in the world for well over a thousand years. Other notable groups of tholoi stand at Mycenae, Orchomenos (the so-called Treasury of Minyas), Pylos and in Messenia. Round tombs of earlier and possibly ancestral form are known from the Mesara plain of Crete, and tholos burials also appear in Bronze Age Iberia.

Interpretation

Tholoi were prestige burials for ruling families, used for successive interments over generations. Their scale and engineering display the wealth and organisation of the Mycenaean palace states in their final centuries.
