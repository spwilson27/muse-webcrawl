Singara (Greek: τὰ Σίγγαρα) was a strongly fortified post at the northern extremity of Mesopotamia, which commanded the eastern road to the Tigris and the western plain. Located near the foot of the Sinjar mountains, in the vicinity of the modern Iraqi town of Sinjar, it was repeatedly contested between the Roman Empire and Sasanian Persia during the third and fourth centuries CE.

History

Singara first rose to prominence during the Roman–Parthian conflicts; the emperor Trajan captured it during his Mesopotamian campaign of 114–117 CE, and it later formed part of the Roman frontier defence system (the limes) of the province of Mesopotamia. It served as a base for the legion I Parthica. The city was wealthy enough to be raised to the status of a Roman colony under Septimius Severus or his successors.

During the Persian wars of the fourth century, Singara was the scene of the Battle of Singara (344 CE), a hard-fought night battle between Constantius II and Shapur II which ended inconclusively with heavy losses. In 360 CE Shapur II captured and largely destroyed the city after a celebrated siege described by Ammianus Marcellinus, deporting its inhabitants; the emperor Jovian formally ceded Singara to Persia in the treaty of 363 CE, after which it faded from Roman control.

Identification and remains

The site is generally placed near the modern town of Sinjar, whose name preserves the ancient one. Surface remains are scant, and the ancient city has been little excavated, in part because of the region's modern conflicts.
