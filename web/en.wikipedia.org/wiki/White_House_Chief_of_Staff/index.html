White House Chief of Staff

The White House chief of staff is the highest-ranking member of the Executive Office of the President of the United States and a senior aide to the president. The chief of staff manages the White House staff, controls access to the president, oversees the flow of information and decision-making, and negotiates with Congress and cabinet departments. The role is often described as the second most powerful position in Washington.

History

The position evolved from the president's private secretary. Franklin D. Roosevelt formalized a White House staff system; in 1946 the post of Assistant to the President was created, and under Dwight D. Eisenhower — who imported military staff practice — Sherman Adams became the first to hold the title "Chief of Staff" in 1953. The modern, powerful chief of staff model was cemented in the Nixon and Carter eras, and James Baker under Ronald Reagan is often cited as the gold standard of the job.

Duties

  • Selecting and supervising key White House staff
  • Structuring the president's schedule and controlling access to the Oval Office
  • Managing the flow of paper and policy advice to the president
  • Protecting the president's political interests and negotiating with Congress, the executive departments, and outside groups
  • Crisis management and directing the implementation of presidential decisions

The chief of staff is appointed by and serves at the pleasure of the president, without Senate confirmation. The average tenure is a little over two years; Andrew Card, under George W. Bush, served nearly five and a half years, among the longest.
