The Treaty of Limerick was signed on 3 October 1691, ending the Williamite War in Ireland between the Jacobite supporters of the deposed James II and the forces of William III. The Jacobite commander Patrick Sarsfield negotiated the surrender of Limerick after its second siege, following the decisive Williamite victory at Aughrim.

Articles

The treaty had military and civil articles. The military articles allowed the Jacobite soldiers to march out with the honours of war and to choose either submission to William or transport to France to enter French service; some 12,000–14,000 sailed — the "Flight of the Wild Geese" — and Irish regiments in French service became a fixture of 18th-century European warfare. The civil articles promised Irish Catholics freedom of worship "consistent with the laws of Ireland", protection of the estates of those who submitted, and a general pardon.

The "broken treaty"

Within a few years the Protestant-dominated Irish Parliament repudiated the civil articles' spirit, enacting the Penal Laws restricting Catholic worship, education, landholding and political rights. In Irish nationalist memory Limerick is consequently "the city of the broken treaty", and the Treaty Stone on the Shannon's bank remains its symbol.
