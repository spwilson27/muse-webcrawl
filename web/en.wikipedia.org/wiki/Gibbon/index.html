Gibbon

Gibbons are apes in the family Hylobatidae. The family historically contained one genus, but is now split into four genera and about 20 species based on chromosomal and molecular evidence. Gibbons live in subtropical and tropical forests from eastern Bangladesh to Southeast Asia and southern China. Also called the lesser apes or small apes, gibbons differ from the great apes (chimpanzees, bonobos, gorillas, orangutans and humans) in being smaller, exhibiting low sexual dimorphism, and generally forming long-term pair bonds. Their principal mode of locomotion, brachiation, allows them to swing through the canopy at speeds up to 55 km/h (34 mph), leaping distances of up to 8 metres (26 ft).

Anatomy and locomotion

Gibbons are masters of brachiation: their extremely long arms, hook-like hands, short thumbs and highly mobile ball-and-socket wrist and shoulder joints let them move hand-over-hand beneath branches with a pendulum-like gait, covering ground faster in the trees than most animals on land. They are tailless, as are all apes, walk bipedally with raised arms when on the ground or on large limbs, and have dense fur ranging from pale blond to black depending on species and sex. The siamang (Symphalangus syndactylus), the largest gibbon at up to about 14 kg, has an inflatable throat sac used in calling.

Behaviour and song

Gibbons live in small family groups of a mated pair and their offspring, defending a territory with spectacular vocal displays. Pairs sing coordinated duets — among the most complex primate vocalisations — which reinforce the pair bond and advertise occupation of the territory. Their diet is dominated by ripe fruit, especially figs, supplemented by leaves and invertebrates. Unusually among primates, pair-bonding and biparental territory defence structure gibbon society.

Conservation

Nearly all gibbon species are endangered or critically endangered, threatened by deforestation for palm oil and logging, hunting for food and the pet trade, and capture for traditional medicine. The Hainan gibbon (Nomascus hainanus) is one of the world's rarest primates, with only a few dozen individuals remaining on Hainan Island, China.
