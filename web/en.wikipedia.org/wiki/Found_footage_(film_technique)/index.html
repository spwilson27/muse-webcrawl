Found footage (film technique)

Found footage is a cinematic technique and film genre in which all or a substantial part of the film is presented as if it were recordings discovered afterwards — typically shot by the characters themselves with handheld cameras, surveillance equipment or camcorders. It is most strongly associated with horror cinema, where the shaky, first-person style is used to create immediacy and realism on a low budget.

History

The technique has antecedents in films such as Cannibal Holocaust (1980), which framed its story around recovered film reels. The breakthrough came with The Blair Witch Project (1999), made for a tiny budget, marketed as genuine recovered footage, and grossing nearly $250 million worldwide. It was followed by hits including Paranormal Activity (2007), [REC] (2007), Cloverfield (2008) and Chronicle (2012), which extended the style into science fiction and superhero genres.

Characteristics and criticism

Typical features include amateur-looking camerawork, naturalistic acting, minimal music, and an in-story justification for continued filming. Critics have praised the genre's immersion while criticising contrived camera logic and overuse of jump scares; some viewers also report motion sickness from the unstable camera. The term is shared with the distinct experimental practice of found footage filmmaking that reuses existing archival material.
