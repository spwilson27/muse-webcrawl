Rockefeller Center is a large complex of commercial buildings in Midtown Manhattan, New York City, built by the Rockefeller family during the Great Depression. Conceived in 1928 as a new home for the Metropolitan Opera, the project was re-imagined as a commercial centre after the 1929 crash, when John D. Rockefeller Jr. decided to proceed alone — the largest private building project ever undertaken in modern times, employing some 40,000 workers.

Description

The original complex comprises 14 Art Deco buildings (1931–1939). Its centrepiece is 30 Rockefeller Plaza ("30 Rock"), a 69-storey limestone slab, home to NBC Studios and the Rainbow Room, with the Top of the Rock observation deck at its summit. The sunken Lower Plaza hosts the famous ice rink (opened 1936) and Paul Manship's gilded Prometheus statue (1934); each winter it holds the Rockefeller Center Christmas Tree, a tradition dating to 1933. Other elements include Radio City Music Hall (1932), the Channel Gardens, the International Building, and extensive public art — murals, sculpture, and mosaics in the Moderne style.

History

The land was leased from Columbia University. Diego Rivera's 1933 mural Man at the Crossroads in the RCA Building lobby was destroyed because it included a portrait of Lenin — one of the era's great art controversies. During the Second World War the complex was complete and fully leased; afterwards it expanded west of Sixth Avenue. The complex was declared a National Historic Landmark in 1987.
