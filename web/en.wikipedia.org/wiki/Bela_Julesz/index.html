Bela Julesz (19 February 1928 – 31 December 2003) was a Hungarian-born American visual neuroscientist and experimental psychologist. He is best known for inventing the random-dot stereogram in 1959, a tool that transformed the scientific study of depth perception, and for founding the Laboratory of Vision Research at Rutgers University.

Life and career

Julesz studied electrical engineering in Budapest and earned his doctorate from the Hungarian Academy of Sciences in 1956. After the Hungarian Revolution of 1956 he emigrated to the United States and joined Bell Laboratories in New Jersey, where he headed departments in sensory and perceptual processes. In 1989 he moved to Rutgers University, where he established the Laboratory of Vision Research and remained until his death.

Research

At Bell Labs, Julesz used a computer to generate pairs of images made of random dots that were identical except for a shifted region. Viewed stereoscopically, the shifted region floated vividly in depth even though it was invisible to either eye alone. This demonstrated that stereoscopic depth perception arises in the brain from the matching of the two eyes' images — before, not after, objects are recognized — overturning the assumption that depth is computed from monocular form cues. Julesz called this domain of inquiry "cyclopean perception", after the one-eyed Cyclops, imagining a single "cyclopean eye" in the brain. He later proposed the theory of textons — elementary features such as elongated blobs and line terminators — as the units of preattentive texture discrimination. His books Foundations of Cyclopean Perception (1971) and Dialogues on Perception (1995) summarize his work. He received a MacArthur "genius" Fellowship in 1983.
