Christian socialism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Christian socialism is a religious and political philosophy that blends Christianity and socialism, endorsing socialist political and economic principles on the basis of the teachings of Jesus and the Bible. Adherents emphasise the biblical concern for the poor, communal sharing in the early church, and the prophetic critique of wealth and injustice.

[Hero image: portrait of F. D. Maurice, founder of the English Christian socialist movement — image placeholder]

British movement

The term gained currency in Britain in 1848, when the theologian Frederick Denison Maurice, the novelist Charles Kingsley and John Malcolm Ludlow founded the Christian Socialist movement in response to Chartism and industrial poverty. They promoted co-operative associations of working men and working-class education; Maurice helped found the Working Men's College in 1854. Later British figures include Stewart Headlam, who founded the Guild of St Matthew, and the "squire and parson" socialist Conrad Noel.

Elsewhere

In North America, the Social Gospel movement of Walter Rauschenbusch overlapped with Christian socialism, as did the work of Washington Gladden and the Baptist minister George D. Herron. In Latin America, liberation theology drew Christians towards socialist conclusions, while in Britain the tradition fed into the Labour Party through figures such as Keir Hardie and later the Christian Socialist Movement (now Christians on the Left).

Criticism

Christian socialism has been criticised both by conservatives, who hold that socialist economics conflict with Christian teaching on property, and by Marxists, who have viewed religion as an obstacle to class consciousness. Popes from Leo XIII onwards rejected socialist doctrine in encyclicals such as Rerum novarum (1891) while endorsing social reform.
