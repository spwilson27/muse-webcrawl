Attis

Attis
OriginPhrygia (Anatolia)
Consort / counterpartCybele
SymbolsPine tree, Phrygian cap, violets
FestivalMegalesia; March rites (Hilaria)

Attis was a Phrygian deity of vegetation, the consort and attendant of the great mother goddess Cybele. His myth centres on his death and rebirth: in the best-known version, driven mad by the goddess's jealousy, Attis castrated himself beneath a pine tree and died, from whose blood violets sprang; he was subsequently restored to life. He is typically depicted as a young man wearing a Phrygian cap and trousers.

Cult

The cult of Cybele and Attis spread from Anatolia to Greece and, in 204 BC, officially to Rome, where the Megalesia festival was celebrated in April. By the imperial period, an elaborate cycle of rites in March re-enacted Attis's death and resurrection: mourning and fasting (including the Day of Blood, when devotees wounded themselves and some priests, the galli, castrated themselves) gave way to the rejoicing of the Hilaria. The taurobolium, a baptism in bull's blood, was also associated with the cult.

Interpretation

Scholars have classed Attis among the dying-and-rising gods of the ancient Near East and Mediterranean, alongside figures such as Adonis and Osiris, though the antiquity and meaning of his "resurrection" are debated. His cult was one of the last pagan mysteries to flourish in competition with early Christianity.
