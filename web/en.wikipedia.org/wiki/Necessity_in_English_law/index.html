Necessity in English law

In English criminal law, necessity is a defence whereby a defendant argues that they committed an offence in order to avoid a greater harm. English courts have traditionally been reluctant to recognise necessity as a general defence, distinguishing it from the related, narrower defence of duress of circumstances, and have generally held that it is no defence to a charge of murder. Its modern scope has been shaped by a series of restrictive decisions in the 19th and 20th centuries and by the conjoined twins case Re A in 2000.

Historical development

Early commentators were divided: Hale suggested necessity might excuse theft of food in famine, while Blackstone doubted it. In R v Dudley and Stephens (1884), two shipwrecked sailors who killed and ate the cabin boy were convicted of murder; the court held that necessity was no defence to murder, rejecting the argument that killing one to save two could be justified. The decision remains the leading authority that necessity cannot excuse murder in English law.

In R v Martin (1989) and related cases the courts developed "duress of circumstances", where the defendant acts under an imminent threat of death or serious injury arising from the situation rather than from another person's threats — for example driving while disqualified to escape an attacker. The distinction between necessity and duress of circumstances has been criticised as artificial, but the courts have maintained it.

Re A (Children)

In Re A (Children) (Conjoined Twins: Surgical Separation) [2001], the Court of Appeal authorised an operation to separate conjoined twins knowing it would kill the weaker twin, Mary, whose heart and lungs were sustained by her sister Jodie. The court held the operation lawful, reasoning by analogy to necessity and self-defence: Mary was, albeit innocently, killing Jodie, and the surgery was the only way to save Jodie's life. The case is the closest English authority to recognising necessity as a justification for an act certain to cause death.

Scope and limits

  • Necessity is not available as a defence to murder (Dudley and Stephens), subject to the exceptional reasoning in Re A.
  • The threatened harm must be imminent and the response proportionate and the only reasonable course.
  • The defence has been rejected where Parliament has set the balance itself (e.g. R v Quayle [2005], medicinal cannabis).
  • Related statutory and common law doctrines — self-defence, duress, and medical necessity — cover much of the ground in practice.
