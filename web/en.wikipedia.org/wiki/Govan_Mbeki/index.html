Govan Archibald Mvunyelwa Mbeki (9 July 1910 – 30 August 2001) was a South African politician, journalist and intellectual, a senior figure in the African National Congress (ANC) and the South African Communist Party (SACP). He was the father of Thabo Mbeki, who became president of South Africa.

Early life and education

Mbeki was born in the Nqamakwe district of the Transkei, the son of a Mfengu chief's descendant. He attended mission schools including Healdtown, and graduated from the University of Fort Hare with a BA and a teaching diploma (1937). While at Fort Hare he joined the ANC and the Communist Party. He worked as a teacher, journalist and political organiser in the Transkei and Durban, and edited the newspaper Inkundla Ya Bantu.

Anti-apartheid struggle

A leading theorist of the liberation movement, Mbeki wrote the influential study The Peasants' Revolt (1964) about rural resistance in Pondoland and the Transkei, and campaigned against the Bantu Authorities system, being detained and banished to remote districts. After the banning of the ANC in 1960 he went underground and helped organise its armed wing, Umkhonto we Sizwe. Arrested at Liliesleaf Farm in Rivonia in July 1963, he was convicted in the Rivonia Trial of 1963–64 alongside Nelson Mandela, Walter Sisulu and others, and sentenced to life imprisonment, which he served on Robben Island. His wife Epainette "MaMbeki" sustained the family and his political work in the Transkei.

Release and later life

Mbeki was released from Robben Island on 5 November 1987, after 24 years in prison, and played a leading role in the unbanned ANC, serving in its national leadership and on the National Executive Committee. After the first democratic elections in 1994 he became Deputy President of the Senate (later the National Council of Provinces) in the new Parliament, serving until 1997. He received the ANC's highest honour, the Isitwalandwe Award, and the Order of Friendship from several countries. He died in Port Elizabeth in 2001.
