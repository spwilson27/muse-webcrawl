Articular cartilage is the smooth, white tissue that covers the ends of bones where they come together to form joints. It is a type of hyaline cartilage that allows the bones to glide over each other with very little friction and acts as a shock absorber, distributing loads across the joint surface. Healthy articular cartilage makes painless, efficient movement possible; its deterioration is a hallmark of osteoarthritis.

Structure

Articular cartilage is composed of a dense extracellular matrix produced and maintained by specialized cells called chondrocytes. The matrix consists mainly of water (roughly 65–80% by weight), type II collagen fibres, and proteoglycans such as aggrecan, whose negatively charged glycosaminoglycan chains attract water and give the tissue its resistance to compression. The tissue is organized into zones — superficial (tangential), middle (transitional), deep (radial), and calcified — differing in collagen orientation and cell shape.

Unlike most tissues, articular cartilage contains no blood vessels, nerves, or lymphatics. Chondrocytes obtain nutrients by diffusion from the synovial fluid, a process assisted by the pumping action of joint loading and unloading.

Function

The cartilage provides a nearly frictionless bearing surface (coefficients of friction lower than ice on ice under favourable conditions) and spreads compressive forces over a wide area of subchondral bone, protecting it from damage. Its thickness varies by joint, from about 1 mm in small joints to several millimetres in the knee.

Injury and disease

Because it lacks a blood supply, articular cartilage has very limited capacity for self-repair. Damage may result from trauma, such as a sports injury producing a focal chondral defect, or from degenerative disease. In osteoarthritis, progressive loss of cartilage leads to pain, stiffness, and bone-on-bone contact. Treatments range from physiotherapy and analgesics to surgical procedures such as microfracture, autologous chondrocyte implantation, osteochondral grafting, and, in advanced disease, joint replacement.
