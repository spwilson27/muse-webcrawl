Moral foundations theory

Moral foundations theory is a social psychological theory intended to explain the origins of and variation in human moral reasoning on the basis of innate, modular foundations. It was first proposed by the psychologists Jonathan Haidt, Craig Joseph and others, drawing on the work of cultural anthropologist Richard Shweder. It has been developed by Haidt and Jesse Graham and popularised in Haidt's 2012 book The Righteous Mind.

The foundations

The theory originally proposed five foundations, each conceived as the psychological preparation for reacting to certain patterns in the social world, analogous to taste buds:

  • Care/harm – sensitivity to suffering and neediness, underpinning virtues of kindness and compassion.
  • Fairness/cheating – sensitivity to cooperation and deception, related to justice, rights and reciprocity; later reframed as equality and proportionality.
  • Loyalty/betrayal – related to tribalism, patriotism and group cohesion.
  • Authority/subversion – respect for tradition, hierarchy and legitimate authority.
  • Sanctity/degradation – rooted in disgust psychology, extended to religious and purity concerns.

A sixth foundation, Liberty/oppression – sensitivity to coercion and domination – was subsequently proposed. Researchers have also proposed dividing fairness into equality and proportionality.

Method and findings

The foundations are measured principally with the Moral Foundations Questionnaire. Studies using it report that political liberals tend to rely most heavily on the care and fairness foundations, whereas political conservatives tend to endorse all foundations more evenly, including loyalty, authority and sanctity — a pattern Haidt uses to explain mutual incomprehension between political sides ("the conservative advantage").

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether the foundations are truly innate modules, whether the list is complete or parsimonious, how the foundations relate to Kohlberg's stages of moral development and other models, and whether self-report questionnaires capture actual moral behaviour. The theory nonetheless remains influential in moral psychology and political psychology.
