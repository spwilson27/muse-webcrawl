The ankle, or talocrural joint, is the joint connecting the foot with the leg. It is formed where the tibia and fibula of the leg meet the talus of the foot: the distal ends of the tibia and fibula form a socket (mortise) into which the dome of the talus fits, allowing the foot to move up (dorsiflexion) and down (plantarflexion).

Structure

The bony prominences on either side are the medial malleolus (tibia) and lateral malleolus (fibula); the longer lateral malleolus helps resist sideways displacement. The joint is stabilised medially by the strong fan-shaped deltoid ligament and laterally by three ligaments — the anterior talofibular (ATFL), calcaneofibular (CFL) and posterior talofibular (PTFL). Tendons crossing the ankle include the Achilles tendon (to the calcaneus) and those of tibialis anterior and the peroneal muscles; the posterior tibial artery and tibial nerve pass behind the medial malleolus.

Clinical significance

The ankle is the most commonly injured major joint. Lateral ankle sprains — usually inversion injuries damaging the ATFL — are extremely common in sport. Fractures of the malleoli occur with rotational trauma and may need surgical fixation. Other conditions include ankle osteoarthritis (often post-traumatic), Achilles tendinopathy, gout, and instability following repeated sprains. Assessment tools such as the Ottawa ankle rules guide whether X-ray is needed after injury.
