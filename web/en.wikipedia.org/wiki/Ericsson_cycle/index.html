Ericsson cycle

The Ericsson cycle is a thermodynamic cycle named after the Swedish-American inventor John Ericsson, who designed several heat engines based on variations of it. The ideal Ericsson cycle consists of four reversible processes: isothermal compression, isobaric heat addition (in a regenerator), isothermal expansion, and isobaric heat rejection (in the regenerator).

The cycle

The four stages are:

  1. Isothermal compression — the working gas is compressed at constant low temperature, rejecting heat.
  2. Isobaric heating — the compressed gas passes through a regenerator at constant pressure, absorbing heat stored in the regenerator matrix.
  3. Isothermal expansion — the hot gas expands at constant high temperature, absorbing heat from the source and producing work.
  4. Isobaric cooling — the gas passes back through the regenerator at constant pressure, depositing heat for the next cycle.

With perfect regeneration, the only external heat exchanges occur during the two isothermal processes, so the thermal efficiency of the ideal Ericsson cycle equals that of the Carnot cycle operating between the same temperatures: η = 1 − Tcold/Thot. Like the closely related Stirling cycle — which uses two isochores instead of two isobars — the Ericsson cycle is a fully reversible regenerative cycle in the ideal limit.

History and applications

Ericsson built hot-air "caloric" engines from the 1820s onward, including the large caloric ship engine of the USS Ericsson (1852) and many small pumping engines sold later in the century. Practical Ericsson-cycle machines were hindered by the difficulty of building regenerators and heaters able to withstand continuous high temperatures. The cycle has attracted renewed interest for closed-cycle gas turbines, solar thermal power, and micro combined-heat-and-power systems, where external combustion and a gaseous working fluid are advantageous.
