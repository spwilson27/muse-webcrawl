Rømer's determination of the speed of light

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rømer's determination of the speed of light in 1676 was the first quantitative measurement of the speed of light. The Danish astronomer Ole Rømer, working at the Paris Observatory, inferred from observations of eclipses of Jupiter's moon Io that light travels at a finite speed, and estimated the time light takes to cross the diameter of Earth's orbit.

Background

Whether light travelled instantaneously or with a finite speed had been debated since antiquity; Galileo had attempted, inconclusively, to detect a delay with lanterns on hilltops. Giovanni Domenico Cassini, Rømer's superior in Paris, had compiled tables of the eclipses of Io intended to serve as a celestial clock for determining longitude.

The observation

Rømer noticed that the intervals between successive eclipses of Io varied systematically through the year: eclipses occurred progressively later than predicted when Earth was moving away from Jupiter, and earlier when Earth approached Jupiter. He correctly explained this by the changing distance the light had to travel. In September 1676 he dramatically predicted that an eclipse of Io on 9 November would occur about ten minutes later than expected — which it did.

The estimate

Rømer concluded that light takes roughly 22 minutes to cross the diameter of Earth's orbit (the modern value is about 16.7 minutes). Christiaan Huygens, using Rømer's data and the contemporary estimate of the astronomical unit, computed a speed of light of about 220,000 kilometres per second — remarkably close to the modern value of 299,792 km/s, given the data available. Rømer himself never published a numerical speed; his achievement was to demonstrate that the speed was finite and to give its approximate scale.

Reception

The result was initially contested, including by Cassini, but was accepted by Huygens, Newton and others, and was confirmed observationally in the next century by James Bradley's discovery of the aberration of light (1729), which yielded an independent value.
