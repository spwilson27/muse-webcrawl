Land tenure

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Overview
Field:Property law, land economics
Common forms:Freehold, leasehold, customary tenure, communal tenure, allodial title

In common law systems, land tenure is the legal regime governing the terms and conditions under which land is owned, occupied or used. The word derives from the Latin tenere, "to hold": historically, in feudal England all land was held of the Crown in return for services, and the doctrine of tenure described the relationship between lord and tenant. Modern tenure systems describe the bundle of rights — to occupy, use, transfer, exclude others from, and inherit land — and how those rights are allocated and secured.

Types of tenure

Major forms include freehold (ownership in fee simple, the most complete estate in common-law systems), leasehold (possession for a term under a landlord), customary tenure (rights governed by local custom and community institutions, dominant in much of Africa and the Pacific), communal tenure, state ownership and allocation, and religious endowments such as the Islamic waqf. In practice most systems mix statutory and customary rules, and informal or unregistered occupation is widespread in rapidly urbanising countries.

Tenure security

The degree to which landholders are protected against arbitrary eviction — tenure security — is a major concern of development policy. Insecure tenure discourages long-term investment in land and housing, while formal titling programmes, championed by economists such as Hernando de Soto, argue that documented rights unlock credit and capital. Critics respond that formalisation can disrupt customary systems and dispossess vulnerable users. The Food and Agriculture Organization's Voluntary Guidelines on Land Tenure (2012) set out internationally endorsed principles for recognising legitimate tenure rights, including those of indigenous peoples and women.
