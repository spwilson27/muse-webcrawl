Earth jurisprudence is a philosophy of law and governance holding that humans are only one part of a wider community of beings, and that the welfare of the whole Earth community should guide human law. The term was coined by cultural historian and "geologian" Thomas Berry (1914–2009), who argued that legal systems treating nature merely as property are a root cause of ecological destruction, and that a new jurisprudence should recognise the inherent rights of all members of the Earth community to exist, thrive and evolve.

Principles

Earth jurisprudence proposes that (1) the universe is the source of "law" in the deepest sense, (2) every component of the Earth community has inherent rights, (3) human law should be embedded in and consistent with the functioning of ecological systems, and (4) governance should cultivate mutually enhancing human–Earth relationships. South African lawyer Cormac Cullinan popularised the approach in his book Wild Law (2002) and drafted documents for the rights-of-nature movement.

Influence

The philosophy underpins the rights of nature movement: Ecuador's 2008 constitution granted rights to nature (Pachamama); Bolivia adopted a Law of the Rights of Mother Earth in 2010; New Zealand's 2017 Te Awa Tupua Act recognised the Whanganui River as a legal person; and courts and legislatures in Colombia, India, Uganda and elsewhere have extended rights or personhood to rivers, ecosystems and species. A Universal Declaration of the Rights of Mother Earth was proposed at the 2010 World People's Conference in Cochabamba. Critics question how such rights are enforced, who speaks for nature, and whether they conflict with human development needs.
