Gran Fury

Gran Fury placed its graphic campaigns in public spaces such as bus stops and billboards.

Gran Fury was an artistic collective active in New York City from 1988 to 1995, formed by members of ACT UP (the AIDS Coalition to Unleash Power). The group produced powerful graphic art, posters, stickers and public installations addressing the AIDS crisis, government inaction and media silence.

Formation and methods

The collective took its name from the Plymouth Gran Fury, a car model used by the New York City Police Department as an undercover vehicle — a reference to the group's tactic of infiltrating mainstream visual culture. Its members, most of them trained in art, design and advertising, worked anonymously and collectively, borrowing the visual language of advertising and mass media to deliver political messages.

Notable works

Among Gran Fury's best-known works are the Silence = Death-era campaigns in ACT UP's orbit, the bus-shelter poster Kissing Doesn't Kill: Greed and Indifference Do (1989), which mimicked Benetton advertising, and Read My Lips (1988), which paired images of kissing couples with slogans commandeering George H. W. Bush's famous phrase. Their work appeared on billboards, bus shelters, T-shirts and in exhibitions, including the 1990 Venice Biennale, where their installation criticised the Catholic Church's position on condoms.

Legacy

Gran Fury disbanded in 1995. The group's archive is held by the New York Public Library, and its work is credited with redefining the possibilities of art as activism during the AIDS epidemic.
