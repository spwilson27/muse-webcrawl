Structure of Earth

The internal structure of Earth is divided into layers by their chemical and physical (rheological) properties. From the outside in, these are the crust, the mantle, the liquid outer core, and the solid inner core. The structure was established primarily by seismology, from the behaviour of seismic waves generated by earthquakes.

Layers

  • Crust: the thin outer shell, 5–70 km thick; oceanic crust is thinner and denser (basaltic) than continental crust (granitic).
  • Mantle: extends to about 2,900 km depth and makes up most of Earth's volume; it is solid silicate rock that convects slowly, driving plate tectonics. The uppermost mantle together with the crust forms the rigid lithosphere, above the weaker asthenosphere.
  • Outer core: a liquid layer of iron and nickel about 2,200 km thick; its motion generates Earth's magnetic field via the dynamo effect.
  • Inner core: a solid sphere of iron–nickel alloy about 1,220 km in radius, with temperatures comparable to the surface of the Sun.

Discontinuities

Sharp boundaries between layers are marked by seismic discontinuities, notably the Mohorovičić discontinuity (crust–mantle boundary) and the Gutenberg discontinuity (mantle–core boundary).
