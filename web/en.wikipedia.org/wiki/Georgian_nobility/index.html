Georgian nobility

Georgian princes in traditional dress, nineteenth century.

The Georgian nobility (Georgian: თავადაზნაურობა, tavadaznaurba) was the privileged estate of feudal Georgia, consisting of hereditary aristocratic families who held land, offices, and military obligations under the Georgian monarchs. Its ranks and customs developed over the medieval period and survived, in adapted form, under Persian, Ottoman, and finally Russian imperial rule.

Ranks and structure

The estate was headed by the great princes: the didebuli ("the great ones") and the princes (tavadi), many of them hereditary governors (eristavi, roughly "dukes") of provinces, together with royal dynasts of the Bagrationi house and its branches. Below them stood the lesser nobility, the aznauri, divided into royal, princely, and church aznauri according to whose vassals they were. The system was intensely hierarchical, with elaborate precedence rules and patterns of patronage binding each rank to those above.

Under the Russian Empire

After Russia annexed the Georgian kingdoms of Kartli-Kakheti (1801) and Imereti (1810), Georgian princely and noble families were progressively integrated into the Russian nobility (dvoryanstvo). Many Bagrationi and other princely families were confirmed in the princely title of the Russian Empire—becoming such families as Bagration, Bagration-Mukhransky, and Bagration-Davydov—while lesser aznauri often struggled to document their status and some lost noble recognition entirely. Land reforms of the 1860s emancipated the serfs and eroded the estate's economic basis.

Abolition and legacy

The Russian Revolution and the Soviet annexation of Georgia in 1921 abolished noble privileges, and many aristocratic families were repressed or emigrated. After Georgian independence in 1991, descendants' associations revived interest in the genealogies and heraldry of the old families, though the nobility has no legal status in the modern republic.
