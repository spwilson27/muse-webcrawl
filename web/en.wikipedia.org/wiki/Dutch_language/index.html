Dutch language

Dutch (endonym: Nederlands) is a West Germanic language spoken mainly in the Netherlands and Belgium, where it is the majority language of Flanders. It is the third most widely spoken Germanic language, after English and German, and the official language of Suriname and several Caribbean territories. Its closest relative is Afrikaans, which developed from Dutch dialects in South Africa and is largely mutually intelligible in written form.

History

Dutch descends from Old Low Franconian (Old Dutch, c. 500–1150), through Middle Dutch (c. 1150–1500) to Modern Dutch. The Statenvertaling Bible of 1637 played a major role in standardization. Dutch vocabulary contributed words to English (skipper, yacht, cookie, boss, landscape) and to many languages through Dutch seafaring and colonial reach in the 17th century.

Grammar and sounds

Dutch has largely lost the Germanic case system, retains two genders in the standard northern language (common and neuter) and three in parts of Flanders, and is known for its guttural consonants, the digraph ij, and extensive diminutives. Verb placement follows the V2 rule in main clauses and verb-final order in subordinate clauses, as in German.

Standardization

The Dutch Language Union (Taalunie), founded in 1980 by the Netherlands and Belgium (with Suriname as an associate member), maintains the official spelling and the standard vocabulary list (the "Green Booklet").
