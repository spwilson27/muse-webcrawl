Roman emperor

The Roman emperor was the ruler and head of state of the Roman Empire. The office developed from the position established by Augustus in 27 BC as the Principate, in which the emperor accumulated republican powers — tribunician authority, supreme military command (imperium) and religious office — while formally preserving the Senate and magistracies. In the later empire (the Dominate, from Diocletian onwards) the emperor ruled as an open autocrat.

Powers and titles

Emperors bore the names and titles Imperator, Caesar and Augustus, controlled the army, provincial appointments, taxation and coinage, and were frequently worshipped as divine after death (and in the eastern provinces during life). Succession was never governed by a fixed hereditary rule: adoption, designation, marriage, military acclamation and assassination all determined who reached the throne, contributing to recurring civil wars.

Eras

Historians divide the imperial office among the Julio-Claudian, Flavian, Nerva–Antonine, Severan and later dynasties, the Crisis of the Third Century, the Tetrarchy, and the Christian empire from Constantine onward. The western line ended in AD 476 with the deposition of Romulus Augustulus, while the eastern (Byzantine) emperors continued in Constantinople until 1453.
