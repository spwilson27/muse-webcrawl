An arrondissement (French pronunciation: [aʁɔ̃dismɑ̃]) is any of various administrative divisions of France, Belgium and certain other francophone countries. Literally "a rounding off", the word derives from the French verb arrondir, "to round off". The general concept should not be confused with the specific municipal arrondissements of Paris.

France

France's 101 departments are subdivided into 332 departmental arrondissements (the number was adjusted by boundary reforms in 2017). Each is administered by a subprefect (sous-préfet) based in a subprefecture, except the arrondissement containing the departmental prefecture, which is run by the prefect's secretary-general. Arrondissements are grouped into cantons for elections and are used for organising state services, but they have no elected assembly of their own and are not legal entities.

Municipal arrondissements

The three largest French cities — Paris, Lyon and Marseille — are divided into municipal arrondissements, which elect their own councils and mayors. Paris has twenty, known in French simply as arrondissements, numbered in a spiral pattern from the centre. Lyon has nine and Marseille sixteen grouped into eight sectors.

Belgium and other countries

Belgium is divided into 43 administrative arrondissements, used for elections and statistics, alongside judicial and electoral variants. In Canada, several Quebec cities, most prominently Montreal, are divided into boroughs officially called arrondissements. The term is also used in Benin, Cameroon, Haiti, Mali, Niger, Senegal, and in the Netherlands historically (spelled arrondissement for judicial districts).
