The Geographical Pivot of History

"The Geographical Pivot of History" is an article by Halford John Mackinder, a British geographer and politician, first presented as a paper to the Royal Geographical Society in London on 25 January 1904 and published in The Geographical Journal that year. In it, Mackinder set out the Heartland theory, one of the foundational ideas of modern geopolitics.

Summary of the argument

Mackinder argued that the coming of the railway age had ended the "Columbian epoch," during which sea powers held the advantage over land powers. He divided the world into three zones: the Pivot Area (later renamed the Heartland), the inaccessible interior of Eurasia drained by rivers flowing to the frozen Arctic or to enclosed seas; the Inner or Marginal Crescent of coastal lands around it; and the Outer or Insular Crescent of islands and the Americas.

Whoever controlled the Heartland, Mackinder contended, could command the resources of the vast Eurasian "World-Island" and, with modern rail transport, move forces faster than sea powers could respond. He saw Russia's position athwart the pivot as the decisive geopolitical fact of the age.

Influence

Mackinder restated and extended the theory in Democratic Ideals and Reality (1919), where he coined the famous dictum: "Who rules East Europe commands the Heartland; who rules the Heartland commands the World-Island; who rules the World-Island commands the World." The pivot idea deeply influenced twentieth-century strategic thought, including German Geopolitik, and was revived during the Cold War to explain containment policy, though critics have faulted it for overemphasising land power and underestimating air power, trade and technology.
