Rye bread
TypeBread
Main ingredientsRye flour, water, sourdough starter or yeast
RegionNorthern, Central, and Eastern Europe

Rye bread is a type of bread made with various proportions of flour from rye grain. It can be light or dark in colour, depending on the type of flour used and the colouring agents added, and is typically denser than bread made from wheat flour. Rye has been a staple grain in the cooler climates of northern, central, and eastern Europe, where it grows in poorer soils and shorter seasons than wheat.

Characteristics

Rye flour contains less gluten than wheat flour and a high proportion of soluble fibre (pentosans), which absorb water and give rye doughs their sticky texture and the finished loaf its moist, dense crumb. Traditional rye breads are often leavened with sourdough: the acidity inhibits the rye amylases that would otherwise break down starches during baking, and gives the bread its characteristic tang and keeping quality.

Varieties

Notable varieties include German Pumpernickel, a very dark, sweet bread made from coarsely ground whole rye and steamed for many hours; Danish rugbrød, a thin-sliced, seeded sourdough loaf that forms the base of smørrebrød; Finnish ruisleipä, often baked as a flat disc with a hole for storage on a pole; and the marble and caraway-seeded rye breads of Jewish delicatessens in North America. Historically, contamination of rye with the ergot fungus caused outbreaks of ergotism ("St Anthony's fire") in communities dependent on rye bread.

Nutrition

Rye bread is richer in dietary fibre than white wheat bread and tends to have a lower glycaemic response. It provides B vitamins, magnesium, and other minerals, and its satiating quality has made it a subject of research into appetite regulation.
