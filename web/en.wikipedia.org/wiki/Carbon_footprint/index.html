Carbon footprint

[Image placeholder: A foot-shaped collage of factories, vehicles and power stations emitting CO₂]

A carbon footprint is the total greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions caused directly and indirectly by an individual, event, organisation, service, place or product, expressed as carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e). It accounts for emissions of carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide and other greenhouse gases across the full life cycle of the activity being measured.

Origins of the concept

The term derives from the ecological footprint concept developed in the 1990s by Mathis Wackernagel and William Rees. It was popularised in the 2000s, including through a large advertising campaign by the oil company BP, which promoted personal carbon footprint calculators — a history that has led some commentators to argue the concept individualises responsibility for emissions.

Measurement

Corporate footprints are commonly organised using the GHG Protocol into three scopes: Scope 1 (direct emissions from owned sources), Scope 2 (indirect emissions from purchased energy), and Scope 3 (all other indirect emissions in the value chain, often the largest category). Product footprints are assessed using life-cycle assessment (LCA) methods.

Per capita footprints

Average per-capita emissions vary widely: the global average is around 4–5 tonnes of CO2 per person per year, while figures for the United States are roughly three times that, and those of many low-income countries far below it.
