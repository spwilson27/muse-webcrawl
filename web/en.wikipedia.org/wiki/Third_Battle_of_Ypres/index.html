The Third Battle of Ypres (31 July – 10 November 1917), commonly called the Battle of Passchendaele, was a major campaign of the First World War, fought by the British and their allies against the German Empire for control of the ridges south and east of the Belgian city of Ypres in West Flanders. It became synonymous with the mud, slaughter and apparent futility of trench warfare.

Background and plan

Field Marshal Sir Douglas Haig sought a breakthrough in Flanders that would liberate the Belgian coast and its U-boat bases, relieve pressure on the French army after the failed Nivelle Offensive and its mutinies, and wear down German reserves. Preliminary operations captured the Messines Ridge in June 1917 with the detonation of enormous mines.

The battle

The main assault opened on 31 July 1917 (the Battle of Pilckem Ridge) after a massive artillery bombardment. Unseasonably heavy rain turned the shelled, low-lying battlefield into a quagmire in which men, horses and guns foundered; tanks bogged down and infantry advanced at walking pace through mud. General Plumer's methodical "bite-and-hold" attacks in September and early October — Menin Road, Polygon Wood and Broodseinde — won limited but real successes. Renewed attacks in the mud in late October, spearheaded by the Canadian Corps, captured the ruined village of Passchendaele by 6–10 November, whereupon Haig closed the offensive.

Legacy

Passchendaele gained a few kilometres of devastated ground at a cost of several hundred thousand casualties on both sides; its strategic value remains debated by historians. The battle — commemorated in Siegfried Sassoon's and Wilfred Owen's poetry and in the vast Tyne Cot Cemetery and the Menin Gate at Ypres — endures as the archetypal image of the Great War's horror.
