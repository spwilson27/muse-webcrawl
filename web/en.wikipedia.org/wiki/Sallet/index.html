Sallet

The sallet (also called celata, salade or Schaller) was a type of war helmet that replaced the bascinet in northern and western Europe during the mid-15th century. Some sallets were part of full head-to-toe plate harness, worn by both infantry and cavalry. They are characterised by a smooth, rounded skull drawn out into a tail at the back that protected the neck, and a single-piece construction often combined with a pivoted visor.

Forms

Two main families are recognised. The Italian sallet (celata) was typically shorter, with a rounded profile and often an articulated or framed visor; Milanese workshops exported them across Europe. The German sallet (Schaller) had a more pronounced, elongated tail and was frequently worn with a separate bevor protecting the chin and throat. Sallets could be worn with a flat or roped comb, and high-quality examples display elegant fluted decoration in the Gothic style. Some were fitted with a bellows-faced visor for foot combat in tournaments.

Use and decline

The sallet afforded excellent protection while allowing the wearer good vision and ventilation when the visor was raised. It remained in use into the early 16th century, when it was gradually supplanted by helmets such as the burgonet and the close helmet, though German sallets influenced armour design for decades. Surviving examples are prized in collections such as the Wallace Collection and the Metropolitan Museum of Art.
