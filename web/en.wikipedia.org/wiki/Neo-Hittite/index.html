The Syro-Hittite states, traditionally called Neo-Hittite states, were a group of Iron Age kingdoms in southeastern Anatolia and northern Syria that flourished from about 1180 BC, after the collapse of the Hittite Empire, until their conquest by the Neo-Assyrian Empire in the late 8th century BC. They combined Luwian-speaking dynasties descended, in some cases, from Hittite royal lines with increasingly Aramaic-speaking populations; modern scholarship prefers "Syro-Hittite" or "Syro-Anatolian" to reflect this mixed character.

History

When the Hittite Great Kingdom collapsed amid the Late Bronze Age collapse (c. 1180 BC), vice-regal lines at Carchemish and Melid survived. Kuzi-Teššub, king of Carchemish and descendant of the Hittite viceroys, even assumed the title "Great King". Over the following centuries the region fragmented into numerous city-states, including Carchemish, Melid (Malatya), Kummuh, Gurgum, Sam'al (Zincirli), Bit-Adini, Patin (later Unqi), Hamath, Lu'aš, and further north the kingdoms of Que (Cilicia) and Tabal.

From the 9th century BC the states faced growing pressure from Assyria. Shalmaneser III campaigned repeatedly against coalitions of Syro-Hittite and Aramaean kings, and the battle of Qarqar (853 BC) — in which a coalition including Hamath confronted the Assyrians — is recorded on the Kurkh Monolith. Through the 8th century BC, under Tiglath-Pileser III, Shalmaneser V and Sargon II, the states were progressively annexed as provinces: Bit-Adini fell in 856 BC, Hamath in 720 BC, and Carchemish, the most prestigious of the states, in 717 BC.

Culture

Syro-Hittite art is best known from monumental stone reliefs (orthostats) lining palace and gate complexes, showing kings, gods, processions and banquet scenes in a distinctive blend of Hittite, Syrian and Assyrian styles. Royal inscriptions were carved in Hieroglyphic Luwian, and increasingly also in Aramaic and Phoenician; the bilingual Karatepe inscription (Luwian–Phoenician) was a key to deciphering the Luwian hieroglyphs. Palaces of the bit-hilani type, with columned porticoes, influenced architecture across the region. Major excavated sites include Carchemish, Zincirli (Sam'al), Tell Tayinat, Tell Halaf and Karatepe.
