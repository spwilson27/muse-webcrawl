Eskimo words for snow

The claim that Eskimo words for snow are extraordinarily numerous is a popular linguistic legend — sometimes called the "Great Eskimo Vocabulary Hoax". It is often cited to support the idea, related to linguistic relativity, that language shapes perception of the world. In reality, Inuit and Yupik languages have a modest number of roots for snow comparable to English, though their polysynthetic structure allows very many derived forms.

Origins

Anthropologist Franz Boas in 1911 mentioned four unrelated Inuit roots for snow (such as aput, "snow on the ground", and qana, "falling snow") to illustrate how languages carve up experience differently. Benjamin Lee Whorf in 1940 expanded the example, and later textbooks and journalism inflated the count successively to dozens, then hundreds, of words. Linguist Laura Martin traced this escalation in a 1986 paper, and Geoffrey Pullum popularised the debunking in his essay "The Great Eskimo Vocabulary Hoax" (1989).

Analysis

Eskimo–Aleut languages build words by stringing together many morphemes, so counting "words for snow" depends on counting conventions; by the same method English could be said to have vast numbers of snow expressions (snowfall, snowdrift, slush, blizzard, powder, and so on). Specialists estimate the number of distinct snow roots at roughly a dozen or fewer — unremarkable for peoples living in snowy environments, and matched by the vocabularies of skiers and other specialists in English-speaking cultures.
