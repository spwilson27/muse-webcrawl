Flood control in the Netherlands

Flood control in the Netherlands is a centuries-old national undertaking necessitated by the country's geography: about one third of the Netherlands lies below sea level, and roughly two thirds is vulnerable to flooding, while the country is densely populated and economically vital. Successive generations have built one of the world's most sophisticated water-management systems of dikes, dunes, storm surge barriers, pumping stations and polders. The Dutch saying "God created the Earth, but the Dutch created the Netherlands" reflects this history of land reclamation.

The Oosterscheldekering, part of the Delta Works.

History

Early inhabitants built artificial dwelling mounds (terpen) as early as 500 BC. From the Middle Ages, local water boards (waterschappen) — among the oldest democratic institutions in the Netherlands — coordinated dike building and drainage. Windmills drained polders, famously in the Kinderdijk complex. Large-scale reclamation culminated in the twentieth century with the Zuiderzee Works, which dammed the Zuiderzee with the Afsluitdijk (1932) and created the Flevoland polders.

The Delta Works

The catastrophic North Sea flood of 1953, which killed 1,836 people in the Netherlands, prompted the Delta Works in the southwestern delta: a series of dams, sluices and storm surge barriers including the Oosterscheldekering (1986), with its movable gates, and the Maeslantkering near Rotterdam (1997), one of the largest moving structures on Earth. The Delta Plan aimed to shorten the coastline and protect the estuaries of the Rhine, Meuse and Scheldt.

Modern policy

More recent policy, influenced by river floods in 1993 and 1995 and by climate-change projections, has shifted from raising dikes ever higher towards "Room for the River": lowering floodplains, relocating dikes inland and creating water-storage areas. The national Delta Programme, overseen by a Delta Commissioner and financed through a dedicated Delta Fund, plans adaptive flood protection with safety standards among the strictest in the world — in the most protected areas, as low as a 1-in-100,000 annual probability of failure.
