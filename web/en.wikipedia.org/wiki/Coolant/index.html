Coolant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Coolant
FunctionHeat transfer fluid
Common typesWater, ethylene glycol mixtures, oils, liquid metals, gases
ApplicationsEngines, HVAC, electronics, nuclear reactors, machining

A coolant is a substance, typically liquid or gas, that is used to reduce or regulate the temperature of a system. An ideal coolant has high thermal capacity, low viscosity, is low-cost, non-toxic, chemically inert and causes neither corrosion nor electrical interference in the system it cools.

Applications

Engines

The most familiar coolant is the antifreeze mixture used in internal combustion engines, typically water mixed with ethylene glycol or propylene glycol. The glycol lowers the freezing point, raises the boiling point and carries corrosion inhibitors that protect the radiator, water pump and engine block. Coolant circulates through passages in the engine, absorbing heat, and releases it through the radiator.

Industry and machining

In metalworking, cutting fluids cool and lubricate the interface between tool and workpiece, extending tool life and improving surface finish. Power electronics, computers and data centres use liquid cooling loops, sometimes with dielectric fluids for direct immersion.

Nuclear reactors

Nuclear reactors use coolants such as ordinary (light) water, heavy water, gases like carbon dioxide and helium, and liquid metals such as sodium. In pressurised water reactors the coolant also acts as the neutron moderator. Loss of coolant is a critical accident scenario addressed by emergency core cooling systems.

Properties

Coolants are selected for high specific heat capacity and thermal conductivity, an appropriate liquid range for the operating temperatures, chemical compatibility with system materials, and stability under irradiation or heat. Water remains the benchmark coolant because of its exceptional heat capacity, but its freezing and boiling limits motivate additives or alternative fluids in many applications.
