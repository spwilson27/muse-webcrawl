The Pirate Bay trial

The Pirate Bay trial
CourtStockholm District Court; Svea Court of Appeal
Decided17 April 2009 (district court); 26 November 2010 (appeal)
DefendantsFredrik Neij, Gottfrid Svartholm, Peter Sunde, Carl Lundström
ChargesAssisting in making copyrighted content accessible / copyright infringement
OutcomeConvictions upheld on appeal; prison sentences and joint damages

The Pirate Bay trial was a joint criminal and civil prosecution in Sweden against four men connected with The Pirate Bay, one of the world's largest BitTorrent indexing sites. Operators Fredrik Neij, Gottfrid Svartholm and Peter Sunde, and businessman Carl Lundström, who had supplied services to the site, were charged with promoting other people's infringements of copyright law. The trial, held at the Stockholm District Court in February and March 2009, became a global cause célèbre over file sharing, copyright enforcement and internet freedom.

Background

Swedish police had raided The Pirate Bay's hosting facilities on 31 May 2006, seizing servers; the site returned within days and grew further in popularity. Prosecutor Håkan Roswall filed charges on 31 January 2008, alleging the defendants assisted in making copyrighted works available and profited from advertising. A group of entertainment-industry companies, including Warner Bros., MGM, EMI, Universal, Sony and Columbia Pictures, joined the case seeking compensation initially exceeding 100 million Swedish kronor — later calculated at over 46 million kronor in damages for a sample of works including films, games and albums.

Trial and verdict

The defence argued the site was a neutral search engine that hosted no infringing content itself, relying in part on the principle later nicknamed the "King Kong defence" — that individual users, not the platform, decided what to share. On 17 April 2009 the district court found all four defendants guilty, sentencing each to one year in prison and ordering them jointly to pay 30 million kronor (about US$3.5 million) in damages. The verdict drew protests; Sweden's Pirate Party reported a surge in membership, and in the June 2009 European Parliament election the party won a seat.

Appeal and aftermath

The district court judge, Tomas Norström, was accused of bias after it emerged that he belonged to copyright-industry-adjacent associations, but the Svea Court of Appeal found no disqualifying conflict. On 26 November 2010 the court of appeal upheld the convictions while differentiating the sentences: Neij received ten months, Sunde eight, and Lundström four, and total damages were increased to 46 million kronor. Svartholm, who had not appeared, was tried separately and his one-year sentence stood. The Supreme Court of Sweden declined to hear the case in February 2012; the defendants pursued the matter to the European Court of Human Rights, which declared their application inadmissible in 2013, finding the convictions a justified restriction of expression. The defendants served their sentences at various points during the 2010s, while The Pirate Bay itself continued operating under changing domains and hosting arrangements.
