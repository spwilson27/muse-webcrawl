Reverse transcription

Reverse transcription is the process of generating complementary DNA (cDNA) from an RNA template by the enzyme reverse transcriptase (RNA-dependent DNA polymerase). It runs counter to the usual direction of the central dogma of molecular biology, in which genetic information flows from DNA to RNA.

Mechanism

Reverse transcriptase uses a short primer to begin synthesis of a DNA strand complementary to the RNA template, degrading the original RNA strand with its RNase H activity and then synthesising the second DNA strand. The enzyme is characteristic of retroviruses such as HIV, which package an RNA genome plus reverse transcriptase and copy their genome into DNA after infecting a host cell, allowing integration into the host's chromosomes. A cellular reverse transcriptase, telomerase, extends the ends of chromosomes (telomeres) using its own RNA component as template.

Discovery

Reverse transcription was reported independently in 1970 by Howard Temin and David Baltimore; their finding overturned the strict unidirectionality of the central dogma and won them the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1975 (shared with Renato Dulbecco). It was quickly accepted because it explained how RNA tumour viruses could cause cancer by inserting DNA copies of their genomes into host cells.

Applications

Reverse transcriptase is a core laboratory tool: in RT-PCR (reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction), messenger RNA is first converted to cDNA, which is then amplified and quantified — a technique widely used to measure gene expression and to detect RNA viruses in clinical diagnostics, notably during the COVID-19 pandemic.
