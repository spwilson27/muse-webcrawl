Audio time stretching and pitch scaling

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Time stretching is the process of changing the speed or duration of an audio signal without affecting its pitch, while its counterpart pitch scaling (pitch shifting) changes the pitch without affecting the duration. Together they let producers fit loops to a tempo, transpose recordings to a new key, slow speech for study, or create special effects — feats impossible with simple resampling, which changes tempo and pitch together, as when a tape or record is played at the wrong speed.

Techniques

Time-domain methods cut the signal into short overlapping grains and repeat or drop them. Overlap-add methods (OLA, and its synchronized variant SOLA and pitch-aware PSOLA) work well for speech and monophonic material. The phase vocoder analyzes the signal into frequency bands with the short-time Fourier transform and re-synthesizes it at a new rate, handling polyphonic music better at the cost of transient smearing and "phasiness." Modern commercial algorithms combine approaches with transient detection and formant preservation so that vocals remain natural when pitch-shifted. Pitch scaling is often achieved by time-stretching and then resampling, which shifts pitch while restoring the original duration.

Applications

Time stretching is built into every major digital audio workstation: matching sample loops to a song's tempo, quantizing timing in audio editors such as Ableton Live's warping and Pro Tools' Elastic Audio, and correcting or stylizing vocals. Broadcasters use small stretches to fit programs into schedules; language learners slow speech without the "chipmunk" or "Darth Vader" artifacts of resampling; DJs historically used pitch controls on turntables and CD players, now largely replaced by key-lock algorithms. Extreme stretching is itself a creative genre — slowing recordings by huge factors turns short pieces into ambient works lasting hours.

Limitations

Artifacts grow with the stretch factor: doubling or halving duration is usually clean, but extreme changes produce graininess, smeared transients, or metallic phasing, and drums and other percussive sounds are especially difficult to process convincingly.
