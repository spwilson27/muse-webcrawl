Project 523

Project 523 (Chinese: 523项目) was a secret Chinese military research programme launched on 23 May 1967 (hence "523") during the Vietnam War, at the request of North Vietnam, to find a new treatment for malaria that was effective against drug-resistant strains of the parasite. Involving some 500 scientists across about 60 institutes, the project led to the discovery of artemisinin (qinghaosu) from the plant Artemisia annua (sweet wormwood) by a team led by Tu Youyou — a discovery that transformed malaria treatment worldwide and earned Tu a share of the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Background

Chloroquine-resistant malaria was devastating North Vietnamese forces and affecting Chinese troops in the south. Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai authorised a national effort combining a search for synthetic drugs with a systematic screening of traditional Chinese medicines. The project was coordinated from Beijing under wartime secrecy, during the Cultural Revolution.

Discovery of artemisinin

Tu Youyou's group at the Institute of Chinese Materia Medica reviewed thousands of traditional recipes and tested hundreds of herbal extracts. In 1971 they obtained a fully effective extract of Artemisia annua after Tu realised, from Ge Hong's 4th-century Handbook of Prescriptions for Emergencies, that the herb should be steeped in cold rather than hot water, and switched to a low-temperature ether extraction. The active compound was isolated in 1972 and its structure determined by 1975; clinical trials showed rapid clearance of parasites, including resistant strains.

Legacy

Artemisinin and its derivatives (artemether, artesunate) became the basis of artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), recommended by the WHO as first-line malaria treatment and credited with a major share of the sharp global decline in malaria deaths in the 2000s. China's official recognition of the project's scientists came slowly because of its secrecy; Tu Youyou received the Lasker Award in 2011 and the Nobel Prize in 2015.
