Novelty architecture

Also calledMimetic architecture, programmatic architecture
FlourishedEarly–mid 20th century, especially United States
ContextRoadside commerce and automobile culture
Famous examplesLucy the Elephant, the Brown Derby, the Big Duck, Longaberger Basket Building

Novelty architecture, also called mimetic or programmatic architecture, is a style of building in which the structure is shaped to look like the product it sells or the function it houses — a hot-dog stand shaped like a hot dog, a camera shop shaped like a camera, a basket company's headquarters shaped like a giant basket. It flourished along American highways in the 1920s–1950s, when businesses competed for the attention of passing motorists.

History

Precursors include follies and exhibition pavilions, and structures such as Lucy the Elephant (1881) at Margate City, New Jersey, built to attract property buyers. The style took off with the automobile: in car-oriented Southern California, architects designed instantly readable buildings — the hat-shaped Brown Derby restaurant (1926), doughnut shops (Randy's Donuts, 1953), ice-cream stands shaped like owls and freezers. Architects and theorists later celebrated this "architecture of communication"; Robert Venturi contrasted the decorated shed with the "duck" — his name, after the Big Duck of Flanders, Long Island (1931), for a building that is itself a sign.

Characteristics and examples

Novelty buildings literalise advertising: form follows product. Notable survivors include the Big Duck, Randy's Donuts, the Donut Hole in La Puente, the Teapot Dome Service Station in Washington, Thailand's Elephant Building, Taiwan's Houtong cat-themed structures, and the Longaberger Basket Building in Ohio (1997), a late corporate example. Many examples were demolished as tastes changed; preservationists now campaign to save the survivors as icons of roadside Americana.

Criticism

Establishment architects long dismissed novelty architecture as vulgar kitsch, but postmodernism rehabilitated it as honest commercial vernacular and an influence on pop art and later "iconic" landmark buildings designed to be instantly recognisable images.
