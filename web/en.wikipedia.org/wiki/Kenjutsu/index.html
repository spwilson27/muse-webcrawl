Kenjutsu

Paired kata practice with bokken

Kenjutsu (Japanese: 剣術, "sword technique") is an umbrella term for the classical Japanese martial arts of swordsmanship as practised by the samurai of feudal Japan. Unlike the modern sport-oriented kendo, kenjutsu refers to the traditional schools (koryū) that preserve combat methods developed for the battlefield, typically taught through paired forms (kata) using wooden swords (bokken), and sometimes live blades.

History

Swordsmanship traditions flourished during Japan's centuries of warfare. The oldest extant school, Tenshin Shōden Katori Shintō-ryū, traces its founding to the 15th century. Hundreds of schools (ryūha) emerged, each with its own curriculum of techniques, strategies and philosophical teachings; famous examples include Kashima Shintō-ryū, Shinkage-ryū (founded by Kamiizumi Nobutsuna), Ittō-ryū, and the Niten Ichi-ryū of Miyamoto Musashi.

With the Meiji Restoration (1868) and the abolition of the samurai class, kenjutsu declined, but many schools survived as cultural traditions and were adapted into modern forms such as kendo and iaido.

Training

Classical training emphasises kata — pre-arranged sequences practised with a partner — rather than free sparring, together with cutting practice and study of strategy, etiquette and the school's oral teachings. Many koryū curricula are comprehensive (sōgō bujutsu), including grappling, staff, spear and other weapons alongside the sword. Instruction is traditionally organised through licensing (menkyo) rather than dan ranks.

Kenjutsu and kendo

Kendo derives historically from kenjutsu but repackages it as a competitive, character-building discipline with bamboo swords and armour; kenjutsu preserves the older technical repertoire and its combative context. Many practitioners study both.
