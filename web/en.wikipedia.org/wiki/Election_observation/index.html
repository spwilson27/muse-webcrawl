Election observation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Election observation (election monitoring) is the systematic observation of an election by independent organisations or states to assess whether the process is conducted in a free and fair manner according to national and international standards for democratic elections — covering the legal framework, campaign environment, media access, voter registration, voting, counting and complaints, not merely election day. Modern observation crystallised after the Cold War (UN, OSCE/ODIHR, EU, Commonwealth, African Union and Carter Center missions, under the 2005 UN Declaration of Principles for International Election Observation).

Method and politics

Professional missions deploy long-term observers weeks ahead, issue interim and preliminary statements (the politically explosive headline judgment, delivered before full tabulation), and follow complaints to a final report with recommendations; domestic observer networks (often far larger) conduct parallel vote tabulations that statistically verify official counts. Authoritarian responses define the field's current era: "zombie" pseudo-observer missions endorsing flawed polls, sovereigntist counters at the SCO and CIS, refusal of access or visa games, and elections designed to pass surface tests while being rigged upstream (candidate registration, media capture) — forcing methodology toward whole-cycle assessment. Observers' dilemma is structural: their presence legitimises even where their reports criticise, a bargain each mission weighs.
