Ferdinand Christian Baur (21 June 1792 – 2 December 1860) was a German Protestant theologian and the founder of the Tübingen School of theology. A professor at the University of Tübingen from 1826 until his death, Baur applied rigorous historical criticism — informed by Hegelian philosophy — to the New Testament and early church history, profoundly reshaping nineteenth-century biblical scholarship.

Life and career

Baur was born at Schmiden, near Stuttgart, the son of a pastor. He was educated at the theological seminary at Blaubeuren and then at the University of Tübingen. After teaching at Blaubeuren, he returned to Tübingen in 1826 as professor of theology, delivering influential lectures on the New Testament, church history, and dogmatics. His pupils included figures who carried the Tübingen approach across German scholarship, though his school's influence waned after his death as its conclusions were progressively modified.

The Tübingen thesis

Baur's central claim, developed from his 1831 essay on the Christ-party in Corinth onward, was that early Christianity was marked by a fundamental conflict between two parties: a Jewish-Christian party associated with Peter and the original apostles, and a Gentile, law-free party associated with Paul. The Catholic Church of the second century, on his reading, arose through the gradual reconciliation (synthesis) of these opposed tendencies (thesis and antithesis) — a scheme Baur adapted from Hegel's dialectic.

This historical principle, known as tendency criticism (Tendenzkritik), required each early Christian writing to be dated and interpreted according to the partisan tendency it expressed. Applying it drastically, Baur accepted as genuinely Pauline only four epistles — Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, and Galatians, the Hauptbriefe — and dated most of the rest of the New Testament, including the Gospels, deep into the second century. His major works include Paulus, der Apostel Jesu Christi (1845; "Paul, the Apostle of Jesus Christ") and his multi-volume history of the Christian church.

Influence and criticism

Baur's specific datings were largely abandoned by later scholars, and even his students softened his late chronology. Yet his insistence that New Testament documents must be studied as historical products of identifiable communities, and his demonstration that the book of Acts reflects later ecclesiastical interests rather than straightforward reportage, made him one of the founders of modern historical-critical study of early Christianity. J. B. Lightfoot in England led the most famous rebuttal of the Tübingen reconstruction, while Adolf von Harnack later offered a more moderate synthesis that retained Baur's historical method with an earlier chronology.
