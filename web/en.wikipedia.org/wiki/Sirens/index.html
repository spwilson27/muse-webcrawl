Sirens

FieldGreek mythology
Known fromHomer's Odyssey
FormPart woman, part bird

In Greek mythology, the Sirens were dangerous creatures, part woman and part bird, whose enchanting song lured sailors to shipwreck on the rocky coasts of their island. They are best known from Homer's Odyssey, in which Odysseus has himself tied to the mast to hear their song safely while his crew row on with wax-sealed ears.

Mythology

Later authors placed the Sirens' island near Sicily and the coast of Italy, and gave them names such as Parthenope, Ligeia and Leucosia. In one story they were companions of Persephone, transformed after her abduction; in another they threw themselves into the sea after Odysseus escaped them. The Argonauts passed them too, with Orpheus drowning out their song with his own music.

Depictions

In archaic Greek art Sirens appear as birds with women's heads; over time they became increasingly human in form, and in medieval tradition they merged with mermaids. They appear as mourners on funerary monuments and as symbols of temptation.

Legacy

The Sirens gave their name to the sirenian sea mammals (manatees and dugongs) and to acoustic warning devices. The phrase 'siren song' denotes an irresistible but dangerous appeal.
