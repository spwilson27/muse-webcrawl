Bayes factor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A balance scale comparing two hypotheses, tipping toward the better-supported one]

In Bayesian statistics, a Bayes factor is a ratio of the likelihoods of two competing hypotheses, measuring how much a body of data shifts the odds between them. Multiplying prior odds by the Bayes factor gives posterior odds — the quantitative heart of Bayes' theorem applied to model comparison.

The term and systematic use were developed by Harold Jeffreys in his Theory of Probability (1939). Conventional scales (notably Jeffreys' own, and Kass and Raftery's 1995 version) grade Bayes factors from 'barely worth mentioning' (below 3) to 'decisive' (above 100).

Debates

Unlike p-values, Bayes factors compare two hypotheses directly and can support a null hypothesis, not merely fail to reject it. Critics note their sensitivity to prior distributions on parameters — vague priors can artefactually favour the null (the Jeffreys–Lindley paradox).

Alan Turing used a wartime version, the 'ban' and 'deciban', to weigh evidence in codebreaking at Bletchley Park.
