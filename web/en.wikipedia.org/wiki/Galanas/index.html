Galanas was, in medieval Welsh law (Cyfraith Hywel, the law of Hywel Dda), the blood-fine or compensation payable for homicide. When a person was killed, the slayer and his extended kindred were obliged to pay the galanas to the kindred of the victim, the amount being calculated according to the victim's legal status (sarhaed value) and the circumstances of the killing.

Calculation and payment

The galanas was reckoned in cattle and silver and scaled by rank: the fine for killing a king or a breyr (nobleman) was vastly greater than that for a bondsman or an unfree person. Liability was spread across the killer's paternal and maternal kin to defined degrees of relationship, so that a single act of homicide engaged the resources — and the responsibility — of a wide family group. Payment was in turn divided among the victim's kin. The system was designed to buy off the feud: once galanas was paid and accepted, the injured kindred renounced vengeance, a settlement guaranteed by oath and by the lord's authority.

Agnatic responsibility

Closely connected was the rule that the kindred also received the sarhaed payment for insults and injuries to a member. The galanas framework placed collective responsibility at the heart of Welsh justice: kinship groups policed their own members because they bore the cost of their offences, and they protected them because an unpaid galanas exposed the whole group to lawful retaliation.

Decline

Galanas survived in the Welsh Marches and Welshries into the later Middle Ages but was progressively displaced by English criminal law after the Laws in Wales Acts of 1535 and 1542, which extended English common law and its capital treatment of homicide to Wales. The institution is comparable to the Anglo-Saxon wergild and to blood-money systems in other early medieval legal traditions.
