Licensing Act 1737

The Licensing Act 1737 (10 Geo. II c. 28) was an Act of the Parliament of Great Britain that introduced stage censorship in London and Westminster. It required all new plays to be submitted to the Lord Chamberlain for approval at least fourteen days before performance, and restricted legitimate spoken drama to the patent theatres. The Act, driven through by Prime Minister Robert Walpole in response to stage satires mocking his government, remained a central control on the British theatre until the Theatres Act 1968 abolished the Lord Chamberlain's censorship.

Background

During the 1730s, London theatres mounted increasingly sharp political satires. John Gay's The Beggar's Opera (1728) had popularised ballad opera mocking Walpole; Henry Fielding's plays at the Little Theatre in the Haymarket — including Tom Thumb and Pasquin (1736) — ridiculed the ministry directly. The ostensible trigger in 1737 was the scurrilous anonymous play The Golden Rump, possibly fabricated or exaggerated to justify legislation. Walpole, who had long resented theatrical attacks, used the furore to pass the Act with a large majority.

Provisions

The Act required that a copy of every new play, prologue, or epilogue be sent to the Lord Chamberlain at least fourteen days before performance; the Lord Chamberlain could prohibit any piece "for the preservation of good manners, decorum, or of the public peace". Outside London and Westminster, spoken drama was effectively confined to the theatres royal — Drury Lane and Covent Garden (and, in summer, the Haymarket) — while unlicensed performers could be treated as rogues and vagabonds and fined £50. Theatres evaded the restrictions with devices such as "concerts" with a play given free, burlettas (musical pieces), and Samuel Foote's invitation-only "tea" performances.

Effects and repeal

The Act ended Fielding's career as a dramatist (he turned to the novel) and froze the London stage's repertoire, pushing new writing toward musical and spectacular forms. The censorship provisions were consolidated in the Theatres Act 1843, which ended the patent monopoly but retained licensing of plays. The Lord Chamberlain's power to refuse or cut plays survived into the 20th century — famously delaying or banning works by Shaw, Ibsen, and Edward Bond — until the Theatres Act 1968 finally abolished stage censorship in Britain.
