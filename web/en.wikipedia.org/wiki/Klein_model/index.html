Klein model

In the Klein model, hyperbolic lines appear as straight chords of the boundary circle.

In mathematics, the Klein model, also known as the projective model, the Beltrami–Klein model, or the Cayley–Klein model, is a model of hyperbolic geometry in which points are represented by the points in the interior of a unit disk (or an n-dimensional ball), and lines are represented by the chords of the boundary circle — that is, the open line segments with endpoints on the boundary.

The model is named after Felix Klein, with roots in the work of Eugenio Beltrami and Arthur Cayley. Its chief advantage over the Poincaré disk model is that geodesics are drawn as straight Euclidean line segments, making incidence constructions simple; its chief disadvantage is that it is not conformal, so angles and circles are distorted.

Distance

Distance in the Klein model is measured using the cross ratio. Given two points P and Q inside the disk, let the chord through them meet the boundary circle at A and B. The hyperbolic distance is proportional to the logarithm of the cross ratio (A, B; P, Q), with a factor of one half in the standard normalisation. This construction comes from the Cayley–Klein metric of projective geometry.

Relation to other models

The Klein model is related to the Poincaré disk model by a radial projection: a point at distance r from the centre in the Klein model corresponds to a point at distance 2r/(1 + r²) in the Poincaré model. It can also be obtained from the hyperboloid model by projecting from a point onto a tangent plane. Circles in the Klein model appear as ellipses, and angles are not preserved, but the straightness of lines makes the model popular for constructions and for visualising hyperbolic tilings.

History

Beltrami introduced the model in 1868 as part of his demonstration that hyperbolic geometry is consistent if Euclidean geometry is. Cayley's projective metric provided the distance notion, and Klein, in 1871, clarified the relationship between the two, establishing the model in its modern form.
