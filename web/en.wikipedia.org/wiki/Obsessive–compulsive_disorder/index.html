Obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) is a mental disorder in which an individual has intrusive thoughts (obsessions) and feels the urge to perform certain routines repeatedly (compulsions) to try to relieve the distress caused by the obsessions, to an extent that interferes with daily life. Common obsessions include fears of contamination, harm coming to oneself or others, unwanted taboo thoughts, and a need for symmetry or exactness; common compulsions include excessive cleaning, checking, counting, ordering, and seeking reassurance.

Signs and presentation

Most people with OCD recognise that their obsessions and compulsions are excessive or unreasonable (good insight), though insight varies. Symptoms are time-consuming — often more than an hour a day — and cause significant distress or impairment. OCD frequently co-occurs with anxiety disorders, depression, tic disorders, and related conditions such as body dysmorphic disorder and hoarding disorder.

Causes

The cause is unknown; genetics contributes substantially (twin studies suggest heritability around 40–50%), and neuroimaging implicates cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuits and serotonergic signalling. A controversial proposed subtype follows streptococcal infection in children (PANDAS).

Treatment

First-line treatments are cognitive behavioural therapy, specifically exposure and response prevention (ERP) — gradually facing feared situations while refraining from compulsions — and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), often at higher doses than used for depression. Combination treatment helps many; for severe, treatment-resistant cases, options include augmentation strategies, intensive programmes, and, rarely, neurosurgical interventions such as deep brain stimulation.

Epidemiology and history

OCD affects roughly 1–2% of adults in a given year and about 2–3% over a lifetime, with onset typically in childhood, adolescence, or early adulthood. Historical descriptions stretch back centuries under religious framings ("scrupulosity"); the disorder took its modern form in 19th- and 20th-century psychiatry and became far more widely recognised after effective behavioural and drug treatments emerged in the 1960s–1980s.
