Waterspout

A waterspout is an intense columnar vortex, usually appearing as a funnel-shaped cloud, that forms over a body of water and connects the surface to a cumuliform cloud. Waterspouts are essentially tornadoes over water, though many are weaker than most land tornadoes.

Types

Meteorologists distinguish two categories. Tornadic waterspouts are true tornadoes that form over water or move from land to water; they are associated with supercell thunderstorms and can be dangerous. Fair-weather waterspouts, the more common type, develop along the surface of the water beneath developing cumulus clouds on light-wind days, grow upward, and are generally short-lived and weak.

Formation

Fair-weather waterspouts pass through recognisable stages: a dark spot on the water, a spiral pattern, a spray ring or "cascade", a mature vortex with a visible funnel, and finally decay, often with a rope-like appearance. They typically occur in tropical and subtropical waters, with the Florida Keys reporting the highest frequency in the world.

Hazards

Although usually weak, waterspouts can damage boats, injure swimmers and occasionally move onshore as landfalling tornadoes. Mariners are advised to keep a safe distance, as wind speeds within a strong tornadic waterspout can exceed 200 kilometres per hour.
