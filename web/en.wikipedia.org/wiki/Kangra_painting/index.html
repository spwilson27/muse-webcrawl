Kangra painting

Kangra painting is the pictorial art of Kangra, named after the Kangra State, a former princely state of Himachal Pradesh, which patronised the art. It became prevalent with the fading of the Basohli school of painting in the mid-18th century, and soon produced such a magnitude in paintings both in content as well as volume, that the Pahari painting school came to be known as the Kangra school.

History

The Kangra school developed in the second half of the 18th century when Kashmiri painters trained in Mughal style sought the patronage of the Rajas of Kangra. Raja Sansar Chand (c. 1765–1823) of the Katoch dynasty was the school's greatest patron, and under him the Kangra atelier produced thousands of lyrical miniatures, especially series illustrating the Gita Govinda, the Rasikapriya, the Bhagavata Purana and the Ramayana.

Characteristics

Kangra paintings are distinguished by their delicate line, brilliant and luminous colours of natural pigments, and lyrical treatment of landscape. Nature is rendered with loving attention — flowering trees, rivers, monsoon clouds and distant hills form a backdrop to scenes of love, above all the love of Radha and Krishna. Female figures are drawn with refined features: a straight nose in line with the forehead, narrow waists and graceful hands. The use of cool greens and blues gives many works a serene, romantic mood.

Legacy

After the death of Sansar Chand and the Sikh and British annexations of the Punjab hills, patronage declined, but Kangra painting remains one of the most celebrated schools of Indian miniature art, with major collections in museums in Chandigarh, Boston, New York and London. The town of Kangra and nearby centres such as Guler, Nurpur and Chamba each contributed local variants to the broader Pahari tradition.
