Historicism (Christianity)

Historicism is a method of interpretation in Christian eschatology that associates biblical prophecies — especially in the books of Daniel and Revelation — with actual historical events, persons and periods across the whole course of church history, from the first century to the end of time. It is one of the four major schools of prophetic interpretation, alongside preterism, futurism and idealism.

Principles

Historicist interpreters typically apply the "year-day principle" (a prophetic day symbolising a literal year) and read the successive visions of Daniel 2 and 7 as sequences of empires — Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, Rome — followed by the divided kingdoms of Europe. The 1,260 prophetic days are commonly understood as 1,260 years, often reckoned from 538 to 1798 AD. Protestant historicists historically identified the papacy with features of the Antichrist or "man of sin," an interpretation prominent from the Reformation through the 19th century.

History and legacy

Elements of the method appear in Joachim of Fiore and the Reformers; it was the dominant Protestant reading of prophecy through the Millerite movement of the 1840s in the United States. Historicism remains the official interpretive approach of Seventh-day Adventism, whose "investigative judgment" doctrine is built on a historicist reading of Daniel 8:14, and is retained by some other churches, though futurist dispensationalism largely displaced it in evangelicalism during the 20th century.
