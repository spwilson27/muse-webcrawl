Elliptic curve primality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Elliptic curve primality testing, and in particular the elliptic curve primality proving (ECPP) algorithm, is a method for proving that a given integer is prime by means of the arithmetic of elliptic curves. Introduced by Shafi Goldwasser and Joe Kilian and developed into a practical algorithm by A. O. L. Atkin and François Morain, ECPP produces a short, efficiently checkable primality certificate and is among the fastest methods in practice for general numbers of hundreds or thousands of digits.

Idea of the algorithm

Classical primality proofs such as Pocklington's criterion rely on finding a group whose order is known and sufficiently factored. For elliptic curves modulo the candidate prime n, Hasse's theorem says the number of points lies close to n + 1, within an interval of width 4√n. The algorithm constructs a curve whose point count m has a large prime factor q greater than the Hasse bound's threshold; a point of order q on the curve then certifies that n is prime provided q itself is prime. Because q is substantially smaller than n, the process recurses, generating a chain of certificates that descends to small primes that can be checked directly.

Finding the curve order

The practical ECPP algorithm of Atkin and Morain works in reverse: it uses complex multiplication to construct curves with a predetermined number of points. For a candidate order m = n + 1 − t, the Hilbert class polynomial for a suitable imaginary quadratic field is solved modulo n to obtain a curve with exactly m points. Choosing discriminants with small class numbers keeps the polynomials manageable.

Complexity and use

Heuristically ECPP runs in time roughly O(log5+ε n), and certificate verification is faster still. Implementations in systems such as Primo have certified primes of tens of thousands of digits. ECPP contrasts with the deterministic polynomial-time AKS test, which is elegant but generally slower in practice for large inputs, and with probabilistic tests such as Miller–Rabin, which cannot furnish a proof.
