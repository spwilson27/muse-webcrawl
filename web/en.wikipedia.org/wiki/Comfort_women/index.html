Comfort women

Comfort women were women and girls forced into sexual slavery by the Imperial Japanese Armed Forces in occupied countries and territories before and during World War II. The term translates the Japanese ianfu, a euphemism for the victims, who were held in military brothels known as "comfort stations." Estimates of the number of victims vary widely, from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand; most were Korean, with many others from China, the Philippines, Indonesia, the Netherlands, and elsewhere in occupied Asia.

Background and system

The first comfort stations were established in the early 1930s around Shanghai, partly, Japanese military authorities claimed, to reduce rape of local populations and venereal disease among troops. As Japan expanded its war in China and across the Pacific, the system grew substantially. Women were recruited through deception (offers of factory or nursing work), coercion, abduction, and purchase from impoverished families. Conditions in the stations were brutal: women were confined, subjected to repeated sexual violence, and often suffered disease, injury, and forced abortions. Many died or were killed; survivors often faced lifelong physical and psychological trauma and social stigma.

Post-war silence and redress movement

The issue received little public attention for decades. In 1991 the Korean survivor Kim Hak-sun became the first to testify publicly and sue the Japanese government, sparking an international redress movement. Japan's 1993 Kono Statement acknowledged the military's involvement and coercive recruitment; the Asian Women's Fund (1995) offered compensation financed largely by private donations, which some survivors refused as insufficiently official. In 2015 Japan and South Korea reached an agreement under which Japan apologised and contributed ¥1 billion to a foundation for survivors; the deal was controversial and the foundation was later dissolved by Seoul.

Memorials and ongoing disputes

Memorial statues, notably the Statue of Peace placed opposite the Japanese embassy in Seoul in 2011 and copies abroad, have been a focus of diplomatic friction. The comfort women issue remains a major point of contention in Japan–South Korea relations, touching on questions of historical memory, state responsibility, and the adequacy of past apologies and compensation. In 2021 a South Korean court ordered Japan to compensate surviving plaintiffs, a ruling Tokyo rejected on state-immunity grounds; the issue continues to be litigated and debated.
