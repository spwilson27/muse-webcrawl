Exothermic reaction

Sparks emitted by a thermite reaction, a classic exothermic process.

An exothermic reaction is a chemical reaction that releases energy by light or heat. It is the opposite of an endothermic reaction. Expressed in a chemical equation: reactants → products + energy. The total energy of the products is less than the total energy of the reactants, so the change in enthalpy (ΔH) is negative.

Exothermic reactions are generally spontaneous in the sense that they do not require an input of energy to proceed once initiated, although many require a small activation energy to begin, such as the spark needed to ignite a fuel.

Examples

Common examples of exothermic reactions include:

  • Combustion — burning of fuels such as wood, coal, natural gas, and hydrocarbons in engines.
  • Neutralization — the reaction of an acid with a base, which releases heat.
  • Respiration — the oxidation of glucose in cells, releasing energy stored as ATP.
  • The thermite reaction — aluminium reacting with iron(III) oxide, producing molten iron.
  • Setting of cement and concrete, which releases heat as it cures.
  • Nuclear fission and fusion, which release very large amounts of energy.

Energy considerations

In an exothermic reaction, energy is released because the total bond energy of the products is greater than the total bond energy of the reactants — forming the new bonds releases more energy than was consumed breaking the old ones. The released energy typically raises the temperature of the reaction mixture and its surroundings.

The standard enthalpy change of reaction, ΔH°, can be measured with a calorimeter. Reactions with a large negative ΔH are sometimes described as strongly exothermic; the detonation of explosives is an extreme case, coupling a large energy release with a rapid increase in volume from gaseous products.
