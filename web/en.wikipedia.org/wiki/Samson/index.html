Samson

Samson (Hebrew: שִׁמְשוֹ֩, Šimšōn, "man of the sun") was the last of the judges of ancient Israel mentioned in the Book of Judges (chapters 13 to 16) in the Hebrew Bible. He is sometimes considered to be one of the most recognizable figures in the Bible, being known for his superhuman strength derived from his uncut hair. Samson was a Nazirite from birth; his strength was lost when his lover Delilah ordered a servant to cut his hair while he was sleeping and turned him over to his Philistine enemies, who gouged out his eyes. In the end, when his hair had begun to regrow, God gave Samson the strength to push apart the pillars of the Philistine temple of Dagon, destroying it, the Philistine lords inside, and himself.

Biblical narrative

Birth

The Book of Judges relates that the Israelites had again done evil and were delivered into the hands of the Philistines for forty years. An angel appeared to the wife of Manoah, of the tribe of Dan, who was barren, and promised her a son who would be a Nazirite to God from the womb and would begin to deliver Israel from the Philistines. The boy was to abstain from wine and his hair was never to be cut.

Marriage to a Philistine

As a young man, Samson went to Timnah and saw a Philistine woman he wished to marry, against his parents' wishes. On the journey he tore apart a young lion with his bare hands. Later he found bees and honey in the lion's carcass, from which he composed his famous riddle, "Out of the eater came something to eat, and out of the strong came something sweet," which his wedding companions could not solve until they threatened his wife.

Exploits

Enraged by betrayals, Samson caught three hundred foxes, tied torches to their tails and loosed them in the Philistines' grain fields. He slew a thousand men with the jawbone of a donkey. In Gaza he carried off the city gates on his shoulders. Judges records that he judged Israel for twenty years.

Delilah and death

Samson loved a woman in the Valley of Sorek named Delilah. The lords of the Philistines bribed her to discover the secret of his strength. After three false answers, he told her the truth: his uncut hair, the sign of his Nazirite vow. She lulled him to sleep and had his seven locks shaved; his strength left him, and the Philistines seized him, blinded him, and set him to grind grain in prison at Gaza. At a great festival in the temple of Dagon, the blinded Samson was brought out to entertain the crowd. Praying for strength one last time, he pushed against the two central pillars and brought the temple down, killing more in his death than in his life.

Interpretation and culture

Samson's story has been compared to other "strong man" figures in folklore, including Heracles, and his name's connection with the sun has prompted suggestions of solar-myth elements. He is depicted in the New Testament's Epistle to the Hebrews as a hero of faith. Samson has been a popular subject of art, literature, and film, including works by Rubens, Milton's Samson Agonistes, and Handel's oratorio Samson.
