Frontier

A frontier is a geographical region or zone adjacent to a border, or, more broadly, the edge of settled or civilised territory where it meets wilderness or another society. The term derives from the Old French frontier, originally the front line of an army. Unlike a boundary, which is a precise line, a frontier is typically understood as a transitional zone of interaction, exchange and conflict.

The American frontier

The concept is most strongly associated with the westward expansion of European settlement in North America. In his influential 1893 essay "The Significance of the Frontier in American History", the historian Frederick Jackson Turner argued that the experience of the frontier — the availability of "free" land and the repeated recreation of society at the edge of settlement — had shaped American democracy, individualism and national character. The United States Census of 1890 had declared the frontier line closed, as settlement had spread across the continent. Turner's "frontier thesis" dominated American historiography for decades and has since been extensively debated and revised, particularly regarding its treatment of Native American peoples, for whom the frontier meant invasion and dispossession.

Other frontiers

Comparable frontier zones have existed around the world: the Roman limes, the marches of medieval Europe, the expanding Russian frontier across Siberia, the Australian outback, the South African interior, and the borders of imperial China. The metaphor is also applied to new domains of endeavour, as in the description of space as "the final frontier", and to scientific and technological frontiers of knowledge.
