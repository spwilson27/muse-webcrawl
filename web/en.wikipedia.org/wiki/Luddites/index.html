Luddites

Active years1811–1816
RegionNorthern and Midlands England
NamesakeNed Ludd (possibly apocryphal)

The Luddites were members of a movement of English textile workers in the early 19th century who destroyed weaving machinery as a form of protest. The movement began in Nottinghamshire in 1811 and spread across the industrial districts of the North and Midlands over the following years.

Background

The artisans objected to the growing use of mechanized looms and knitting frames operated by cheap, unapprenticed labour, which undermined their wages and craft status at a time of hardship caused by the Napoleonic Wars. They took their name from the possibly mythical Ned Ludd, an apprentice said to have smashed a stocking frame in 1779; threatening letters were signed in the name of "General Ludd" or "King Ludd."

Suppression

The government responded with mass deployments of troops — at times more soldiers than Wellington had in Spain — spies, and harsh legislation. The Frame Breaking Act of 1812 made machine-breaking a capital offence; Lord Byron spoke against it in his maiden speech in the House of Lords. After trials at York in 1813, numerous Luddites were hanged or transported, and the movement was crushed by 1816.

Legacy

In modern usage, "Luddite" denotes a person opposed to new technology, though historians note the original movement was a form of collective bargaining aimed at the terms on which machinery was introduced, not at technology in itself.
