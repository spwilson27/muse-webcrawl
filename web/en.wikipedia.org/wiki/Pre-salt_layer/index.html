Pre-salt layer

The pre-salt layer (Portuguese: pré-sal) is a geological formation on the continental shelves of Brazil and West Africa consisting of petroleum-bearing rocks lying beneath a thick layer of evaporite salt, itself covered by more recent "post-salt" sediments. Off Brazil, the pre-salt province extends some 800 km along the Santos and Campos basins, about 300 km offshore, beneath roughly 2,000 m of water and several kilometres of rock and salt. Discoveries there from 2006 onward — above all the giant Lula (formerly Tupi) field — ranked among the largest oil finds of recent decades and transformed Brazil into a major oil producer.

Geology

The formations were laid down about 120 million years ago as the South Atlantic opened during the break-up of Gondwana. Carbonate reservoirs — microbialites and coquinas formed in great rift lakes — were sealed when restricted marine waters evaporated, depositing the immense salt sequence. The salt later deformed under sediment loading into domes and walls, complicating seismic imaging and drilling.

Exploration and production

Petrobras announced the Tupi discovery in 2007, with recoverable volumes estimated in the billions of barrels. Exploiting the pre-salt requires drilling through abrasive salt that can creep and close boreholes, handling high-pressure reservoirs with high CO₂ content, and mooring floating production (FPSO) vessels in deep water far from shore. Despite these challenges, pre-salt output grew rapidly and by the early 2020s supplied the large majority of Brazil's roughly 3 million barrels per day, with Búzios becoming one of the world's most productive offshore fields.

Regulation and politics

The discoveries prompted Brazil to adopt a production-sharing regime for pre-salt areas in 2010, with Petrobras as mandatory operator — rules later relaxed (2016) to attract foreign investment, followed by large transfer-of-rights and production-sharing auctions. Revenues are earmarked in part for education and health funds.
