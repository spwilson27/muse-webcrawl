Bioregionalism

Bioregionalism is a philosophy and political perspective that emphasizes the bioregion — a territory defined by natural features such as watersheds, mountain ranges, climate zones and native ecosystems, rather than by human-made political boundaries — as the proper scale for organizing human society, culture and governance. Adherents argue that communities become more sustainable and self-reliant when they are organized around the ecological characteristics of the place they inhabit.

History

The term was developed in the 1970s by figures associated with the San Francisco-based Planet Drum Foundation, founded by Peter Berg and Judy Goldhaft. Berg's writings, and the work of poet Gary Snyder, helped spread the idea through North American environmental and countercultural networks. The first North American Bioregional Congress was held in 1984.

Principles

Bioregional thought stresses "reinhabitation" — learning to live in a place in a way that supports its long-term ecological health. Practical expressions include watershed councils, local food systems, native plant restoration, and the mapping of bioregions such as Cascadia in the Pacific Northwest. Critics note difficulties in translating bioregional ideals into governance within existing states and economies.

Influence

Bioregionalism influenced green politics, permaculture and localist movements, and continues to inform watershed-based management and ecoregional planning by conservation organizations.
