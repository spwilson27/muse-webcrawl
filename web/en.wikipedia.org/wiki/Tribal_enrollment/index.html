Tribal enrollment

Tribal enrollment is the formal process by which a person becomes a citizen or member of a Native American tribe in the United States. Because federally recognized tribes are sovereign political entities, each tribe sets its own criteria for citizenship; enrollment determines eligibility for tribal services, voting rights in tribal elections, per-capita payments where they exist, and certain federal benefits tied to membership.

Criteria

The two most common criteria are lineal descent — proof of descent from a person listed on a historical base roll, such as the Dawes Rolls for the Five Civilized Tribes — and blood quantum, a minimum documented fraction of tribal ancestry, commonly one-quarter. Many tribes use a combination, and some require residency, language, or community ties. The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 influenced many tribal constitutions, and the Bureau of Indian Affairs issues Certificates of Degree of Indian Blood (CDIB), though the certificate itself does not confer tribal citizenship.

Controversies

Enrollment rules are politically sensitive. Disenrollment disputes, the status of the Cherokee Freedmen (descendants of people enslaved by the Cherokee Nation, whose citizenship was affirmed by treaty rights and a 2021 Cherokee Nation Supreme Court decision), and debates over whether blood quantum requirements will mathematically extinguish tribal citizenries over generations, are recurring issues. The Supreme Court's decision in Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez (1978) affirmed that membership criteria are largely a matter of tribal sovereignty.
