Affordable housing

Affordable housing is housing that is deemed affordable to those with a household income at or below a defined threshold, usually relative to the median income of an area. A widely used rule of thumb, originating in United States policy, holds that housing is affordable when a household spends no more than 30 percent of its gross income on housing costs; households spending more are considered "cost-burdened".

Measurement

Affordability is commonly measured with the median multiple (median house price divided by median household income) or with area median income (AMI) bands used to set eligibility for subsidy programmes. Critics note that ratio measures ignore transport costs, household size and residual income after housing expenses.

Policy approaches

  • Supply-side subsidies — public housing, low-income housing tax credits, and social housing built or financed by government.
  • Demand-side subsidies — housing vouchers (such as the US Section 8 programme) that help tenants pay market rents.
  • Regulation — inclusionary zoning requiring affordable units in new developments, rent control and rent stabilisation, and land-use reform to allow denser building.
  • Non-market housing — community land trusts, housing cooperatives and limited-equity models that remove housing from speculation.

Causes of unaffordability

Economists attribute rising unaffordability in many cities to constrained housing supply from zoning and planning restrictions, population and income growth, construction costs, and the financialisation of housing. Shortages are often most acute for the lowest-income renters.
