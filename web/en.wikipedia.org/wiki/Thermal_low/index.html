Thermal low

Thermal low (heat low)
Type: non-frontal surface low-pressure area
Cause: intense surface heating and a warm, deep column of air
Typical locations: deserts and plateaus in summer — Sahara, Arabian Peninsula, Iberia, southwestern USA/northern Mexico, Australia, Indus valley
Features: weak pressure gradient, light and variable winds, hot dry weather, dust devils; no fronts

A thermal low, or heat low, is a low-pressure area that forms over land in summer as a direct result of intense solar heating of the surface. Unlike the mobile, frontal lows of the mid-latitudes, a thermal low is stationary or slow-moving, shallow in horizontal circulation terms, and unaccompanied by fronts or significant precipitation. Thermal lows are characteristic features of the summer climate of deserts and hot continental interiors.

Formation

Strong summer sunshine heats the ground, which warms the air above it. The heated air expands, becomes less dense, and the surface pressure falls. The resulting column of warm air extends through much of the troposphere, so the low pressure at the surface is often capped by relatively high pressure aloft — the opposite vertical structure from a deep extratropical cyclone. Surface air flows gently inward toward the low, where it rises; in arid regions there is too little moisture for this weak ascent to produce much cloud or rain.

Examples and effects

Notable thermal lows include the heat low over the Sahara, the Iberian thermal low that develops over the Spanish meseta in summer, the low over the Lower Indus valley and Thar Desert (part of the seasonal monsoon trough system), the Persian Gulf and Arabian lows, and the thermal low over the deserts of the southwestern United States and northern Mexico. The Australian summer heat low similarly contributes to the monsoon circulation of the north.

Where a thermal low interacts with moist air — for example on its eastern flank in the American Southwest, drawing moisture from the Gulf of California and the Gulf of Mexico — it can help trigger the seasonal monsoon thunderstorms. Otherwise, heat lows are associated with some of the hottest and driest conditions on Earth, with near-calm, shimmering air, dust devils, and extreme afternoon temperatures moderated only slightly by the weak inflow.

Diurnal and seasonal behaviour

Thermal lows are fundamentally seasonal, reaching maximum intensity in the local summer and vanishing in winter, when the same regions may lie under strong anticyclones. They also pulse diurnally, deepest in the late afternoon when heating peaks and weakest in the early morning.
