110 film

110 is a cartridge-based film format introduced by Kodak in 1972 for its Pocket Instamatic cameras. The plastic cartridge contains both feed and take-up spools and a paper-backed roll of 16 mm wide film; it can be loaded in daylight and dropped into the camera without threading, which made it extremely popular for simple snapshot photography through the 1970s and 1980s.

110 film

Introduced: 1972 (Kodak)

Film width: 16 mm

Frame size: 13 × 17 mm

Exposures: 12, 20 or 24 per cartridge

Used in: Pocket Instamatic and other miniature cameras

Format

Each frame measures 13 × 17 mm on 16 mm film stock. The cartridge carries a single perforation per frame that engages a pin in the camera to stop the advance, and tabs on the cartridge mechanically encode the film speed for cameras able to read it. Because the film is held only by the cartridge and paper backing rather than a pressure plate, image sharpness was limited, and the small negative made large enlargements grainy — a trade-off accepted for convenience.

History

Kodak launched 110 together with the Pocket Instamatic line in 1972; the cameras were small enough to fit in a shirt pocket and sold in the tens of millions. Other manufacturers, including Minolta, Canon, Rollei and Pentax (with the Auto 110, the only interchangeable-lens SLR system made for the format), produced 110 cameras. Kodak revived the name in 1982 for its short-lived disc film system, but 110 proper faded with the rise of compact 35 mm cameras and then digital photography. Fujifilm ceased production in 2009, but the format was revived in 2012 when Lomography began manufacturing new 110 films, and other small producers have followed.
