Primary explosive

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
In a detonator, a small primary charge initiates a less sensitive secondary charge.

A primary explosive is an explosive that is extremely sensitive to stimuli such as impact, friction, heat, static electricity, or electromagnetic radiation, and that readily undergoes a rapid transition from deflagration to detonation. Primary explosives are used in small quantities in detonators and percussion caps to initiate larger, less sensitive secondary explosives.

Properties

Primary explosives detonate when subjected to relatively weak stimuli that would not initiate a secondary explosive such as TNT or RDX. Their defining practical property is a short and reliable deflagration-to-detonation transition (DDT): even a small flame or spark can produce a full detonation from a tiny charge, which makes them suitable as the first link in an explosive train. The trade-off is danger—primary explosives must be handled in milligram-to-gram quantities and often only in protected facilities.

Examples

The earliest widely used primary explosive was mercury(II) fulminate, used in percussion caps from the early 19th century; it was largely replaced because mercury is toxic and corrodes brass. Lead azide is the most common primary explosive in modern detonators, usually with a layer of lead styphnate above it to ensure ignition from a bridge wire or flame. Other examples include silver azide, diazodinitrophenol (DDNP), and tetrazene. Concerns about lead toxicity have driven research into "green" replacements such as copper(I) nitrotetrazolate (DBX-1).

Applications

Primary explosives are found in blasting caps, detonators, primers for small-arms cartridges and artillery, stab initiators, and pyrotechnic devices. In a typical commercial detonator, a few tens of milligrams of primary explosive sit above a base charge of a secondary explosive such as PETN or RDX, which in turn initiates the main charge.
