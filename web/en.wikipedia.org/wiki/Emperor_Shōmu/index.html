Emperor Shōmu (701 – 4 June 756) was the 45th emperor of Japan, reigning from 724 to 749. A devout Buddhist, he is remembered for promoting Buddhism as a state religion during the Nara period and for commissioning the colossal Great Buddha of Tōdai-ji.

Reign

Shōmu's reign was troubled by plague, crop failures, earthquakes and rebellion, which he interpreted as signs of spiritual disorder. In 741 he ordered the construction of a system of provincial temples (kokubunji) — a monastery and nunnery in every province — each with a copy of the Lotus Sutra. In 743 he issued the edict for the construction of the Great Buddha (Daibutsu), a bronze statue of Vairocana nearly 15 metres tall at Tōdai-ji in Nara; its dedication ceremony in 752 drew thousands of monks, including visitors from across Asia.

Legacy

Shōmu abdicated in 749 in favour of his daughter, Empress Kōken, and took Buddhist orders. After his death, his widow Empress Kōmyō donated his personal possessions to Tōdai-ji; they survive in the Shōsōin treasury, one of the world's great collections of eighth-century art, including objects that travelled the Silk Road from Persia and China.
