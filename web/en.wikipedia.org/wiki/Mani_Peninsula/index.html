Mani Peninsula

The Mani Peninsula (Greek: Μάνη) is a mountainous peninsula in southern Greece, forming the central of the three peninsulas extending southward from the Peloponnese. It runs from the Taygetus mountain range down to Cape Matapan (Cape Taenarum), the southernmost point of mainland Greece.

Geography

The peninsula is divided into the Outer Mani in the northwest, administered from Kardamyli, and the harsher, more remote Inner (Deep) Mani around Areopoli and the southern tip. The landscape is dry and rocky, with terraced hillsides, small coves and villages of distinctive stone tower houses built during centuries of clan feuding.

History

The Maniots, the inhabitants of the peninsula, claim descent from the ancient Spartans. The region preserved a fierce independence: it was among the last parts of Greece to be Christianised (in the 9th–10th centuries), and it was never fully subdued by the Ottoman Empire, paying at most a nominal tribute. Local clans governed themselves and frequently fought blood feuds with one another.

On March 17, 1821, the Maniots under Petrobey Mavromichalis declared war on the Ottomans at Areopoli, one of the opening acts of the Greek War of Independence. In 1826 Maniot forces repelled the invasion of Ibrahim Pasha of Egypt at the Battle of Dyros. After independence, the tower houses and feuding traditions gradually declined, and today the peninsula is known for its historic villages, Byzantine churches, the Diros caves and tourism.
