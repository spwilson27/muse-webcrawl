Bacillus subtilis

Bacillus subtilis, historically known as the "hay bacillus", is a Gram-positive, rod-shaped bacterium commonly found in soil and in the gastrointestinal tracts of humans and ruminants. It is one of the best-studied bacteria and a model organism for Gram-positive cell biology, famous for two developmental programmes: the formation of highly resistant endospores and natural genetic competence (the ability to take up DNA from the environment). It is harmless to humans and is used industrially to produce enzymes and fermented foods.

Biology

The bacterium is aerobic, motile by peritrichous flagella, and forms biofilms and chains on surfaces. Under starvation it initiates sporulation, an asymmetric cell division producing a dormant endospore that resists heat, radiation and desiccation — spores have survived years of exposure to outer space. Its genome of about 4.2 million base pairs and ~4,100 genes was sequenced in 1997, making it among the first bacteria fully sequenced. Laboratory strains such as 168 are naturally competent for transformation, which made B. subtilis a workhorse of bacterial genetics.

Uses

Industrial strains secrete large quantities of enzymes — amylases and proteases for detergents, starch processing and leather treatment — and produce the antibiotic-like compound surfactin. A strain of B. subtilis var. natto ferments soybeans into the Japanese food nattō. Certain strains are sold as probiotics and used as plant-growth-promoting biocontrol agents in agriculture.
