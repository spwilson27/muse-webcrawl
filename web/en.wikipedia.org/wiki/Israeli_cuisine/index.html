Israeli cuisine

Israeli cuisine comprises both dishes of the local Levantine tradition and foods brought to Israel by Jewish immigrants from the diaspora, including Sephardic, Ashkenazi and Mizrahi communities. It draws on Mediterranean ingredients — olive oil, wheat, chickpeas, fresh vegetables, citrus and herbs — that have been cultivated in the region for millennia, and it overlaps substantially with Palestinian and broader Middle Eastern cooking.

Characteristic dishes

Widely eaten foods include falafel, hummus, msabbaha, shakshouka (eggs poached in spiced tomato sauce), sabich, bourekas, malawach and jachnun from Yemenite tradition, schnitzel adapted from Austrian cooking, and Mediterranean grilled meats and fish. A chopped tomato-and-cucumber "Israeli salad" accompanies many meals, and the traditional Israeli breakfast features eggs, cheeses, salads, olives and breads. Street food and market stalls (shuk) play a central role in food culture.

Influences and modern developments

Jewish dietary laws (kashrut) have shaped home and restaurant cooking, as have the austerity years of the early state, when ingredients such as eggplant were adapted as substitutes. Since the 1980s a "New Israeli" restaurant scene has reinterpreted local ingredients with international techniques, gaining worldwide recognition, while debate continues over the origins and attribution of dishes shared with neighbouring Arab cuisines.
