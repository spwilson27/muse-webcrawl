Kilonova

A kilonova (also called a macronova or r-process supernova) is a transient astronomical event that occurs when two neutron stars, or a neutron star and a black hole, merge. The merger ejects a few hundredths of a solar mass of extremely neutron-rich matter, in which rapid neutron capture (the r-process) forges about half of the universe's elements heavier than iron — including gold, platinum and uranium. The radioactive decay of these freshly made nuclei heats the expanding debris, producing a glow roughly a thousand times brighter than a classical nova — hence "kilonova" — but far fainter than a supernova.

Observations

The first strong kilonova candidate followed the short gamma-ray burst GRB 130603B in 2013, when the Hubble Space Telescope saw a fading near-infrared excess. Definitive confirmation came on 17 August 2017, when the LIGO and Virgo detectors recorded gravitational waves (GW170817) from a neutron star merger in the galaxy NGC 4993, accompanied by a gamma-ray burst and the optical transient AT 2017gfo. Telescopes worldwide watched the kilonova evolve from blue to red over days, exactly as predicted as lanthanide-rich ejecta formed, and spectra showed freshly synthesised heavy elements such as strontium.

Significance

Kilonovae confirmed neutron star mergers as a principal cosmic factory for the heaviest elements, explained the origin of short gamma-ray bursts, and opened the era of multi-messenger astronomy combining gravitational waves and light. Their rates and yields suggest mergers can account for most of the universe's gold and platinum.
