Tunnels of Củ Chi

The tunnels of Củ Chi are an immense network of connecting underground tunnels located in the Củ Chi District of Ho Chi Minh City (Saigon), Vietnam, forming part of a much larger network underlying much of the country. The tunnels were used by Viet Cong soldiers as hiding spots during combat in the Vietnam War, as well as serving as communication and supply routes, hospitals, food and weapon caches and living quarters.

History

Digging began in the late 1940s during the war against French colonial rule and expanded dramatically in the 1960s. Củ Chi sat in the "Iron Triangle," a Viet Cong stronghold close to Saigon at the end of the Ho Chi Minh trail. US and South Vietnamese forces launched major operations — including Operation Crimp in 1966 — to destroy the system, and volunteer "tunnel rats" crawled into the tunnels armed with pistols and flashlights. Despite bombing that turned the district into one of the most devastated areas of the war, the tunnels were never fully eliminated, and Củ Chi-based forces attacked Saigon during the 1968 Tet Offensive.

Construction and life underground

Excavated by hand from hard laterite clay, the system ran on several levels, some 3–12 metres deep, with narrow passages, trapdoors, booby traps, false turns and water-filled U-bends to block gas and flooding. Below ground were kitchens with chimneys that dispersed smoke far from the entrance, meeting rooms, storerooms and crude hospitals. Conditions were harsh: inhabitants endured darkness, poor air, parasites, snakes and scorpions.

Today

The preserved sections at Bến Dược and Bến Đình are major tourist attractions and war memorials, where visitors can crawl through enlarged tunnel segments and view captured equipment and trap displays.
