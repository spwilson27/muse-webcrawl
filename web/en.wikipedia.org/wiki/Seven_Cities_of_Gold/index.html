Seven Cities of Gold

The Seven Cities of Gold, also known as the Seven Cities of Cíbola, were a myth of fabulous wealthy cities supposedly located in the deserts of the North American Southwest. The legend spurred Spanish exploration of what is now the southwestern United States in the 16th century, most famously the expedition of Francisco Vázquez de Coronado.

Origins

The myth drew on an Iberian legend of seven bishops who, fleeing the Muslim conquest of Hispania in the 8th century, sailed west to found seven rich cities on the island of Antillia. In the Americas the story fused with reports from the survivors of the Narváez expedition (1527–1536), particularly Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca, whose account of his wanderings hinted at wealthy settlements to the north of New Spain.

Expeditions

In 1539 the viceroy Antonio de Mendoza sent the Franciscan friar Marcos de Niza north with the Moorish slave Esteban (Estevanico) as guide; Esteban was killed at the Zuni pueblo of Hawikuh, and Marcos, reporting from a distance, described Cíbola as a great city, feeding extravagant expectations. On the strength of this report, Mendoza dispatched Coronado's expedition of 1540–1542, which found at Cíbola not golden cities but the adobe pueblos of the Zuni, and after taking Hawikuh travelled as far as central Kansas (Quivira) in search of further riches, returning empty-handed.

Legacy

Though the cities proved illusory, the search for them led Europeans across vast areas of the Southwest and Great Plains and into first contact with many Indigenous peoples. The names Cíbola and Quivira survived in Spanish place-lore and later in literature and popular culture, and the legend exemplifies the pattern of European exploration driven by expectations of sudden wealth, parallel to El Dorado in South America.
