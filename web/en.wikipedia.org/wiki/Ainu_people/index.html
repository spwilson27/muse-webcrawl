The Ainu are an indigenous ethnic group of northern Japan and eastern Russia, native to Hokkaido, the northern part of Honshu, Sakhalin and the Kuril Islands. They are distinguished from the ethnic Japanese (Yamato) population by their language, culture and history, and are believed to descend largely from the Jōmon-period peoples of northern Japan.

Culture

Traditional Ainu society was based on hunting, fishing and gathering, with salmon and deer as staples. Ainu religion centres on kamuy, spiritual beings inhabiting animals, plants and natural phenomena; the most important ceremony was the iomante, the sending-back of a bear spirit. Ainu oral literature, especially the yukar epics, is a major tradition, and Ainu crafts — elm-bark cloth (attush) and carved wood — are noted for distinctive spiral and thorn patterns.

History

From the medieval period, Japanese expansion pushed into Ainu lands (Ezo). The Ainu resisted in conflicts such as Shakushain's revolt (1669–1672) and the Menashi–Kunashir rebellion (1789). After the Meiji Restoration, Hokkaido was formally annexed and colonised; the 1899 Hokkaido Former Aborigines Protection Act imposed assimilation, banned many customs and appropriated land, while officially classifying the Ainu as "former aborigines".

Recognition

The assimilation act was repealed in 1997 by the Ainu Culture Promotion Act. In 2008 the Japanese parliament recognised the Ainu as an indigenous people of Japan, and the 2019 Ainu Policy Promotion Act ("Upopoy Act") was the first law to state this recognition explicitly and to prohibit discrimination. The national Ainu centre Upopoy opened in Shiraoi, Hokkaido, in 2020.
