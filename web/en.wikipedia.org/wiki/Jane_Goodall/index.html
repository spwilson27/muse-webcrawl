Jane Goodall

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jane Goodall (born 1934) is a British primatologist and anthropologist, considered the world's foremost expert on chimpanzees. Beginning in 1960 at Gombe Stream in Tanzania, under the mentorship of Louis Leakey, she observed chimpanzees making and using tools, hunting and eating meat, and displaying complex personalities and social bonds — findings that transformed understanding of primates and of what distinguishes humans.

Research

Goodall's long-term field study — among the longest continuous wildlife studies — documented chimpanzee tool use (termite fishing with stripped stems), warfare between communities, adoption, and individual life histories across generations. Naming rather than numbering her subjects and crediting them with emotions was initially criticised as unscientific; it is now widely accepted practice in primatology.

Conservation

In 1977 she founded the Jane Goodall Institute for wildlife research, conservation and community-centred development, and in 1991 the Roots & Shoots youth programme. She travels extensively speaking on conservation, animal welfare and hope, and was named a UN Messenger of Peace in 2002.
