Louis XIV

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Louis XIV (5 September 1638 – 1 September 1715), also known as Louis the Great or the Sun King, was King of France from 1643 until his death in 1715 — a reign of 72 years and 110 days, the longest verified of any monarch of a sovereign European state. Succeeding at age four under the regency of his mother Anne of Austria and Cardinal Mazarin, he assumed personal rule in 1661 and made himself the emblem of absolute monarchy: the court at Versailles, to which he moved the government in 1682, domesticated the great nobility in attendance on the king, whose person and ritual embodied the state — in the phrase tradition attributes to him, “L'état, c'est moi” (“I am the state”; its authenticity is doubted).

His reign centralised France (intendants, a standing army under Louvois, Colbert's mercantilist finances), revoked the Edict of Nantes (1685), driving out perhaps 200,000 Huguenots, patronised arts and letters on a regal scale (Molière, Racine, Lully, the Academy), and fought a series of great wars — against the Dutch, the League of Augsburg and, finally, the War of the Spanish Succession (1701–14), which placed his grandson on the Spanish throne but left France financially exhausted. He died in 1715, succeeded by his five-year-old great-grandson Louis XV. Historians judge the reign as the high point of French power and the seedbed of the fiscal crisis that destroyed the monarchy in 1789.
