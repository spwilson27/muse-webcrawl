Indoor tanning

Indoor tanning is the practice of using artificial devices that emit ultraviolet (UV) radiation — most commonly tanning beds and tanning booths — to produce a cosmetic tan. The devices use fluorescent lamps or high-pressure bulbs that emit primarily UVA radiation, and in some designs UVB, stimulating melanin production in the skin in a process called melanogenesis.

History

Sunlamps were used for supposed health benefits in the early 20th century, but commercial indoor tanning grew rapidly in the 1970s and 1980s, particularly in northern Europe and North America, where salons offered year-round tanning regardless of weather. At its peak, the industry served tens of millions of customers annually in the United States alone.

Health effects

Health authorities warn that indoor tanning significantly increases the risk of skin cancer. In 2009 the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified UV-emitting tanning devices as Group 1 carcinogens — "carcinogenic to humans" — the same category as tobacco and asbestos. Studies associate tanning-bed use, especially before the age of 35, with sharply elevated risks of melanoma, as well as squamous cell and basal cell carcinoma, premature skin ageing, and eye damage. The intensity of UV from some beds can be several times that of the midday sun.

Regulation

Many jurisdictions restrict indoor tanning. Brazil and Australia have banned commercial solariums outright, while numerous US states and European countries prohibit use by minors, require protective eyewear and warning labels, or license salons. The United States has levied a federal excise tax on indoor tanning services since 2010. Use has declined in many countries as awareness of the risks has grown and spray tanning and self-tanning products have become alternatives.
