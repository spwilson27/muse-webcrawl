Tax treaty

A tax treaty, also called a double taxation agreement (DTA) or double tax convention, is an agreement between two countries that allocates taxing rights over cross-border income and provides relief from double taxation — the same income being fully taxed in both the taxpayer's country of residence and the country where the income arises. There are more than 3,000 bilateral tax treaties in force worldwide, most based on the OECD Model Tax Convention or the similar UN Model.

Typical provisions

A treaty generally: defines residence and resolves "tie-breaker" cases of dual residence; limits source-country withholding taxes on dividends, interest and royalties; provides that business profits are taxable in the source state only if attributable to a permanent establishment there; sets rules for employment income, pensions, capital gains (usually reserving rights over land-rich entities), artists and sportspeople; and grants relief by exemption or by a foreign tax credit. Treaties also contain administrative provisions: exchange of information, mutual agreement procedures for resolving disputes, and increasingly, anti-abuse rules such as limitation-on-benefits articles and the principal purpose test introduced by the OECD's BEPS project and the 2017 Multilateral Instrument (MLI), which modified thousands of treaties at once.

History and issues

The first modern treaties date from the late nineteenth century, and the League of Nations developed model conventions in the 1920s. Critics argue the network favours capital-exporting states, that treaties signed by developing countries surrender taxing rights for uncertain investment gains, and that treaty shopping — routing investment through conduit entities — erodes bases. The OECD and G20 BEPS reforms and the global minimum tax (Pillar Two) mark continuing efforts to rebalance the system.
