Thomas Tompion, "the father of English watchmaking".

Thomas Tompion (baptised 25 July 1639 – 20 November 1713) was an English clock and watch maker, widely considered the greatest of his age and often called "the father of English watchmaking". His workshop produced some 300 clocks and 5,500 watches, and his standards of precision helped establish London as the centre of the horological world for a century.

Life

Tompion was born in Northill, Bedfordshire, the son of a blacksmith, and began as an apprentice in his father's trade. By 1671 he was in London, joining the Worshipful Company of Clockmakers in 1671 and becoming its Master in 1703. He set up at the sign of the Dial and Three Crowns in Fleet Street. Through his connection with the scientist Robert Hooke, Tompion gained the patronage of Charles II and made some of the earliest watches with balance springs, at the centre of the famous priority dispute between Hooke and Christiaan Huygens. In 1676 he made two pioneering year-going clocks for the new Royal Observatory at Greenwich at the request of the first Astronomer Royal, John Flamsteed.

Work

Tompion's clocks — longcase ("grandfather") clocks, bracket and spring clocks, and turret clocks — were distinguished by superb workmanship, restrained design, and systematic numbering, an innovation that also helps historians date his output today. He is credited with the invention (with Edward Barlow and William Houghton) of the cylinder escapement patented in 1695, and with early use of the Sautuola–Nunez style repeating mechanism for watches. His most famous surviving piece is the "Mostyn Tompion" (No. 32), a year-going spring clock of exceptional quality. His workshop was continued by his nephew and successor George Graham, inventor of the deadbeat escapement, who had begun as Tompion's apprentice.

Legacy

Tompion was buried in Westminster Abbey — a rare honour for a tradesman — sharing a grave later used also by Graham. His name became a byword for English horological excellence; genuine Tompion clocks and watches are among the most prized by collectors, and major examples are held by the British Museum and the Royal Collection.
