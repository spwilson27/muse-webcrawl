Chemical weapons in World War I

Chemical weapons in WWI
First large-scale useChlorine at the Second Battle of Ypres, 22 April 1915
Main agentsChlorine, phosgene, mustard gas, tear gases

Legal backdropHague Conventions of 1899 and 1907

Chemical weapons in World War I saw the first large-scale battlefield use of poison gas. Despite the Hague Conventions' prohibitions on projectiles diffusing asphyxiating gases, all major belligerents developed and deployed chemical agents, delivered by cylinder release, artillery shell, and projector.

Development

Germany led development under chemist Fritz Haber. The first lethal large-scale attack released chlorine at the Second Battle of Ypres on 22 April 1915. Phosgene, more lethal, followed (first used December 1915), and mustard gas — a persistent blister agent — was introduced by Germany in July 1917 near Ypres, earning it the French name Ypérite. The Allies responded in kind, developing their own production and the Livens Projector.

Effects and legacy

Gas caused roughly 1.3 million casualties but a relatively small share of total war deaths; its main effects were terror, incapacitation, and the burden of protective equipment as gas masks improved. The horror of gas warfare led to the Geneva Protocol of 1925 banning chemical and biological weapons use in war, a precursor to the 1993 Chemical Weapons Convention.
