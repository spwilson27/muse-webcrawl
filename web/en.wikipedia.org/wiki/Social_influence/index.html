Social influence

Other people continuously shape our judgements and behaviour
FieldSocial psychology
Main typesConformity, compliance, obedience, persuasion, minority influence
Classic studiesSherif (1936), Asch (1951), Milgram (1963), Moscovici (1969)
Key distinctionNormative vs informational influence (Deutsch & Gerard, 1955)

Social influence refers to the ways in which individuals adjust their behaviour, attitudes, beliefs and opinions in response to the real or imagined presence of others. It is a central topic in social psychology and ranges from subtle, automatic effects such as mimicry to deliberate persuasion campaigns and coercive authority.

Types of influence

Conformity is changing one's behaviour or beliefs to match those of a group. Muzafer Sherif's autokinetic experiments (1936) showed group norms forming in ambiguous situations, while Solomon Asch's line-judgement experiments (1951) showed participants publicly agreeing with an obviously wrong majority. Compliance is acceding to a direct request, studied through techniques such as foot-in-the-door and door-in-the-face. Obedience is following a direct order from an authority figure, most famously in Stanley Milgram's shock experiments (1963), in which a majority of participants administered what they believed were dangerous shocks when instructed by an experimenter.

Normative and informational influence

Deutsch and Gerard (1955) distinguished normative influence — conforming to gain acceptance and avoid rejection — from informational influence — accepting others' judgements as evidence about reality, especially under uncertainty. Herbert Kelman further distinguished compliance (public agreement for reward or to avoid punishment), identification (adopting behaviour to maintain a valued relationship) and internalisation (genuine private acceptance).

Minority influence

Although majorities exert powerful influence, Serge Moscovici showed that consistent, confident minorities can also change majority views, typically producing slower, deeper private conversion rather than superficial public compliance. Minority influence is an important driver of innovation and social change.

Applications

Research on social influence informs marketing and advertising, health campaigns, organisational management, political communication and the design of interventions that use descriptive norms — for example, telling hotel guests that most guests reuse their towels. Contemporary research also examines influence in online social networks, including viral spread, echo chambers and the effects of social proof in digital interfaces.
