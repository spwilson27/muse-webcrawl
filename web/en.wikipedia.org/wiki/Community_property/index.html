Community property

[Image: Community property jurisdictions in the US]

Community property is a form of marital property regime under which most property acquired during a marriage is presumed to belong equally to both spouses. It originated in civil law jurisdictions, deriving from Spanish and French law, and contrasts with the common law system of separate property used in most other places.

In the United States, nine states follow community property as their default regime: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin. Alaska, South Dakota and Tennessee allow couples to opt in to community property arrangements. Property owned before marriage, and property acquired during marriage by gift or inheritance, generally remains the acquiring spouse's separate property, while earnings and acquisitions during the marriage are community property owned in equal undivided shares.

Consequences

On divorce, community property is typically divided equally (in California and New Mexico, equally by law; in other states, in a manner the court finds just and right). On the death of a spouse, each spouse may dispose of their half of the community property by will. Community property also has significant federal tax consequences in the United States: the entire community asset receives a stepped-up basis at the death of the first spouse, rather than only the decedent's half.

Origins

The regime descends from the Visigothic code and the customs of Castile, under which the gananciales (gains of the marriage) were shared. It spread through the Spanish and French colonial empires, which is why it persists in former Spanish and French territories in the Americas, and analogous regimes exist in many civil-law countries.
