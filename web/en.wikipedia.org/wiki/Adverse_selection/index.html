Adverse selection

Adverse selection is a market situation in which one party to a transaction has more information than the other, and this information asymmetry tends to drive higher-quality goods and lower-risk participants out of the market, leaving a pool dominated by lower-quality goods or higher-risk participants. The term originated in insurance, where it describes the tendency of people who most expect to claim to be the most eager to buy cover.

The market for lemons

The concept was formalised by George Akerlof in his 1970 paper "The Market for 'Lemons': Quality Uncertainty and the Market Mechanism". In a market for used cars, sellers know whether their car is a good "peach" or a defective "lemon", but buyers cannot tell. Buyers therefore offer only an average price, at which owners of good cars refuse to sell; the average quality of cars actually offered falls, prices fall further, and the market can unravel entirely. Akerlof shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics for this work, with Michael Spence and Joseph Stiglitz.

Insurance

In insurance, adverse selection arises because insurers cannot perfectly observe each applicant's risk. If premiums are set at the average risk, low-risk customers may find cover poor value and leave, raising the average risk — and hence premiums — in a self-reinforcing "death spiral". Insurers counter this with risk classification, medical underwriting, deductibles, and waiting periods.

Solutions

  • Signalling — the informed party credibly conveys quality (e.g. warranties, education credentials).
  • Screening — the uninformed party designs menus of contracts that induce self-selection.
  • Regulation — mandatory participation (as in some health-insurance systems), standardised disclosure, and certification.
  • Reputation and intermediation — dealers, inspectors, and review systems that verify quality.

Related concepts

Adverse selection is a pre-contractual problem of hidden information; it is contrasted with moral hazard, a post-contractual problem of hidden action. Both fall under the economics of information asymmetry and agency theory.
