The Metaxas Line was a chain of fortifications constructed along the Greek–Bulgarian border during the regime of Ioannis Metaxas, designed to protect Greece from a Bulgarian invasion. Built between 1936 and 1941, the line consisted of a series of underground complexes and independent forts — including Rupel, Istibey, Lisse, and Echinos — connected by tunnels and equipped with artillery, machine-gun positions, observation posts, and anti-tank defences. Some complexes extended deep into mountainsides, with underground barracks, hospitals, and generators.

Battle of the Metaxas Line

When Germany invaded Greece in April 1941 (Operation Marita), German forces attacked the line frontally while other units advanced through Yugoslavia. Despite the rapid collapse elsewhere, many Metaxas Line forts resisted fiercely. Fort Rupel and others held out for days against infantry, artillery, and Stuka dive-bombers, and several garrisons surrendered only after the armistice, when ordered to do so; German officers reportedly saluted some Greek commanders as a mark of respect. The line was ultimately outflanked through the Yugoslav corridor rather than broken head-on.

Legacy

Several forts, notably Rupel and Istibey, are preserved as museums with annual commemorations. The Metaxas Line remains a symbol of Greek resistance in the Second World War, alongside the Greco-Italian War of 1940–41.
