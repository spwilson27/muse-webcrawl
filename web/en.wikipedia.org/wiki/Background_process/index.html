Background process

Listing background jobs in a Unix shell
TypeComputer process
Related conceptsForeground process, daemon, job control

A background process is a computer process that runs in the background of an operating system without direct user interaction, in contrast to a foreground process, which occupies the user's terminal or window. Background processes allow a user or system to carry out other tasks concurrently; they are fundamental to multitasking and underpin system services, batch jobs, and long-running computations.

In Unix and Linux

In Unix shells, a command can be launched in the background by appending an ampersand (&) to the command line. The shell's job control facilities let users list jobs (jobs), bring a job to the foreground (fg), resume a stopped job in the background (bg), and suspend the foreground job with Ctrl-Z. Utilities such as nohup and disown detach a process from the terminal so it survives logout, while schedulers like cron and at start background processes at specified times.

Daemons and services

Long-lived background processes that provide system services are called daemons on Unix-like systems and services on Microsoft Windows. Examples include web servers, database servers, print spoolers, and system logging. Modern init systems such as systemd manage their startup, restart policies, and logging.

On other platforms

On desktop and mobile platforms, background execution is more tightly controlled. iOS and Android restrict background activity to preserve battery life, permitting limited background tasks such as audio playback, location updates, and background fetch under operating-system supervision. On Windows, applications may run minimized in the system tray, and the Task Manager displays background processes separately from interactive apps.

Priority

Background processes are often scheduled at lower priority so interactive work remains responsive. On Unix systems the nice command adjusts a process's scheduling priority; CPU- and I/O-intensive background jobs can likewise be throttled by resource control mechanisms such as cgroups.
