Chimera (mythology)

The Chimera (Greek: Χίμαιρα, Chímaira) is a monstrous fire-breathing hybrid creature of Greek mythology, composed of the parts of more than one animal. Depicted as a lion, with the head of a goat arising from its back, and a tail that ends in a snake's head, the Chimera was said to have terrorised Lycia in Asia Minor. It was the offspring of Typhon and Echidna and the sibling of monsters such as Cerberus and the Hydra. The hero Bellerophon, riding the winged horse Pegasus, slew the Chimera at the command of King Iobates of Lycia.

Mythology

Homer describes the Chimera in the Iliad as "a thing of immortal make, not human, lion-fronted and snake behind, a goat in the middle, and snorting out the breath of the terrible flame of bright fire." Hesiod's Theogony gives it three heads — lion, goat and dragon. Bellerophon defeated the beast by following the instruction to tip his spear with lead: mounted on Pegasus, he thrust the spear into the Chimera's mouth, where its fiery breath melted the lead, which flowed down its throat and killed it.

In art and culture

The Chimera of Arezzo, a bronze statue discovered in Tuscany in 1553, is among the finest surviving works of Etruscan art. In later usage, "chimera" came to denote any imaginary composite creature, and by extension a fanciful, impossible idea or hope. The term was adopted by biology for organisms composed of genetically distinct cells, and in palaeontology for fossil assemblages mistakenly combining bones of different species.
