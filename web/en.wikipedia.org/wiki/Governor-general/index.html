Governor-general

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A governor-general (plural governors-general) is the representative of a monarch in an independent realm or a senior official in a former colonial government. In the modern Commonwealth realms — the sovereign states that share the same monarch — the governor-general acts as the monarch's viceregal representative, performing the head-of-state functions in the monarch's absence, while in practice acting on the advice of the realm's government.

Governors-general are typically appointed by the monarch on the advice of the realm's prime minister, for terms of about five years. Their formal powers resemble those of the monarch in a constitutional monarchy: appointing the prime minister, assenting to legislation, summoning and dissolving parliament, and serving as commander-in-chief. Reserve powers, exercisable in exceptional circumstances without or against ministerial advice, survive in theory and were famously used in the Australian constitutional crisis of 1975, when Governor-General Sir John Kerr dismissed Prime Minister Gough Whitlam.

History

The office descends from colonial governors-general who governed groups of colonies or large territories — in British North America, French Indochina, the Dutch East Indies and elsewhere. As colonies gained independence as realms, the office became a domestic, non-political headship of state embodied in the Crown's representative. Several former realms, including India (1947–1950) and Ireland, had governors-general before becoming republics.

Current realms

Fifteen Commonwealth realms exist as of the mid-2020s; most, including Canada, Australia, New Zealand and several Caribbean and Pacific states, have governors-general. A governor-general is styled Her/His Excellency and in some realms is increasingly a figure drawn from public life rather than the aristocracy or military.
