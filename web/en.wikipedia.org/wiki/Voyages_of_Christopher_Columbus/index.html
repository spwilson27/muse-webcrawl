Between 1492 and 1504, the Genoese explorer Christopher Columbus led four transatlantic voyages sponsored by the Crown of Castile, seeking a westward sea route to Asia. His expeditions were the first documented European voyages to the Caribbean, Central America and South America since the Norse voyages centuries earlier, and they inaugurated sustained contact between Europe and the Americas.

First voyage (1492–1493)

Columbus sailed on 3 August 1492 from Palos de la Frontera with the carrack Santa María and the caravels Niña and Pinta. On 12 October he made landfall in the Bahamas — an island he named San Salvador, inhabited by the Taíno people — believing he had reached the East Indies. He went on to explore Cuba and Hispaniola, where the Santa María was wrecked; the settlement of La Navidad was founded from its timbers. He returned to Spain in March 1493 to a hero's reception.

Second voyage (1493–1496)

With 17 ships and over a thousand men, the second voyage was an expedition of colonisation. Columbus charted much of the Lesser Antilles, Puerto Rico and Jamaica, founded the settlement of La Isabela on Hispaniola, and instituted the exploitation of the Taíno population through tribute and enslavement.

Third voyage (1498–1500)

Sailing further south, Columbus reached Trinidad and the mainland of South America at the Orinoco delta, recognising a "very great continent". Complaints about his governance led the Crown to send Francisco de Bobadilla, who arrested Columbus and shipped him back to Spain in chains; he was freed but lost his governorship.

Fourth voyage (1502–1504)

On his final voyage Columbus explored the coasts of Honduras, Nicaragua, Costa Rica and Panama in a last search for a passage to Asia, surviving storms, shipworm and a year marooned on Jamaica before rescue. He returned to Spain in November 1504 and died in Valladolid in 1506.

Legacy

The voyages opened the era of European colonisation of the Americas and the Columbian exchange of plants, animals, technologies and diseases. Their consequences for Indigenous peoples were catastrophic, including massive population loss from disease and violence — a legacy that has led to sustained modern reassessment of Columbus commemoration.
