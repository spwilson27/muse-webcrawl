Investigative genetic genealogy (IGG), also called forensic genetic genealogy, is the emerging practice of utilizing genetic genealogy principles to help identify suspects or unidentified bodies in cold cases. The technique combines DNA analysis with traditional genealogical research, using publicly available direct-to-consumer genetic databases to find relatives of an unknown person and build family trees until the individual can be identified.

Unlike traditional forensic DNA databases such as CODIS, which store short tandem repeat profiles of offenders and arrestees, IGG uses dense single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) profiles of the kind generated by consumer testing companies, which can detect matches with relatives as distant as third or fourth cousins.

History

Genetic genealogy had been used by hobbyists and adoptees searching for biological relatives since the 2000s. Its forensic use broke into public view on 24 April 2018, when the FBI and Sacramento County authorities announced the arrest of Joseph James DeAngelo as the Golden State Killer. Investigators had uploaded a SNP profile derived from crime-scene DNA to the GEDmatch database, found distant cousins of the suspect, and — with genealogist Barbara Rae-Venter's help — built out family trees converging on DeAngelo, whose identity was confirmed by a surreptitiously collected DNA sample.

Method

  1. A SNP profile is generated from crime-scene or unidentified-remains DNA, often of low quantity or quality.
  2. The profile is uploaded to databases permitting law-enforcement matching (principally GEDmatch and Family Tree DNA, whose users may opt in).
  3. Matches are ranked by shared DNA measured in centiMorgans; genealogists construct the matches' family trees backward to common ancestral couples, then forward to living descendants of the right age, sex, and location.
  4. A candidate is confirmed by conventional STR DNA comparison.

Cases and impact

Within a few years of the DeAngelo arrest, IGG had been credited in hundreds of cases, including the identification of the "Bear Brook" victims' killer (Terry Peder Rasmussen) and many long-unidentified bodies ("Does"), such as the Buckskin Girl (Marcia King). Companies and laboratories, including Parabon NanoLabs and Othram, commercialized the workflow, and genealogists such as CeCe Moore became prominent practitioners.

Ethics, privacy, and regulation

The technique raises privacy questions because a person's DNA can implicate relatives who never consented to testing. GEDmatch changed its terms in 2019 so that law-enforcement matching applied only to users who opted in, after public backlash over use of the database in a non-homicide assault case. Maryland enacted the first comprehensive statute regulating forensic genetic genealogy in 2021, requiring judicial authorization and limiting use to serious crimes; the U.S. Department of Justice issued an interim policy in 2019 restricting federal use of the technique.
