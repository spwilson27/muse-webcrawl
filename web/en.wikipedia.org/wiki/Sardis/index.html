Sardis

Sardis (Lydian: Sfard; Greek: Σάρδεις, Sardeis) was an ancient city at the location of modern Sart, near Salihli in Manisa Province, Turkey. It was the capital of the kingdom of Lydia, one of the important cities of the Persian Achaemenid Empire, and later a significant Roman and Byzantine centre. Sardis is famed as the place where coinage was first minted, under King Alyattes in the 7th century BCE, and as the home of the fabulously wealthy King Croesus.

History

Sardis stood at the western end of the Persian Royal Road, on the Pactolus stream at the foot of Mount Tmolus. Under the Mermnad dynasty — Gyges, Alyattes and Croesus — Lydia became a wealthy empire dominating western Anatolia, enriched by the electrum and gold washed from the Pactolus and by Sardis' gold-refining industry; the Lydians are credited with inventing coinage. Croesus' conquest by Cyrus the Great in 546 BCE made Sardis a Persian satrapal capital.

The city passed to Alexander the Great, then to Hellenistic kingdoms, and in 133 BCE to Rome. The great Temple of Artemis, one of the largest Ionic temples, and a monumental marble gymnasium-bath complex and one of the largest known ancient synagogues attest its Roman prosperity. Sardis was one of the seven churches of Asia addressed in the Book of Revelation. The city declined after earthquakes and the Persian and Arab invasions of the 7th century CE, dwindling to a village under its citadel.

Excavations

Sardis has been excavated since 1958 by the Harvard–Cornell Archaeological Exploration of Sardis, which uncovered the Lydian gold refinery, city walls, the gymnasium (restored in part), the synagogue, and extensive Roman and Byzantine remains.
