Sociobiology

Sociobiology is a field of biology that aims to explain social behaviour in terms of evolution, considering animal behaviour — including, controversially, human behaviour — as a product of natural selection. It was launched as a named discipline by E. O. Wilson's Sociobiology: The New Synthesis (1975), which systematised research on altruism, aggression, mating systems, parental care and social insects using concepts such as kin selection and inclusive fitness developed by W. D. Hamilton, reciprocal altruism (Robert Trivers) and parental investment theory.

Content

Sociobiology explains apparently self-sacrificing behaviour — sterile worker castes in ants and bees, alarm calls in ground squirrels — by gene-level selection: a gene promoting help to relatives can spread because relatives share copies of it (Hamilton's rule, rB > C). Wilson's synthesis covered communication, dominance hierarchies, territoriality, and the evolution of eusociality. Richard Dawkins's The Selfish Gene (1976) popularised the gene-centred view for a general audience.

Controversy

The final chapter of Wilson's book, applying sociobiology to humans, provoked intense criticism from scientists and activists, including the Sociobiology Study Group (among them Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin), who warned of genetic determinism and feared the work would be used to justify existing hierarchies of sex, race and class. Wilson and defenders replied that explaining a behaviour's evolutionary origin does not endorse it (the naturalistic fallacy). Much of the study of human behaviour in this tradition subsequently proceeded under the label evolutionary psychology, while sociobiology remains a standard term for the evolutionary study of animal social behaviour.
