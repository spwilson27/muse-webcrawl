Yamabe problem

The Yamabe problem is a question in differential geometry: given a compact Riemannian manifold (M, g) of dimension at least three, does there exist a metric conformal to g whose scalar curvature is constant? The problem is named after the Japanese mathematician Hidehiko Yamabe, who stated and claimed to solve it in a 1960 paper.

History

Yamabe's original proof contained an error, identified by Neil Trudinger in 1968, who resolved the problem in the case of non-positive Yamabe invariant. Thierry Aubin extended the result in 1976, proving it whenever the Yamabe invariant of the manifold is strictly less than that of the round sphere — in particular for manifolds of dimension at least six that are not locally conformally flat. The remaining cases were settled by Richard Schoen in 1984, whose argument used the positive mass theorem of general relativity. The combined work of Yamabe, Trudinger, Aubin, and Schoen therefore gives a complete affirmative solution.

Formulation

Writing the conformal metric as ĝ = u4/(n−2) g for a positive smooth function u, the requirement of constant scalar curvature reduces to a semilinear elliptic partial differential equation of the form −Δgu + c(n)Rgu = λu(n+2)/(n−2), where the exponent is critical for the Sobolev embedding. This criticality is the source of the problem's difficulty: variational minimizing sequences may lose compactness by "bubbling off" a spherical solution. The minimizing constant, the Yamabe invariant (or sigma constant), has become an important conformal invariant of smooth manifolds.

Related topics

The non-compact analogue of the problem remains only partially understood. A related gradient flow, the Yamabe flow introduced by Richard Hamilton, deforms a metric within its conformal class in the direction of constant scalar curvature. The problem is closely related to the prescribed scalar curvature (Kazdan–Warner) problem and to the Riemannian geometry of Einstein metrics.
