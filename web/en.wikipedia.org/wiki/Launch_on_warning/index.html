Launch on warning

Launch on warning (LOW) is a nuclear weapons strategy in which a country retaliates to a perceived nuclear attack by launching its own missiles as soon as sensors detect incoming warheads, before those warheads arrive — that is, on the warning of an attack rather than after absorbing one. It is closely related to the concept of launch under attack.

Rationale

The posture is intended to prevent an adversary from destroying land-based missiles (particularly those in fixed silos) in a first strike: if missiles can be launched within the roughly 15–30 minutes of warning provided by early-warning satellites and radars, they cannot be caught on the ground. Supporters argue this strengthens deterrence; critics note that the compressed decision timeline leaves little room to verify that a warning is genuine.

False alarms

The dangers of relying on warning were illustrated by incidents such as the 1983 Soviet nuclear false alarm, when Stanislav Petrov judged a satellite's report of incoming US missiles to be a false alarm, and the 1995 Norwegian rocket incident, when a scientific rocket briefly triggered Russian early-warning systems. Neither the United States nor Russia publicly confirms a formal launch-on-warning doctrine, though both maintain the technical capability to retaliate rapidly.
