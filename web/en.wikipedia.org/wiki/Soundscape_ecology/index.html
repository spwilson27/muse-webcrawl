Soundscape ecology

Soundscape ecology is the study of the acoustic relationships between living organisms, human and other, and their environment. The field, formalized in a 2011 special issue of Landscape Ecology and in the work of Bernie Krause, Bryan Pijanowski, Almo Farina and others, applies the concepts of landscape ecology — pattern, scale, and spatial heterogeneity — to the sounds produced in a landscape, collectively called the soundscape.

Components of the soundscape

Soundscape ecology divides the soundscape into three sources: biophony, the sounds produced by organisms (bird song, insect calls, amphibian choruses); geophony, natural non-biological sounds such as wind, rain, and running water; and anthrophony, sounds produced by humans and their technology. The acoustic niche hypothesis holds that species partition the available frequency bands and time windows so that their signals do not mask one another.

Methods

Researchers deploy autonomous recording units that collect long-duration audio in the field, then analyze recordings with acoustic indices (such as acoustic diversity and entropy measures) and spectrogram inspection to infer species presence, community structure, and ecosystem condition. Because recording is non-invasive and repeatable, passive acoustic monitoring is increasingly used in conservation biology and environmental assessment.

Applications and threats

Studies have used soundscapes to track recovery after disturbance, compare protected and exploited habitats, and document the masking effects of anthropogenic noise on animal communication. Rising anthrophony from transport, industry, and resource extraction is argued to degrade acoustic habitats in much the same way as physical habitat loss.
