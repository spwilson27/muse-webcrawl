Tomography

FieldMedical imaging, geophysics, materials science
UsesCT, PET, SPECT, seismic imaging, industrial inspection
Key mathematicsRadon transform, filtered back projection, iterative reconstruction

Tomography is imaging by sections through the use of penetrating waves of energy. It is used in radiology, archaeology, biology, geophysics, oceanography, materials science, astrophysics and other sciences. The word derives from the Ancient Greek tomos (slice) and graphein (to write). A device used for tomography is called a tomograph; the image produced is a tomogram.

Principle

In most tomographic methods, measurements are made of energy transmitted through (or emitted from within) an object along many lines and directions. Because a single projection mixes information from all points along its path, many views from different angles are combined to recover the object's internal structure. The mathematical foundation is the Radon transform (1917), which shows that a function can be reconstructed from the set of its line integrals; practical algorithms include filtered back projection and iterative methods such as ART and SIRT.

Types

Computed tomography (CT) uses X-ray projections reconstructed with the method developed by Allan McLeod Cormack and built into a practical scanner by Godfrey Hounsfield at EMI in the early 1970s, earning both the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Emission tomography — positron emission tomography (PET) and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) — images radiotracers distributed within the body. Magnetic resonance imaging applies related reconstruction in Fourier space. In geophysics, seismic tomography uses earthquake waves to map the Earth's mantle; electrical resistivity tomography and muon tomography image everything from archaeology to volcano interiors.
