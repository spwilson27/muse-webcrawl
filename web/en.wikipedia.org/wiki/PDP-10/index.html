PDP-10

The PDP-10 was a mainframe computer family manufactured by Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) from 1966 into the early 1980s. It was the successor to DEC's PDP-6, sharing the same 36-bit architecture. PDP-10 systems running the TOPS-10 or TENEX operating systems were enormously influential in university and research computing, and were central to the early ARPANET and hacker culture.

Architecture

The PDP-10 used 36-bit words and a 36-bit (later extended) address space. Instructions were stored in one word, with a 9-bit opcode, a 4-bit register field, and an indirect bit plus index register field and address field. There were 16 general-purpose registers, which were also the first 16 locations of memory.

Models included the original KA10 processor, the KI10, and the later KL10 and KS10. The KL10 was used in the DECsystem-10 and DECSYSTEM-20 product lines.

Software and influence

PDP-10s ran TOPS-10, TENEX (developed at Bolt, Beranek and Newman), and ITS (developed at MIT). Stanford's SAIL, CMU, and MIT's AI Lab all ran PDP-10 systems. The machine hosted early versions of Emacs, the Zork text adventure, and the Colossal Cave Adventure. Much early Lisp and AI research was conducted on PDP-10 hardware, and the machine was a mainstay of the ARPANET, with many of its host computers running TENEX.

Cancellation

DEC cancelled the PDP-10 line in 1983, including the in-development "Jupiter" (KC10) project, to focus on its 32-bit VAX line. The cancellation was unpopular with the large installed base, and the architecture continued in use at some sites into the 1990s. The 36-bit instruction set lives on in emulators such as SIMH and KLH10, and in the XKL Toad-1 systems built afterwards.
