Code of silence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A code of silence enforces loyalty by forbidding members of a group from informing on each other.

A code of silence is an informal rule or social norm within a group not to report the misconduct of group members to outsiders, and not to cooperate with external investigations. Codes of silence are enforced by loyalty, fear of ostracism or retaliation, and the stigma attached to "snitching" or "ratting".

In organized crime

The most famous code of silence is omertà, the honour code of the Sicilian Mafia and related organizations, which obliges members never to cooperate with authorities and to endure imprisonment rather than inform. Omertà began to crack significantly only with the pentiti (repentant mafiosi) such as Tommaso Buscetta in the 1980s, whose testimony enabled the Maxi Trial prosecutions.

In policing

The "blue wall of silence" describes an alleged unwritten rule among police officers not to report colleagues' errors, misconduct, or brutality. It has been documented by commissions of inquiry such as the Knapp Commission in New York (1970s) and is widely cited as a barrier to accountability. Similar dynamics are studied in medicine (reluctance to report colleagues' errors), the military, prisons, and workplaces generally.

Consequences and countermeasures

Codes of silence protect wrongdoing, deter witnesses, and erode public trust. Countermeasures include whistleblower protections, anonymous reporting channels, witness protection programmes, and immunity deals for cooperating witnesses. In street culture, the "stop snitching" phenomenon has been criticized by community leaders for shielding violent criminals.
