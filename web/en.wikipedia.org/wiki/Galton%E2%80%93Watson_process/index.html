Galton–Watson process

The Galton–Watson process is a branching stochastic process arising from Francis Galton's statistical investigation of the extinction of family names. The process models a population in which each individual in generation n produces a random number of offspring according to a fixed probability distribution, independently of all other individuals, and those offspring form generation n+1.

History

Galton posed the problem of the probability of extinction of a surname in 1873, and the Reverend Henry William Watson replied with a solution in 1874. Their joint paper, "On the probability of the extinction of families", appeared in the Journal of the Anthropological Institute. The process was later studied in depth by Andrey Kolmogorov and others, and became a foundational model in the theory of branching processes.

Extinction criterion

If each individual has on average μ offspring, then the population dies out with probability one if μ ≤ 1 (except in the trivial case where every individual has exactly one offspring). If μ > 1, there is a positive probability that the population survives and grows indefinitely. The extinction probability is the smallest non-negative root of the equation s = G(s), where G is the probability-generating function of the offspring distribution.

Applications

Galton–Watson processes model the spread of epidemics, nuclear chain reactions, the propagation of computer viruses, and the survival of mutant genes in population genetics.
