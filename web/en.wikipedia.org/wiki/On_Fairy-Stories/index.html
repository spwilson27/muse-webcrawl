"On Fairy-Stories" is an essay by J. R. R. Tolkien on the nature of fairy tales and fantasy literature, first delivered as the Andrew Lang Lecture at the University of St Andrews in 1939 and published in 1947. It is regarded as a foundational text of modern fantasy criticism and the clearest statement of Tolkien's own creative theory, underpinning The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings.

Summary

Tolkien defines the fairy-story not by fairies but by Faërie, the realm of enchantment itself. He rejects the confinement of fairy tales to children, traces their origins in myth and folklore, and argues for their serious literary value.

Key concepts

The essay introduces sub-creation — the human storyteller as a "little maker" building a Secondary World with its own inner consistency, which the reader enters by willing suspension of disbelief. Fairy-stories offer three benefits: Recovery (seeing the familiar world afresh), Escape (not desertion but release from a narrow world), and Consolation, above all the eucatastrophe — the sudden joyous turn, a happy ending that denies universal final defeat. Tolkien closes with an epilogue linking the eucatastrophe of fairy-story to the Christian gospel.

Publication history

The lecture text was published in 1947 in Essays Presented to Charles Williams (Oxford University Press) and reprinted in Tree and Leaf (1964) alongside the story Leaf by Niggle. An expanded critical edition, edited by Verlyn Flieger and Douglas A. Anderson, appeared in 2008.
