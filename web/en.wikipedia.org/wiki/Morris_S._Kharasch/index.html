Morris Selig Kharasch (August 24, 1895 – October 9, 1957) was a pioneering organic chemist best known for his work with free radical reactions of alkenes, in particular his demonstration of the "peroxide effect," which explained why the addition of hydrogen bromide to alkenes could give the anti-Markovnikov product.

Life

Kharasch was born in the Russian Empire and emigrated to the United States at the age of thirteen, settling in Chicago. He studied chemistry at the University of Chicago, earning his PhD in 1919 under Jean Felix Piccard. After a period at the University of Maryland, he returned to the University of Chicago in 1924, where he spent the rest of his career, becoming a full professor in 1930.

Work

In 1933, Kharasch and Frank Mayo showed that traces of peroxides in allyl bromide samples reversed the regiochemistry of hydrogen bromide addition: through a radical-chain mechanism, the product obeyed the opposite of Markovnikov's rule. This "abnormal" or peroxide effect opened the field of radical addition chemistry and changed how chemists understood ionic versus radical mechanisms. Kharasch went on to develop atom-transfer reactions of polyhalomethanes and, with Benjamin Sosnovsky, the copper-catalysed allylic oxidation of alkenes with tert-butyl peresters known as the Kharasch–Sosnovsky reaction. During the Second World War he directed a laboratory in the American synthetic rubber programme, contributing to the development of GR-S rubber. He was awarded the Willard Gibbs Medal in 1953.
