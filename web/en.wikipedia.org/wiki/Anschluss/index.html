Anschluss

The Anschluss (German for "joining" or "connection"), also known as the Anschluss Österreichs, was the annexation of the Federal State of Austria into the German Reich on 13 March 1938. The idea of an Anschluss — uniting Austria and Germany into a "Greater Germany" — had begun after the union of Germany excluded Austria and the German Austrians from the Prussian-dominated German Empire in 1871. After the end of World War I and the fall of the Habsburg monarchy, the newly formed Republic of German-Austria attempted unification with Germany, but the Treaty of Versailles and the Treaty of Saint-Germain forbade it.

Events of 1938

Under growing pressure from Adolf Hitler, Austrian Chancellor Kurt Schuschnigg met Hitler at Berchtesgaden in February 1938 and was forced to appoint Austrian Nazis, including Arthur Seyss-Inquart, to his cabinet. When Schuschnigg announced a plebiscite on Austrian independence for 13 March, Hitler demanded his resignation. Schuschnigg resigned on 11 March and Seyss-Inquart became chancellor; on 12 March German troops crossed the border unopposed, welcomed by cheering crowds in many places. On 13 March, Seyss-Inquart signed the "Reunification of Austria with the German Reich" law. A referendum staged in April returned an official approval of over 99 percent.

Response and consequences

The Western powers protested only formally; Britain and France took no action, and appeasement continued. The annexation was followed immediately by the persecution of Austria's approximately 192,000 Jews, with public humiliations, arrests and the seizure of property; tens of thousands emigrated, and some 65,000 Austrian Jews were later murdered in the Holocaust. Austria ceased to exist as a state until 1945, when it was re-established; the 1943 Moscow Declaration had declared the Anschluss null and void, while also holding Austria responsible for participation in the war on Germany's side.
