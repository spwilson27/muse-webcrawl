Separation of concerns

In computer science, separation of concerns (SoC) is a design principle for separating a computer program into distinct sections, each of which addresses a separate concern — a set of information or functionality that affects the code of the program. A program that embodies the principle is modular: concerns are encapsulated so that each section can be developed, understood, and modified with minimal impact on the others.

History

The term is associated with Edsger W. Dijkstra, who argued in the 1970s that effective problem-solving requires focusing attention on one aspect of a problem at a time, even though the aspects are not truly independent. Related ideas include David Parnas's work on information hiding and modular decomposition, and the Unix philosophy of small, single-purpose tools.

Examples

Common realizations include the division of a web application into presentation, business logic, and data-access layers, as in the model–view–controller architectural pattern; the separation of document content (HTML) from presentation (CSS); and aspect-oriented programming, which tackles concerns such as logging or security that "cross-cut" many modules. Layered architectures, microservices, and modular libraries all apply the principle at different scales.

Benefits and limits

Separating concerns improves maintainability, reusability, and the ability of teams to work in parallel, but drawing module boundaries involves trade-offs: over-decomposition can scatter related logic and add indirection, and some concerns resist clean separation altogether.
