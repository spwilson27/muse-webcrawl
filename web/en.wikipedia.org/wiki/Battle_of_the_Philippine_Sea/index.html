The Battle of the Philippine Sea was a major naval battle of the Pacific War fought between the United States Navy and the Imperial Japanese Navy on 19–20 June 1944, during the American invasion of the Mariana Islands. It was the largest carrier-to-carrier battle in history, involving 15 American fleet and light carriers of the Fifth Fleet against nine Japanese carriers of the Mobile Fleet. The lopsided air fighting of 19 June, in which Japanese attacking aircraft were shot down in hundreds by radar-directed American fighters and anti-aircraft fire, was dubbed the "Great Marianas Turkey Shoot" by American aircrew.

Background

The Japanese plan, Operation A-Go, sought a decisive fleet action to destroy the American carrier force once it attacked the Marianas, which the Japanese high command regarded as part of Japan's inner line of defence. Vice Admiral Jisaburō Ozawa's Mobile Fleet could supplement its carrier aircraft with land-based planes from the Marianas, shuttling between fleet and shore bases. Unknown to Ozawa, American submarines and signals intelligence had given Admiral Raymond Spruance ample warning, and most of the Japanese land-based aircraft had already been destroyed in preliminary American strikes.

The battle

On 19 June Ozawa launched four large raids totalling over 370 aircraft. Forewarned by radar, Task Force 58's fighters — led by combat air patrols of F6F Hellcats — intercepted each wave far from the carriers. American losses were about 30 aircraft; Japanese losses exceeded 300, for negligible damage to the American fleet. Meanwhile American submarines torpedoed and sank two of Ozawa's largest carriers, Taihō (his flagship) and Shōkaku. On 20 June, Mitscher's carriers located the retreating Japanese fleet and launched a long-range strike at dusk, sinking the carrier Hiyō and damaging others; many American aircraft were lost to fuel exhaustion on the night return, though most crews were rescued. Japanese aircraft losses over the two days are estimated at 550–645, most of them irreplaceable trained aircrew.

Aftermath

The battle destroyed the Imperial Japanese Navy's carrier air power as an effective force; its carriers, though several hulls survived, could never again embark adequately trained air groups. The Marianas fell, placing Japan within range of B-29 bombers, and the Japanese government of Hideki Tojo fell in July 1944.
