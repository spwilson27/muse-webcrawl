Rug

A rug is a textile floor covering typically consisting of an upper layer of pile attached to a backing. Unlike a carpet, which in modern usage often covers an entire floor wall-to-wall, a rug covers only part of the floor and can be moved. Rugs have been made for thousands of years by weaving, knotting, tufting, braiding and hooking, in materials ranging from wool and silk to cotton, jute and synthetic fibres.

History

The oldest known pile rug, the Pazyryk carpet (c. 5th century BC), was preserved in a frozen Scythian burial mound in Siberia, demonstrating that sophisticated knotted-pile weaving is ancient. Oriental rugs—from Persia (Iran), Anatolia, the Caucasus, Central Asia, China and India—developed rich traditions of pattern, with motifs encoding tribal identity, religious symbolism and regional style. The great Persian carpets of the Safavid era, such as the Ardabil Carpet (1539–40), now in the Victoria and Albert Museum, are considered masterpieces of Islamic art.

Types and construction

Hand-knotted rugs are made by tying individual knots of yarn into a warp; the two main knot types are the asymmetric (Persian/Senneh) and symmetric (Turkish/Ghiordes) knots, with knot density a rough measure of quality. Other constructions include flat-woven kilims and dhurries, hand-tufted rugs (yarn punched through a backing), braided rugs and hooked rugs. Machine-made rugs, produced on power looms since the 19th century, made patterned floor coverings cheap and ubiquitous, though fine handmade rugs remain valuable trade and collector items—often subject to rules of origin labelling and, historically, concerns over child labour, addressed by certification schemes such as GoodWeave.

Uses

Beyond decoration and insulation, rugs define seating areas, protect floors, dampen sound, serve in prayer (the prayer rug or sajjada), and function as portable wealth and dowry goods in nomadic cultures. Care typically involves vacuuming, rotation to even wear, and professional cleaning for valuable pieces.
