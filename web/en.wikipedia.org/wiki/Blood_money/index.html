Blood money

Silver pieces, the archetypal "blood money" (placeholder).

Blood money is a term with several related meanings. In its oldest legal sense it is money paid by a killer, or by the killer's family or kin group, as compensation to the family of the victim. In a second sense it is money paid to the next of kin of a person who has been killed, as a form of damages. In a third, figurative sense, it denotes money obtained at the cost of another's life, or a payment for betraying or murdering someone — as in the thirty pieces of silver paid to Judas Iscariot.

Compensation systems

Many historical legal systems institutionalised compensation for homicide and injury. In early Germanic law, the wergild ("man price") set a tariff according to the victim's rank, payable to the kin to avert a blood feud. Anglo-Saxon law codes contain detailed schedules of such payments. In Islamic law, diyya is financial compensation payable to the victim or heirs in cases of homicide, bodily harm or damage to property; the Qur'an prescribes it as an alternative to retaliation (qisas) when the victim's family forgives. Similar institutions include the Irish éraic and the Welsh galanas.

Blood feud alternative

The central social function of blood money was to substitute a material settlement for cycles of revenge killing. Acceptance of payment by the victim's kin formally ended the feud; refusal could preserve the right of vengeance. As centralised states grew stronger, compensation increasingly went to the state as a fine, and homicide came to be treated as a public crime rather than a private wrong.

Figurative usage

In modern usage "blood money" most often condemns profits or payments morally tainted by death or betrayal — for example payments to informers, rewards for assassination, or compensation accepted by families after industrial disasters. The phrase gained currency through the biblical account of Judas, who, in the Gospel of Matthew, returns the silver in remorse, and the priests use it to buy the potter's field, called the "field of blood".
