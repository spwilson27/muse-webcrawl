Cohesion (chemistry)

In chemistry and physics, cohesion refers to the sticking together of alike molecules. It is the intrinsic property of a substance caused by the shape and structure of its molecules, which makes the distribution of orbiting electrons irregular when molecules get close to one another, creating electrical attraction that can maintain a macroscopic structure such as a water drop. Cohesion allows for surface tension, creating a "solid-like" state upon which light (in weight) materials can be placed.

Cohesion
CauseIntermolecular forces between like molecules
ContrastAdhesion (unlike molecules)
EffectsSurface tension; meniscus; capillary action (with adhesion)

Causes

Cohesive forces arise from van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonding, and, in metals, metallic bonding. Water exhibits especially strong cohesion because each molecule can form up to four hydrogen bonds with its neighbours. Mercury has even higher cohesion, which is why it forms nearly spherical beads and does not wet most surfaces.

Effects

Cohesion is responsible for surface tension, the tendency of a liquid's surface to resist rupture. Combined with adhesion—the attraction between unlike molecules—cohesion explains the meniscus formed by liquids in tubes and capillary action, by which water rises in narrow spaces and through the xylem of plants. The cohesion-tension theory holds that evaporation from leaves (transpiration) pulls continuous columns of water upward through tall trees because the cohesive water column resists breaking under tension.

Cohesive energy

The cohesive energy of a material is the energy required to separate its constituent atoms or molecules to infinite distance; it is related to latent heats of vaporisation and sublimation and is used in estimating solubility, for example in Hildebrand solubility parameters.
