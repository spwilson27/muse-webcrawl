Balak (Hebrew: בָּלָק Bālāq) was a king of Moab described in the Book of Numbers in the Hebrew Bible, where his dealings with the prophet Balaam form one of the best-known narratives of the Torah. According to Numbers 22–24, Balak, son of Zippor, ruled Moab at the time when the Israelites, having wandered from Egypt, camped on the plains of Moab opposite Jericho.

Biblical narrative

Alarmed by Israel's numbers and by their victories over the Amorite kings Sihon and Og, Balak joined with the elders of Midian and sent messengers to Balaam son of Beor at Pethor, offering him rich payment to curse the Israelites. After first refusing at God's command, Balaam eventually accompanied Balak's princes. On three occasions Balak built seven altars and offered sacrifices on high places overlooking the Israelite camp — Bamoth-Baal, Pisgah, and Peor — but each time Balaam, instead of cursing, pronounced blessings over Israel, including the oracle "How goodly are thy tents, O Jacob" (Numbers 24:5). Furious, Balak dismissed Balaam, who in departing delivered further prophecies, including the "star out of Jacob" oracle.

The narrative continues indirectly: the following chapter describes Israel's apostasy with the women of Moab at Baal-Peor, which later tradition, including Numbers 31:16 and the Book of Revelation, attributes to Balaam's counsel to Balak. Balak himself is mentioned in later texts as a byword for enmity toward Israel: Joshua 24 recalls his summons to Balaam, the prophet Micah (6:5) urges Israel to remember "what Balak king of Moab devised", and Revelation 2:14 condemns the teaching of Balaam, "who taught Balak to put a stumbling block before the sons of Israel."

Interpretation

Commentators have long debated the composition of the Balak–Balaam cycle, which includes the unique episode of Balaam's talking donkey and the Angel of the Lord. Extra-biblical interest grew with the discovery in 1967 of the Deir Alla inscriptions in Jordan, which mention a "Balaam son of Beor", a seer of the gods, in a text dated to roughly the 8th century BCE, suggesting that Balaam was remembered as a prophetic figure in the region beyond Israel.
