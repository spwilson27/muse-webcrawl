Lemurs are a group of strepsirrhine primates found only on the island of Madagascar. All known lemur species are endemic to Madagascar and the nearby Comoro Islands, where they evolved in isolation for tens of millions of years after Madagascar separated from Africa. They range in size from the tiny Madame Berthe's mouse lemur (Microcebus berthae), which weighs about 30 grams and is the smallest primate in the world, to the indri (Indri indri), which can weigh up to about 9.5 kilograms.

The name "lemur" derives from the Latin word lemures, meaning ghosts or spirits of the dead, a reference to their nocturnal habits, reflective eyes, and the haunting calls of some species. Roughly 100 living species are recognized, and at least 17 species of giant lemurs, some as large as gorillas, have gone extinct since humans arrived on the island roughly 2,000 years ago.

Evolution and distribution

Lemur ancestors are thought to have colonized Madagascar by rafting across the Mozambique Channel on mats of vegetation from mainland Africa roughly 50–60 million years ago. On Madagascar, freed from competition with monkeys and other mammals that dominate on the mainland, lemurs diversified into a wide range of ecological niches, including nocturnal insectivores, leaf-eaters, seed predators, and highly social diurnal species such as the ring-tailed lemur (Lemur catta).

Characteristics

Like other strepsirrhines, lemurs retain a moist nose (rhinarium) and a strong sense of smell, and possess a reflective layer called the tapetum lucidum behind the retina that aids night vision. Most lemurs have a dental comb formed by the lower incisors and canines, used for grooming and feeding, and a specialized grooming claw on the second toe of each hind foot. Scent marking is an important means of communication. Many species are sexually dimorphic, and unusually among primates, females are socially dominant over males in many lemur species.

Conservation

Lemurs are among the most threatened groups of mammals on Earth. The IUCN has estimated that the great majority of lemur species are Endangered or Critically Endangered, threatened primarily by deforestation for slash-and-burn agriculture, logging, and charcoal production, as well as hunting. Conservation efforts include protected areas such as Ranomafana and Andasibe-Mantadia national parks, and captive breeding programs.
