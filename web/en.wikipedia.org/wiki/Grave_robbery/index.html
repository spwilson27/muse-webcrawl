Grave robbery
Other namesTomb robbing, grave robbing, body snatching (for cadavers)
MotivesValuables buried with the dead; sale of corpses for anatomical study; artefacts for the antiquities market
Legal statusCriminal offence in most jurisdictions
Famous casesTutankhamun's tomb (robbed in antiquity); Burke and Hare murders; Charlie Chaplin coffin theft (1978)

Grave robbery, tomb robbing, or tomb raiding is the act of uncovering a grave, tomb or crypt to steal commodities. It is usually perpetrated to take and profit from valuable artefacts or personal property. A related act is body snatching, a term denoting the contested or unlawful taking of a body (usually from a grave), often for the purpose of dissection or anatomy lectures.

Grave robbing has caused the loss of a great deal of archaeological data: many sites have been looted by robbers who seek marketable artefacts, destroying the scientific context of the finds in the process. In many countries, grave robbery is treated as a serious criminal offence, and in some jurisdictions carried severe historical penalties, including death.

History

Ancient Egypt

The tombs of ancient Egypt were frequently robbed, often within years or even months of burial. The royal necropolis workers of Deir el-Medina were implicated in tomb robbery trials recorded in papyri of the late New Kingdom. The famous tomb of Tutankhamun was entered by robbers at least twice shortly after his burial, though most of its treasures remained in place when Howard Carter opened it in 1922. Ancient Egyptian law treated tomb robbery as a capital crime, punishable by impalement.

China

Grave robbing in China dates back to antiquity. Cao Cao, a warlord of the late Han dynasty, was said to have organised a special unit to loot tombs to fund his armies. The legendary general Xiang Yu was accused of looting the mausoleum of Qin Shi Huang. Chinese looters have traditionally been divided into northern and southern schools, with folklore holding that a candle lit in the south-east corner of a tomb would go out if the occupant objected to the theft.

Classical world

Grave robbery was also common in the Greek and Roman worlds, where curse inscriptions (defixiones) were placed on tombs warning robbers of divine punishment. Roman law under the Twelve Tables already prohibited disturbance of graves, and later penalties became increasingly severe.

18th and 19th century Britain: body snatching

In 18th- and early 19th-century Britain, the growing number of medical schools created heavy demand for cadavers, but legal supply was restricted to the bodies of executed criminals. "Resurrectionists" dug up freshly buried corpses and sold them to anatomists. The trade reached its scandalous peak with the Burke and Hare murders of 1828, in which sixteen people were murdered in Edinburgh so their bodies could be sold to the anatomist Robert Knox. Public outrage led to the Anatomy Act 1832, which greatly expanded the legal supply of cadavers and ended the body-snatching trade.

The Americas

Native American burial mounds and graves were extensively looted in North America. In Central and South America, huaqueros looted pre-Columbian tombs, particularly on the north coast of Peru, feeding an international market in antiquities. The US Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA) of 1990 criminalised trafficking in Native American human remains and cultural items.

Modern grave robbery

Modern grave robbery ranges from opportunistic theft of metal or flowers to organised looting of archaeological sites, which Interpol and UNESCO regard as one of the largest categories of illicit cultural-property trafficking. The looting of sites in Iraq after 2003 and in Syria during its civil war drew international attention. In 1978, the coffin of film star Charlie Chaplin was stolen from a Swiss cemetery and held for ransom before being recovered.

Prevention

Historical deterrents included mortsafes (iron cages over graves), watch houses and hired guards in churchyards, heavy stone slabs, and coffin collars fixed to the corpse. Modern prevention uses cemetery security, sealed vaults, and legislation protecting archaeological sites and cultural heritage.
