Letter of attorney

Legal conceptWritten authorisation to act for another
PartiesPrincipal (grantor) and attorney-in-fact (agent)
FormsGeneral, special/limited, durable, springing
Related termPower of attorney

A letter of attorney is a written document by which one person (the principal) authorises another (the attorney-in-fact or agent) to act on their behalf in legal, financial or business matters. The term is historically the older name for the instrument now more commonly called a power of attorney; "letter" survives in older legal usage and in phrases such as "letters of attorney" in early modern deeds and commissions.

Scope and types

A letter of attorney may be general, granting broad authority over the principal's affairs, or special (limited), confined to a specified transaction such as selling a property, operating a bank account or representing the principal in a lawsuit. Ordinarily the authority ends on the principal's incapacity or death; a durable power survives incapacity, and a springing power takes effect only upon a defined future event. The agent named need not be a lawyer — "attorney" here is used in its older sense of a substitute or deputy, hence "attorney-in-fact", distinct from an "attorney-at-law".

Formalities

Most jurisdictions require the document to be signed by the principal, and many require witnesses or notarisation, particularly for real-estate dealings or lasting powers used in health and welfare decisions. The agent owes the principal fiduciary duties of loyalty and care, and third parties may require evidence that the authority remains in force.
