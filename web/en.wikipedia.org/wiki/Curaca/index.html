Curaca

[Image: An Andean curaca in traditional dress receiving a Spanish colonial official]

A curaca (Quechua: kuraka), also rendered kuraka or, in the colonial period, cacique, was an ethnic lord or chief of an Andean community (an ayllu or group of ayllus) in the Inca Empire and in the Spanish colonial system that succeeded it.

Role under the Inca

Curacas formed the local and regional nobility of the Inca state. They organised labour taxation (the mit'a), supervised storehouses, maintained shrines, and represented their communities before higher authorities, in return receiving exemption from tribute and privileges such as fine cloth, coca, and multiple wives. Positions were generally hereditary within a lineage, subject to confirmation by the Sapa Inca.

Colonial period

The Spanish crown retained curacas as intermediaries of colonial rule, making them responsible for collecting tribute and drafting labour for mines and encomiendas. Although abuses and legal challenges eroded their authority over time, many curacas adapted, litigating in Spanish courts and defending community lands; some, such as the curacas who backed the great rebellion of Túpac Amaru II (1780–81), turned against colonial rule, after which the office was progressively suppressed.
