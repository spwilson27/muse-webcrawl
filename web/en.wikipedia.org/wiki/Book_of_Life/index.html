Book of Life

In Judaism and Christianity, the Book of Life (Hebrew: ספר החיים, Sefer HaChaim; Greek: βιβλίον τῆς ζωῆς) is a book in which God records the names of the righteous who will live forever, and from which the names of the wicked may be blotted out. The concept appears throughout the Hebrew Bible and the New Testament, most prominently in the Book of Revelation, where the Book of Life is opened at the Last Judgment and anyone whose name is not found in it is cast into the lake of fire.

In Judaism

The Hebrew Bible refers to a divine register of the living: Moses pleads to be blotted from God's book (Exodus 32), and the Psalmist asks that the wicked be "blotted out of the book of the living" (Psalm 69:28). Daniel 12:1 speaks of "every one that shall be found written in the book" being delivered at the time of trouble. In later Jewish tradition, God opens the Book of Life on Rosh Hashanah, inscribing the righteous for another year of life, while the fate of the intermediate is suspended until Yom Kippur, when the book is sealed. The liturgy of the High Holy Days — including the prayer Unetanneh Tokef and the greeting "may you be inscribed in the Book of Life" — is structured around this imagery.

In Christianity

In the New Testament, Jesus tells his disciples to rejoice that their names are "written in heaven" (Luke 10:20), and Paul mentions fellow workers "whose names are in the book of life" (Philippians 4:3). Revelation repeatedly invokes the book: the dead are judged according to what is written in the books, and the Book of Life of the Lamb determines entry into the New Jerusalem. Theologians have debated whether names can be removed from the book, a question bound up with doctrines of predestination and perseverance of the saints.
