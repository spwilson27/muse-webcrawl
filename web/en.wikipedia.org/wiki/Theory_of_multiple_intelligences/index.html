Theory of multiple intelligences

The theory of multiple intelligences (MI theory) is a model of intelligence proposed by Howard Gardner, a developmental psychologist at Harvard University, in his 1983 book Frames of Mind: The Theory of Multiple Intelligences. Gardner argued that the traditional psychometric view of intelligence — a single general ability measured by IQ tests — is too narrow, and proposed instead that human beings possess several relatively autonomous cognitive capacities, or "intelligences", each with its own developmental trajectory and its own symbolic and neural correlates.

The intelligences

Gardner originally identified seven intelligences: (1) linguistic — sensitivity to spoken and written language; (2) logical-mathematical — the capacity to analyse problems logically and perform mathematical operations; (3) musical — skill in performance, composition and appreciation of musical patterns; (4) bodily-kinaesthetic — control of one's bodily motions and skill in handling objects; (5) spatial — the ability to recognise and manipulate patterns of space; (6) interpersonal — understanding the intentions and motivations of others; and (7) intrapersonal — understanding oneself. He later added an eighth, naturalistic intelligence (recognising and classifying species and natural phenomena), and considered a ninth, existential intelligence, concerning ultimate questions of existence.

Criteria

Gardner selected candidate intelligences by applying eight criteria drawn from biological and psychological evidence, including potential isolation by brain damage, the existence of prodigies and savants, an identifiable set of core operations, a distinctive developmental history, evolutionary plausibility, support from psychometric findings, susceptibility to encoding in a symbol system, and support from experimental psychology.

Reception and criticism

The theory has been enormously influential in education, inspiring curricula and schools that seek to teach to children's differing strengths rather than through language and logic alone. Among psychologists and psychometricians, however, it is controversial: critics note that most of the proposed intelligences are highly correlated with each other and with general intelligence (g), that Gardner provided no standardised tests for them, and that subsequent empirical studies have found little support for the independence of the intelligences or for measurable educational benefits from matching instruction to "intelligence profiles". Gardner has responded that his intention was to broaden the concept of intelligence and to draw on evidence beyond factor analysis, rather than to generate a new battery of tests.
