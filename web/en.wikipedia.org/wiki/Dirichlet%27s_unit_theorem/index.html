Dirichlet's unit theorem

Dirichlet's unit theorem is a fundamental result in algebraic number theory, proved by Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet in 1846. It describes the structure of the group of units of the ring of integers of an algebraic number field.

Statement

Let K be a number field with r1 real embeddings and r2 pairs of complex embeddings, and let OK be its ring of integers. The theorem states that the unit group of OK is a finitely generated abelian group of the form μ(K) × ℤr, where μ(K) is the finite cyclic group of roots of unity in K and the rank is r = r1 + r2 − 1.

Examples and the regulator

For the rational numbers the rank is 0 and the only units are ±1. For a real quadratic field the rank is 1, so all units are powers of a single fundamental unit (times ±1) — for ℚ(√5) this is the golden ratio. The proof uses the logarithmic embedding of the units into Euclidean space, where they form a lattice; the covolume of that lattice is essentially the regulator of the field, an important arithmetic invariant appearing in the class number formula.
