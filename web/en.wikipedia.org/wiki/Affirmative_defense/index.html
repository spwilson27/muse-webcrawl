Affirmative defense

An affirmative defense is a defense in which the party raising it introduces evidence which, if found to be credible, will negate criminal liability or civil liability, even if it is proven that the defendant committed the alleged acts. In other words, it is a defense that says: even if the plaintiff's or prosecution's facts are true, the defendant should not be liable for another reason.

In civil cases, affirmative defenses must generally be pleaded in the answer under Rule 8(c) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, or they may be waived. The defendant typically bears the burden of proving an affirmative defense by a preponderance of the evidence; in criminal cases burdens vary by jurisdiction and defense.

Examples in civil law

  • Statute of limitations and statute of frauds
  • Res judicata, collateral estoppel, accord and satisfaction, release
  • Contributory / comparative negligence, assumption of risk
  • Duress, estoppel, laches, unclean hands, waiver
  • Failure of consideration, illegality, payment

Examples in criminal law

  • Self-defense, insanity, duress, necessity, entrapment
  • Mistake of fact, intoxication (in limited circumstances), alibi (sometimes classified separately)

Burden of proof

Unlike a failure-of-proof defense, which attacks an element of the claim, an affirmative defense assumes the elements arguendo and adds new matter. Courts distinguish the two because it determines who must plead and prove the issue.
