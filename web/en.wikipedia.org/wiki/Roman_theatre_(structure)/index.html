Roman theatre (structure)

[Image: The Roman theatre at Orange, France — the best-preserved stage building (scaenae frons)]

Overview

A Roman theatre is a building type developed across the Roman world for dramatic performances. Modelled on Greek theatres but structurally distinct, Roman theatres were built on level ground with their semicircular seating (cavea) supported on vaulted substructures rather than relying on a hillside, allowing them to stand in flat urban centres.

Anatomy

The parts include the semicircular orchestra, the cavea (often divided by social rank, with the velarium awning above), the raised stage (pulpitum) and its permanent architectural backdrop, the scaenae frons — a multi-storey facade of columns, niches and statues against which plays were performed. The earliest permanent stone theatre at Rome was Pompey's (55 BC). Superb survivors include Orange (France), Aspendos (Turkey — the best-preserved stage building), Bosra (Syria), Leptis Magna and the theatres of Mérida and Taormina. Roman theatres were smaller and more enclosed than Greek ones, designed for spectacle as much as drama.
