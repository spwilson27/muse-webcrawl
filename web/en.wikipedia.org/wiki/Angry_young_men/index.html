Angry young men

The angry young men were a group of mostly working- and middle-class British playwrights and novelists who rose to prominence in the 1950s. Their works expressed the disillusionment of a generation that felt betrayed by the compromises and class-bound structures of post-war British society, and they are associated with the "kitchen sink realism" movement in theatre and film.

Origin of the term

The phrase was popularised by the press after the success of John Osborne's play Look Back in Anger (1956), whose protagonist Jimmy Porter became the archetype of the angry young man. A publicist for the Royal Court Theatre is credited with applying the label, which journalists extended to a loose grouping of writers including Osborne, Kingsley Amis, John Braine, Alan Sillitoe, John Wain, Arnold Wesker, and Shelagh Delaney.

Works and themes

Key works include Amis's novel Lucky Jim (1954), Braine's Room at the Top (1957), Sillitoe's Saturday Night and Sunday Morning (1958) and The Loneliness of the Long Distance Runner (1959), and Delaney's play A Taste of Honey (1958). Their writing turned away from the drawing-room gentility of earlier British drama toward vernacular dialogue, provincial settings, and frank treatment of class, sex, and boredom.

Legacy

Many of the works were adapted into defining films of the British New Wave cinema of the early 1960s. The writers themselves resisted the label, and their later careers diverged sharply in politics and style, but the term remains shorthand for the cultural shift of 1950s Britain.
