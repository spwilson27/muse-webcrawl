The United States Coast Guard (USCG) is the maritime security, search and rescue, and law enforcement service branch of the United States Armed Forces. It is one of the country's eight uniformed services and the smallest of its armed forces. The Coast Guard is simultaneously a military service and a federal law enforcement agency, with missions including maritime safety, security and stewardship: search and rescue, drug and migrant interdiction, fisheries enforcement, aids to navigation, icebreaking, marine environmental protection and port security.

History

The service traces its founding to 4 August 1790, when Congress authorised the Revenue-Marine (later the Revenue Cutter Service) to enforce customs laws. The modern Coast Guard was formed on 28 January 1915 by merging the Revenue Cutter Service with the U.S. Life-Saving Service; the Lighthouse Service was added in 1939. Coast Guard cutters and crews have served in every major US conflict, from the Quasi-War with France to the Second World War — where Coast Guardsmen manned landing craft at Normandy and in the Pacific — and the wars in Vietnam, Iraq and Afghanistan. The service moved from the Department of Transportation to the new Department of Homeland Security in 2003; by law it may operate as part of the Navy during wartime.

Organisation and insignia

The Coast Guard is led by the Commandant, a four-star admiral, and operates through Atlantic and Pacific Area commands and nine districts. Its cutters are recognisable by the blue "racing stripe" emblem adopted in the 1960s. Officer training is centred at the Coast Guard Academy in New London, Connecticut, and enlisted training at Cape May, New Jersey. The volunteer Coast Guard Auxiliary supports recreational boating safety, while the Coast Guard Reserve provides surge personnel.
