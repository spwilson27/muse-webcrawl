Reuptake

Reuptake is the reabsorption of a neurotransmitter by a neurotransmitter transporter located along the plasma membrane of an axon terminal (i.e., the pre-synaptic neuron at a synapse) or glial cell after it has performed its function of transmitting a neural impulse. Reuptake is necessary for normal synaptic physiology because it allows for the recycling of neurotransmitters and regulates the level of neurotransmitter present in the synapse, thereby controlling how long a signal resulting from neurotransmitter release lasts.

Mechanism

Transporter proteins specific to each neurotransmitter — such as the serotonin transporter (SERT), dopamine transporter (DAT), and norepinephrine transporter (NET) — move neurotransmitter molecules from the synaptic cleft back into the cytosol of the presynaptic neuron, powered by ion gradients. Once back inside, the neurotransmitter may be repackaged into synaptic vesicles or broken down by enzymes such as monoamine oxidase.

Pharmacology

Blocking reuptake prolongs and enhances neurotransmitter signaling, a mechanism exploited by many drugs. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) such as fluoxetine are widely used antidepressants; serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) and the norepinephrine–dopamine reuptake inhibitor bupropion are also common. Cocaine exerts its stimulant effects largely by blocking the dopamine transporter.
