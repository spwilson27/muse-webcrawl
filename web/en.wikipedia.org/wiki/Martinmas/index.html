Martinmas

Date11 November

Martinmas is the feast day of Saint Martin of Tours, celebrated on 11 November. In medieval and early modern Europe it marked the end of the autumn harvest season and the beginning of winter: livestock that could not be fed through winter were slaughtered, the new wine was tasted, rents and accounts were settled, and annual hirings of servants and farm labourers took place. In Britain it was one of the traditional Scottish term days and a major hiring-fair season; in Scotland the term "Martinmas" is still used in legal and university contexts.

Customs

The feast was traditionally a time of feasting before the rigours of winter (and, in some places, the Advent fast). Roast goose — the "Martinsgans" in German-speaking lands — is the characteristic dish, linked to the legend that Martin, hiding from those who wished to make him bishop, was betrayed by the cackling of geese. In Germany, the Rhineland and the Netherlands, children take part in lantern processions (Martinszug) on the evening of 10 or 11 November, singing songs about St Martin and receiving sweets; in some regions a man dressed as St Martin rides at the head of the procession, and bonfires (Martinsfeuer) are lit.

Agricultural year

Martinmas closed the farming year: the wheat sowing was finished, animals were slaughtered and their meat salted down ("Martlemas beef" in England), and the new season's wine — like France's Beaujolais nouveau and Austria's Heuriger celebrations in later centuries — was sampled. The period of mild weather that sometimes occurs around the feast is called "St Martin's summer," analogous to the North American "Indian summer."

Historical notes

St Martin's Day coincides with Armistice Day (11 November 1918, the end of the First World War), now observed as Remembrance Day in Commonwealth countries and Veterans Day in the United States. Shakespeare refers to the feast and its season in his plays, and the day features in the calendars of many European folk traditions.
