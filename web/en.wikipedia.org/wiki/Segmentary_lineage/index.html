Segmentary lineage

A segmentary lineage: segments unite or divide depending on the level of opposition.

A segmentary lineage, also called a segmentary lineage system, is a type of social organisation found in some stateless societies, in which kinship groups (lineages) are hierarchically nested within one another and political allegiance depends on the context of conflict. The model was developed by British social anthropologists of the mid-twentieth century, above all E. E. Evans-Pritchard in his study of the Nuer of Sudan, and was also applied to the Tiv of Nigeria, the Somali, and Bedouin and other tribal societies of the Middle East and North Africa.

Structure

In a segmentary system, descent groups are organised at several levels: minimal lineages of a few generations' depth combine into minor, major and maximal lineages, each tracing descent from a more remote common ancestor. Groups that feud with one another at a lower level unite at a higher level against a common external opponent — a principle summarised in the Bedouin proverb, "I against my brothers; my brothers and I against my cousins; my brothers, my cousins and I against the world." Evans-Pritchard called this "balanced opposition": there is no permanent central authority, and political unity is relative and situational rather than fixed.

Functioning and criticism

The system provides order without government: disputes are settled by compensation negotiated between the segments involved, with figures such as the Nuer "leopard-skin chief" acting as mediators without coercive power. Later anthropologists criticised the model as an idealisation that Evans-Pritchard imposed on messier realities: subsequent fieldwork showed that actual Nuer politics involved prophets, age-sets, and domination of neighbouring Dinka, that lineages are as much ideological charters as real groups, and that colonial and state contexts profoundly reshaped the societies described. Nevertheless the segmentary lineage remains a classic concept in political anthropology and is still used, cautiously, to analyse clan politics in places such as Somalia and Afghanistan.
