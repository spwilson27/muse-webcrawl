The lake trout (Salvelinus namaycush) is a freshwater char native to North America, living mainly in large, deep, cold lakes in Canada, Alaska and the northern United States, including the Great Lakes. Despite its common name it is a char rather than a true trout, closely related to the brook trout and Arctic char. Other names include mackinaw, namaycush, lake char, touladi and grey trout.

Description

Lake trout are the largest of the chars: the largest recorded specimen, caught in a gill net in Lake Athabasca, Saskatchewan, weighed 46.3 kg (102 lb). The body is typically grey or olive-green with cream or yellow spots over the back, sides and dorsal and caudal fins, and a deeply forked tail. Average fish weigh 6.8–13.6 kg (15–30 lb). They are long-lived and slow-growing; individuals may live 40 years or more.

Ecology

Lake trout require cold, well-oxygenated water and inhabit deep areas of lakes, often between 15 and 60 metres down. They feed on zooplankton and insect larvae when young and shift to fish — such as ciscoes, whitefish, sculpins and alewives — as adults, though some populations remain plankton feeders. Spawning occurs in autumn over rocky shoals, where eggs are scattered among boulders and left unattended; males and females spawn at night and the eggs hatch the following spring.

Great Lakes decline and restoration

Lake trout were the top native predator of the upper Great Lakes until the mid-20th century, when overfishing and predation by the invasive sea lamprey devastated stocks. By the 1950s the species was effectively extirpated from lakes Michigan and Erie and greatly reduced elsewhere. Sea lamprey control using lampricides and large-scale stocking allowed recovery in Lake Superior, where self-sustaining populations returned; stocked fish continue to dominate in the lower lakes, and naturally reproducing populations have slowly rebuilt. Hybrid crosses with brook trout, called splake, are stocked in some waters.

Introductions

Lake trout have been introduced outside their native range, notably into Yellowstone Lake in Wyoming, where they are an invasive predator threatening native cutthroat trout and are subject to an intensive suppression programme, and into lakes in the western United States and abroad.
