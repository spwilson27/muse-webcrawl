Appeal to emotion

For the use of emotion in persuasion more generally, see Rhetoric.
Appeal to emotion
Other namesArgumentum ad passiones
TypeLogical fallacy (informal)
Related conceptsAppeal to pity, appeal to fear, appeal to flattery

An appeal to emotion (also called argumentum ad passiones, from Latin "argument from passion") is an informal fallacy characterized by the manipulation of the recipient's emotions in order to win an argument, especially in the absence of factual evidence. This kind of appeal to emotion is an instance of the red herring fallacy, in which an irrelevant consideration is introduced to distract from the merits of the case.

The appeal to emotion is fallacious when emotions are used as evidence for the truth of a conclusion, when emotions are not directly relevant to the subject matter, or when such an appeal diverts attention from evidence that is relevant. Argumentation theorists who regard this type of argument as informal also recognize appeals to pity and appeals to fear as specific forms of the broader appeal to emotion.

History

The persuasive power of emotion was recognized in classical rhetoric: Aristotle, in his Rhetoric, identified pathos as one of the three modes of persuasion, alongside ethos (character) and logos (reason). While Aristotle treated emotional appeal as a legitimate part of oratory, later logicians drew attention to cases in which emotion is substituted for, rather than combined with, evidence.

As a fallacy

An appeal to emotion is fallacious when it is used instead of valid reasoning. Examples include attempts to invoke feelings of pity for the subject of a proposition, to flatter an audience into accepting a claim, or to frighten an audience into compliance. However, not every appeal to emotion is fallacious: emotions may legitimately enter an argument when they are themselves relevant to the conclusion, as in arguments about well-being or harm.

Types

  • Appeal to pity — urging acceptance on the basis of sympathy
  • Appeal to fear — invoking dread of a consequence
  • Appeal to flattery — praising the audience to win assent
  • Appeal to ridicule — presenting the opponent's view as laughable
  • Appeal to nature — framing something as good because it is "natural"
