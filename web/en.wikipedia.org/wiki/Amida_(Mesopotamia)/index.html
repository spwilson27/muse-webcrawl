Amida (Mesopotamia)

Amida (Greek: Ἄμιδα) was an ancient city in Upper Mesopotamia, situated on the high bluffs of the western bank of the Tigris River. It corresponds to the old walled city of modern Diyarbakır in southeastern Turkey. A strategic fortress on the frontier between the Roman (later Byzantine) Empire and Persia, Amida was repeatedly besieged, captured and retaken during the Roman–Persian Wars.

History

The site was known in Assyrian records and its name is connected with Amedi. The Romans established it as a frontier stronghold; the emperor Constantius II refortified it around 337–350, enclosing it with the immense circuit of black basalt walls that still dominates the modern city. In 359 the Sasanian king Shapur II captured Amida after a famous 73-day siege, described in detail by the historian Ammianus Marcellinus, who was present inside the city and narrowly escaped. The Persians took the city again in 502–503 during the Anastasian War, massacring or deporting much of the population, before the Byzantines recovered it by ransom and negotiation.

Amida was an important centre of Syriac Christianity, with bishops attested from the 4th century, and the birthplace of writers such as Aëtius of Amida, the 6th-century physician. The city passed to the Arabs in 639–640, and under Islamic rule became known as Amid or Diyar Bakr. It later came under Artuqid, Ayyubid and finally Ottoman control (1515). In the Ottoman period, as Diyarbakır, it was a major provincial capital and caravan city known for its metalwork, textiles and watermelons.

The walls

The walls of Amida, built mainly of black basalt, extend for roughly 5.5 km with numerous towers and four principal gates. Successive Roman, Byzantine, Artuqid and Ottoman inscriptions and reliefs mark their fabric. In 2015 the fortress and the adjoining Hevsel Gardens were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.
