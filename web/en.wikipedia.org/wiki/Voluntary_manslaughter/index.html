Voluntary manslaughter

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Voluntary manslaughter is the killing of a human being in which the offender acted in the heat of passion, under circumstances that would cause a reasonable person to become emotionally or mentally disturbed to the point that they cannot reasonably control their emotions. It is an intentional killing, but the law treats the provocation as a partial defence that mitigates what would otherwise be murder.

Provocation

At common law, four elements traditionally must be shown: (1) adequate provocation, (2) the killing occurred in the heat of passion, (3) without a cooling-off period, and (4) a causal connection between the provocation, passion and killing. Classic examples include discovering a spouse in the act of adultery or being subjected to a serious assault. Mere words were traditionally insufficient, though modern statutes and case law have broadened the doctrine in many jurisdictions.

Imperfect self-defence

In some jurisdictions, voluntary manslaughter also covers "imperfect self-defence": an honest but unreasonable belief that deadly force was necessary. The genuine belief negates malice, reducing the offence from murder, even though the belief was not objectively reasonable.

Comparison

Voluntary manslaughter differs from involuntary manslaughter, which involves an unintentional killing through recklessness or criminal negligence. Sentences for voluntary manslaughter are substantially lower than for murder; under the U.S. federal guidelines and most state codes the maximum is commonly around ten to fifteen years, varying by jurisdiction.
