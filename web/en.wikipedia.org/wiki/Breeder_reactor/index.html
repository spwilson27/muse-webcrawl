A breeder reactor is a nuclear reactor that generates more fissile material than it consumes. These reactors can be fuelled with more commonly available isotopes such as uranium-238 or thorium-232, converting them into fissile plutonium-239 or uranium-233 respectively. Because breeders extract far more energy from natural uranium than conventional once-through reactors — in principle some sixty times as much — they were long regarded as the key to long-term nuclear energy sustainability.

Principles

Breeding occurs when the number of neutrons produced per fission is high enough that, after sustaining the chain reaction, surplus neutrons are captured in a "fertile" blanket material. Uranium-238 captures a neutron to become uranium-239, which decays through neptunium-239 to fissile plutonium-239. The breeding ratio is the ratio of fissile atoms produced to fissile atoms consumed; values above 1.0 indicate net breeding. Fast neutron spectra maximize the neutron yield of plutonium-239 fission, so most breeder designs are fast reactors without a moderator, while the thorium–uranium-233 cycle can breed in a thermal spectrum.

History

The concept dates to the 1940s. The U.S. Experimental Breeder Reactor I (EBR-I) in Idaho produced the first electricity from nuclear energy in December 1951. Major programs followed in the Soviet Union/Russia (BN-350, BN-600, BN-800), France (Phénix and Superphénix), Japan (Monju), and India, whose fast breeder program underpins its three-stage thorium strategy. Commercial deployment was slowed by the falling price of uranium, proliferation concerns about plutonium recycle, and the technical challenges of liquid-metal coolants; Superphénix closed in 1998 and Monju in 2016.

Types

  • Fast breeder reactor (FBR) — sodium-, lead-, or gas-cooled, using a uranium–plutonium cycle.
  • Thermal breeder reactor — uses thorium-232 to breed uranium-233; demonstrated at Shippingport and pursued in molten-salt designs.
  • Traveling wave reactor — a proposed design that breeds and burns fuel in a slow-moving wave within a single core load.

Waste and proliferation

Breeder fuel cycles can consume long-lived transuranic actinides, reducing the radiotoxic lifetime of nuclear waste, but reprocessing separates plutonium, raising weapons-proliferation concerns that have shaped policy since the 1970s.
