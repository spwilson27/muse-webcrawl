Tuskegee University, founded in 1881 as the Tuskegee Normal School and long known as Tuskegee Institute, is a private, historically Black land-grant university in Tuskegee, Alabama. Established under an act of the Alabama legislature with an initial appropriation of $2,000, it grew under its first leader, Booker T. Washington, into the most famous institution of African American education in the United States. The campus's historic core was designated a National Historic Site in 1974; the university's name was adopted in 1985.

Booker T. Washington era

Washington, a 25-year-old Hampton Institute graduate and formerly enslaved man, opened the school on 4 July 1881 in a borrowed church and shanty with about 30 students. His philosophy of industrial education, self-help, and institution-building — and his formidable fundraising among Northern philanthropists — built Tuskegee into a campus whose bricks students made and whose buildings they raised. Washington's 1895 Atlanta Exposition Address made him the most prominent Black leader in America, though his public accommodationism was criticised by W. E. B. Du Bois and others. From Tuskegee he ran an extensive network of influence, including the National Negro Business League, and advised presidents Theodore Roosevelt and William Howard Taft.

Carver, Rosenwald schools, and the Airmen

In 1896 Washington recruited George Washington Carver, whose agricultural research and extension work — crop rotation, peanuts and sweet potatoes, the mobile "Jesup wagon" school — made Tuskegee a centre of Black agronomy. Tuskegee's architecture faculty, led by Robert Robinson Taylor, the first accredited Black architect in the United States, designed much of the campus. With philanthropist Julius Rosenwald, Tuskegee helped design and build nearly 5,000 "Rosenwald schools" for Black children across the rural South. During the Second World War, Tuskegee's Moton Field trained the Tuskegee Airmen, the first Black military aviators in the U.S. armed forces.

Later history and the university today

Successive presidents included Robert Russa Moton and Frederick D. Patterson, founder of the United Negro College Fund. The institute is also associated with the notorious Tuskegee syphilis study (1932–1972), conducted by the U.S. Public Health Service on and around its campus, in which treatment was withheld from Black men with syphilis — an abuse that reshaped American research-ethics law; the university now hosts a bioethics centre named in response. Tuskegee remains a leading HBCU with particular strength in veterinary medicine (its school has trained a large share of America's Black veterinarians), engineering, agriculture, and nursing, and its marching band and choir are nationally known.
