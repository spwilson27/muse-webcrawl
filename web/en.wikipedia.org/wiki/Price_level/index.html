The price level is a hypothetical measure of the overall prices of goods and services in an economy at a given time. It is one of the most closely watched variables in macroeconomics: a sustained rise in the price level is inflation, while a sustained fall is deflation.

Measurement

Because millions of individual prices cannot be reduced to a single number exactly, the price level is measured with index numbers. The most common measures are the consumer price index (CPI), which tracks the cost of a basket of goods and services bought by households, and the GDP deflator, which covers all domestically produced goods and services. Each index sets a base period equal to 100 and expresses other periods relative to it.

Theory

In the quantity theory of money, the price level is determined by the money supply relative to real output, as summarised in the equation of exchange MV = PY. In the long run, most economists accept that the price level is largely a monetary phenomenon, while in the short run prices may be sticky, so changes in demand affect output and employment as well as prices. The general price level also enters the distinction between nominal and real values: a real variable is a nominal variable divided by the price level.

Significance

Unexpected changes in the price level redistribute wealth between debtors and creditors, distort relative price signals and complicate long-term contracting, which is why central banks typically target a low, stable rate of inflation such as two per cent per year.
