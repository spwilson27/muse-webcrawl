Sautéing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Mushrooms sautéing in a wide pan with butter and herbs]

Sautéing is a method of cooking that uses a relatively small amount of oil or fat in a shallow pan over relatively high heat. The word comes from the French sauter, "to jump", describing both the tossing motion used to move food in the pan and the way pieces jump in hot fat. Unlike deep frying, the food is not submerged; unlike stir frying, it is cooked in a flat pan, often in a single layer so surfaces brown rather than steam.

Sautéing is a cornerstone of French technique: mushrooms, chicken breasts, veal scallops and vegetables are sautéed and the browned pan juices (fond) deglazed with wine or stock to make a quick pan sauce — the basis of many classic dishes.

Technique

The pan must be hot before the fat and food are added, and the food should be dry and uncrowded; excess moisture or crowding drops the temperature and the food steams in its own juices instead of browning. Pieces are left undisturbed to form a crust, then turned or tossed. Butter gives flavour but burns easily, so it is often combined with oil or added late.

Equipment

A sauté pan has straight sides and a lid; a sauteuse has sloping sides for easier tossing. A wide frying pan or skillet serves the same purpose. Heavy bases (cast iron, clad stainless steel) hold heat when food is added.

See also

Related dry-heat methods include grilling, roasting and pan-frying; sweating vegetables gently in fat over low heat is a mild form of sautéing used to build the base of soups and sauces.
