Fundamental theorem on homomorphisms

In abstract algebra, the fundamental theorem on homomorphisms (also known as the fundamental homomorphism theorem) relates the structure of two objects between which a homomorphism is given, and of the kernel and image of that homomorphism. It is essentially the first isomorphism theorem stated in general form, and it applies to groups, rings, vector spaces, modules, and many other algebraic structures.

Statement for groups

Let G and H be groups, and let f : G → H be a group homomorphism. Let K = ker(f) be the kernel of f, a normal subgroup of G, and let φ : G → G/K be the natural surjective homomorphism onto the quotient group. Then there exists a unique homomorphism f̄ : G/K → H such that f = f̄ ∘ φ. Moreover, f̄ is injective, and its image is the image of f, so that

G / ker(f) ≅ im(f).

In particular, if f is surjective then G / ker(f) ≅ H.

Proof sketch

Well-definedness follows because elements of the same coset of ker(f) differ by an element mapped to the identity, hence have the same image under f. The induced map respects the group operation because f does, and injectivity follows because f̄ sends a coset to the identity only when that coset is ker(f) itself.

Versions in other structures

Analogous statements hold for ring homomorphisms (with kernel an ideal and image a subring), for linear maps between vector spaces (yielding the rank–nullity theorem), for module homomorphisms, and, in universal algebra, for algebras of any fixed type, where congruences play the role of normal subgroups.
