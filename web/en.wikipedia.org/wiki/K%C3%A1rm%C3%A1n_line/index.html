Kármán line

Altitude100 km (62 mi) above sea level (FAI definition)
Named afterTheodore von Kármán
DefinesBoundary between aeronautics and astronautics

The Kármán line is an attempt to define a boundary between Earth's atmosphere and outer space, set at an altitude of 100 kilometres (62 miles) above mean sea level by the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale (FAI). It is conventionally used to distinguish aircraft flight from spaceflight and to determine records and treaty regimes.

Origin

The concept comes from calculations by the Hungarian-American engineer Theodore von Kármán, who found that at around 84–100 km the atmosphere becomes too thin to support aeronautical flight: a vehicle would need to travel faster than orbital velocity to obtain sufficient aerodynamic lift. The FAI rounded the figure to 100 km and uses crossing it as the criterion for astronaut status in record-keeping.

Debate

The boundary is not physical or universally agreed. The United States has historically used 50 miles (about 80 km) for awarding astronaut wings, and some researchers argue, from the behaviour of satellites whose perigees dip below 100 km, that a value near 80–90 km is better supported. Even so, the 100 km Kármán line remains the most widely cited definition of the edge of space.
