Spin–orbit interaction

In physics, the spin–orbit interaction (also called spin–orbit effect or spin–orbit coupling) is a relativistic interaction of a particle's spin with its motion inside a potential. A key example is the interaction of an electron's spin with its orbital motion around an atomic nucleus, which produces fine structure in atomic spectra — the splitting of spectral lines into close doublets.

Explanation

In the electron's rest frame, the electric field of the nucleus appears partly as a magnetic field, which couples to the electron's magnetic moment associated with its spin. A careful treatment includes Thomas precession, which halves the naive estimate and brings the prediction into agreement with experiment. The interaction energy depends on the relative orientation of the spin and orbital angular momenta, so states with different total angular momentum j = l ± s have slightly different energies.

Magnitude

Spin–orbit splitting grows rapidly with atomic number (roughly as Z4 in hydrogen-like atoms) and is therefore much more important in heavy elements, where it affects chemical properties such as the colour of gold and the liquid state of mercury. In LS coupling (Russell–Saunders coupling), the total spin and orbital angular momenta couple first before combining into total angular momentum.

Other systems

Spin–orbit coupling also occurs for nucleons in the nuclear shell model, where a strong spin–orbit force explains nuclear magic numbers, and in condensed matter physics, where Rashba and Dresselhaus effects underpin spintronics and topological insulators.
