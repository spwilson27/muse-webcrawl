Ethnic minorities in China

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ethnic minorities in China are the non-Han population of the People's Republic of China. The government officially recognises 56 ethnic groups (minzu): the Han majority, about 91 percent of the population, and 55 minority groups totalling roughly 125 million people in the 2020 census. The largest minority groups include the Zhuang, Uyghurs, Hui, Miao, Manchu, Yi, Tujia, Tibetans and Mongols.

Recognition and classification

The list of groups derives from the Ethnic Classification Project (minzu shibie) carried out in the 1950s, which applied criteria influenced by Soviet ethnography — common territory, language, economy and culture — to more than 400 applicant groups. A small number of peoples remain officially "undistinguished", and some classified groups, notably the Hui, are defined largely by religion and ancestry rather than a distinct spoken language.

Autonomy and policy

Minorities are concentrated in the west and southwest, regions covering a majority of China's land area. The system of ethnic regional autonomy provides five autonomous regions (Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang, Tibet, Ningxia and Guangxi) plus numerous autonomous prefectures and counties. Minority citizens historically received preferential policies including exemptions and later modifications under the one-child policy, bonus points in university admissions, and reserved representation in the National People's Congress. Minority languages such as Tibetan, Uyghur, Mongolian, Korean and Zhuang have official status in their autonomous areas and appear on currency and documents; many are also endangered under pressure from Mandarin-dominant education and media.

Debates

State policies toward Tibetans, Uyghurs and other groups — particularly in Xinjiang since the mid-2010s — have drawn substantial international criticism and official Chinese rebuttals, while in other regions minority cultures are prominently promoted through tourism and heritage programmes.
