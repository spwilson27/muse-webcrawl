Till

Till, or glacial till, is unsorted glacial sediment deposited directly by glacier ice, without reworking by meltwater. It consists of a heterogeneous mixture of clay, silt, sand, gravel, and boulders, reflecting the debris the glacier picked up as it moved. In older British usage the same material is called boulder clay. When till is lithified into rock it forms tillite, which geologists use as evidence of ancient glaciations.

Characteristics

Unlike river or lake sediments, till shows no stratification and no sorting by size: fine rock flour sits beside metre-scale erratics. Clasts are often angular or sub-angular and may be striated by their passage along the glacier bed. Because ice can carry material of any size, a single till bed can contain particles ranging from micron-scale clay to blocks weighing hundreds of tonnes.

Deposition

Lodgement till is plastered onto the bed beneath moving ice as friction melts and pressure release cause debris-rich ice at the base to deposit its load; it is typically dense, compact, and forms drumlins when moulded by ice flow. Ablation till accumulates when stagnant or retreating ice melts in place, letting supraglacial and englacial debris settle onto the surface, producing hummocky ground. Tills deposited at glacier margins are the building material of moraines — terminal, lateral, and ground moraines — which map the former extent of ice sheets such as the Laurentide and Fennoscandian ice sheets of the last glacial period.

Significance

Till sheets cover large parts of Canada, northern Europe, and the northern United States, where they influence soils, drainage, and agriculture. Tillites in the geological record — for example those of the late Ordovician and the Neoproterozoic "Snowball Earth" events — provide key evidence for ancient ice ages.
