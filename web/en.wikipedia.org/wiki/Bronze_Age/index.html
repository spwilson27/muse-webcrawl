Bronze Age

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Bronze Age swords, axes and a chariot of the kind used across the Near East and Europe]

The Bronze Age is a historical period characterised by the use of bronze, the presence of writing in some areas, and other early features of urban civilisation. In the Near East it lasted roughly from 3300 BC to 1200 BC; dates vary by region, and in much of Europe it continued to about 800 BC. Bronze — an alloy of copper and tin — gave harder, more durable tools and weapons than stone or pure copper, and the need for tin, rarely found near copper, drove long-distance trade networks linking distant civilisations.

Civilisations

The Bronze Age saw the first literate urban societies: Sumer and Akkad and later Babylon and Assyria in Mesopotamia; Egypt of the Old, Middle and New Kingdoms; the Indus Valley Civilisation; Shang China; and in the Aegean world the Minoan civilisation of Crete and Mycenaean Greece. Palace economies organised agriculture, craft production and trade, while diplomatic correspondence such as the Amarna letters reveals a connected international system of great powers in the Late Bronze Age.

Collapse

Around 1200 BC the palace civilisations of the eastern Mediterranean collapsed within a few decades — cities burned, trade broke down, and writing was lost in Greece. Causes debated by historians include invasion or migration by the 'Sea Peoples', drought and famine, earthquakes, internal revolt and the fragility of interdependent trade. Egypt and Assyria survived in weakened form. In the following Iron Age, new peoples — Phoenicians, Aramaeans, Israelites, Persians — reshaped the region.
