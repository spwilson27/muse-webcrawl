Norwegian literature is literature produced in Norway or by Norwegian authors, in the Norwegian language (in its Bokmål and Nynorsk written standards) and, in earlier centuries, in Old Norse, Danish and Latin. Its most famous figure worldwide is the playwright Henrik Ibsen, and four Norwegian writers have won the Nobel Prize in Literature.

Early literature and the sagas

The oldest Norwegian texts are runic inscriptions and skaldic poetry; much of the prose saga tradition was written down in Iceland but draws on Norwegian kings and courtiers, as in Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla. After the Black Death and the union with Denmark, Danish became the written language of Norway for centuries, though Ludvig Holberg (1684–1754), born in Bergen, wrote in Danish and is considered a founder of both Danish and Norwegian literature.

Romanticism and the golden age

National romanticism in the 19th century, led by the poet Henrik Wergeland, celebrated Norwegian nature and identity and championed the break with Denmark after 1814. The later 19th century was a golden age: Ibsen founded modern drama with plays such as A Doll's House and Hedda Gabler; Bjørnstjerne Bjørnson wrote novels and the national anthem; and Alexander Kielland and Jonas Lie completed the "four greats". Folklore collectors Asbjørnsen and Moe preserved Norwegian fairy tales, while Ivar Aasen constructed the Landsmål (later Nynorsk) written standard from rural dialects.

20th and 21st centuries

Knut Hamsun's Hunger (1890) pioneered psychological modernism, while Sigrid Undset's medieval trilogy Kristin Lavransdatter won her the Nobel Prize. Tarjei Vesaas wrote lyrical Nynorsk prose including The Ice Palace. Contemporary Norwegian writing has an outsized global reach: Karl Ove Knausgård's autobiographical My Struggle, Jo Nesbø's crime novels, and Jon Fosse — whose minimal, rhythmic plays and prose won the 2023 Nobel Prize — are translated worldwide.
