Texas Hill Country

The Texas Hill Country is a geographic region of Central and South Texas, forming the southern part of the Edwards Plateau. Given its location, climate, terrain, and vegetation, the Hill Country can be considered the borderline between the American Southwest and Southeast. The region is notable for its karst limestone terrain and tall, rugged hills of limestone, granite and schist.

Geography

The Hill Country is bounded by the Balcones Fault on the east, a curve of faults running from near Del Rio northeastward through San Antonio and Austin to near Waco, where the land rises abruptly from the coastal plain. The Colorado, Guadalupe, Medina, Frio and Llano rivers cross the region, fed by numerous clear springs. Cities and towns in the region include Fredericksburg, Kerrville, Boerne, New Braunfels, Wimberley, Dripping Springs and San Marcos.

History

The area was home to Comanche, Apache and Tonkawa peoples before European settlement. In the 1840s and 1850s large numbers of German immigrants settled the region under the Adelsverein, founding Fredericksburg and New Braunfels and leaving a lasting German-Texan culture visible in the region's food, festivals and architecture. Enchanted Rock, a massive pink granite batholith, is one of the region's best-known landmarks.

Economy and culture

Once dominated by ranching, the modern Hill Country economy features tourism, wineries along the Wine Road 290 corridor near Fredericksburg, hunting leases, and second homes and retirement communities drawn by the scenery. The region is famous for spring wildflowers, especially Texas bluebonnets, and for the huge maternity colonies of Mexican free-tailed bats at sites such as Eckert James River Bat Cave.
