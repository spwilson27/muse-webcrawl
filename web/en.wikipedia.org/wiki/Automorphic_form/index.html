In mathematics, an automorphic form is a well-behaved function from a topological group G to the complex numbers (or a complex vector space) which is invariant under the action of a discrete subgroup of G and satisfies certain smoothness and growth conditions. Automorphic forms generalise the classical modular forms — functions on the upper half-plane invariant under the modular group SL(2, ℤ) — and lie at the heart of modern number theory.

History

Henri Poincaré introduced automorphic functions in the 1880s while studying Fuchsian groups, and the theory was developed through the work of Klein, and later Hecke, Maass, and Siegel, who extended modular forms to several variables and to non-holomorphic (Maass) forms. In the mid-20th century the subject was recast in representation-theoretic and adelic language by Gelfand, Harish-Chandra, Selberg, and especially Robert Langlands.

Modern formulation

In the adelic setting, an automorphic form is a function on G(𝔸) (for a reductive algebraic group G over a global field) that is invariant under G(F), finitely generated under the action of the maximal compact subgroup and the centre of the universal enveloping algebra, and of moderate growth. Cuspidal automorphic forms generate automorphic representations, whose associated L-functions generalise the Riemann zeta function and Dirichlet L-functions.

The Langlands program

Automorphic forms are one side of the web of conjectures known as the Langlands program, which predicts deep correspondences between automorphic representations and Galois representations/motivic L-functions. Famous achievements in this circle include the proof of the Taniyama–Shimura–Weil conjecture (used by Andrew Wiles in proving Fermat's Last Theorem) and the Sato–Tate conjecture.
