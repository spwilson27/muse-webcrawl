The Federalist Papers

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Federalist Papers is a collection of 85 articles and essays written by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison and John Jay under the collective pseudonym "Publius" to promote the ratification of the 1787 Constitution of the United States. Published in New York newspapers in 1787–88 against Anti-Federalist opposition, they are now the most authoritative commentary on the Constitution's meaning used by American courts and scholars — an odd fate for campaign journalism, explained by their authors' centrality to the founding and the essays' analytical brilliance.

Famous numbers

No. 10 (Madison) argues that an extended republic controls faction by multiplying interests; No. 51 (Madison) explains the structure — "ambition must be made to counteract ambition" — behind separation of powers and federalism; No. 78 (Hamilton) defends judicial review and life-tenured judges as the "least dangerous" branch. Others treat taxation, war powers, the Senate, and the executive's energy (No. 70). Authorship of a dozen essays was disputed between the Hamilton and Madison lists; statistical stylometry (Mosteller and Wallace, 1964) assigned most to Madison. The papers remain foundational texts of republican theory — read worldwide wherever constitution-makers study the American experiment.
