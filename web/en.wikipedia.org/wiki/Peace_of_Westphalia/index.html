The Peace of Westphalia is the collective name for two peace treaties signed in October 1648 in the Westphalian cities of Osnabrück and Münster. They ended the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) in the Holy Roman Empire and the Eighty Years' War (1568–1648) between Spain and the Dutch Republic, formally recognising Dutch independence.

Negotiations

The congress was among the first great multilateral diplomatic conferences in European history, with delegations from some 16 states and over a hundred Imperial estates; talks dragged on from 1643 in parallel venues because Catholic and Protestant delegations were kept apart. The Treaty of Münster between Spain and the Dutch Republic was signed separately in January 1648.

Terms

The settlements confirmed the principle of cuius regio, eius religio from the Peace of Augsburg (1555) and extended legal recognition to Calvinism as well as Lutheranism and Catholicism. The states of the Holy Roman Empire received near-sovereign rights, including the right to conclude alliances, drastically weakening imperial authority. Switzerland's independence from the Empire was formally recognised. Sweden gained territories in northern Germany and a large indemnity; France acquired parts of Alsace and confirmed possession of Metz, Toul and Verdun.

Legacy

The peace is regarded as a founding moment of the modern state system: the "Westphalian system" of sovereign states recognising one another's territorial integrity and non-interference in domestic affairs remains a touchstone of international relations theory, though historians debate how far the actual treaties established the principles later attributed to them.
