Lineage (anthropology)

A lineage is, in anthropology, a group of people who are related to each other by descent from a common ancestor. Lineages are a type of descent group, distinguished from the wider clan in that lineage members can trace their genealogical links to the common ancestor exactly, whereas clan members merely stipulate descent from a common (sometimes mythical or non-human) ancestor.

Types of lineage

Lineages are classified by the rule of descent used to recruit members:

  • Patrilineage — descent traced through the male line (agnatic descent).
  • Matrilineage — descent traced through the female line (uterine descent).
  • Cognatic (bilateral) lineage — descent traced through either or both parents.
  • Ambilineal lineage — membership chosen through either parent, as in the ramage described for some Polynesian societies.

Functions

Lineages are typically corporate groups: they may jointly hold property such as land, organise labour, arrange marriages (often being exogamous), and regulate disputes and ritual obligations. In segmentary lineage systems, famously described by E. E. Evans-Pritchard for the Nuer of South Sudan, lineages of different levels of inclusiveness unite or divide situationally for feud and warfare, providing political order without a centralised state.

Lineage mode of production

In Marxist anthropology, the "lineage mode of production", associated with scholars such as Claude Meillassoux, describes economies in which senior men control the circulation of subsistence goods and the marriages of junior men and women, grounding authority in descent-group organisation.
