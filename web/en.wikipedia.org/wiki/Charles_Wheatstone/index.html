Sir Charles Wheatstone FRS (6 February 1802 – 19 October 1875) was an English scientist and inventor of the Victorian era, noted for his contributions to the electric telegraph, electrical measurement, optics and acoustics. He was appointed professor of experimental philosophy at King's College London in 1834, and knighted in 1868.

Telegraph

With William Fothergill Cooke, Wheatstone patented the first commercially successful electric telegraph in 1837; their five-needle telegraph was installed along the Great Western Railway and famously used in 1845 to alert police to the arrival of the murderer John Tawell, the first arrest effected with the aid of telecommunications. Wheatstone later developed an automatic high-speed telegraph transmitter and contributed to submarine telegraphy.

Wheatstone bridge

Wheatstone popularised and improved the resistance-measuring circuit described by Samuel Hunter Christie in 1833, which became known as the Wheatstone bridge. The balanced-bridge method allowed precise comparison of electrical resistances and became a standard laboratory instrument.

Other inventions

Wheatstone invented the concertina, demonstrated the first reflecting stereoscope (1838, later developed with photographic images), devised the Playfair cipher encryption system used by the British military into the twentieth century, and made early experiments on the measurement of the velocity of electricity and on spectrum analysis with photography.
