Multi-tiered system of supports

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A multi-tiered system of supports (MTSS) is a framework used in schools, primarily in the United States, to provide targeted support to students who are struggling academically or behaviourally. It organises instruction and intervention into layers ("tiers") of increasing intensity, guided by screening and progress-monitoring data, so that help is matched to each student's level of need.

The three tiers

Tier 1 is high-quality, evidence-based core instruction delivered to all students in the general classroom, together with universal screening, typically three times a year, to identify those at risk. Tier 2 provides targeted small-group interventions to the roughly 10–15% of students whose screening results show they need more than core instruction; their progress is monitored frequently and the intervention adjusted. Tier 3 is intensive, individualised intervention for the small proportion (around 5%) who have not responded adequately to Tier 2; it may involve specialist staff, smaller ratios and more frequent sessions.

Origins and relationship to RTI and PBIS

MTSS grew out of two earlier movements: Response to Intervention (RTI), developed around early reading and promoted under the 2004 reauthorisation of the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act as a way to identify learning disabilities through a student's response to intervention rather than an IQ–achievement discrepancy; and Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports (PBIS), which applied the same tiered logic to behaviour. MTSS is generally used as the umbrella term that integrates academic (RTI) and behavioural (PBIS) strands, together with social-emotional learning and data-based decision-making at school and district level.

Implementation

Successful implementation is typically described as depending on leadership teams, regular review of screening and progress-monitoring data, fidelity checks on instruction and interventions, family engagement, and professional development. Critics note the burden of data collection, variability in how faithfully the model is applied, and debates over whether tiered intervention delays formal evaluation for special education.
