Angiostatin

Angiostatin is an endogenous protein fragment that inhibits angiogenesis — the growth of new blood vessels. It is produced by proteolytic cleavage of plasminogen, the precursor of the fibrinolytic enzyme plasmin, and consists of the first three or four of plasminogen's kringle domains. Angiostatin was discovered in the laboratory of Judah Folkman and reported in 1994, in work led by Michael O'Reilly.

Discovery

Researchers observed that a primary tumour in laboratory mice could suppress the growth of its own metastases, suggesting the tumour secreted a circulating inhibitor of blood-vessel growth. The responsible factor was isolated from serum and urine and identified as a 38-kilodalton internal fragment of plasminogen. Administered angiostatin caused dormant, avascular micrometastases in experimental models, generating great interest in anti-angiogenic cancer therapy.

Mechanism

Angiostatin binds several targets on endothelial cells, including ATP synthase on the cell surface, angiomotin and integrins. Its actions inhibit endothelial cell proliferation and migration and can induce apoptosis, thereby starving growing tumours of a blood supply while sparing established vasculature.

Clinical development

Recombinant angiostatin entered clinical trials in the late 1990s and early 2000s. It proved essentially non-toxic but showed limited efficacy as a single agent in humans, and development was not pursued to approval; it remains of interest in combination strategies. Related endogenous inhibitors include endostatin, a fragment of collagen XVIII discovered in the same research programme.
