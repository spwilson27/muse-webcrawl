Lagrange's four-square theorem

Lagrange's four-square theorem, also known as Bachet's conjecture, states that every natural number can be represented as the sum of four integer squares. That is, for every natural number n there exist integers a, b, c and d such that:

n = a² + b² + c² + d²

For example, 3 = 1² + 1² + 1² + 0² and 31 = 5² + 2² + 1² + 1².

History

The theorem first appeared in Claude Gaspard Bachet's 1621 edition of Diophantus' Arithmetica, where it was stated without proof. Fermat claimed to have a proof by infinite descent but never published it. Joseph Louis Lagrange gave the first published proof in 1770, building on work by Euler, who had proved the four-square identity — that the product of two numbers each expressible as a sum of four squares is itself expressible as a sum of four squares. In 1798 Legendre strengthened the result with his three-square theorem, characterising the numbers that need all four squares (those of the form 4a(8b + 7)).

Proof outline

By Euler's four-square identity it suffices to prove the theorem for prime numbers. Using the pigeonhole principle, one shows that for an odd prime p there is a multiple mp with 0 < m < p expressible as a sum of four squares; a descent argument (related to Fermat's method) then reduces m to 1, proving that p itself is a sum of four squares.

Generalizations

The Jacobi four-square theorem counts the number of representations of a number as a sum of four squares. The theorem is a special case of Waring's problem: g(2) = 4. Ramanujan gave a list of 54 universal quadratic forms in four variables generalising the result. The theorem also follows from the classification of quadratic forms and is connected with Hurwitz quaternions, used in Adrien-Marie Legendre's and later proofs.
