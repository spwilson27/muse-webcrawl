Vsevolod III Yuryevich, known as Vsevolod the Big Nest (Russian: Всеволод Юрьевич Большое Гнездо; 1154–1212), was Grand Prince of Vladimir from 1176 to 1212. During his long reign the principality of Vladimir-Suzdal reached the peak of its power and cultural flowering. His nickname — "the Big Nest" — refers to his numerous progeny: he fathered at least fourteen children, including eight sons, whose descendants ruled many of the principalities of north-eastern Rus'.

Early life

Vsevolod was the youngest son of Yuri Dolgorukiy, founder of Moscow. After his father's death in 1157 he was expelled from Rus' in 1162 together with his mother, a Byzantine princess, and his brothers, and spent part of his youth at the court of Constantinople. Returning to Rus', he served his elder brother Andrei Bogolyubsky, and after the struggles following Andrei's assassination in 1174, Vsevolod defeated his nephews and rival princes to secure the throne of Vladimir in 1176, confirmed by his victory at the Battle of Lipitsa's precursor engagements against the Rostislavichi and the men of Ryazan.

Reign

Vsevolod subordinated the rival principalities of Ryazan and exerted decisive influence over Novgorod, whose princes were effectively appointed with his consent. He campaigned repeatedly against the Volga Bulgars and the Mordvins, extending the principality's influence eastward toward the Volga. Princes of Kiev and Chernigov acknowledged his seniority; the author of The Tale of Igor's Campaign addressed him as a prince whose warriors could "splash away the Volga with their oars and bail out the Don with their helmets".

His reign saw a building boom in white stone: the Dormition Cathedral in Vladimir was rebuilt and enlarged, and the Cathedral of St Demetrius (1194–1197), famed for its carved reliefs, was erected as his court church. The towns of the Vladimir land flourished as centres of trade and craft.

Family and succession

Vsevolod's first wife, Maria Shvarnovna, was a patron of chronicle-writing and founder of a convent in Vladimir. His sons included Konstantin of Rostov, Yuri II of Vladimir, and Yaroslav II — father of Alexander Nevsky — so that Vsevolod stands as an ancestor of the later grand princes of Moscow. Contention among his sons after his death in 1212 led to the Battle of Lipitsa (1216), in which the elder branch triumphed.
