Townshend Acts

The Townshend Acts were a series of measures passed by the British Parliament in 1767, named after Charles Townshend, the Chancellor of the Exchequer. They imposed duties on imports into the American colonies — including glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea — and created new machinery to enforce customs collection.

Provisions

The Revenue Act of 1767 placed import duties on specified goods. Companion measures established an American Board of Customs Commissioners in Boston, expanded the use of vice-admiralty courts, and renewed the use of writs of assistance (general search warrants). The duties were intended to pay the salaries of royal governors and judges in America, making them independent of colonial assemblies.

Colonial response

Colonists, who had just forced repeal of the Stamp Act, again protested "taxation without representation." John Dickinson's Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania argued the duties were unconstitutional taxes. Colonies organised non-importation agreements and boycotts of British goods; tensions in Boston led to the stationing of British troops and, in 1770, the Boston Massacre.

Repeal

In 1770 Lord North's ministry repealed all the Townshend duties except the tax on tea, retained to assert Parliament's right to tax the colonies. The surviving tea duty fed directly into the crisis that produced the Tea Act of 1773 and the Boston Tea Party.
