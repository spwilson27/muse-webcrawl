Compressed sensing

Compressed sensing (also known as compressive sensing or compressive sampling) is a signal processing technique for efficiently acquiring and reconstructing a signal by exploiting its sparsity: a sparse signal can be recovered from far fewer samples than required by the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem.

Overview

Developed in the mid-2000s by Emmanuel Candès, Terence Tao, David Donoho, and others, compressed sensing shows that if a signal is sparse in some basis (most of its coefficients are zero), then a small number of random linear measurements suffice to reconstruct it exactly. Reconstruction is typically performed by ℓ1-minimisation — finding the sparsest signal consistent with the measurements — which can be solved as a convex optimisation problem provided the measurement matrix satisfies the restricted isometry property.

Applications

Applications include faster MRI scans (reducing scan time by sampling less of k-space), single-pixel cameras, radio astronomy, radar, analog-to-information conversion, and genomics. Compressed sensing reframed data acquisition: instead of sampling densely and compressing later, one can sense in a compressed form directly.
