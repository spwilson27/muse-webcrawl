Vapor chamber


Cross-section of a vapor chamber heat spreader

A vapor chamber is a flat, sealed heat-transfer device that spreads heat over a surface using the evaporation and condensation of a working fluid—essentially a planar (two-dimensional) version of a heat pipe. Vapor chambers are used to even out temperature differences ("hot spots") on electronic components such as computer processors and high-power LEDs, moving heat from a small, intense source to a larger area where it can be dissipated by a heatsink or fan.

Structure and operation

A vapor chamber consists of a hermetically sealed, evacuated metal enclosure, usually made of copper, whose inner walls are lined with a capillary wick structure made of sintered metal powder, mesh or grooves. A small amount of working fluid—commonly deionized water—is sealed inside. Where heat is applied, the fluid evaporates; the vapor flows to cooler regions of the chamber and condenses, releasing its latent heat; capillary action in the wick returns the condensed liquid to the hot zone. The effective thermal conductivity of the device can exceed that of solid copper by an order of magnitude.

Applications

Vapor chambers appear in high-performance computing (GPUs and CPUs), gaming consoles, thin laptops and smartphones, aerospace electronics and power semiconductors, where space is limited and heat fluxes are high. Compared with conventional heat pipes, a vapor chamber spreads heat in two dimensions rather than along a line, but it is typically more expensive to manufacture and can be damaged by bending.
