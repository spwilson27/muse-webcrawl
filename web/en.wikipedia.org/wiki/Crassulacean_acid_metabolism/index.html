Crassulacean acid metabolism


Overview of CAM

Crassulacean acid metabolism, also known as CAM photosynthesis, is a carbon fixation pathway that evolved in some plants as an adaptation to arid conditions. A plant using full CAM can keep its leaf stomata closed during the day to conserve water, opening them at night to take in carbon dioxide, which is stored as the organic acid malate in vacuoles. During the day the stored acid is decarboxylated, releasing CO₂ for photosynthesis behind closed stomata.

Mechanism

At night, CO₂ is fixed by the enzyme PEP carboxylase into oxaloacetate and then malate, which accumulates in the vacuole (making CAM leaves taste sour in the morning and bland by evening — the phenomenon from which the pathway was named, after studies on the family Crassulaceae). By day, malate is released and decarboxylated, and the CO₂ is refixed by RuBisCO in the Calvin cycle.

Distribution and significance

CAM occurs in many families of desert and epiphytic plants, including cacti, orchids, bromeliads (including pineapple), agaves, and stonecrops. It dramatically improves water-use efficiency at the cost of slower growth, and is studied as a possible route to engineering drought tolerance in crops.
