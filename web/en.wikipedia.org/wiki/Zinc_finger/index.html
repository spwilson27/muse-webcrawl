Zinc finger

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A zinc finger is a small protein structural motif that is characterized by the coordination of one or more zinc ions (Zn2+) in order to stabilize the fold. It was originally coined to describe the finger-like appearance of a hypothesized structure from the African clawed frog (Xenopus laevis) transcription factor TFIIIA. Zinc fingers often occur in groups, forming modules that bind DNA, RNA, proteins, or small molecules, and they are among the most common DNA-binding domains in eukaryotic genomes.

Classes

The classic Cys2His2 (C2H2) finger folds into an alpha helix and a beta hairpin held together by a zinc ion coordinated by two cysteine and two histidine residues. Other classes include C4 fingers found in nuclear hormone receptors and C6 (binuclear cluster) fingers found in fungal transcription factors such as GAL4. Each finger typically recognizes about three base pairs of DNA, and tandem arrays can be engineered to target specific sequences.

Applications

Engineered zinc finger arrays are combined with nuclease domains to create zinc finger nucleases (ZFNs), among the first programmable genome-editing tools. Zinc finger transcription factors have also been explored for gene regulation therapies.
