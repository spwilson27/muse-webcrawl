Francis Xavier, S.J. (born Francisco de Jasso y Azpilicueta; 7 April 1506 – 3 December 1552), venerated as Saint Francis Xavier, was a Spanish Catholic priest and missionary, and co-founder of the Society of Jesus (Jesuits). He was a companion of Ignatius of Loyola and one of the first seven Jesuits to take vows of poverty and chastity at Montmartre in 1534.

He led an extensive mission into Asia, mainly in the Portuguese Empire of the time, working in India, the Malay Archipelago and Japan, and died on an island off the coast of China while seeking entry to the mainland. He has been called the "Apostle of the Indies" and the "Apostle of Japan".

Missions

Arriving in Goa in 1542 as a papal nuncio, Xavier worked among the Paravar pearl-fishers of the Fishery Coast, in Travancore, Ceylon, Malacca and the Maluku Islands. In 1549 he reached Kagoshima in Japan, where he spent over two years establishing missions before the hostility of local circumstances and his own ambitions turned him toward China. He reached Shangchuan Island in 1552 and died there of fever, awaiting passage to the mainland.

Veneration

Xavier's largely incorrupt body is enshrined in the Basilica of Bom Jesus in Goa, where expositions of the relics draw large crowds; his right forearm is kept in Rome. He was canonised in 1622 alongside Ignatius of Loyola and Teresa of Ávila, and in 1927 was named, with Thérèse of Lisieux, patron saint of Catholic missions. His feast day is 3 December.
