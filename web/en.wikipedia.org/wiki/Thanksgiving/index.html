Thanksgiving is a national holiday celebrated in the United States, Canada, and some other countries, originating as a day of giving thanks for the blessing of the harvest and of the preceding year. In the United States it is observed on the fourth Thursday of November; in Canada, on the second Monday of October.

History

The American tradition is commonly traced to the 1621 harvest feast shared by the English colonists of Plymouth and the Wampanoag people, though days of thanksgiving were proclaimed by colonial and later state governments on many occasions. During the Civil War, President Abraham Lincoln proclaimed a national Thanksgiving Day in 1863, following advocacy by writer Sarah Josepha Hale. In 1941, Congress fixed the date as the fourth Thursday in November. Canadian thanksgiving traditions draw on earlier explorer and harvest observances, and the holiday was set on the second Monday of October in 1957.

Observances

The holiday centres on a large family meal whose traditional centrepiece is roast turkey, accompanied by stuffing, mashed potatoes, cranberry sauce, and pumpkin pie. Other customs include the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade in New York (begun 1924), professional and college football games, charitable food drives, and the ceremonial presidential "pardoning" of a turkey. The following day, Black Friday, marks the traditional start of the Christmas shopping season.

Criticism and other observances

For many Native Americans, Thanksgiving is a day of mourning that recalls the colonisation of indigenous lands and peoples; the National Day of Mourning has been observed at Plymouth since 1970. Similar harvest thanksgiving festivals exist worldwide, such as Erntedankfest in Germany and the Mid-Autumn Festival in China.
