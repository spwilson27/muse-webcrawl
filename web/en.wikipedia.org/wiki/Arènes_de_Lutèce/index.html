The Arènes de Lutèce ("Arenas of Lutetia") are among the most important remains of the Gallo-Roman city of Lutetia, the predecessor of Paris, France. Built in the 1st century AD on the slopes of the Montagne Sainte-Geneviève in what is now the 5th arrondissement, the structure combined the functions of an amphitheatre and a theatre, and is one of the oldest surviving monuments in Paris.

Description

The building was a hybrid typical of Roman Gaul: a semi-circular theatrical stage building (scaena) at one end faced a more-than-semicircular arena surrounded by stepped seating (the cavea) cut into the hillside. The arena could host gladiatorial combats and wild-beast hunts while the stage served dramatic performances. Estimates of capacity vary, commonly reaching as high as 15,000 spectators—far larger than the population estimates for early Lutetia, suggesting it served the surrounding region. Five animal cages were built into the podium wall, and a long aqueduct-fed channel carried drainage away toward the Bièvre.

History

The amphitheatre flourished during the Pax Romana. With the invasions of the late 3rd century, Lutetia contracted onto the Île de la Cité, and stone from the arena was reused in the city walls; the site was gradually filled in and forgotten, eventually becoming vineyards and then a cemetery and convent grounds.

Rediscovery and preservation

The remains came to light in 1869–1870 when the Compagnie Générale des Omnibus cut a depot site near the rue Monge. Part of the monument had already been destroyed, and a public campaign—led by writers including Victor Hugo, who appealed to the Paris municipal council not to destroy the arena—preserved the surviving portion. The city purchased the land, and the archaeologist Théodore Vacquer studied the remains. Restoration in 1915–1918, supervised by the architect Jules Formigé and financed by Dr. Louis Capitan, reconstructed the cavea seating; the site is now the Square Capitan, a quiet public garden entered from the rue Monge, the rue de la Clef, and the rue de Navarre.

Today

Today the arenas function as a neighbourhood park where local residents play pétanque and football, children use the space, and occasional concerts and festivals are held. A plaque and bust commemorate its preservation campaign, and the original stage area and animal cages remain visible among the reconstructed tiers.
