Somatosensory system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The somatosensory system is the part of the sensory nervous system concerned with the perception of touch, pressure, vibration, temperature, pain, itch, and proprioception (the sense of body position). It is a three-neuron system that relays sensations from receptors throughout the body to the somatosensory cortex in the parietal lobe of the brain. Unlike the special senses, somatosensory receptors are distributed over the whole body surface and in muscles, joints, and viscera.

Receptors

Mechanoreceptors in the skin include Meissner corpuscles (light touch and flutter), Pacinian corpuscles (deep pressure and high-frequency vibration), Merkel discs (fine detail and texture), and Ruffini endings (skin stretch). Thermoreceptors respond to warmth and cold; nociceptors are free nerve endings that signal potentially damaging stimuli as pain. Muscle spindles and Golgi tendon organs provide proprioceptive information.

Pathways

Fine touch, vibration, and proprioception ascend in the dorsal column–medial lemniscus pathway, crossing in the medulla. Pain and temperature travel in the anterolateral (spinothalamic) system, crossing near their level of entry in the spinal cord. Both pathways relay in the thalamus before reaching the primary somatosensory cortex, where the body is represented in an orderly map — the sensory homunculus — with area proportional to receptor density rather than body size, so the lips and fingertips occupy large territories. The differing levels of crossing explain the clinical patterns of sensory loss after spinal cord or brainstem lesions, such as the dissociated sensory loss of Brown-Séquard syndrome.
