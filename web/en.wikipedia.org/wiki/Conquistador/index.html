Conquistador

Conquistadors were the explorer-soldiers of the Spanish and Portuguese Empires during the Age of Discovery in the 15th through 17th centuries, who sailed beyond Europe to conquer territory and open trade routes across the Americas, the Philippines, and parts of Asia and Africa.

Background

Following the Reconquista of Iberia (completed 1492) and Columbus's voyages, Spanish monarchs licensed private expeditions (entradas) whose leaders expected land, titles, gold, and encomienda grants in return for conquering and Christianising new territories at their own expense.

Major figures and conquests

  • Hernán Cortés — conquest of the Aztec Empire (1519–1521), aided by Indigenous allies such as the Tlaxcalans and interpreter La Malinche.
  • Francisco Pizarro — conquest of the Inca Empire (1532–1533), capturing Atahualpa at Cajamarca.
  • Pedro de Alvarado, Francisco Vázquez de Coronado, Hernando de Soto — expeditions in Central and North America.
  • Vasco Núñez de Balboa — reached the Pacific in 1513.

Factors and consequences

Small conquistador forces combined steel weapons, horses, ships, and — decisively — alliances with Indigenous peoples and the catastrophic spread of Eurasian diseases such as smallpox, which devastated American populations. The conquests brought colonisation, the encomienda and mita labour systems, the Columbian exchange, and immense silver flows (Potosí, Zacatecas) that reshaped the world economy, alongside demographic collapse and lasting controversy over violence and exploitation.
