The Slave Trade Act 1807, officially An Act for the Abolition of the Slave Trade, was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom prohibiting the slave trade in the British Empire. Although it did not abolish slavery itself, it outlawed the transatlantic trade in enslaved people for British subjects and ships. It received royal assent on 25 March 1807 — a date now commemorated by the United Nations as the International Day of Remembrance of the Victims of Slavery and the Transatlantic Slave Trade.

Background

The British abolition campaign, led in Parliament by William Wilberforce and organised by the Committee for the Abolition of the Slave Trade (founded 1787, with Thomas Clarkson gathering evidence and Olaudah Equiano publicising the trade's horrors), waged a two-decade campaign of petitions, boycotts and bills. Wilberforce's first abolition bill failed in 1791, and repeated attempts were blocked through the 1790s. Strategic groundwork came with the Foreign Slave Trade Act 1806, which barred British subjects from supplying enslaved people to foreign colonies, cutting away much of the trade before the general act passed amid the Ministry of All the Talents government of Lord Grenville.

Provisions

The Act made it unlawful for any British ship or subject to engage in the slave trade, imposing fines of £100 per enslaved person carried and forfeiture of the vessel. From 1808 the Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron policed the trade off West Africa, eventually capturing some 1,600 slave ships and freeing about 150,000 Africans over the following decades. Britain then pressed other nations — through treaties with Spain, Portugal, the Netherlands and others — to abandon the trade.

Limitations and aftermath

The Act left slavery itself intact in British colonies, where roughly 800,000 people remained enslaved, and an illegal trade persisted. Continued campaigning led to the Slavery Abolition Act 1833, which emancipated enslaved people across most of the Empire from 1834 (followed by an "apprenticeship" period to 1838) and paid £20 million in compensation to slave owners — not to the enslaved — a fact central to modern debates about the Act's legacy.
