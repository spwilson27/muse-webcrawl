Wilson cycle
Named afterJohn Tuzo Wilson (1908–1993)
FieldPlate tectonics, geology
DescribesOpening and closing of ocean basins over hundreds of millions of years

The Wilson cycle is a model in plate tectonics describing the opening and closing of ocean basins driven by the movement of Earth's tectonic plates. Named for Canadian geophysicist John Tuzo Wilson, who proposed key elements of it in the 1960s — beginning with his 1966 paper asking "Did the Atlantic close and then re-open?" — the cycle explains how continents repeatedly rift apart to create new oceans and later converge to close them, assembling supercontinents that eventually break up again.

Stages

A simplified cycle passes through the following stages, each named for a modern example:

  1. Embryonic — a continent begins to rift, forming a fault-bounded valley (example: East African Rift).
  2. Juvenile — rifting succeeds and a narrow sea with young oceanic crust forms (example: Red Sea).
  3. Mature — a wide ocean basin with passive margins develops (example: Atlantic Ocean).
  4. Declining — subduction begins on one or both margins, and the ocean starts to shrink (example: Pacific Ocean).
  5. Terminal — the ocean narrows to a sea as continents approach (example: Mediterranean Sea).
  6. Suturing / continental collision — the ocean closes completely and the continents collide, raising mountain ranges along a suture zone (example: Himalayas, where the former Tethys Ocean has closed).

After collision, the assembled continent may later break up — often roughly along the old suture — beginning the cycle anew. Wilson's original insight was that the Appalachian and Caledonian mountain belts record the closing of a precursor ocean, the Iapetus Ocean, whose successor, the Atlantic, reopened in nearly the same place.

Significance

The Wilson cycle provides a framework linking the supercontinent cycle, mountain building, basin formation, and the geological record of past oceans preserved in ophiolite complexes and orogenic belts. A full cycle typically takes several hundred million years.
