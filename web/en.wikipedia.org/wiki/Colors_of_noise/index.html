Colors of noise

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Power spectra of the principal colours of noise.

In audio engineering, electronics, and physics, the colors of noise (or colours of noise) refer to the power spectrum of a noise signal. Different colors of noise have significantly different characteristics — the analogy comes from the spectrum of light, where a signal carrying equal power at all frequencies resembles white light.

Principal colors

  • White noise has equal power per unit frequency (a flat spectrum), like the hiss of static; uncorrelated samples make it sound bright and hissy.
  • Pink noise (1/f noise) has equal power per octave, falling 3 dB per octave; it sounds balanced and is used to tune audio systems and in studies of natural processes, where 1/f fluctuations are ubiquitous.
  • Brown(ian) noise, named for Robert Brown, falls 6 dB per octave; its random-walk waveform sounds deep and rumbling, like distant thunder or a waterfall.
  • Blue noise rises 3 dB per octave, sounding bright and thin; it is used in dithering images and audio.
  • Violet noise rises 6 dB per octave and sounds very shrill.
  • Grey noise is shaped to sound equally loud at all frequencies, compensating for the ear's sensitivity curve.
  • Green, black, orange and other informal colors are used less consistently in specific technical niches.

Applications

Noise colours are used for testing loudspeakers and rooms, masking unwanted sounds in offices and for sleep, calibrating instruments, and as starting points in sound synthesis. Pink-noise generators and measurements are standard tools in professional audio.
