Spliceosome

A spliceosome is a large ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex found primarily within the nucleus of eukaryotic cells. The spliceosome is assembled from small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs) and numerous proteins, and it carries out RNA splicing: the removal of non-coding introns from a transcribed pre-mRNA and the joining of the remaining exons to produce mature messenger RNA. Each spliceosome assembles anew on every intron, undergoing a choreographed cycle of conformational changes driven by ATP-dependent RNA helicases.

Mechanism

Splicing proceeds by two transesterification reactions. First, the 2′ hydroxyl of a branch-point adenosine within the intron attacks the 5′ splice site, freeing the first exon and forming a lariat intermediate. Second, the freed exon's 3′ hydroxyl attacks the 3′ splice site, ligating the exons and releasing the intron lariat, which is then degraded. The U5 and U6 snRNAs and the Prp8 protein form the catalytic heart of the complex, and structural studies since 2015 — notably from the groups of Kiyoshi Nagai and Yigong Shi — have revealed near-atomic snapshots of successive assembly states (B, Bact, C, C* and post-catalytic complexes).

Alternative splicing and disease

Interactions between the spliceosome and regulatory proteins enable alternative splicing, allowing one gene to produce multiple protein isoforms — a major source of proteomic diversity in humans. Mutations in core spliceosomal factors (e.g. SF3B1, U2AF1, SRSF2) are recurrent drivers in myelodysplastic syndromes and chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, and splicing defects underlie diseases such as spinal muscular atrophy, whose treatment nusinersen (Spinraza) works by redirecting splicing of the SMN2 gene.
