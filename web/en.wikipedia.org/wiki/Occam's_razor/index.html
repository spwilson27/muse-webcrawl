Occam's razor

Occam's razor, also spelled Ockham's razor, is the problem-solving principle that, all else being equal, entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity. It is usually attributed to the English Franciscan friar and scholastic philosopher William of Ockham (c. 1287–1347), although similar ideas were expressed by earlier thinkers. The principle is often paraphrased as "the simplest explanation is usually the best one."

Formulation

The principle is most commonly associated with the Latin phrase entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem ("entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity"), although this exact wording does not appear in Ockham's own writings. Ockham himself wrote formulations such as "it is pointless to do with more what can be done with fewer" (frustra fit per plura quod potest fieri per pauciora) and "plurality must not be posited without necessity" (pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate).

History

Antecedents of the razor can be found in the works of Aristotle, who wrote that nature operates in the shortest way possible, and in Ptolemy, who stated that hypotheses should account for phenomena in the simplest manner. Medieval philosophers including Robert Grosseteste, Thomas Aquinas, and John Duns Scotus all appealed to simplicity in explanation. After Ockham, the principle was taken up by figures such as Isaac Newton, who wrote in the Principia that no more causes of natural things should be admitted than are both true and sufficient to explain their appearances.

Applications

Occam's razor is widely used as a heuristic in science. In model selection, it counsels against overfitting data with unnecessarily complex theories. In medicine, clinicians are sometimes reminded of the complementary warning — sometimes called Hickam's dictum — that a patient can have more than one condition, so the razor must be applied with care. The principle also figures in philosophy of religion, probability theory (where related ideas appear in the Solomonoff theory of inductive inference), and debates over the interpretation of quantum mechanics.

Criticism

Critics note that the razor is a rule of thumb rather than an irrefutable principle: the simplest explanation is not always the correct one, and "simplicity" itself can be difficult to define objectively. Nonetheless, the razor remains one of the most influential methodological maxims in Western thought.
