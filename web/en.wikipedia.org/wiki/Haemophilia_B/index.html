Haemophilia B

Haemophilia B, also spelled hemophilia B and historically called Christmas disease, is a blood clotting disorder caused by a mutation of the gene for factor IX, resulting in a deficiency of functional factor IX protein. It is the second most common form of haemophilia, after haemophilia A, and is inherited in an X-linked recessive manner, so it predominantly affects males.

Signs and symptoms

The deficiency of factor IX impairs the coagulation cascade, leading to prolonged bleeding after injury or surgery, easy bruising, and spontaneous bleeding into joints (haemarthrosis) and muscles in severe cases. Severity correlates with residual factor IX activity: severe disease is defined by levels below 1% of normal. The condition is named after Stephen Christmas, the first patient described with the disease in 1952.

Treatment

Treatment involves replacement of factor IX with plasma-derived or recombinant concentrates, given on demand or as regular prophylaxis. Extended half-life products have reduced infusion frequency. Gene therapy for haemophilia B, using adeno-associated virus vectors to deliver a functional F9 gene, has been approved in several jurisdictions, offering sustained factor IX expression after a single infusion.
