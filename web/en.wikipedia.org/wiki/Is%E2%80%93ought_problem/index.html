Is–ought problem

Is–ought problem

Field: Meta-ethics

Introduced by: David Hume

Also called: Hume's law, Hume's guillotine

The is–ought problem, as articulated by the Scottish philosopher David Hume, arises when one makes claims about what ought to be that are based solely on statements about what is. Hume found that there seems to be a significant difference between descriptive statements (about what is) and prescriptive statements (about what ought to be), and that it is not obvious how one can coherently move from the former to the latter.

Hume's formulation

In Book III of A Treatise of Human Nature (1739), Hume noted that writers on morality often proceed from ordinary statements of fact to conclusions about what ought to be done, without explaining the inference. He insisted that this transition requires justification, since the conclusions appear "entirely different" from the premises.

Responses and influence

Philosophers have responded in varied ways: some defend a strict gap (the naturalistic fallacy debate following G. E. Moore), others argue that institutional facts, functional concepts, or thick ethical concepts bridge the divide. The problem remains central to meta-ethics and to debates over deriving values from facts in science and policy.
