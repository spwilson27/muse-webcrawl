Payroll deduction

A payroll deduction is an amount withheld by an employer from an employee's wages or salary before the employee is paid, so that the employee receives the net ("take-home") pay. Deductions may be required by law, ordered by a court, or authorised voluntarily by the employee. The process of calculating and remitting them is a central part of payroll administration.

Mandatory deductions

In most countries, employers must withhold income tax under pay-as-you-earn systems, together with employee contributions to social insurance. In the United States these include federal and state income tax withholding and the employee share of Social Security and Medicare taxes under FICA. In the United Kingdom, Pay As You Earn (PAYE) income tax and National Insurance contributions are deducted at source. Court-ordered deductions include wage garnishments for debts, child support and tax levies.

Voluntary deductions

Employees may authorise deductions for retirement plan contributions (such as 401(k) or pension contributions), health, dental and life insurance premiums, union dues, charitable giving schemes, savings plans and repayment of employer loans or wage advances. Because some voluntary deductions are taken before tax is calculated, they can reduce taxable pay; these are called pre-tax deductions, in contrast to after-tax (post-tax) deductions.

Employer obligations

Employers must remit withheld amounts to the relevant tax authorities and benefit providers on prescribed schedules, report them on pay statements and annual wage statements (such as the U.S. Form W-2), and keep records. Failure to remit trust-fund taxes can result in penalties and personal liability for responsible persons. Laws generally restrict the deductions an employer may make without consent and prohibit deductions that reduce pay below the minimum wage, except for those required by law.
