Exculpatory evidence

Exculpatory evidence is evidence favourable to a criminal defendant that tends to exonerate them of guilt — by negating an element of the offence, supporting an alibi or defence, or undermining the credibility of prosecution witnesses or evidence. It is the opposite of inculpatory evidence.

Disclosure obligations

In the United States, the Supreme Court held in Brady v. Maryland (1963) that the prosecution's suppression of evidence favourable to the accused, where the evidence is material to guilt or punishment, violates due process — regardless of the prosecutor's good or bad faith. Materiality, refined in United States v. Bagley (1985), means a reasonable probability that disclosure would have changed the outcome. Related obligations arise from Giglio v. United States (1972) for information impeaching prosecution witnesses, and from rules such as the Jencks Act. Failure to disclose "Brady material" is a leading cause of overturned convictions and wrongful-conviction exonerations.

Other jurisdictions impose comparable duties: prosecutors in England and Wales must disclose material that might undermine the prosecution case or assist the defence under the Criminal Procedure and Investigations Act 1996. Modern sources of exculpatory evidence include DNA testing — the basis of most post-conviction exonerations — surveillance footage, mobile-phone location data, and digital records.
