Nitria (monastic site)

Nitria was one of the earliest centres of Christian monasticism in Egypt, founded around AD 330 by Amoun. It lay in the Nitrian Desert (Wadi El Natrun region, northwest of Cairo), near deposits of natron salt from which it took its name.

History

Amoun, advised by Anthony the Great, established Nitria as a settlement of anchorites living in individual cells within walking distance of a common church. By the late 4th century it housed thousands of monks and was visited by figures such as Melania the Elder, Rufinus and Evagrius Ponticus (briefly, before he moved to Kellia).

Organisation

Monks lived alone or in pairs, gathered on Saturdays and Sundays for worship, and were visited by large numbers of pilgrims. Nitria, together with Kellia and Scetis, formed the great monastic triangle of the Egyptian desert described in the Historia Monachorum and Palladius's Lausiac History.

Decline

The community was caught up in the Origenist controversies around 400, when Patriarch Theophilus of Alexandria expelled the Tall Brothers and their supporters. Monastic life in the immediate area faded, though the neighbouring Wadi El Natrun monasteries survive to this day.
