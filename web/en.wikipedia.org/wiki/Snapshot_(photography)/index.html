Snapshot (photography)

A snapshot is an informal photograph taken spontaneously and quickly, most often without artistic or journalistic intent and usually made with a relatively cheap and simple camera. Common snapshot subjects include events of everyday life, often portraying family members, friends, pets and children.

The term originated in hunting ("snap shot", a quick shot without careful aim) and was applied to photography in the 1890s, after George Eastman's Kodak box cameras and the slogan "You press the button, we do the rest" made casual photography accessible to amateurs. The roll film camera and later inexpensive point-and-shoot, disposable and camera-phone devices made the snapshot the most common form of photograph in the world.

Snapshot aesthetic

Although snapshots are usually considered vernacular rather than art, a recognisable "snapshot aesthetic" — tilted framing, harsh flash, red-eye, unposed subjects — was adopted by art photographers in the 20th century, including figures such as Robert Frank, Garry Winogrand and Nan Goldin, and snapshot-style images entered museum collections. Critics have debated whether such work elevates the mundane or merely imitates it.
