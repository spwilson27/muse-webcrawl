Income in the United States


Median household income in the United States

Income in the United States is measured by several federal agencies, principally the U.S. Census Bureau and the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and is commonly reported as personal income, household income, or earnings by demographic group. Median household income in the early 2020s was on the order of $75,000–$78,000 per year, though figures vary substantially by state, race, education, and household composition.

Distribution

The United States has one of the most unequal income distributions among high-income countries. The top quintile of households receives roughly half of aggregate income, and since the late 1970s income growth has been fastest at the top of the distribution, a pattern driven by rising returns to education, technological change, globalization, and changes in tax and labor-market institutions.

By demographics

Income varies markedly by educational attainment: households headed by holders of bachelor's or advanced degrees earn several times the median of those without a high school diploma. Asian and White households have higher median incomes than Black and Hispanic households. Men continue to earn more on average than women, though the gender earnings gap has narrowed over recent decades. Regionally, incomes are highest in the Northeast and West and in metropolitan areas such as San Jose, San Francisco, and Washington, D.C.

Measurement issues

Statistics differ depending on whether income is measured before or after taxes and transfers, whether it includes non-cash benefits and capital gains, and whether the unit is the individual, family, or household. Real (inflation-adjusted) median income rose slowly over the late twentieth century and stagnated or fell during the recessions of 2008–2009 and 2020.
