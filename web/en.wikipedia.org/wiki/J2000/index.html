J2000

J2000.0 is the standard epoch currently used in astronomy. It is precisely the Julian date 2451545.0 TT (Terrestrial Time), equivalent to noon on January 1, 2000, Terrestrial Time. In the Gregorian calendar this corresponds approximately to 11:59:27.816 UTC on January 1, 2000.

Use

The J2000 epoch serves as the reference point for equatorial coordinates in most modern star catalogues and for the International Celestial Reference System (ICRS). Right ascension and declination are commonly quoted "at epoch J2000," meaning the positions are given in the coordinate frame fixed to that instant. Positions of Solar System objects, satellite orbits, and the mean elements of orbits are also frequently referenced to J2000.

The prefix "J" indicates a Julian epoch, defined in terms of Julian years of exactly 365.25 days, as opposed to the older Besselian epochs (prefixed "B"), which were based on tropical years. The previous standard epoch was B1950.0, used by the FK4 catalogue; the J2000 system was adopted with the FK5 catalogue in the 1980s.

Julian date conversion

The Julian date for J2000.0 is 2451545.0. Any instant's offset from J2000 can be expressed in Julian days or Julian centuries of 36,525 days, a unit used throughout the VSOP and other planetary theories. For example, the parameter T in many astronomical algorithms is defined as Julian centuries elapsed since J2000.0.

Why a fixed epoch matters

Because of precession and nutation, the direction of Earth's axis changes continuously, so equatorial coordinates slowly drift. Fixing coordinates to a standard epoch lets astronomers compare observations taken decades apart and provides a stable frame for pointing telescopes. When observations are made, coordinates are transformed from the fixed J2000 frame to the apparent frame of the date of observation.
