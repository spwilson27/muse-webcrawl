Revolutions of 1848 in the Austrian Empire

Revolutions of 1848 in the Austrian Empire
DateMarch 1848 – November 1849
LocationAustrian Empire: Vienna, Hungary, Bohemia, Lombardy–Venetia, Galicia, Croatia, Transylvania
CausesLiberal and national demands, economic hardship, Metternich's reactionary system
OutcomeRevolutions suppressed; neo-absolutism under Franz Joseph I; serfdom permanently abolished

The Revolutions of 1848 in the Austrian Empire were a series of liberal and national uprisings that shook the Habsburg monarchy during the wider European Revolutions of 1848. Beginning in Vienna in March 1848, revolts spread to Hungary, Bohemia, the Italian provinces of Lombardy–Venetia, Galicia, Croatia and Transylvania. Although the imperial government of the young Emperor Franz Joseph I ultimately crushed the revolutions with military force—and, in Hungary, with Russian assistance—they destroyed the system of Prince Klemens von Metternich and permanently ended serfdom in the empire.

Outbreak

News of the February revolution in Paris ignited simmering discontent. On 3 March 1848 Lajos Kossuth delivered a celebrated speech in the Hungarian Diet demanding constitutional government. On 13 March crowds in Vienna stormed the Landhaus; troops fired on demonstrators, and Chancellor Metternich—the symbol of the post-1815 reactionary order—resigned and fled to England. The Emperor Ferdinand I promised a constitution.

Vienna

The Viennese revolution unfolded in waves (March, May and October 1848). Students of the Academic Legion and workers manned barricades; a constituent assembly, the Reichstag, was elected and later moved to Kremsier (Kroměříž), where it drafted a liberal constitution. In October, when troops were ordered to march against Hungary, Vienna rose again; imperial forces under Prince Windischgrätz bombarded and retook the city, executing leaders including the German democrat Robert Blum. Ferdinand abdicated on 2 December 1848 in favour of his eighteen-year-old nephew, Franz Joseph.

Hungary

The Hungarian revolution was the most formidable. The April Laws created a modern, responsible Hungarian government; in September 1848, as imperial policy turned to confrontation, Kossuth organised national defence. Croatian Ban Josip Jelačić invaded Hungary in Vienna's interest. In April 1849 the Hungarian Diet declared independence and deposed the Habsburgs. Only the intervention of some 200,000 Russian troops, requested by Franz Joseph, enabled Austria to prevail; the Hungarian army surrendered at Világos on 13 August 1849, and General Lajos Batthyány and thirteen Hungarian generals were executed.

Other theatres

  • Bohemia — the Prague Slav Congress and the Whitsun uprising (June 1848) were suppressed by Windischgrätz, who bombarded Prague.
  • Italy — Milan's Five Days drove Field Marshal Radetzky from the city; Piedmont-Sardinia intervened but was defeated at Custoza (1848) and Novara (1849). Venice under Daniele Manin held out until August 1849.
  • Galicia and Transylvania — Polish and Romanian national movements collided with each other and with Vienna; peasant grievances were exploited by the authorities.

Consequences

The victorious dynasty imposed "neo-absolutism" (the Bach system), revoking the Kremsier and March constitutions. Yet one revolutionary achievement survived: the abolition of serfdom and peasant dues, enacted in September 1848, was never reversed. The national conflicts left unresolved in 1848–49 would trouble the monarchy until its collapse in 1918.
