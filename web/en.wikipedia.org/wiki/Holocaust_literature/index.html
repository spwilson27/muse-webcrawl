Holocaust literature

Holocaust literature is a body of writing — memoir, diary, testimony, fiction, poetry and drama — that bears witness to, documents or reflects upon the Holocaust, the genocide of some six million Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators during the Second World War. It includes works written during the events themselves, in ghettos and camps, as well as the vast literature produced by survivors and later generations in the decades since 1945.

Contemporaneous writing

Despite conditions designed to erase them, victims documented their experiences in real time. The Oyneg Shabes archive assembled by Emanuel Ringelblum in the Warsaw Ghetto preserved diaries, essays and records buried in milk cans and recovered after the war. Diaries such as those of Anne Frank, Etty Hillesum, Victor Klemperer and Hélène Berr have become central texts. Members of the Sonderkommando at Auschwitz buried manuscripts, later recovered, that describe the extermination process from inside the crematoria.

Survivor memoir and testimony

The best-known survivor accounts include Elie Wiesel's Night (1958 in Yiddish; 1960 in English), Primo Levi's If This Is a Man (1947) and The Drowned and the Saved (1986), Tadeusz Borowski's short stories in This Way for the Gas, Ladies and Gentlemen, Charlotte Delbo's Auschwitz and After, and the works of Jean Améry, Imre Kertész, Ruth Klüger and Viktor Frankl, whose Man's Search for Meaning (1946) combined testimony with psychological reflection.

Fiction, poetry and drama

Poets such as Paul Celan ("Todesfuge"), Nelly Sachs, Dan Pagis and Abraham Sutzkever wrestled with how language could carry the event at all. Fiction includes works by survivors and by later writers, from Aharon Appelfeld and Ida Fink to international works such as Schindler's List by Thomas Keneally. The adequacy of representation has been a persistent theme: Theodor Adorno's dictum that "to write poetry after Auschwitz is barbaric" has been debated for decades, and the boundary between testimony and fiction remains contested, particularly around fabricated memoirs.

Second and third generations

Children and grandchildren of survivors have produced a substantial literature of inherited memory, sometimes termed "postmemory" (Marianne Hirsch), including Art Spiegelman's graphic novel Maus (1980–1991), which won a Pulitzer Prize, and works by Helen Epstein and Daniel Mendelsohn. Holocaust literature is now studied and taught worldwide, preserved by archives such as Yad Vashem, the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum and the USC Shoah Foundation's video testimony collection.
