Hikikomori

Hikikomori (Japanese: ひきこもり, "pulling inward") is a Japanese term for acute social withdrawal, and for people — mostly adolescents and young adults — who withdraw from social life, often seeking extreme isolation for months or years. Psychologist Tamaki Saitō popularised the term in 1998.

Hikikomori
Country most associated: Japan
Term popularised: Tamaki Saitō, 1998
Estimated in Japan: ~1.5 million (2023 Cabinet Office survey, ages 15–64)
Related: NEET, social anxiety, the 8050 problem

Definition and prevalence

Japanese government criteria include spending most of the day at home, avoiding school or work, and withdrawal persisting at least six months, excluding psychosis. Surveys suggest prevalence around 1–2% of the population in Japan, with cases also reported in South Korea, France, Italy, Spain, the United States and elsewhere. Many are male, but recent surveys show a growing share of women and middle-aged hikikomori.

Causes

Factors discussed include school refusal (futōkō), bullying, academic and job-market pressure, family dynamics, shame culture, developmental disorders such as autism spectrum conditions, depression and social anxiety, and internet/gaming immersion. No single cause is agreed.

The 8050 problem

As hikikomori age while dependent on elderly parents — parents in their 80s supporting children in their 50s — financial and care crises emerge when parents die or become infirm.

Treatment and support

Approaches include outreach visits, family counselling, peer-support "rental sister/brother" services, employment support, and government regional support centres. Experts caution that forced removal usually worsens withdrawal.

In culture

Works exploring the phenomenon include the novel/anime Welcome to the N.H.K. and Michael Zielenziger's Shutting Out the Sun.
