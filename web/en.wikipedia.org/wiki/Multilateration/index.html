Multilateration

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Multilateration (abbreviated MLAT; more completely, pseudo-range multilateration) is a technique for determining the position of an object by measuring the differences in arrival times of a signal at several receivers at known locations. It is also called hyperbolic positioning, because each time-difference measurement defines a hyperbola (in two dimensions) or hyperboloid (in three) on which the object must lie; the intersection of several such curves gives its location.

Principle

If a pulse is emitted at an unknown time from an unknown position, receivers at known stations record its arrival times. The difference in arrival times between two receivers, multiplied by the speed of propagation (usually the speed of light for radio signals), equals the difference in distances from the emitter to those receivers. With at least three receivers, a two-dimensional position can be found; four or more allow a three-dimensional fix, usually over-determined and solved by least-squares methods. The technique requires the receivers to be precisely synchronised with one another, but — unlike true-range (trilateration) systems — the emitter's clock need not be known.

Applications

Multilateration was the basis of historic radio navigation systems such as Gee and LORAN. Today it is widely used in air traffic control: networks of ground stations receive aircraft transponder replies (including ADS-B and Mode S transmissions) and compute aircraft positions independently of radar, a technique deployed for airport surface surveillance and wide-area en-route coverage, including in mountainous regions where radar coverage is poor. Variants are also used in locating mobile phones, in seismic and lightning location networks, and in indoor positioning systems.
