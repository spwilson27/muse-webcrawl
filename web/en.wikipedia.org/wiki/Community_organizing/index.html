Community organizing

Community organizing is a process where people who live in proximity to each other or share some common problem come together into an organization that acts in their shared self-interest. Community organizers generally assume that social change necessarily involves conflict and social struggle in order to generate collective power for the powerless. A core goal of community organizing is to generate durable power for an organization representing the community, allowing it to influence key decision-makers on a range of issues over time.

History

Modern community organizing is usually traced to Saul Alinsky, who founded the Back of the Yards Neighborhood Council in Chicago in 1939 and the Industrial Areas Foundation (IAF) in 1940, and distilled his method in Reveille for Radicals (1946) and Rules for Radicals (1971). The civil rights movement developed parallel traditions — Ella Baker's emphasis on group-centred leadership and the organizing of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, Septima Clark's citizenship schools, and Fred Ross's Community Service Organization, which trained Cesar Chavez and Dolores Huerta. Later networks include ACORN, the Gamaliel Foundation, and PICO (now Faith in Action).

Approaches

  • Broad-based organizing — coalitions of institutions (congregations, unions, schools), as in the IAF model.
  • Faith-based organizing — mobilising religious congregations around shared values.
  • Door-to-door / neighbourhood organizing — building membership organisations block by block.
  • Asset-based community development — building on a community's existing strengths rather than deficits.
  • Digital organizing — online mobilisation combined with offline action.

Practice

Organizers build relationships through one-to-one meetings, identify local leaders, research issues and power structures, choose winnable and widely felt issues, and stage public actions — accountability sessions, rallies, negotiations — to win concessions and build the organisation. Unlike advocacy or service provision, organizing aims to shift who holds power, not merely to deliver services or speak on others' behalf.

Criticism

Critics have questioned organizer influence over agendas, the tension between professional organizers and grassroots leadership, and (from the right) the confrontational tactics codified by Alinsky; other critics argue some models avoid deeper structural change.
