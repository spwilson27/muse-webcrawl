A hand axe (or handaxe) is a prehistoric stone tool with two faces that is the longest-used tool in human history, although the term itself is somewhat of a misnomer, as many were not used as axes. It is made from a piece of stone, commonly flint, chert, or similar conchoidally fracturing material, reduced and shaped by the control of fractures to form a roughly bifacial, teardrop- or oval-shaped tool, usually with a sharp cutting edge around much of its perimeter.

Hand axes were the characteristic product of the Acheulean tool industry of the Lower Palaeolithic, first appearing in East Africa around 1.76 million years ago, associated with Homo erectus and later with archaic humans and Neanderthals in the Mousterian industry of the Middle Palaeolithic.

Manufacture

Hand axes were produced by striking flakes from a core or large flake blank using a hammerstone (hard hammer percussion) and later soft hammers of antler, bone, or wood, which allowed finer thinning and more regular, symmetrical outlines. The progressive refinement of form over hundreds of thousands of years is a key line of evidence in debates about the cognitive abilities of early humans.

Function

Despite the name, use-wear studies suggest hand axes were multipurpose tools: butchering large animals, digging, chopping wood, and processing plant material. Some unusually large or perfectly symmetrical examples may have had a social or display function, a suggestion sometimes called the "sexy handaxe" hypothesis in sexual-selection terms.

Distribution and chronology

Acheulean hand axes are found across Africa, Europe, and western Asia. The so-called Movius Line separates this biface-using west from East and Southeast Asia, where chopper tools predominated — a pattern variously explained by raw material availability (such as bamboo substitution) or by population history.
