Prosody (linguistics)

In linguistics, prosody is the study of the elements of speech that are not individual phonetic segments (vowels and consonants) but properties of syllables and larger units — intonation, stress, rhythm, tempo, tone and phrasing. These are often called suprasegmental features, because they extend over stretches of speech larger than a single segment. Acoustic correlates of prosody include fundamental frequency (perceived as pitch), duration (length) and intensity (loudness), along with voice quality.

Functions of prosody

Prosody plays grammatical, discourse and expressive roles:

  • Lexical — in tone languages such as Mandarin, pitch distinguishes words; in stress-accent languages, stress can distinguish words (English REcord vs reCORD).
  • Grammatical — intonation distinguishes statements from questions in many languages; prosodic phrasing disambiguates syntactic structure ("Let's eat, Grandma" versus "Let's eat Grandma").
  • Discourse and information structure — prominence marks new or contrasted information and helps manage turn-taking.
  • Emotional and attitudinal — prosody signals irony, doubt, excitement and politeness.

Prosodic units and analysis

Analysts describe prosody in terms of hierarchical units — the utterance, intonational phrase, phonological phrase, prosodic word, foot, syllable and mora. English intonation is often modelled with sequences of high (H) and low (L) tones, as in the autosegmental-metrical framework and the ToBI transcription system. Rhythm has traditionally been described by a typology of stress-timed, syllable-timed and mora-timed languages, though this classification is debated.

Prosody in poetry

The same word is used in literary studies for the study of metre and versification — the patterns of stressed and unstressed syllables in verse. This usage reflects the shared Greek root prosōidia, "song sung to music".

Disorders and applications

Abnormal prosody (aprosodia) can follow right-hemisphere brain damage, and atypical prosody is a feature of some developmental conditions. Prosody is central to speech technology: natural-sounding text-to-speech and accurate speech recognition both require modelling of phrasing, prominence and intonation.
