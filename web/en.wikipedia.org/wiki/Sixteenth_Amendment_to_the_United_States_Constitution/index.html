Sixteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution

The Sixteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution allows Congress to levy an income tax without apportioning it among the states on the basis of population. It was passed by Congress on 2 July 1909 and ratified by the requisite number of state legislatures on 3 February 1913.

"The Congress shall have power to lay and collect taxes on incomes, from whatever source derived, without apportionment among the several States, and without regard to any census or enumeration."

Background

The federal government had levied income taxes during the Civil War and again in 1894, but in Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co. (1895) the Supreme Court held that taxes on income from property were direct taxes that had to be apportioned among the states in proportion to population — a requirement that made a general income tax impractical. Supporters of the tax, including President William Howard Taft, sought a constitutional amendment to remove the obstacle.

Ratification and effects

The amendment was proposed in 1909, partly in the expectation by some conservative opponents that it would fail in the states; instead it was ratified swiftly. The Revenue Act of 1913 promptly imposed a federal income tax with rates from 1% to 7%, and the income tax went on to become the principal source of federal revenue. Later litigation has largely rejected claims that the amendment was improperly ratified; the Supreme Court upheld the modern tax's reach in cases such as Brushaber v. Union Pacific Railroad (1916).
