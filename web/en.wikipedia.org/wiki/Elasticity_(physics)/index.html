Elasticity (physics)

In physics and materials science, elasticity is the ability of a body to resist a distorting influence and to return to its original size and shape when that influence or force is removed. Solid objects will deform when adequate forces are applied to them; if the material is elastic, the object will return to its initial shape and size after removal. This is in contrast with plasticity, in which the deformation is permanent.

Hooke's law

For most materials, elastic deformation is described by Hooke's law: the strain (deformation) is proportional to the stress (force per unit area) that causes it, as long as the material is not loaded beyond its elastic limit. In one dimension, stress σ and strain ε are related by σ = Eε, where E is Young's modulus. Other elastic constants include Poisson's ratio, the shear modulus and the bulk modulus; for an isotropic linear elastic material, any two of these determine the rest.

Theory of elasticity

The mathematical theory of elasticity, developed in the 19th century by Cauchy, Navier, Saint-Venant and others, treats the stress and strain as tensor fields related by a constitutive law and governed by equations of motion or equilibrium. In the general anisotropic case, the stiffness tensor relating stress and strain has up to 21 independent components. Nonlinear elasticity is needed for large deformations, such as those of rubber, whose behaviour is described by hyperelastic models such as the neo-Hookean and Mooney–Rivlin laws.

Factors and limits

Elastic behaviour arises from interatomic forces: stretching a solid increases the spacing between atoms against attractive forces. Beyond the yield point a material deforms plastically by dislocation motion or other mechanisms. Temperature, strain rate and microstructure all affect elastic constants, and some materials — such as rubber — owe their elasticity mainly to entropy changes in coiled polymer chains rather than to bond stretching.

Applications

Elasticity underlies structural engineering, seismology (the propagation of elastic waves), acoustics, spring and sensor design, and the study of residual stresses. Linear elastic fracture mechanics uses the theory to predict crack behaviour in loaded solids.
