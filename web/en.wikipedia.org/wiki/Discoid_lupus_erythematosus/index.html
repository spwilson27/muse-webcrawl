Discoid lupus erythematosus

Discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE) is the most common form of chronic cutaneous lupus erythematosus, an autoimmune skin disease. It is characterised by coin-shaped (discoid), red, scaly plaques that heal with scarring, atrophy and changes in pigmentation. The lesions favour sun-exposed sites — the face, ears, scalp and neck — and scalp involvement can cause permanent scarring hair loss (alopecia). Unlike systemic lupus erythematosus, DLE usually remains confined to the skin, though a minority of patients (around 5–10%) develop systemic disease.

Signs and symptoms

Early lesions are well-demarcated red-violet plaques with adherent scale; removing the scale may reveal spines plugging the hair follicles (the "carpet tack" sign). Over time the centre of the lesion becomes pale and scarred while the rim may remain active and darker, particularly in patients with skin of colour, in whom post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is prominent. Lesions on the concha of the ear are characteristic. DLE is photosensitive — sunlight provokes and worsens lesions — and smoking is associated with more severe disease and poorer response to treatment. A small risk of squamous cell carcinoma arising in long-standing scars has been reported.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by skin biopsy showing an interface dermatitis with follicular plugging and basement membrane thickening, and by direct immunofluorescence of lesional skin (the lupus band test). Blood tests, including antinuclear antibodies, are usually negative or low-titre, and are used to screen for systemic involvement.

Treatment

Management centres on rigorous photoprotection, smoking cessation, and topical corticosteroids or calcineurin inhibitors. Widespread or refractory disease is treated with antimalarial drugs, especially hydroxychloroquine; other options include methotrexate, retinoids, dapsone and thalidomide in selected cases. Early treatment is important to prevent irreversible scarring and hair loss.
