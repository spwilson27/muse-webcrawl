Perry Preschool Project

Perry Preschool Project
LocationPerry Elementary School, Ypsilanti, Michigan, USA
Years1962–1967 (programme); follow-up continuing
Participants123 low-income African American children
Principal investigatorDavid Weikart (HighScope Educational Research Foundation)

The Perry Preschool Project was a landmark longitudinal study of early childhood education, conducted in Ypsilanti, Michigan, beginning in 1962. It randomly assigned 123 disadvantaged African American children, aged three and four, to either a high-quality preschool programme with home visits or to a no-preschool control group, and followed both groups for decades.

The programme

The intervention consisted of two school years of part-day preschool led by certified teachers with low child-to-teacher ratios, using what became the HighScope "plan–do–review" active-learning curriculum derived from Piagetian theory, plus weekly home visits by teachers. The study was designed by psychologist David Weikart and colleagues.

Findings

Follow-ups through age 40 found that the preschool group, compared with controls, had higher earnings and employment rates, higher rates of high-school graduation (especially among women), fewer arrests, and lower use of social services. Initial IQ gains faded in the early school years, but differences in achievement, behaviour and life outcomes persisted. Cost–benefit analyses estimated returns of roughly $7–$12 to society per $1 invested, making the study — together with the Abecedarian Project — a central piece of evidence in economic arguments (notably by James Heckman) for public investment in early childhood education.

Criticism

Commentators have noted the small sample size, the single site, the intensity of the programme relative to typical preschools, and questions about the randomisation procedure, cautioning against simple generalisation to large-scale programmes; the study's long-run non-cognitive effects nonetheless remain influential in developmental science and policy debates.
