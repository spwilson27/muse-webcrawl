An alleycat race is an informal bicycle race held in urban environments, traditionally organised by and for bicycle messengers. Rather than following a fixed course, riders receive a manifest listing a series of checkpoints around a city; the first rider to visit all checkpoints — in any order they choose — and return to the finish wins.

Format

Alleycats test the skills professional couriers use daily: knowledge of city streets, route planning, speed in traffic, and sometimes messenger-style tasks at checkpoints, such as picking up or dropping off packages. Checkpoints may only be revealed at the start, or released one at a time. Prizes are often donated by local bike shops and courier companies, and many races award a special prize to the first finisher riding fixed-gear and to the first woman ("first WTF" in modern usage). Results are frequently recorded on a spoke card placed between the wheel spokes.

History and spread

The first documented alleycat was held in Toronto on Halloween 1989, taking its name from the alley-cat-like agility of couriers threading through the city. The format spread through the messenger community in the 1990s alongside major messenger championships, and in the 2000s reached a much wider audience with the growth of fixed-gear cycling culture; famous recurring races include New York's Monstertrack, founded in 2000. Large alleycats have been criticised by authorities for encouraging dangerous riding and running red lights, and some have been banned or curtailed by police.

Related events

Alleycats sit within a broader culture of messenger racing that includes regional championships and the Cycle Messenger World Championships, first held in Berlin in 1993.
