Hair

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Human hair shafts under magnification, showing the overlapping cuticle scales.

Hair is a protein filament that grows from follicles found in the dermis. It is one of the defining characteristics of mammals: most mammals have some or much of their body covered in hair (fur or pelage), while humans are unusual in having greatly reduced body hair. Hair is made mainly of the tough protein keratin, and in humans it grows on the scalp (about 100,000–150,000 hairs), in the armpits and pubic region, and variably over the rest of the body.

Structure

A hair consists of a shaft above the skin, a root below, and a follicle — a pocket of epidermis extending into the dermis, ending in a bulb where living cells divide and build the shaft. From outside in, the shaft has three layers: the cuticle (overlapping flat scales), the cortex (the bulk, containing keratin bundles and melanin pigment), and in thicker hairs a central medulla. Attached to each follicle are sebaceous glands (which secrete sebum) and the arrector pili muscle, whose contraction produces "goose bumps".

Growth cycle

Hair grows in cycles: a long growing phase (anagen, 2–7 years for scalp hair, determining maximum length), a brief transitional phase (catagen), and a resting phase (telogen) after which the hair is shed. Humans shed roughly 50–100 scalp hairs per day. Hair grows about 1.25 cm per month on average.

Colour and texture

Hair colour is determined by melanin: eumelanin (brown/black) and pheomelanin (red/yellow), produced by melanocytes in the bulb; greying results from the age-related loss of pigment production. Natural colours range from black (most common worldwide) through brown and blond to red (rarest, ~1–2% of people). Texture — straight, wavy, curly, coily — depends largely on follicle shape and is genetically determined.

Functions and significance

In mammals hair serves insulation, protection, camouflage, and sensory roles (vibrissae/whiskers). In humans, scalp hair protects against UV and heat loss, while eyebrows and eyelashes shield the eyes. Beyond biology, hairstyles and hair removal carry immense cultural, religious, and social meaning, and hair analysis is used in forensics and toxicology because the shaft records drug exposure and isotopic signatures over months.
