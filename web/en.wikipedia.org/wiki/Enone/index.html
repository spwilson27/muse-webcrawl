Enone

In organic chemistry, an enone is an unsaturated carbonyl compound consisting of an alkene conjugated with a ketone. The simplest enone is methyl vinyl ketone (butenone, CH2=CHCOCH3). Enones in which the carbonyl is an aldehyde are called enals; acrolein is the simplest example. The conjugated C=C–C=O unit gives enones reactivity distinct from either functional group alone.

Reactivity

Because the π system is conjugated, nucleophiles can attack either the carbonyl carbon (1,2-addition) or the β-carbon (1,4- or conjugate addition). Conjugate addition — typified by the Michael reaction — is a cornerstone of carbon–carbon bond formation, and its asymmetric variants (organocatalytic and metal-catalysed) are widely used in synthesis. Enones also serve as dienophiles and heterodienophiles in cycloadditions and as substrates in the Nazarov cyclisation, which converts divinyl ketones into cyclopentenones.

Synthesis

Common preparations include aldol condensation of ketones and aldehydes, oxidation of allylic alcohols, selenoxide elimination from α-seleno carbonyl compounds, and the Meyer–Schuster rearrangement of propargyl alcohols. The Robinson annulation builds a fused enone ring system by Michael addition followed by intramolecular aldol condensation and is a classic route to steroid frameworks.

Occurrence and uses

Many natural products and pharmaceuticals contain enone units — carvone and cyclopentenone prostaglandins are examples — and the enone motif acts as a "warhead" in some covalent enzyme inhibitors. Industrially, simple enones are intermediates and monomers, though compounds such as methyl vinyl ketone are lachrymatory and toxic and must be handled with care.
