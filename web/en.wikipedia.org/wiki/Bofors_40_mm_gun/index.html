The Bofors 40 mm gun is a Swedish anti-aircraft autocannon designed in the 1930s by AB Bofors. One of the most widely used medium anti-aircraft weapons of the twentieth century, it armed ships and ground forces of dozens of countries during and after the Second World War and, in its modernized L/70 form, remains in production and service today.

Development

In 1928 the Swedish Navy asked Bofors for a 40 mm anti-aircraft gun to replace its Vickers pom-poms. Engineers including Victor Falkman and others developed a practical design tested in 1933–34; the resulting 40 mm L/60 (60 calibers long) was offered in land and naval versions. Poland, Hungary, and other nations bought licenses before the war, and both the Axis and the Allies ended up using the gun.

Second World War

The United States adopted the Bofors in 1941 and mass-produced it (with Chrysler among the makers) as the standard medium naval anti-aircraft weapon, mounting it in twin and quad mounts across the fleet; it is credited with a large share of U.S. Navy aircraft kills in the Pacific. Britain manufactured it under license for army and naval use. Its combination of shell weight, rate of fire, and reliability made it the benchmark against which other medium AA guns were judged.

Postwar and L/70

After the war Bofors developed the faster-firing L/70, adopted by many NATO navies and armies and fitted to vehicles such as Sweden's CV9040 infantry fighting vehicle and the famous Breda twin naval mounts of Italy. Modern versions fire programmable airburst ammunition (3P) effective against aircraft, missiles, and small boats.
