Pannonia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pannonia was a province of the Roman Empire bounded on the north and east by the Danube, coterminous roughly with modern-day western Hungary, eastern Austria, northern Croatia, north-western Serbia, south-western Slovakia, and northern Slovenia and Bosnia and Herzegovina. Roman authors sometimes used the name more loosely for the whole Danubian plain between the Alps and the river.

History

The region was originally inhabited by Illyrian and Pannonian tribes, among them the Breuci, Andizetes and Amantini, with Celtic peoples such as the Scordisci settling in the late Iron Age. Roman expansion into the middle Danube began under Augustus; the great Pannonian revolt (the Bellum Batonianum) of AD 6–9 delayed full annexation, but by the reign of Tiberius the territory had been organised as a province.

Under Trajan, around AD 103–107, Pannonia was divided into Pannonia Superior in the west, with its capital at Carnuntum, and Pannonia Inferior in the east, centred on Aquincum (modern Budapest). A further subdivision under Diocletian produced the provinces of Pannonia Prima, Pannonia Valeria, Pannonia Savia and Pannonia Secunda. Legions stationed on the frontier included Legio X Gemina at Vindobona (Vienna) and Legio II Adiutrix at Aquincum.

Pannonia was the birthplace of several Roman emperors, including Decius, Aurelian, Probus, Valentinian I and Valentinian II. During the Migration Period the province passed successively to Huns, Ostrogoths, Lombards and Avars, and in the 9th–10th centuries it became part of the Hungarian principality after the Magyar conquest of the Carpathian Basin.

Geography and economy

The province lay largely on the fertile Pannonian Plain, drained by the Danube and its tributaries the Drava and Sava. It was a major source of grain, timber and recruits for the Roman army. Important towns included Carnuntum, Vindobona, Aquincum, Brigetio, Savaria, Sopianae, Mursa and Sirmium. The Amber Road from the Baltic crossed the province to Italy.
