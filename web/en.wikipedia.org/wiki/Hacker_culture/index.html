Hacker culture

The hacker culture is a subculture of individuals who enjoy the intellectual challenge of creatively overcoming the limitations of software systems and hardware to achieve novel and clever outcomes. The culture originated at American universities in the late 1950s and 1960s, particularly around the Tech Model Railroad Club and the artificial intelligence laboratory at MIT, and later flourished through the home computer revolution, the free software movement, and the internet.

Origins

At MIT, "hack" originally meant an elaborate student prank, and a "hacker" was someone who applied ingenuity to technical work. Programmers working on early mainframes and minicomputers such as the PDP-1 developed an informal, playful and anti-authoritarian ethos, documented by Steven Levy in his 1984 book Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution. The culture overlapped with phone phreaking and early bulletin board systems.

The hacker ethic

Levy summarised the hacker ethic in principles including: access to computers should be unlimited and total; all information should be free; mistrust authority and promote decentralisation; hackers should be judged by their hacking, not by degrees or position; and computers can change life for the better. These values underpin the hacker ethic and influenced the development of open, decentralised networks.

Free and open-source software

Richard Stallman's launch of the GNU Project in 1983 and the founding of the Free Software Foundation institutionalised hacker values in software licensing. Eric S. Raymond's essay The Cathedral and the Bazaar (1997) contrasted hacker-style decentralised development with top-down models. The Jargon File, maintained since the 1970s, documents hacker slang and folklore.

Other branches

Closely related communities include the security hacker scene (with "white hat", "black hat" and "grey hat" distinctions in popular usage), hardware hackers and the maker movement, demo scene artists, and participants in events such as DEF CON and the Chaos Communication Congress. In media usage, "hacker" often denotes a computer criminal, a meaning many in the culture reject in favour of "cracker".
