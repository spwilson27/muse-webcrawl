Elliptic-curve cryptography

Elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC) is an approach to public-key cryptography based on the algebraic structure of elliptic curves over finite fields. ECC allows smaller keys compared to non-EC cryptography (based on plain Galois fields) to provide equivalent security — a 256-bit elliptic-curve key offers security comparable to a 3072-bit RSA key. Elliptic curves are applicable for key agreement (ECDH), digital signatures (ECDSA, EdDSA), and encryption schemes.

History

The use of elliptic curves in cryptography was suggested independently by Neal Koblitz and Victor Miller in 1985. ECC schemes entered wide use in the mid-2000s as patents expired and standards matured (TLS, Bitcoin uses the secp256k1 curve). In 2013, revelations about the Dual EC DRBG random number generator — suspected of containing an NSA backdoor — damaged trust in some standardized parameters and spurred adoption of "nothing-up-my-sleeve" curves and newer designs such as Curve25519 (Daniel J. Bernstein).

Mathematical basis

An elliptic curve over a finite field consists of points satisfying an equation such as y² = x³ + ax + b (with non-zero discriminant), together with a point at infinity that acts as the group identity. Points form an abelian group under the chord-and-tangent rule. The security of ECC rests on the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem (ECDLP): given points P and Q = dP, finding the scalar d is computationally infeasible for well-chosen curves; the best generic attacks (Pollard's rho) run in time proportional to the square root of the group order, which is why ECC key sizes grow only linearly with security level in bits.

Schemes and standards

Common ECC schemes include ECDH (key agreement), ECDSA and EdDSA (signatures), and ECIES (encryption). Widely used curves include the NIST curves P-256 and P-384, Curve25519/Ed25519, secp256k1 (Bitcoin, Ethereum), and Brainpool curves. Implementations must guard against invalid-curve attacks, small-subgroup attacks, timing side channels, and twist insecurity.

Quantum vulnerability

Like RSA and Diffie–Hellman, ECC is broken in principle by Shor's algorithm on a sufficiently large quantum computer — indeed, ECDLP requires fewer qubits to attack than comparable RSA. Migration plans toward post-quantum cryptography therefore include ECC systems, and hybrid key exchange combining ECDH with lattice-based KEMs has been deployed in TLS.
