Rhyme royal

Rhyme royal is a rhyming stanza form consisting of seven lines, usually in iambic pentameter, with the rhyme scheme ABABBCC. It became a prestigious form for long narrative and reflective poems in late medieval and early modern English literature.

Form

The stanza combines an opening alternating quatrain-like movement with a sequence of couplets, although its seven-line design creates a more spacious and ceremonious pace than a simple series of couplets. The closing couplet often supplies a summary, turn or emotional emphasis.

The pattern moves from line to answering line,
With first and third in one clear rhyme aligned;
The second sound returns, resolved in time,
Then pairs again, as if by art designed;
Two closing lines complete the form’s design.

The example above illustrates rhyme placement in shortened form rather than the full seven-line pentameter stanza.

History

Geoffrey Chaucer popularized rhyme royal in English, using it in works including Troilus and Criseyde, The Parliament of Fowls and several Canterbury Tales. The name may reflect use by royalty—King James I of Scotland used the form in The Kingis Quair—or simply its elevated status.

Later use

William Shakespeare used rhyme royal in The Rape of Lucrece. Later poets adopted it for narratives, elegies and sonnet sequences. John Milton, William Morris, Emma Lazarus and W. H. Auden among others used the stanza, whose formal architecture suits stately narration and meditative argument.

Relation to other forms

Rhyme royal is closely related to the French ballade stanza and to ottava rima, which has eight lines rhyming ABABABCC. It should not be confused with the rhyme scheme of a Spenserian stanza, which has nine lines and a final alexandrine.
