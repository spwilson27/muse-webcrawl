Shire Jama Ahmed (Somali: Shire Jaamac Axmed, 1936–1999) was a Somali linguist who is credited with inventing the Latin-based alphabet used for writing the Somali language. His script was adopted as the official orthography of Somalia in 1972, ending decades of debate over how Somali should be written.

Life and work

Shire Jama Ahmed studied in Somalia and in Egypt, where he trained in Arabic and linguistics. In the 1960s, as a member of the Somali Language Commission established by the Somali government, he designed a Latin alphabet adapted to the phonology of Somali, representing its long vowels by doubling and its distinctive consonants with digraphs such as dh, kh, and c and x for pharyngeal sounds. His proposal competed with other scripts, including the indigenous Osmanya and Gadabuursi scripts and Arabic.

In January 1972 the Supreme Revolutionary Council under Siad Barre adopted Shire Jama's Latin script as the national orthography and launched mass literacy campaigns — including the 1974–75 rural literacy campaign, in which students and teachers were sent into the countryside — that made Somali one of the most successfully standardised written languages in Africa. Shire Jama also wrote educational and literary works in Somali and worked on Somali grammar and terminology.
