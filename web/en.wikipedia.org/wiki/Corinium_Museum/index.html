Corinium Museum

Location: Park Street, Cirencester, Gloucestershire, England

Established: 1974 (origins 1857)

Collection: Archaeology of the Cotswolds, esp. Roman Corinium

Operator: Cotswold District Council / Freedom Leisure

The Corinium Museum, in Cirencester, Gloucestershire, England, holds one of the finest collections of Romano-British antiquities in Britain, derived principally from excavations in and around the town — Roman Corinium Dobunnorum, the second largest city in Roman Britain by area and the probable capital of the province of Britannia Prima. The museum is named after the Roman town.

Collections

The museum is particularly noted for its mosaic pavements, including the celebrated Hunting Dogs and Four Seasons mosaics from Dyer Street and the Hare mosaic, discovered in 1971 and considered among the finest Romano-British mosaics. Galleries display tombstones and sculpture — such as the Jupiter column capital and the Matres (mother goddesses) — inscriptions, coins, pottery, glass, jewellery, and the contents of graves from the Roman cemeteries and from the earlier prehistoric burial mounds of the Cotswolds. Anglo-Saxon material from the large cemetery at Lechlade, including the richly furnished Butler's Field burials, is another strength. The museum also interprets medieval Cirencester's wool trade and the town's Civil War history.

History

The collection descends from a museum founded in 1857 by local antiquaries, including the Earl Bathurst, after notable Roman discoveries during building work in the town. It was displayed successively in Tetbury Road and in a building on Sheep Street before moving to its present Park Street home, a converted Georgian wool merchant's house with a modern extension, in 1974. A major redevelopment, the Corinium Project, completed in 2020–21 with National Lottery Heritage Fund support, created new prehistory galleries covering the Neolithic to the Iron Age and a new entrance and discovery zone.

Corinium

Founded as a fort in the AD 50s on Fosse Way at the crossing of the River Churn, Corinium grew into a chartered town of some 240 acres within walls, with a forum, basilica, amphitheatre (whose earthworks survive as the "Bull Ring"), and lavish town houses whose mosaics were the products of a local school of mosaicists, the so-called Corinium School.
