Flint Castle

LocationFlint, Flintshire, Wales
Built1277–1284
BuilderEdward I of England
StatusRuined; managed by Cadw

Flint Castle (Welsh: Castell y Fflint) is a medieval castle in Flint, Flintshire, Wales, overlooking the estuary of the River Dee. It was the first of the great chain of castles built by Edward I during his conquest of Wales, begun in 1277 at the start of the First Welsh War, and its construction launched the programme that produced Conwy, Caernarfon and Harlech.

History

Work began in July 1277 under the master mason Richard l'Engenour and later James of St George. The plan was innovative: a rectangular inner ward with round corner towers and, detached from the south-east corner across the moat, a great round donjon — unique among Edward's Welsh castles — reachable by drawbridge. A planned town (borough) was laid out beside it and settled with English colonists. In 1282 the unfinished castle was attacked by Dafydd ap Gruffudd at the opening of the final Welsh war. Richard II was held at Flint by Henry Bolingbroke in 1399 on his way to imprisonment, a scene dramatised in Shakespeare's Richard II. During the English Civil War the castle changed hands repeatedly and was slighted (deliberately ruined) in 1647 on Parliament's orders.

Today

The surviving ruins — the great tower, curtain walls and gatehouse fragments — are a Grade I listed building and scheduled monument in the care of Cadw, freely open to the public. Flint Castle forms part of the serial World Heritage landscape of Edwardian castles in North Wales by association, though it is not itself among the four inscribed castles.
