Sea control

Sea control (also command of the sea) is a naval warfare concept describing the condition in which a belligerent has the ability to use a sea area for its own purposes — military operations and maritime trade — for a period of time, while denying that use to the enemy. It is rarely absolute or permanent; modern doctrine treats sea control as local, temporary and relative to the mission.

Theory

Theorists such as Alfred Thayer Mahan emphasised command of the sea as the key to national power, won by decisive battle between battle fleets. Julian Corbett argued instead that command is normally disputed and that the practical object is control of maritime communications. In practice, sea control is exercised through a combination of surface fleets, submarines, naval aviation, mine warfare and land-based air and missile forces, and it underpins power projection ashore, amphibious operations and protection of sea lines of communication.

Sea denial

The corollary concept is sea denial: preventing an enemy from using an area without necessarily being able to use it oneself. Weaker navies, and increasingly land-based forces with anti-ship missiles, submarines and mines, may pursue sea denial — an approach associated today with anti-access/area denial (A2/AD) strategies.
