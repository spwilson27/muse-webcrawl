Sandmeyer reaction

Named afterTraugott Sandmeyer
Reaction typeSubstitution reaction
Discovered1884

The Sandmeyer reaction is a chemical reaction used to synthesize aryl halides from aryl diazonium salts using copper salts as reagents or catalysts. It is an example of a radical-nucleophilic aromatic substitution. The reaction was discovered in 1884 by Swiss chemist Traugott Sandmeyer, who accidentally isolated phenylacetylene while attempting another synthesis; pursuing the anomaly led him to the reaction that bears his name.

The Sandmeyer reaction: an aryl diazonium salt reacts with a copper(I) halide to give the aryl halide

Description

An aniline (aryl amine) is first converted to a diazonium salt by treatment with nitrous acid (diazotization), typically at low temperature. Reaction of the diazonium salt with copper(I) chloride, bromide, or cyanide then replaces the diazo group with Cl, Br, or CN respectively, with loss of nitrogen gas. The transformation provides a route to aryl halides and benzonitriles that cannot easily be made by direct electrophilic aromatic substitution.

Mechanism

The reaction is believed to proceed by a single-electron transfer from copper(I) to the diazonium ion, generating an aryl radical and nitrogen. The radical then abstracts a halogen atom (or cyanide) from a copper(II) species, forming the product and regenerating copper(I). Related transformations include the Gattermann reaction, which uses copper powder, and the Schiemann reaction for aryl fluorides.
