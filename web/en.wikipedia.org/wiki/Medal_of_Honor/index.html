The Medal of Honor is the United States' highest military decoration, presented by the president in the name of Congress to members of the armed forces who distinguish themselves by conspicuous gallantry and intrepidity at the risk of their lives above and beyond the call of duty. Because it is often presented posthumously and by the president, it is sometimes called the Congressional Medal of Honor, though its official name is simply the Medal of Honor.

History

The Navy version was authorised in December 1861 and the Army version in July 1862, during the American Civil War. It remained the only American military award for bravery for decades; hundreds of Civil War awards were later reviewed, and a 1916–17 board struck 911 names. The Air Force adopted its own design in 1965. Separate designs exist for the Army, the naval services (Navy, Marine Corps, and Coast Guard), and the Air Force and Space Force.

Criteria and privileges

The standard requires conclusive proof of performance "above and beyond the call of duty" while engaged in action against an enemy. Recipients receive a monthly pension supplement, special burial honours at Arlington National Cemetery, an invitation to presidential inaugurations, and other courtesies; their children may seek appointments to the service academies without nomination quotas. Wearing or selling a medal falsely claimed is restricted by law.

Notable recipients

Nineteen servicemen have received the medal twice. Recipients range from Civil War drummer Willie Johnston, the youngest ever, to Audie Murphy, the most decorated American soldier of the Second World War, and Mary Edwards Walker, the only woman recipient, a Civil War surgeon.
