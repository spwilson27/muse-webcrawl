Square rig

A square-rigged, full-rigged ship with square sails on all three masts.

Square rig is a generic type of sail and rigging arrangement in which a sailing vessel's primary driving sails are carried on horizontal spars called yards, set at right angles ("square") to the keel of the vessel, behind or on the masts. The sails hang from the yards and are roughly square in shape, though they are actually trapezoidal when set. Vessels with square rig are called square-riggers.

Description

A square-rigged mast typically carries, from bottom to top, a course (the lowest and largest square sail), topsail, topgallant sail, and royal sail, with still smaller skysails on some large vessels. Square sails are efficient when sailing with the wind behind the beam, but a square-rigger cannot sail as close to the wind as a fore-and-aft rigged vessel, and must tack through a wide angle or wear ship instead. Most large square-riggers also carry fore-and-aft sails — staysails between the masts and a spanker on the aftermost mast — to aid manoeuvring.

Vessel types

Rig classification depends on the number of masts and the arrangement of sails. A full-rigged ship has three or more masts, all square-rigged. A barque has three or more masts with only the mizzen fore-and-aft rigged. A brig has two square-rigged masts, and a barquentine is square-rigged only on the foremast. Square rig dominated ocean-going commerce and naval warfare from the sixteenth to the late nineteenth century, before steam and fore-and-aft rigs displaced it.

Handling

Setting and furling square sails required large crews working aloft on the yards. Sails were shortened by reefing or by clewing them up to the yard. Many square-riggers survive today as sail-training ships, keeping the skills of the rig alive.
