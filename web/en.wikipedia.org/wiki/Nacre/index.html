Nacre

Nacre, also known as mother-of-pearl, is an organic–inorganic composite material produced by certain molluscs as the inner lining of their shells. It is the material of which pearls are composed. Prized for its iridescence, strength and resilience, nacre has been used for ornament and inlay for thousands of years and is intensively studied by materials scientists.

Structure

Nacre consists of microscopic hexagonal platelets of aragonite, a form of calcium carbonate, each about 0.5 micrometres thick, stacked in sheets and glued by a thin organic matrix of proteins and chitin — a "brick-and-mortar" architecture. The regular spacing of the platelets is comparable to the wavelength of visible light, so interference between reflections from successive layers produces the shifting colours called nacreous lustre. Cracks are deflected along the organic layers, making nacre roughly 3,000 times tougher than the aragonite crystals alone.

Formation

The mantle tissue of bivalves, gastropods such as abalone, and cephalopods such as the nautilus secretes nacre continuously to line the shell interior. A pearl forms when an irritant becomes coated in successive nacre layers. Cultured-pearl production exploits the same process by implanting a nucleus in pearl oysters.

Uses

Mother-of-pearl has been worked into jewellery, buttons, cutlery handles, watch dials and decorative inlay on furniture and musical instruments; guitar fret markers and accordion panels remain common uses. Crushed nacre appears in cosmetics and traditional medicines, and its growth mechanisms inspire synthetic composites and bone-graft materials.
