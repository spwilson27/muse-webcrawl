Picardy (French: Picardie) is a historical territory and former administrative region of northern France, on the English Channel coast north of Paris. Since 1 January 2016 its territory — the departments of Aisne, Oise and Somme — has formed part of the region of Hauts-de-France. Its prefecture and largest city was Amiens, home to one of the largest Gothic cathedrals in France.


Picardy in northern France, bordering the English Channel and Belgium.

History

The name Picardie first appears in the 13th century, possibly derived from picards, the pike-armed soldiers the region supplied. In the Middle Ages, Picardy was a patchwork of counties contested between the French crown and the Dukes of Burgundy; the Treaty of Arras (1435) ceded much of it to Burgundy, and it returned definitively to France in 1477 after the death of Charles the Bold. The region's textile towns — Amiens, Saint-Quentin, Beauvais — prospered on cloth and woad.

Picardy was a major battlefield of the First World War: the Battle of the Somme (1916) was fought across its chalk downland, and the German Spring Offensive and the Battle of Amiens (1918) also took place here. Numerous war cemeteries and memorials, including the Thiepval Memorial, commemorate the fighting.

Geography and economy

The region is largely a low chalk plateau cut by the valleys of the Somme, Aisne and Oise, with the Baie de Somme wetlands on the coast. Agriculture — cereals, sugar beet and potatoes — is important, and the traditional textile industry has declined. Picard (a langue d'oïl variety) is the historic regional language, still spoken by a minority.

Culture

Picardy's landmarks include the cathedrals of Amiens, Beauvais, Laon and Noyon; the Château de Chantilly in the Oise; floating market gardens (hortillonnages) in Amiens; and the bay of the Somme, noted for birdwatching and seals. Jules Verne lived and is buried in Amiens.
