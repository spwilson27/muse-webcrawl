Prose poetry

Prose poetry is poetry written in prose form instead of verse — that is, without line breaks — while retaining poetic qualities such as heightened imagery, figurative language, rhythm and emotional effect. The prose poem is a hybrid form that deliberately blurs the boundary between poetry and prose, and its status has been debated by critics since its emergence in 19th-century France.

Origins

Although earlier writers had experimented with lyrical prose, the prose poem as a conscious form is usually traced to Aloysius Bertrand's Gaspard de la nuit (published 1842). Charles Baudelaire, influenced by Bertrand, wrote the fifty short prose poems of Le Spleen de Paris (also called Petits poèmes en prose, published posthumously in 1869), explaining that he dreamed of "a poetic prose, musical without rhythm and without rhyme, supple and staccato enough to adapt to the lyrical movements of the soul." Arthur Rimbaud's Illuminations extended the form, and it became a hallmark of French symbolism.

Development

In the 20th century the prose poem was taken up by modernists and surrealists in French and Spanish (including Jorge Luis Borges) and flourished in the United States with poets such as Gertrude Stein, William Carlos Williams, Russell Edson, Robert Bly, Charles Simic and Mary Oliver. Contemporary prose poetry overlaps with flash fiction and the lyric essay, and dedicated journals and anthologies now treat the prose poem as an established genre.

Characteristics

Prose poems typically present themselves as a block of text — a paragraph or several — relying for their poetic effect on image, compression, repetition, cadence and surprising juxtapositions rather than metre or lineation. Because they look like prose, they often exploit the expectations of narrative and then subvert them, a tension many practitioners cite as the form's essential pleasure.
