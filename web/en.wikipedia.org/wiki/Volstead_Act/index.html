The National Prohibition Act, known informally as the Volstead Act, was an act of the United States Congress that provided federal enforcement for the Eighteenth Amendment, which prohibited the manufacture, sale and transportation of alcoholic beverages. It was named for Andrew Volstead of Minnesota, chairman of the House Judiciary Committee, who managed the legislation.

Provisions

The act defined "intoxicating liquor" as any beverage containing more than 0.5% alcohol by volume — a definition that banned beer and wine as well as spirits, surprising many who had expected lighter drinks to remain legal. It provided exceptions for medicinal, sacramental and industrial alcohol, and for fruit and cider made at home. Enforcement was assigned to the Treasury Department's Bureau of Prohibition.

Enactment and repeal

President Woodrow Wilson vetoed the bill on constitutional and practical grounds, but Congress overrode the veto the same day, 28 October 1919. Prohibition took effect on 17 January 1920. Widespread evasion, speakeasies and organised crime marked the era; the act was effectively repealed when the Twenty-first Amendment ratified on 5 December 1933 ended national Prohibition, and the Cullen–Harrison Act had already re-legalised beer earlier that year.
