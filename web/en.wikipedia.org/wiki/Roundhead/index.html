Roundhead

Roundheads was the nickname given to the supporters of the Parliament of England during the English Civil War (1642–1651), in contrast to the Royalist "Cavaliers" who fought for King Charles I. The name referred to the short, cropped hairstyles favoured by many Puritan Parliamentarians, in contrast to the long, curled hair fashionable at the royal court.

Origin of the term

The word was in use by late 1641, when crowds of London apprentices — many wearing their hair cut short — demonstrated in favour of Parliament. According to a well-known anecdote, Queen Henrietta Maria, seeing the demonstrators, exclaimed at their "round heads." Initially a term of abuse used by Royalists, it was proudly adopted by Parliament's supporters, just as "Cavalier" (from the Spanish caballero) began as a Parliamentarian insult before being embraced by the King's party.

The Parliamentarian cause

Parliament's supporters included much of the gentry and merchant class, the commercially developed south and east of England, and most Puritans, who sought to reform the Church of England and limit royal power. Under leaders such as John Pym in the Commons and generals including the Earl of Essex, Sir Thomas Fairfax, and Oliver Cromwell, Parliament reorganised its forces into the disciplined New Model Army in 1645, which won the decisive victory at Naseby. After the war, radical Roundheads in the Army purged Parliament and brought about the trial and execution of Charles I in 1649 and the establishment of the Commonwealth.

Usage

"Roundhead" remained a label for Parliamentarian and later Whig or dissenting sympathies in British political writing for centuries after the war, and the Roundhead/Cavalier pairing is still used metaphorically in British culture to contrast puritan plainness with flamboyant traditionalism.
