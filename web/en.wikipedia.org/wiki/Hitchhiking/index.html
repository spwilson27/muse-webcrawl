Hitchhiking

Hitchhiking (also known as thumbing, autostop or hitching) is a means of transportation that is gained by asking individuals, usually strangers, for a ride in their car or other vehicle. A ride is usually, but not always, free. Hitchhikers conventionally signal with an outstretched thumb.

History

Hitchhiking became common in the 1920s and 1930s with the spread of automobiles, and was a routine mode of travel during the Great Depression and the Second World War, when military personnel hitched rides while on leave. It flourished in the counterculture of the 1960s and 1970s, symbolised in works such as Jack Kerouac's On the Road, but declined in later decades amid safety fears, legal restrictions and rising car ownership among young people.

Legality

The legality of hitchhiking varies worldwide. In the United States it is generally legal to solicit rides from off the roadway, though federal regulations prohibit it near prisons and on National Park Service land with restrictions, and some states and municipalities restrict standing on ramps or roadways. In several European countries hitchhiking is tolerated but forbidden on motorways; in some countries, such as Lithuania, it has been semi-institutionalised with designated hitchhiking points.

Safety and culture

Safety concerns for both hitchhikers and drivers are the most common reason given for the practice's decline, though studies suggest the risks, while real, are difficult to quantify. Hitchhiking has a substantial culture of guidebooks, slang and online communities such as Hitchwiki, and organised variants include ridesharing and slugging. Douglas Adams humorously canonised the hitchhiker in The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy (1978).
