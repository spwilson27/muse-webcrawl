Transcatheter aortic valve replacement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), also called transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), is a minimally invasive procedure in which a diseased aortic valve is replaced with a prosthetic valve delivered through a catheter, usually inserted into the femoral artery in the groin. It is used to treat aortic stenosis, a narrowing of the aortic valve that obstructs blood flow out of the heart, and offers an alternative to open-heart surgical valve replacement (SAVR), particularly for patients at high surgical risk.

Procedure

The prosthetic valve — a tissue valve (typically bovine or porcine pericardium) mounted in an expandable metal stent — is crimped onto a catheter and advanced through the arterial system to the heart under imaging guidance. It is then deployed within the native, diseased valve, either by inflating a balloon (balloon-expandable valves) or by unsheathing a self-expanding frame. The new leaflets take over the valve's function immediately, pushing the old leaflets aside. Most procedures are performed via the transfemoral route under local anaesthesia or conscious sedation; alternative access routes (transapical, transaortic, subclavian) are used when femoral access is unsuitable.

History and evidence

The first human implantation was performed by Alain Cribier in Rouen, France, in 2002, on a patient too ill for surgery. Large randomised trials — the PARTNER trials (with the balloon-expandable Sapien valve) and the CoreValve/Evolut trials (with a self-expanding valve) — progressively demonstrated that TAVR was superior to medical therapy in inoperable patients, non-inferior or superior to surgery in high- and intermediate-risk patients, and, in later trials of low-risk patients, at least non-inferior to surgery. Regulatory approvals in Europe (2007) and the United States (2011 onwards) were correspondingly extended from inoperable and high-risk patients to lower-risk groups.

Risks and limitations

Complications can include stroke, vascular injury at the access site, paravalvular leak (leakage around the prosthesis), new heart block requiring a permanent pacemaker (more frequent with self-expanding valves), and, rarely, coronary obstruction or annular rupture. Questions remain about very long-term valve durability, which matters as TAVR is offered to younger, lower-risk patients with long life expectancy, and surgical replacement remains preferred for some anatomies and for patients with bicuspid valves or concurrent disease needing surgery.
