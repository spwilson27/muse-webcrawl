Harold Clayton Urey (April 29, 1893 – January 5, 1981) was an American physical chemist whose pioneering work on isotopes earned him the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1934 for the discovery of deuterium. He is also known for his theories on the development of the Earth and the origin of life, and for supervising the Miller–Urey experiment.

Early life and deuterium

Urey was born in Walkerton, Indiana, worked as a schoolteacher and a chemist in a munitions plant during the First World War, and earned his PhD at Berkeley under Gilbert N. Lewis in 1923, followed by a year with Niels Bohr in Copenhagen. At Columbia University, he discovered deuterium (heavy hydrogen) in 1931 by spectroscopically detecting its faint spectral lines in evaporated liquid hydrogen — work honored with the 1934 Nobel Prize.

Wartime and postwar work

During the Manhattan Project, Urey directed the SAM Laboratories at Columbia, developing gaseous diffusion methods for enriching uranium-235 — part of the same isotope-separation enterprise that fed heavy-water and reactor programs such as those later embodied in the CANDU reactor design. After the war he became a prominent advocate of arms control and wrote The Planets: Their Origin and Development (1952), a founding text of modern planetary science.

Origin of life

In 1952, Urey's graduate student Stanley Miller performed the Miller–Urey experiment, in which electric sparks passed through a mixture of water, methane, ammonia and hydrogen produced amino acids — the first experimental demonstration that the building blocks of life could form under plausible early-Earth conditions, and a founding moment for prebiotic chemistry.
