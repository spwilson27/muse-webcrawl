Raoul Gustaf Wallenberg (4 August 1912 – disappeared 17 January 1945) was a Swedish architect, businessman, diplomat, and humanitarian. Working for Sweden's special envoy in Budapest in 1944, he issued protective passports and sheltered tens of thousands of Jews in buildings designated as Swedish territory, saving many from deportation and massacre during the Holocaust.

Early life

Wallenberg was born into the prominent Wallenberg banking family. He studied architecture at the University of Michigan and worked in business in South Africa, Haifa, and Stockholm before being recruited for the rescue mission.

Rescue mission in Budapest

Financed partly by the American War Refugee Board, Wallenberg arrived in Budapest in July 1944. He designed the Schutz-Pass, an official-looking protective passport that, while legally dubious, was widely accepted by Hungarian and German authorities, and established 32 "Swedish houses" sheltering more than 10,000 people. Estimates of the number he saved range from tens of thousands up to 100,000.

Arrest and fate

After the Red Army took Budapest, Wallenberg was detained by SMERSH on suspicion of espionage on 17 January 1945 and taken to Moscow. The Soviet Union later stated he died on 17 July 1947 in Lubyanka Prison, but his exact fate remained one of the Cold War's enduring mysteries; Sweden formally declared him dead in 2016.

Legacy

Wallenberg was recognised by Yad Vashem as one of the Righteous Among the Nations, made an honorary citizen of the United States (only the second person so honoured, after Winston Churchill), Canada, Hungary, Australia, and Israel, and commemorated by monuments and streets worldwide.
