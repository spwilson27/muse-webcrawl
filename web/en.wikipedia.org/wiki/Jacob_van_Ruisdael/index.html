Jacob Isaackszoon van Ruisdael (c. 1628 – 10 March 1682) is generally considered the pre-eminent landscape painter of the Dutch Golden Age. Prolific and remarkably versatile, he painted dunes, woodland scenes, waterfalls, winter scenes, seascapes, city panoramas and "Haerlempjes" — views of Haarlem with its bleaching fields. His dramatic cloud-filled skies, often occupying two-thirds of the canvas, and his melancholic, monumental treatment of nature influenced landscape painting through the Romantic era and beyond.

Life

Ruisdael was born in Haarlem, the son of the frame-maker and occasional painter Isaack van Ruisdael and nephew of the landscapist Salomon van Ruysdael. He joined the Haarlem Guild of St. Luke in 1648; his earliest dated works, from 1646, already show a mature technique. Around 1650 he travelled with his friend Nicolaes Berchem to the German border region near Bentheim, whose castle and hilly terrain furnished motifs for some of his most famous paintings, and he later painted Scandinavian-style waterfalls inspired by Allaert van Everdingen. By 1657 he had settled in Amsterdam, where he obtained citizenship; he also appears in records as having trained in medicine and practised as a surgeon, though the extent of that career is debated. He taught the great landscapist Meindert Hobbema. He died, apparently in reduced circumstances, in 1682 and was buried in St Bavo's Church, Haarlem.

Work and style

About 700 paintings are attributed to Ruisdael, alongside drawings and a small group of etchings. His mature style is marked by low horizons, turbulent skies built up with subtle greys and whites, intricate rendering of trees and water, and an often sombre, reflective mood distinct from the brighter tonal landscapes of his contemporaries. Major works include The Jewish Cemetery (c. 1655), The Windmill at Wijk bij Duurstede (c. 1670) and the View of Haarlem in the Mauritshuis. His work fell from the highest prices after his death but was championed in the 19th century; John Constable called him one of the great masters, and the German Romantics and the Barbizon and Hudson River schools absorbed his example.
