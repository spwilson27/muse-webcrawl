Theatre of the Absurd

The Theatre of the Absurd is a post–World War II designation for plays by European and American dramatists — notably Samuel Beckett, Eugène Ionesco, Jean Genet, Harold Pinter and Edward Albee — that share an absurdist outlook on human existence, expressed through illogical, repetitive dialogue and plot.

Origins

Critic Martin Esslin coined the term in his 1961 book The Theatre of the Absurd, borrowing "absurd" from Albert Camus's essay The Myth of Sisyphus (1942): the conflict between humanity's search for meaning and a silent, indifferent universe. After the horrors of the Second World War, these playwrights abandoned rational plots and psychological realism for circular, vaudeville-inflected structures in which language itself breaks down.

Key works

  • Waiting for Godot (1953) and Endgame (1957) — Samuel Beckett
  • The Bald Soprano (1950) and Rhinoceros (1959) — Eugène Ionesco
  • The Balcony (1956) and The Maids (1947) — Jean Genet
  • The Birthday Party (1958) and The Caretaker (1960) — Harold Pinter
  • Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf? (1962) — Edward Albee

Characteristics and legacy

Common features include meaningless or non-sequitur dialogue, dreamlike and cyclical plots, characters trapped in hopeless situations, and comedy fused with horror. The movement permanently reshaped modern drama, feeding into the work of Tom Stoppard, Václav Havel, Caryl Churchill and much contemporary writing for stage and screen.
