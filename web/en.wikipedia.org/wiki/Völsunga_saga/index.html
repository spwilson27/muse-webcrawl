Völsunga saga

The Völsunga saga (Old Norse: Vǫlsunga saga) is a legendary saga from Iceland, written in prose in the late 13th century, probably around 1270. It tells the story of the rise and fall of the Völsung clan, drawing on older heroic poems — several preserved in the Poetic Edda — and on traditions also reflected in the German Nibelungenlied.

Contents

The saga follows the lineage from Sigi, said to be a son of Odin, through Völsung and his children — including the incest-born twins Sigmund and Signý — to the saga's central hero, Sigurd (Old Norse Sigurðr), slayer of the dragon Fáfnir. Sigurd tastes the dragon's blood, gains understanding of birds' speech, wins the cursed treasure of the dwarf Andvari, and later becomes entangled with the family of the Burgundian king Gunnar and the valkyrie Brynhildr. The tale ends in betrayal and murder: Sigurd is killed, Brynhildr takes her own life, and Gudrun's successive marriages carry the story on to the fall of the Burgundians at the court of Atli (Attila) and the death of Hamdir and Sorli.

Sources and influence

The anonymous author worked from lost and surviving poems, some now found only in the saga's prose. A fragmentary vellum manuscript (NKS 1824 b 4to) in the Royal Library, Copenhagen, is the main surviving copy. The saga strongly influenced later literature and art: William Morris translated it (with Eiríkr Magnússon) in 1870 as The Story of the Volsungs and Niblungs, and its material fed into Richard Wagner's Der Ring des Nibelungen and, through Morris, into J. R. R. Tolkien's The Legend of Sigurd and Gudrún.
