Tea Act

Enacted byParliament of Great Britain
Royal assent10 May 1773
Citation13 Geo. 3. c. 44
Repealed1861 (Statute Law Revision Act)

The Tea Act 1773 (13 Geo. 3. c. 44) was an Act of the Parliament of Great Britain. The principal objective was to reduce the massive amount of tea held by the financially troubled British East India Company in its London warehouses and to help the struggling company survive. A related objective was to undercut the price of illegal tea, smuggled into Britain's North American colonies.

The Act granted the company the right to directly ship its tea to North America and to export it duty-free from Britain, allowing it to sell tea more cheaply than colonial merchants and smugglers. However, the existing tax on tea imposed by the Townshend Acts remained in force. Colonists who opposed the tax on constitutional grounds — "no taxation without representation" — saw the Act as a device to make them accept Parliament's right to tax them, and organised resistance to the tea shipments.

Background

By 1773 the East India Company held some 17 million pounds of unsold tea and faced bankruptcy, which threatened the British state itself, since the company governed much of India and its credit underpinned the financial system. Lord North's ministry devised the Act to let the company bypass London auctions and American wholesalers, selling through appointed colonial consignees. Even with the Townshend duty, East India tea would be cheaper than the Dutch tea Americans smuggled in.

Colonial response

In most ports, protesters forced the tea ships to turn back or kept the tea from being landed. In Boston, Governor Thomas Hutchinson refused to let the ships leave without unloading. On 16 December 1773, members of the Sons of Liberty, some disguised as Mohawk Indians, boarded three ships — the Dartmouth, Eleanor, and Beaver — and dumped 342 chests of tea into Boston Harbor: the Boston Tea Party.

Parliament responded to the Boston Tea Party with the punitive Coercive Acts (called the "Intolerable Acts" in America) of 1774, closing the port of Boston and restricting Massachusetts self-government — measures that pushed the colonies toward the First Continental Congress and, ultimately, the American Revolutionary War.

Aftermath

The Tea Act itself remained in force, in theory, until its formal repeal by the Statute Law Revision Act 1861. The Townshend duty on tea survived until 1778 in Britain's remaining colonies. Historians note the irony that the Act lowered the price of tea; it was the constitutional principle, and the threat to colonial merchants, rather than cost, that provoked the crisis.
