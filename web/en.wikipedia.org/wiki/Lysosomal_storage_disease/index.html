Lysosomal storage disease

Lysosomal storage diseases (LSDs) are a group of over 70 rare inherited metabolic disorders characterised by an abnormal build-up of toxic materials in the body's cells as a result of enzyme deficiencies affecting the lysosomes. Lysosomes function as the digestive system of the cell, using enzymes to break down complex molecules such as lipids, sugars and proteins; when one of these enzymes is missing or defective, its substrate accumulates, progressively damaging cells, tissues and organs.

Classification

Lysosomal storage diseases are usually classified by the nature of the stored material. Major categories include:

  • Sphingolipidoses — including Gaucher disease, Tay–Sachs disease, Fabry disease, Niemann–Pick disease and Krabbe disease.
  • Mucopolysaccharidoses — including Hurler, Hunter, Sanfilippo and Morquio syndromes, caused by failure to degrade glycosaminoglycans.
  • Glycogen storage diseases affecting lysosomes — notably Pompe disease (acid maltase deficiency).
  • Mucolipidoses and oligosaccharidoses — disorders of glycoprotein degradation and enzyme targeting.
  • Lipid storage disorders and neuronal ceroid lipofuscinoses (Batten disease).

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms vary widely depending on the disorder and the tissues affected. Many LSDs involve enlargement of the liver and spleen (hepatosplenomegaly), skeletal abnormalities, coarse facial features, heart and lung problems, and progressive neurological deterioration. Most disorders begin in infancy or childhood, although late-onset forms with attenuated symptoms are increasingly recognised. Most are inherited in an autosomal recessive manner; a few, including Fabry disease and Hunter syndrome, are X-linked.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is typically made by assaying the activity of the suspected enzyme in blood or cultured cells, followed by genetic testing to identify the causative mutation. Newborn screening programmes for several lysosomal storage diseases have been introduced in some jurisdictions, since early treatment often improves outcomes.

Treatment

Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT), in which a recombinant version of the missing enzyme is infused regularly, is available for several disorders, including Gaucher, Fabry and Pompe diseases and several mucopolysaccharidoses. Other approaches include substrate reduction therapy (small molecules that decrease production of the stored material), pharmacological chaperones, haematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and, increasingly, gene therapy — the first gene therapies for lysosomal diseases have received regulatory approval. ERT generally cannot cross the blood–brain barrier, so neurological manifestations remain difficult to treat.

History

Tay–Sachs disease was described in the 1880s and Gaucher disease in 1882, but the unifying concept of lysosomal storage awaited Christian de Duve's discovery of the lysosome in 1955 and Hers's demonstration in 1963 that Pompe disease was caused by a lysosomal enzyme deficiency — the founding observation of the field.
