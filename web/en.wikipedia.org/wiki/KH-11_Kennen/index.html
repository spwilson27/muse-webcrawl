KH-11 Kennen

The KH-11 Kennen (also written KH-11 KENNEN, and codenamed during development as Crystal and later Evolved Enhanced CRYSTAL System) is a type of reconnaissance satellite first launched by the United States National Reconnaissance Office (NRO) in December 1976. Manufactured by Lockheed (now Lockheed Martin) in California, the KH-11 was the first American spy satellite to use electro-optical digital imaging, and so offer real-time optical observations, in place of film-return canisters.

History

The KH-11 replaced the film-return KH-8 Gambit and KH-9 Hexagon satellites. Instead of exposing photographic film and ejecting it in re-entry capsules, the KH-11 used a charge-coupled device (CCD) sensor behind a large reflecting telescope, transmitting imagery to ground stations via relay satellites. This gave analysts near-real-time access to photographs of denied areas.

Design details were famously leaked in 1978 when CIA employee William Kampiles sold the KH-11 technical manual to Soviet intelligence for $3,000. Successive "blocks" of the spacecraft introduced larger mirrors, improved sensors including infrared capability, and longer service lives.

Design

The satellite is believed to use a telescope with a primary mirror about 2.4 metres in diameter, comparable to that of the Hubble Space Telescope, giving a theoretical ground resolution of roughly 15 centimetres under ideal conditions. KH-11s fly in sun-synchronous polar orbits, typically with perigees around 250–300 km and apogees of 500–1,000 km, allowing consistent lighting conditions over targets.

Launches

Between 1976 and the 2020s, KH-11 satellites were launched on Titan IIID, Titan 34D, Titan IV and Delta IV rockets from Vandenberg Air Force Base in California. Launches are classified and the satellites are officially designated USA-series numbers. Public reports associate several later launches with the KH-11 lineage, including USA-224, USA-245 and USA-290.
