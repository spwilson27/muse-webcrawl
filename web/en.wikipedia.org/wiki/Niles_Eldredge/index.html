Niles Eldredge

Niles Eldredge (born August 25, 1943) is an American biologist and paleontologist. He is best known, with Stephen Jay Gould, for proposing the theory of punctuated equilibrium in 1972, which holds that most evolutionary change in the fossil record occurs in rapid bursts associated with speciation, separated by long periods of relative stability (stasis). Eldredge was a curator of invertebrate paleontology at the American Museum of Natural History in New York.

Career

Eldredge studied anthropology and geology at Columbia University, where he completed his doctorate in 1969 with a dissertation on Devonian trilobites of eastern North America. Observing that trilobite species appeared abruptly in local sections and then persisted unchanged for millions of years, with change concentrated in isolated peripheral populations, he and Gould developed punctuated equilibrium as an application of Ernst Mayr's model of allopatric speciation to the fossil record. The theory challenged the expectation of slow, gradual change (phyletic gradualism) and sparked decades of debate about the tempo and mode of evolution.

Later work

Eldredge has written extensively on evolutionary theory, hierarchy theory, mass extinctions and biodiversity, including Time Frames (1985), Macroevolutionary Dynamics (1989), The Pattern of Evolution (1999) and Why We Do It: Rethinking Sex and the Selfish Gene (2004). He is a prominent defender of evolution against creationism and curated the American Museum's 2005 exhibition on Charles Darwin. An avid jazz cornet player, he has also written about the intersection of his scientific work and music.
