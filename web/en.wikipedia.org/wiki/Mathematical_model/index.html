Mathematical model

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mathematical model is an abstract description of a system using mathematical concepts and language. Mathematical models are used throughout the natural sciences, engineering, economics, and the social sciences to represent systems, study the effects of changing their components, and make predictions about their behaviour. A model may help to explain a system, to forecast future events, or to support decisions and control.

Elements and construction

Building a model typically involves identifying the relevant variables (quantities that describe the state of the system) and specifying relationships between them — equations, inequalities, logical rules or probabilistic statements — together with assumptions that simplify the real system to something tractable. Models are characterised by a trade-off: a model that captures more detail may be harder to analyse or require data that are unavailable, while a simpler model is easier to use but less faithful. Model evaluation includes checking the model against observations (validation), estimating its parameters from data, and analysing how sensitive its predictions are to its assumptions and inputs.

Classification

Models are classified in many ways:

  • Linear vs. nonlinear — whether the relationships between variables are linear.
  • Deterministic vs. stochastic — whether outcomes are fixed by the inputs or involve randomness and probability.
  • Static vs. dynamic — whether the model accounts for change over time; dynamic models often take the form of differential or difference equations.
  • Discrete vs. continuous — whether time and variables take discrete values or vary continuously.
  • Deductive, inductive or "floating" — whether the model is derived from theory, fitted to observations, or built ad hoc around the behaviour it is meant to reproduce.

Examples

Well-known mathematical models include Newton's laws as a model of classical mechanics; the Lotka–Volterra equations for predator–prey populations; compartmental models such as SIR in epidemiology; the Black–Scholes model of option pricing; linear programming models in operations research; general circulation models of the climate; and supply-and-demand models in economics. The map–territory distinction is a standard caution: a model is a representation, useful only insofar as its simplifications are acceptable for its purpose — in George Box's aphorism, "all models are wrong, but some are useful."
