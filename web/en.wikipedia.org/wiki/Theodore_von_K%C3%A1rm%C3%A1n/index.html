Theodore von Kármán

Born: 11 May 1881, Budapest, Austria-Hungary
Died: 6 May 1963, Aachen, West Germany
Fields: Aerospace engineering, fluid mechanics
Institutions: Göttingen, RWTH Aachen, Caltech

Theodore von Kármán (Hungarian: Kármán Tódor; 11 May 1881 – 6 May 1963) was a Hungarian-American mathematician, aerospace engineer and physicist. He was a central figure in the development of aerodynamics and supersonic flight, a founding figure of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, and the first recipient of the United States National Medal of Science.

Early life and career in Europe

Born in Budapest, Kármán studied engineering at the Royal Joseph Technical University and took his doctorate at Göttingen under Ludwig Prandtl, the founder of modern aerodynamics. His analysis of alternating vortices behind bluff bodies — the "Kármán vortex street" — made his name. From 1913 to 1930 he directed the aeronautical institute at RWTH Aachen, where he worked on helicopters, airships and boundary-layer theory.

Career in the United States

In 1930 Kármán accepted the directorship of the Guggenheim Aeronautical Laboratory at the California Institute of Technology and emigrated to the United States, becoming a citizen in 1936. At Caltech he advised the American aircraft industry on high-speed flight (including the Douglas DC-3's design context) and mentored the "Suicide Squad" of rocket experimenters — Frank Malina, Jack Parsons and others — whose work became the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, founded under his leadership in 1943. After the Second World War he chaired the U.S. Air Force's Scientific Advisory Group, shaping American air and space research, and helped found the science arm of NATO (AGARD). The Kármán line, the 100-km boundary of space used by the FAI, commemorates his calculation of the altitude at which aerodynamic flight becomes impossible. Qian Xuesen, a founder of China's rocket programme, was among his doctoral students.

Honours

Kármán received the first National Medal of Science from President John F. Kennedy in 1963, shortly before his death in Aachen. Lunar and Martian craters are named for him, as is the von Kármán Institute for Fluid Dynamics in Belgium.
