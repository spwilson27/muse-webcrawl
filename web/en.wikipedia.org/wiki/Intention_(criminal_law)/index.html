Intention (criminal law)

In criminal law, intention is a form of mens rea — the mental element of an offence — in which the defendant acts with the purpose of bringing about a result, or acts knowing that the result is a virtually certain consequence of their conduct. It is generally the most culpable mental state, ranked above recklessness and negligence.

Direct and oblique intent

Direct intent exists where the result is the defendant's aim or purpose. Oblique (or indirect) intent may be found where the result was not the defendant's purpose but was a virtually certain consequence of their actions and the defendant realised this — the test refined by the House of Lords in R v Woollin (1999), in which a father who threw his infant son in anger was held not necessarily to have intended the child's death. English law treats the virtual-certainty test as the boundary: a jury is entitled to find intention where death or serious injury was a virtual certainty and the defendant appreciated that.

Specific and basic intent

English courts also distinguish crimes of specific intent (requiring a purpose beyond the act itself, such as intent to cause grievous bodily harm in murder) from crimes of basic intent, for which recklessness suffices. The distinction matters for defences such as voluntary intoxication, which may negate specific intent but not basic intent (DPP v Majewski).

Transferred malice

Where a defendant intends harm to one person but accidentally harms another, the doctrine of transferred malice transfers the intention to the actual victim, provided the harm is of the same type.
