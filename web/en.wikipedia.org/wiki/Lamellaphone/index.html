Lamellaphone

A lamellaphone (or lamellophone) is a family of musical instruments in which sound is produced by one or more thin, flexible plates or tongues — lamellae — that are fixed at one end and plucked, or occasionally struck or bowed, at the other. In the Hornbostel–Sachs classification system lamellaphones form category 12 (plucked idiophones), subdivided into instruments in raft form (121.1) and in board or comb form (121.2).

Types

The best-known lamellaphones are African: the mbira of the Shona people of Zimbabwe, played with the thumbs and index finger and often mounted in a gourd resonator (deze); the smaller kalimba, popularised worldwide by Hugh Tracey; the likembe and sanza of Central Africa; and the Afro-Cuban marímbula, a large lamellaphone used as a bass instrument. Outside Africa, the family includes the Jew's harp (a frame lamellaphone, class 121.2), the Vietnamese đàn môi, and Swiss and German music-box mechanisms, in which the tuned teeth of a steel comb are plucked by pins on a rotating cylinder.

Construction and playing

Lamellae were traditionally made of split cane, palm rib or wood; metal tongues — from iron, brass or spring steel — became common with the availability of worked metal. Tuning is adjusted by sliding the tongue to change its free length. Because the pitch set is fixed by the tongues, lamellaphones are often tuned to specific scales used for song accompaniment, ritual music and storytelling.
