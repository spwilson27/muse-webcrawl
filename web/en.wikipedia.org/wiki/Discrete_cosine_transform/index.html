Discrete cosine transform

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A discrete cosine transform (DCT) expresses a finite sequence of data points in terms of a sum of cosine functions oscillating at different frequencies. The DCT, first proposed by Nasir Ahmed in 1972, is a widely used transformation technique in signal processing and data compression, due to its strong "energy compaction" property: most of the signal energy concentrates in a few low-frequency components of the DCT.

DCT-II

The most common form, the DCT-II, is defined for a sequence x0, …, xN−1 by:

Xk = Σn=0N−1 xn cos[ π/N (n + ½) k ],   k = 0, …, N−1

Its inverse is the DCT-III. The DCT-I, DCT-II, DCT-III and DCT-IV differ in the boundary conditions they implicitly impose on the signal; the DCT can be understood as the discrete Fourier transform of a symmetrically extended real sequence, which is why its outputs are purely real.

Applications

The 8×8 DCT-II is the workhorse of lossy compression: it is the core of the JPEG image standard, of MPEG video standards, and of audio codecs such as MP3 (via the related modified DCT, MDCT) and AAC. Blocks of an image are transformed, the coefficients quantised — discarding perceptually minor high-frequency detail — and then entropy-coded. Because smooth image regions are well approximated by a few cosine terms, high compression ratios are achievable with modest visible loss.

Relation to the DFT

Unlike the DFT, which produces complex outputs and implicitly assumes a periodic extension that can introduce a discontinuity at the boundary, the DCT's even extension avoids that discontinuity. This gives the DCT better energy compaction for typical correlated signals such as images and audio.
