Arithmetica (Greek: Ἀριθμητικά, Arithmetika) is an Ancient Greek text on mathematics written by the Hellenized Babylonian mathematician Diophantus (c. 200/214 AD – c. 284/298 AD) in the 3rd century AD. It is a collection of 130 algebraic problems giving numerical solutions of determinate equations (those with a unique solution) and indeterminate equations.

History and influence

Diophantus's work created the foundations of algebra and influenced the development of number theory. Equations in the book are now called Diophantine equations. The method for solving them is known as Diophantine analysis. Most of the Arithmetica problems lead to quadratic equations.

In Book 3, Diophantus solves problems of finding values which make two expressions simultaneously squares or cubes. In Book 4, he finds rational powers between given numbers. He also noticed that numbers of the form 4n + 3 cannot be the sum of two squares, and that every number can be written as the sum of four squares.

Fermat's Last Theorem

The Arithmetica is best known today for the marginal note written by Pierre de Fermat in his copy of the 1621 Latin translation by Claude Gaspard Bachet de Méziriac. Next to Problem 8 of Book II — expressing a given square number as a sum of two squares — Fermat wrote that no three positive integers a, b, and c can satisfy an + bn = cn for any integer value of n greater than 2, claiming he had a "truly marvelous proof" that the margin was too narrow to contain. The resulting statement, Fermat's Last Theorem, was finally proved by Andrew Wiles in 1994.

Manuscripts and editions

Of the original thirteen books, six survive in Greek; four more books survive in an Arabic translation made by Qusta ibn Luqa. The first Latin translation appeared in 1575, and Bachet's influential Greek–Latin edition appeared in 1621.
