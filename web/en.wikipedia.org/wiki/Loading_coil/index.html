Loading coil

A loading coil (also called a Pupin coil) is an inductor inserted into an electronic circuit to increase its inductance. In telecommunications, loading coils were widely used on long telephone lines to reduce distortion and attenuation of voice-frequency signals, greatly extending the useful length of subscriber loops and long-distance circuits before the era of electronic amplifiers.

Principle

A long pair of wires behaves as a transmission line with distributed resistance, inductance, capacitance and leakage. In the 1880s Oliver Heaviside derived the "distortionless condition": if the ratio of inductance to resistance equals the ratio of capacitance to leakage (L/R = C/G), all frequency components travel at the same speed and are attenuated equally, so speech remains intelligible. Ordinary telephone cables have very little inductance, so Heaviside proposed adding inductance deliberately. Inserting lumped inductors at regular intervals approximates the condition and flattens the line's frequency response across the voice band (roughly 300–3400 Hz).

History

The practical loading coil was developed around 1899–1900 by Michael Pupin of Columbia University and independently by George Ashley Campbell of AT&T, leading to a famous patent dispute. Pupin's patents were bought by AT&T, and loading became standard practice. A common arrangement, "H-88 loading", placed 88 millihenry coils every 6,000 feet (about 1.83 km) on a loop.

Effects and modern fate

Loading improves voice transmission but turns the line into a low-pass filter with a sharp cutoff just above the voice band. This is fatal for high-frequency services: loaded loops cannot support DSL broadband, which needs frequencies far above 4 kHz. As DSL spread, telephone companies had to locate and remove loading coils from subscriber lines; they also interfere with some analogue modem and ISDN services. Loading coils are encountered in old plant as cylindrical cans on poles or in manholes, often in strings along a cable route.

The same term is used in radio engineering: in antenna design, a loading coil adds electrical length to a physically short antenna element (for example in mobile whip antennas and amateur radio), bringing it to resonance at a lower frequency.
