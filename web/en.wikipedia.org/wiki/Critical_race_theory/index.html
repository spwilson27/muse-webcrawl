Critical race theory

Critical race theory (CRT) is an academic and legal framework that examines how race and racism intersect with legal systems, social structures, and cultural representations in the United States. It emerged in the mid-1970s and 1980s in the work of legal scholars — including Derrick Bell, Alan Freeman, Richard Delgado, and Kimberlé Crenshaw — who were dissatisfied with the pace of civil-rights reform and with the liberal legal orthodoxy that treated racism primarily as individual prejudice remediable by colour-blind law. The framework holds that racism is not aberrational but an ordinary, embedded feature of American life and law, and it analyses concepts such as interest convergence, the social construction of race, differential racialisation, and storytelling or counter-storytelling.

History

CRT grew out of critical legal studies and the civil-rights movement. Derrick Bell's articles of the 1970s — notably his analysis of Brown v. Board of Education through the lens of "interest convergence", the idea that advances for Black Americans occur when they align with white elite interests — set its early agenda. The movement was named at a 1989 workshop in Madison, Wisconsin, organised by Crenshaw and others. In the 1990s the framework spread from law into education (Gloria Ladson-Billings and William Tate), sociology, and ethnic studies, producing offshoots such as LatCrit, TribalCrit, and AsianCrit.

Core themes

Introductory accounts (such as Delgado and Stefancic's widely used primer) list characteristic tenets: racism is ordinary rather than exceptional; the system of white-over-colour ascendancy serves psychic and material interests ("interest convergence" and material determinism); race is socially constructed rather than biologically grounded; dominant society racialises different groups differently at different times; law is not neutral, and colour-blindness can entrench existing hierarchies; and the distinctive voices and experiential knowledge of people of colour — presented through narrative and counter-storytelling — are legitimate sources of legal insight. Crenshaw's concept of intersectionality, developed within CRT, addresses how race, gender, class, and other categories interact.

Public controversy

From 2020, "critical race theory" became a major flashpoint in American politics. Critics, most prominently activist Christopher Rufo, applied the term broadly to diversity training and school curricula dealing with race and argued such teaching is divisive; several state legislatures enacted restrictions on how race and gender may be discussed in classrooms. Scholars of CRT respond that it is a graduate-level legal framework rarely taught as such in schools, and that the political usage conflates it with any race-conscious pedagogy. The dispute significantly shaped education politics and the 2021–2024 election cycles.

Criticism within academia

Critics in legal scholarship have questioned CRT's reliance on narrative, its scepticism toward liberal rights and merit principles, and the testability of its claims; others defend it as a corrective to formal equality and note the empirical literature on structural racial disparities.
