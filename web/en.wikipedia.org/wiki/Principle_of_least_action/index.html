Principle of least action

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The principle of least action — more precisely the principle of stationary action — is a variational principle that, when applied to the action of a mechanical system, yields the equations of motion for that system. It states that the trajectory a system follows between two states is the one for which the action functional is stationary (usually a minimum or a saddle point).

The action S is defined as the integral over time of the Lagrangian L of the system: S = ∫ L dt. Requiring the first variation of the action to vanish, δS = 0, leads by the calculus of variations to the Euler–Lagrange equations, which are equivalent to Newton's laws of motion for conservative systems.

History

Pierre Louis Maupertuis is credited with formulating an early version of the principle in 1744–1746, regarding the quantity of action as a kind of economy imposed by nature. Leonhard Euler and Joseph-Louis Lagrange put it on a rigorous mathematical footing in the mid-eighteenth century, and William Rowan Hamilton restated it in 1834–1835 in the form (Hamilton's principle) most used today.

Applications

The principle underlies Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics, and generalises naturally to optics (Fermat's principle), electromagnetism, general relativity, and quantum field theory, where the path-integral formulation of Richard Feynman can be read as a quantum generalisation of stationary action. It is also central to Noether's theorem, which connects symmetries of the action to conservation laws.
