Carbon capture and storage

Carbon capture and storage (CCS) is a technology for reducing carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions from industrial and energy sources by capturing CO₂, transporting it, and storing it permanently underground, typically in deep geological formations, rather than releasing it to the atmosphere.

MethodsPost-combustion, pre-combustion, oxy-fuel capture
StorageDepleted oil/gas fields, deep saline aquifers
RelatedCCUS, BECCS, direct air capture

Capture

Capture uses solvents (often amines), membranes or oxy-fuel combustion to separate CO₂ from flue gases or process streams at power plants, cement, steel, chemicals and hydrogen facilities. Capture imposes an energy penalty and cost.

Transport and storage

CO₂ is compressed, shipped or piped, and injected into porous rock capped by impermeable layers. Long-running projects include Sleipner (Norway, since 1996), storing CO₂ in a saline aquifer under the North Sea. Monitoring checks for leakage.

Role and debate

The IPCC and IEA include CCS in many low-cost pathways to net zero, especially for heavy industry. Critics cite high cost, slow deployment, fossil-fuel lock-in and storage permanence. Variants include carbon capture and utilisation (CCU), bioenergy with CCS (BECCS) and direct air capture with storage. See Climate change mitigation.
