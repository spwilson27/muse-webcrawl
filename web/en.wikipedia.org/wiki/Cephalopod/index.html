A cephalopod is any member of the molluscan class Cephalopoda (Greek plural Κεφαλόποδα, "head-feet"), including squid, octopuses, cuttlefish and nautiluses. These exclusively marine animals are characterised by bilateral body symmetry, a prominent head, and a set of arms or tentacles (muscular hydrostats) modified from the primitive molluscan foot. Fishers sometimes call them inkfish, referring to their ability to eject ink.

Anatomy and behaviour

Cephalopods have the most complex nervous systems of any invertebrates, with large brains relative to body size, and sophisticated camera-type eyes. Most can rapidly change skin colour and texture for camouflage and communication using pigment-filled chromatophores. They swim by jet propulsion, expelling water through a siphon. All possess a beak-like mouthpart; many use venom to subdue prey. Sizes range from tiny squid of about 1 cm to the giant and colossal squid, among the largest invertebrates known.

Evolution

Cephalopods appeared in the late Cambrian, evolving from shelled, gastropod-like molluscs. Shelled forms — ammonites, belemnites and nautiloids — dominated Palaeozoic and Mesozoic seas. The soft-bodied coleoids (squid, octopus, cuttlefish) internalised or lost the shell; today only the nautiluses retain an external coiled shell. Cephalopods are important both as predators and as prey in marine food webs, and support major commercial fisheries.

Intelligence

Octopuses and cuttlefish show learning, memory, play and tool use — for example, veined octopuses carry coconut shells as portable shelters — making cephalopods a major subject of research into the evolution of cognition.
