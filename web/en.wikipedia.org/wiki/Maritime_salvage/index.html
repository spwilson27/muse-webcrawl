Maritime salvage

For the practical recovery process, see Marine salvage.

Maritime salvage is a legal doctrine of admiralty law under which a person who voluntarily and successfully saves a vessel, cargo, or other maritime property from peril at sea is entitled to a reward. The award is not payment for services in the ordinary contractual sense but a bounty granted by maritime law to encourage mariners to render aid to property in danger, traditionally on the principle of "no cure, no pay" — no reward is due if the salvage attempt fails.

Elements of a salvage claim

A valid claim for pure salvage requires three elements: (1) a marine peril — the property must be in actual or reasonably apprehended danger; (2) voluntariness — the salvor must act without a pre-existing legal or contractual duty to assist (so a ship's own crew, a pilot, or a tug already under contract generally cannot claim); and (3) success — the effort must contribute to saving the property, in whole or in part. Where these are satisfied, the salvor acquires a maritime lien on the salved property to secure the award, enforceable by arrest of the vessel.

The award

Courts and arbitrators fix awards by reference to criteria such as the value of the property saved, the degree of danger, the skill, labour, and promptitude of the salvors, the risk run to their own vessels and equipment, and the value of the property employed. Awards are paid proportionately by the owners (and insurers) of the vessel, cargo, and freight saved. Historically awards were a fraction of the salved value, increasing with the degree of danger; in cases of derelict (property abandoned at sea with no intention of recovery), awards are especially generous.

Modern framework

The International Convention on Salvage 1989 modernised the law, preserved the no-cure-no-pay principle, and — in response to disasters such as the Amoco Cadiz oil spill — introduced "special compensation" payable to salvors who protect the environment, even where little property value was saved. Standard-form contracts, above all Lloyd's Open Form (LOF), allow masters in distress to agree terms instantly ("no cure, no pay" with arbitration in London), supplemented by the SCOPIC clause to govern environmental compensation. Salvage of life alone gives no claim against property, though life salvors share in any property award.

Distinctions

Maritime salvage differs from the law of finds (which concerns abandoned property, usually on the seabed, and may grant title), from towage (contractual pulling of a vessel not in peril), and from wreck removal ordered by authorities. Treasure salvage involving historic wrecks is frequently restricted by sovereign immunity for sunken warships and by heritage conventions such as the UNESCO Convention on the Protection of the Underwater Cultural Heritage.
