Epistolary novel

An epistolary novel is a novel written as a series of letters between the fictional characters of a narrative. The term is often extended to cover novels that rely on other forms of documents in addition to letters, such as diary entries, newspaper clippings and, more recently, blog posts, emails, transcripts and other records. The form is one of the earliest genres of the novel in English, Spanish, French and German literature.

History

Antecedents include Ovid's Heroides and early Spanish and Italian works, but the genre flourished in the 18th century: Samuel Richardson's Pamela (1740) and Clarissa (1748) made the letter-novel central to the development of the English novel; Montesquieu's Persian Letters (1721), Rousseau's Julie (1761), Goethe's The Sorrows of Young Werther (1774) and Laclos's Les Liaisons dangereuses (1782) are other landmarks. The form allows intimate access to characters' unguarded thoughts and permits multiple, sometimes contradictory, points of view.

Later examples

Bram Stoker's Dracula (1897) is told through letters, diaries, telegrams and newspaper reports. In the 20th and 21st centuries the device continued in works such as C. S. Lewis's The Screwtape Letters (1942), Alice Walker's The Color Purple (1982), and email- and chat-based novels such as Matt Beaumont's e (2000).
