Cuesta


Cross-section of a cuesta, with the steep scarp face and gentle dip slope

A cuesta (from Spanish cuesta, "slope") is a hill or ridge with a gentle slope on one side, and a steep slope on the other. In geology the term is more specifically applied to a ridge where a harder sedimentary rock overlies a softer layer, the whole being tilted at an angle by the underlying structure. The term is also used for the resulting landforms in geomorphology and physical geography.

Definition

The expression of a cuesta's profile depends on the thickness and hardness of the overlying resistant stratum and on the angle of its dip. Cuestas can range in size from a few metres to many kilometres in length. The steep front slope is called the scarp or escarpment face; the gentle back slope follows the dip of the strata and is called the dip slope. Where the dip is very low, the formation approaches a mesa or plateau; where the layers are more strongly tilted, a sharper ridge called a hogback may result.

Formation

Cuestas form in regions of gently tilted sedimentary rocks where alternating hard and soft layers are exposed to erosion. The resistant rock (commonly sandstone, limestone, or dolomite) protects the softer rock beneath it on the dip slope, while erosion cuts back the scarp face, so the ridge slowly migrates in the direction opposite to the dip. Cuestas are common in sedimentary basins and on the flanks of domes and anticlines.

Examples

Notable cuestas include the Niagara Escarpment in Ontario, Canada, and New York State, where hard Silurian dolomite overlies softer shale; the chalk cuestas of southern England, such as the North Downs and South Downs; and the cuestas of the Paris Basin in France. In the American Southwest, cuestas and hogbacks are characteristic of the Colorado Plateau margin.
