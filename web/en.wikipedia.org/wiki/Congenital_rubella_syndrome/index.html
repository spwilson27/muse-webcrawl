Specialty: Teratology, pediatrics
Cause: Rubella infection during pregnancy

Congenital rubella syndrome

Congenital rubella syndrome (CRS) occurs when a pregnant woman contracts rubella (German measles), usually in the first trimester, and the virus infects the developing fetus. The syndrome was first described by the Australian ophthalmologist Norman McAlister Gregg in 1941.

Signs and symptoms

The classic triad of CRS consists of sensorineural deafness, eye abnormalities such as congenital cataracts, and congenital heart defects, particularly patent ductus arteriosus. Other features include growth restriction, microcephaly, developmental delay, and a "blueberry muffin" rash caused by dermal erythropoiesis.

Prevention

CRS is preventable through rubella vaccination, typically given as part of the MMR vaccine. Widespread immunization has nearly eliminated the syndrome in many countries, though it remains a cause of birth defects where vaccination coverage is low.

Prognosis

Outcomes vary with the severity of infection. Late manifestations, such as diabetes mellitus and progressive panencephalitis, may appear years after birth, so long-term follow-up is recommended.
