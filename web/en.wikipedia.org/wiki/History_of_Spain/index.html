History of Spain

The history of Spain spans from prehistoric settlement of the Iberian Peninsula through Roman Hispania, the Visigothic kingdom, Muslim al-Andalus, the Christian Reconquista and unification under the Catholic Monarchs, the Spanish Empire, decline, civil war and dictatorship, to the modern democratic kingdom.

Ancient and Roman Iberia

The peninsula was home to Iberian and Celtic peoples and Phoenician, Greek and Carthaginian colonies before Rome conquered it between 218 and 19 BCE. Hispania became one of the empire's most Romanised provinces, supplying emperors including Trajan and Hadrian, as well as the philosopher Seneca. After Rome's fall, the Visigoths established a kingdom centred on Toledo, converting from Arianism to Catholicism in 589.

Al-Andalus and the Reconquista

In 711 Muslim forces crossed from North Africa and rapidly conquered most of the peninsula, creating al-Andalus, whose Umayyad emirate and later caliphate of Córdoba became one of the most prosperous and learned societies of early medieval Europe. The Christian kingdoms of the north — Asturias, León, Castile, Aragon, Navarre and Portugal — gradually expanded southward in the centuries-long Reconquista, decisively after Las Navas de Tolosa (1212). In 1469 the marriage of Isabella I of Castile and Ferdinand II of Aragon joined the two largest crowns; in 1492 their forces took Granada, the last Muslim state, Columbus reached the Americas under their sponsorship, and Jews who refused conversion were expelled.

Empire and decline

Under Charles I (the Emperor Charles V) and Philip II, Spain ruled a global empire spanning the Americas, the Philippines, Italy and the Netherlands, enriched by American silver and strained by constant warfare and inflation. The 17th century brought revolts, the loss of Portugal (1640) and gradual decline relative to France and England; the War of the Spanish Succession (1701–1714) installed the Bourbon dynasty and centralised the state.

The modern era

The Peninsular War against Napoleon (1808–1814) coincided with the loss of most American colonies. The 19th century saw liberal–conservative conflict, Carlist wars and the loss of Cuba, Puerto Rico and the Philippines in 1898. The Second Republic (1931) was overthrown in the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939), after which Francisco Franco ruled as dictator until his death in 1975. Under King Juan Carlos I, Spain transitioned to democracy, adopted the constitution of 1978 and joined the European Community in 1986. Autonomous communities, including Catalonia and the Basque Country, govern much regional policy; a Catalan independence bid in 2017 produced Spain's gravest constitutional crisis of the democratic era.
