Full-frame digital SLR

A full-frame DSLR uses a 36 × 24 mm sensor, the same size as a 135 film frame.

A full-frame digital single-lens reflex camera (or full-frame DSLR) is a digital SLR with a 35 mm image sensor format (36 mm × 24 mm). Historically, 35 mm was considered a small film format compared with medium format; in digital terms, however, full frame is among the largest sensor sizes available in general-purpose cameras, exceeded by medium-format digital backs and cameras.

Compared with smaller formats such as APS-C, a full-frame sensor offers shallower depth of field at the same aperture and field of view, better low-light performance (larger photosites for a given resolution), and full use of legacy 35 mm lenses at their intended focal lengths, without a crop factor. The disadvantages are cost, size and weight: full-frame sensors are far more expensive to manufacture because fewer fit on a silicon wafer and defects ruin a larger proportion.

History

Early professional DSLRs used APS-C-sized sensors. Kodak's DCS Pro 14n (2002) was among the first full-frame DSLRs, but Canon's EOS-1Ds (2002) established the category at the professional level. Canon's EOS 5D (2005) brought full frame to a lower price point and, with the 5D Mark II (2008), introduced widely used full-HD video, transforming independent filmmaking. Nikon entered with the D3 and D700 in 2007–08, notable for exceptional high-ISO performance. Sony, Pentax and Leica also marketed full-frame DSLRs. From the 2010s, mirrorless cameras progressively displaced DSLRs, but the 36 × 24 mm format remains the reference standard for professional systems.

Characteristics

  • Sensor size 36 × 24 mm; crop factor 1.0×
  • Typical resolutions from 12 to over 50 megapixels in later models
  • Larger, brighter optical viewfinders than crop-sensor DSLRs
  • Compatibility with the full range of 35 mm SLR lenses
