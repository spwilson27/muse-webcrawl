A space elevator, also known as a space bridge, star ladder or orbital lift, is a proposed type of space transportation system: a cable anchored to the Earth's equator, extending into space beyond geostationary orbit (35,786 km), where a counterweight holds it taut by centrifugal effect. Vehicles called climbers would ascend and descend the cable mechanically, potentially reducing the cost of reaching orbit from tens of thousands of dollars per kilogram to a small fraction of that.

Concept and history

Konstantin Tsiolkovsky imagined a free-standing orbital tower in 1895, inspired by the Eiffel Tower. The modern tether concept came from Yuri Artsutanov (1960) and, independently, Jerome Pearson (1975) and John Isaacs and colleagues. Arthur C. Clarke popularised the idea in his 1979 novel The Fountains of Paradise, and a 2003 NASA Institute for Advanced Concepts study by Bradley Edwards detailed a ribbon design. Interest sharpened after the discovery of carbon nanotubes, whose theoretical tensile strength approaches the requirement.

Design and obstacles

The ribbon must withstand its own weight over 100,000 km, requiring a specific strength no bulk material yet achieves; defects, radiation degradation, micrometeoroids and collisions with satellites and space debris pose further risks. Climbers would need power — commonly proposed as laser beaming — and the system would need to survive weather, lightning, oscillation and the danger that a severed cable could wrap around the Earth. Lunar and Martian elevators face far lower strength requirements and are sometimes proposed as nearer-term goals.
