Apostle

The Twelve Apostles were chosen by Jesus to preach the Gospel; the term has since been applied to founders and emissaries of other faiths.

An apostle (from Greek ἀπόστολος, apóstolos, "one who is sent off") is, in its principal sense, one of the early Christian missionaries commissioned by Jesus. In the New Testament the title is applied above all to the Twelve Apostles, the disciples chosen by Jesus during his ministry, and to Paul, who understood himself as apostle to the Gentiles through his vision of the risen Christ on the road to Damascus.

The Twelve

The Gospels list the Twelve as Simon Peter, Andrew, James son of Zebedee, John, Philip, Bartholomew, Thomas, Matthew the tax collector, James son of Alphaeus, Thaddeus (Jude), Simon the Zealot, and Judas Iscariot. After Judas's death, the Book of Acts describes his replacement by Matthias, chosen by lot. Tradition holds that the apostles dispersed from Jerusalem to preach throughout the Roman world and beyond, and that most died as martyrs; their alleged burial places became major pilgrimage sites, such as Santiago de Compostela for James and Rome for Peter and Paul.

Later and wider usage

In Roman Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican theology, bishops are the successors of the apostles, and churches founded by an apostle claim "apostolicity" — one of the four marks of the Church in the Nicene Creed. In Islam, a rasul (often translated "apostle" or "messenger") is a prophet who receives a new revelation or scripture, including Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus and Muhammad. The Latter Day Saint movement restored an office of apostle: the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. The word is also used figuratively for the pioneer of a cause — for example, St Boniface as "Apostle of the Germans" or Patrick as "Apostle of Ireland" — and for leading advocates of doctrines such as free trade.
