Pythagoreanism

Pythagoreanism was the system of esoteric and metaphysical beliefs held by the ancient Greek philosopher Pythagoras (c. 570 – c. 495 BC) and his followers, the Pythagoreans, who founded a community at Croton in southern Italy. It combined mathematics, religion, music, and a distinctive way of life.

Doctrine

The central Pythagorean conviction was that "all is number": the cosmos is ordered by numerical ratios, an insight supported by the discovery that musical harmony corresponds to simple ratios of string lengths. The tetraktys — the triangular arrangement of ten points in four rows — symbolised this cosmic order and was held sacred. Pythagoreans also believed in the transmigration of souls (metempsychosis), practised vegetarianism and communal living, and observed rules known as akousmata, ranging from ritual precepts to the famous prohibition on eating beans.

Mathematics and astronomy

The Pythagoreans are credited with early study of odd and even numbers, figurate numbers, and perfect numbers, and with geometrical results including the theorem that bears Pythagoras's name (known earlier in Babylonian practice). In astronomy they proposed a spherical Earth and a cosmology in which Earth and the planets revolved around a "central fire", anticipating non-geocentric models.

Legacy

Pythagorean ideas profoundly influenced Plato and later Neopythagoreanism and Neoplatonism, shaping Western philosophy, mathematics, and musical theory for centuries.
