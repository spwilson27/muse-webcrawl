Lawrence Kohlberg (October 25, 1927 – January 19, 1987) was an American psychologist best known for his theory of stages of moral development. He served as a professor in the Psychology Department at the University of Chicago and at the Graduate School of Education at Harvard University. Even though it was thought that Kohlberg's research was only about boys, the data was mostly on how males develop morally.

Early life and education

Kohlberg was born in Bronxville, New York, the youngest of four children of Alfred Kohlberg, a successful silk merchant. After serving in the Merchant Marine at the end of the Second World War — including helping to smuggle Jewish refugees through the British blockade of Palestine — he enrolled at the University of Chicago, where he scored so highly on entrance examinations that he was exempted from many courses and earned his bachelor's degree in one year. He completed his PhD in psychology in 1958; his dissertation formed the basis of his stage theory.

Theory of moral development

Building on the work of Jean Piaget, Kohlberg proposed that moral reasoning develops through six stages, grouped into three levels:

  • Pre-conventional — obedience and punishment orientation; self-interest orientation.
  • Conventional — interpersonal accord and conformity; authority and social-order maintaining orientation.
  • Post-conventional — social contract orientation; universal ethical principles.

His research used moral dilemmas, most famously the "Heinz dilemma", asking participants not what a protagonist should do but why, and classifying the structure of their reasoning. Kohlberg also applied his theory to education through "just community" schools, and to the study of moral atmosphere.

Criticism

Carol Gilligan, a former colleague, argued in In a Different Voice (1982) that Kohlberg's theory was biased toward a justice orientation and undervalued an ethic of care she associated with women's moral reasoning. Others questioned the cross-cultural universality of the stages, the rarity of the highest stage, and the gap between moral judgment and moral action.

Death

Kohlberg suffered for many years from a parasitic infection contracted during fieldwork in Belize in 1971, accompanied by chronic pain and depression. He died on January 19, 1987, having walked into the freezing waters of Boston Harbor in an apparent suicide.
