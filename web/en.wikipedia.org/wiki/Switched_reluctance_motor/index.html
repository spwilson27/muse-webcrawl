Switched reluctance motor

The switched reluctance motor (SRM) is a type of electric motor that runs by reluctance torque. Unlike common brushed DC motor types, power is delivered to windings in the stator (case) rather than the rotor. This greatly simplifies mechanical design as power does not have to be delivered to a moving part which eliminates the need for a commutator, but it complicates the electrical design, as some sort of switching system needs to be used to deliver power to the different windings.

Design

Both stator and rotor are made of laminated steel with salient (projecting) poles, and the rotor carries no windings, magnets or brushes, making it exceptionally robust and able to operate at very high speeds and temperatures. Stator windings on opposite poles are connected in series to form phases; energizing a phase attracts the nearest rotor poles into alignment, and by switching the phases in sequence continuous rotation is produced.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages of the SRM include simple and rugged construction, low manufacturing cost, high efficiency over a wide speed range, and inherent fault tolerance, since phases operate largely independently. Its main disadvantages are torque ripple and acoustic noise, and the need for sophisticated power electronics and rotor-position sensing or estimation for control. With modern controllers, switched reluctance drives have found applications in appliances, industrial drives, and some electric vehicle prototypes.

History

The reluctance principle dates to the nineteenth century, but practical switched reluctance drives had to await modern power semiconductor switching. Research programs in the 1960s–1980s, notably at the Universities of Leeds and Nottingham in the UK, developed the modern form of the drive.
