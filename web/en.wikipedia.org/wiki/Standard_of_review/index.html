Standard of review

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In appellate review, a standard of review is the level of deference a reviewing court shows to the decision below. The main standards form a ladder: de novo review (no deference, typically for questions of law and constitutional fact); clearly erroneous review of trial courts' factual findings (Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 52(a)); abuse of discretion review of case-management and evidentiary rulings; and substantial evidence / arbitrary-and-capricious review of administrative action (Administrative Procedure Act § 706, the framework of Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Association v. State Farm, 1983).

Constitutional adjudication uses its own tiers — rational-basis review, intermediate scrutiny and strict scrutiny — calibrated to the right or classification at stake. The standard chosen is frequently outcome-determinative, which is why advocates brief it as carefully as the merits: the same decision may survive deferential review that it would fail de novo. Harmless-error doctrine overlays the framework: errors not affecting substantial rights do not require reversal. In administrative law the landscape shifted when Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo (2024) overruled Chevron deference, instructing courts to decide legal questions independently while respecting agency expertise as persuasive.
