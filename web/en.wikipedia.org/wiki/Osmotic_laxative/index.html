Osmotic laxative

Osmotic laxatives are a class of laxatives that relieve constipation by drawing water into the colon. The active substances are poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, so they hold water in the bowel by osmosis, softening the stool, increasing its volume and stimulating peristalsis. They typically act within one to three days, or within a few hours at the higher doses used for bowel preparation before colonoscopy.

Types

Common osmotic laxatives include:

  • Polyethylene glycol (PEG / macrogol) — an inert polymer solution widely used for chronic constipation and, in large volumes, for colonoscopy preparation.
  • Lactulose — a synthetic disaccharide fermented by colonic bacteria; also used to treat hepatic encephalopathy because it traps ammonia in the gut.
  • Sorbitol — a sugar alcohol with a similar action to lactulose.
  • Saline laxatives — magnesium-containing preparations such as magnesium hydroxide ("milk of magnesia"), magnesium citrate and sodium phosphate, which draw water into the intestine rapidly.

Safety

Osmotic laxatives are generally considered safe for long-term use, with bloating and flatulence (especially from lactulose) the most common side effects. Magnesium- and phosphate-based products must be used cautiously in people with kidney disease, and large fluid shifts during bowel preparation can cause electrolyte disturbances.
