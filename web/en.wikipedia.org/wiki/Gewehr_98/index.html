The Gewehr 98 (abbreviated G98, Gew 98, or M98) is a German bolt-action rifle made by Mauser and adopted as the standard service rifle of the German Empire on 5 April 1898. Firing the 7.92×57mm Mauser cartridge from a five-shot internal clip-loaded magazine, it is the archetype of the Mauser M98 controlled-feed bolt-action system, widely considered the definitive bolt-action design and the basis for countless military and sporting rifles worldwide.

Design

The M98 action introduced features that became standard: a large non-rotating claw extractor giving controlled round feeding, a third "safety" locking lug at the rear of the bolt, a gas shield on the bolt shroud to protect the shooter from escaping gases, and a three-position flag safety. The rifle was long — 1.25 m overall with a 740 mm barrel — and fitted with the distinctive "rollercoaster" Lange Visier tangent rear sight graduated to 2,000 metres.

Service history

The Gewehr 98 was the principal German infantry rifle of the First World War, serving alongside the shortened Karabiner 98AZ and, later, the Karabiner 98a. During the war a sniper variant with telescopic sight, 25-round trench magazine conversions and dust covers were issued. In the interwar period many rifles were updated to the Karabiner 98b pattern. Shortened and modernised, the action reached its final German service form in the Karabiner 98k, the standard rifle of the Wehrmacht in the Second World War.

Derivatives and legacy

Mauser 98-pattern rifles were produced under licence or copied in many countries, including Spain, Czechoslovakia (vz. 24), Yugoslavia, Belgium and China, and influenced designs such as the American M1903 Springfield and the Japanese Arisaka. The controlled-feed Model 98 action remains in production today for high-quality hunting rifles, including Mauser's own M98 reissue.
