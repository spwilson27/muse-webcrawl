Shabbat

Shabbat (Hebrew: שַׁבָּת, "rest" or "cessation"), also known as the Sabbath, is Judaism's day of rest on the seventh day of the week, observed from a few minutes before sunset on Friday evening until nightfall on Saturday. According to halakha (Jewish law), Shabbat commemorates both God's rest after the six days of Creation and the Exodus from Egypt. It is the most important day of observance in Judaism, marked by refraining from work, prayer services, festive meals and study.

Observance

Shabbat begins with the lighting of candles, traditionally by the woman of the household, and the recitation of Kiddush over wine. Three meals are eaten, beginning with challah bread; songs (zemirot) are sung and words of Torah shared. Saturday services include the reading of the weekly Torah portion (parashah). The day ends with the Havdalah ceremony, separating Shabbat from the new week with wine, spices and a braided candle.

Prohibited activities

The Mishnah lists 39 categories of labour (melakhot) forbidden on Shabbat, including ploughing, baking, writing, building, kindling a fire and carrying between domains — derived from the work of constructing the Tabernacle. Orthodox observance interprets these to include driving and using electricity; Conservative and Reform movements apply the laws more leniently. Saving a human life (pikuach nefesh) overrides virtually all Shabbat prohibitions.

Significance

The Fourth Commandment enjoins remembering and keeping the Sabbath holy. Shabbat is often personified in Jewish liturgy as a bride and a queen, welcomed in the Friday-night hymn Lekha Dodi. The essayist Ahad Ha'am famously wrote: "More than the Jewish people have kept Shabbat, Shabbat has kept the Jewish people."
