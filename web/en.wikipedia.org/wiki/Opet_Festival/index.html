The Opet Festival (Egyptian: ḥb nfr n Ipt, "the beautiful festival of Opet") was an ancient Egyptian festival celebrated annually in Thebes during the New Kingdom, in the second month of the season of Akhet, when the Nile floodwaters rose. It was one of the greatest religious celebrations of ancient Egypt.

The procession

During the festival, the cult statues of the Theban Triad — Amun, his consort Mut, and their son Khonsu — were carried in ornate sacred barques from the temple of Karnak to the temple of Luxor, a journey of about three kilometres, either along the Avenue of Sphinxes or by river on the Nile. The pharaoh and priests accompanied the gods, while crowds lined the route with food, drink, and celebration.

Religious significance

At Luxor Temple — called Ipet-resyt, the "southern sanctuary" — rituals renewed the pharaoh's divine kingship: the king entered the temple and emerged reborn, his royal ka rejuvenated through union with Amun. The festival thus reaffirmed both the god's power and the legitimacy of the reigning king.

Depictions and duration

The colonnade hall of Luxor Temple is decorated with detailed reliefs of the festival from the reign of Tutankhamun, showing musicians, dancers, acrobats, soldiers, and offerings along the processional route. Originally lasting about eleven days under the 18th Dynasty, by the late New Kingdom the festival had lengthened to nearly a month. The tradition is continued in modified form by the modern festival of Abu Haggag, held at the mosque within Luxor Temple.
