The 2000 Camp David Summit was a summit meeting at Camp David between United States president Bill Clinton, Israeli prime minister Ehud Barak and Palestinian Authority chairman Yasser Arafat, held from 11 to 25 July 2000. It was intended to reach a "final status" agreement resolving the Israeli–Palestinian conflict, but ended without agreement.

Background

The summit followed the Oslo Accords of the 1990s, which had deferred the hardest issues — borders, Jerusalem, refugees and security — to final-status talks. Barak, elected in 1999, sought a comprehensive settlement before his coalition weakened, and Clinton hoped to conclude an agreement in his final year in office.

Negotiations

Over two weeks, the parties discussed proposals under which Israel would withdraw from most of the West Bank and Gaza Strip. Accounts differ sharply on the exact offers and on what happened: Clinton and Barak later maintained that Barak accepted proposals giving the Palestinians a state on the great majority of the West Bank (figures around 91–95% plus land swaps are cited) with a capital in East Jerusalem, and that Arafat rejected them without a counteroffer. Palestinian negotiators and some later analysts disputed this characterisation, pointing to annexation of settlement blocs, fragmentation of the proposed state, and unresolved positions on Jerusalem's holy sites and the Palestinian right of return.

Aftermath

The summit closed with a Trilateral Statement renouncing violence and committing to further talks. Follow-up negotiations at Taba, Egypt, in January 2001 came closer to agreement but ended when Barak lost the Israeli election to Ariel Sharon. The failure of Camp David, followed within months by the outbreak of the Second Intifada, is widely seen as a turning point that entrenched narratives — especially the Israeli "no partner" thesis — shaping the conflict for decades afterward.
