Biopharmaceutical

A biopharmaceutical, also known as a biologic medical product, or biologic, is any pharmaceutical drug product manufactured in, extracted from, or semisynthesized from biological sources. Different from chemically synthesized pharmaceuticals, they include vaccines, whole blood, blood components, allergenics, somatic cells, gene therapies, tissues, recombinant therapeutic proteins, and living medicines used in cell therapy. Biologics can be composed of sugars, proteins, nucleic acids, or complex combinations of these substances, or may be living cells or tissues.

Biopharmaceuticals are produced in living systems such as cell cultures in bioreactors.

History

The first biopharmaceutical approved for therapeutic use was recombinant human insulin, produced by Genentech and marketed by Eli Lilly as Humulin in 1982 — insulin previously having been extracted from the pancreases of cattle and pigs. Monoclonal antibodies followed, with muromonab-CD3 (OKT3) approved in 1986, and later blockbusters such as rituximab, trastuzumab, and adalimumab (Humira), among the best-selling drugs in the world. In 2020–2021, mRNA vaccines against COVID-19 became a prominent new class of biologic product.

Major classes

  • Recombinant proteins — insulin, growth hormone, erythropoietin, clotting factors.
  • Monoclonal antibodies — used in oncology, autoimmune disease, and infectious disease.
  • Vaccines — including live attenuated, inactivated, subunit, viral vector, and nucleic acid vaccines.
  • Cell and gene therapies — including CAR T-cell therapies and viral-vector gene replacements.
  • Blood products — plasma-derived factors, immunoglobulins, and albumin.

Manufacture

Biologics are typically manufactured in living systems — bacteria, yeast, or mammalian cell cultures (commonly Chinese hamster ovary cells) grown in large bioreactors — followed by complex purification. Because the product is defined by the process, biologics are highly sensitive to manufacturing conditions, and follow-on versions, called biosimilars, can be shown to be highly similar to an approved reference product but not identical, unlike small-molecule generics.

Regulation

In the United States, biologics are licensed under the Public Health Service Act through a Biologics License Application, regulated by the Food and Drug Administration's Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research (CBER) and the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER). In the European Union, the European Medicines Agency oversees approval.
