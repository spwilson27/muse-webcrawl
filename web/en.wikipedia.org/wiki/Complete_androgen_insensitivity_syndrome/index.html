Complete androgen insensitivity syndrome (CAIS) is a condition that results in the complete inability of the body's cells to respond to androgens. People with CAIS have a typically female external appearance despite having a 46,XY karyotype, because the body cannot use testosterone or dihydrotestosterone to masculinise the developing fetus. CAIS is the most severe form of androgen insensitivity syndrome, which also includes partial (PAIS) and mild (MAIS) forms.

Signs and symptoms

Individuals with CAIS are born with female external genitalia and are usually raised as girls. Internal female reproductive structures such as a uterus and fallopian tubes are absent, because anti-Müllerian hormone produced by the testes still functions normally. The vagina is typically shorter than usual and ends blindly. At puberty, breast development occurs normally owing to aromatisation of androgens into oestrogens, but pubic and axillary hair is sparse or absent, and menstruation does not occur (primary amenorrhoea). Undescended testes may be located in the abdomen, the inguinal canals, or the labia majora, and may present in infancy as inguinal hernias.

Genetics

CAIS is caused by mutations in the AR gene on the X chromosome (Xq11–12), which encodes the androgen receptor. It is inherited in an X-linked recessive pattern: a carrier mother has a 50% chance of passing the variant to each XY child, who will then have CAIS. Hundreds of distinct mutations have been catalogued.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis may occur in infancy when testes are found during hernia repair, or in adolescence during investigation of primary amenorrhoea. Karyotyping shows 46,XY, hormone studies show testosterone in or above the typical male range with elevated luteinising hormone, and imaging shows absent Müllerian structures.

Management

Management is multidisciplinary. Decisions about gonadectomy (removal of the testes) involve balancing a small risk of gonadal tumours — generally low before adulthood — against the benefits of natural puberty from the testes' hormone production; many guidelines now support deferring gonadectomy until after puberty with monitoring. After gonadectomy, oestrogen replacement therapy is given to maintain bone health. Vaginal dilation is the usual first-line treatment for vaginal hypoplasia. Psychological support and disclosure counselling are considered essential parts of care.

History

The condition was described in detail in the 1950s, notably by gynaecologist John Morris in 1953, who called it "testicular feminization". Modern terminology has moved away from that label. CAIS is one of several differences of sex development (DSD).
