Alphaherpesvirinae is a subfamily of the virus family Orthoherpesviridae (formerly Herpesviridae), distinguished from the other subfamilies (Betaherpesvirinae and Gammaherpesvirinae) by a faster reproductive cycle, rapid spread in cell culture, efficient destruction of infected cells, and, most characteristically, the ability to establish latent infections primarily in sensory nerve ganglia. Its members infect humans and a wide range of other mammals and birds.

Genera and important species

  • Simplexvirus — includes human alphaherpesvirus 1 (herpes simplex virus 1, HSV-1) and human alphaherpesvirus 2 (HSV-2), which cause oral and genital herpes, herpetic keratitis and, rarely, encephalitis.
  • Varicellovirus — includes human alphaherpesvirus 3 (varicella-zoster virus, VZV), the cause of chickenpox and shingles; also suid alphaherpesvirus 1 (pseudorabies virus), bovine alphaherpesvirus 1, and equid alphaherpesviruses.
  • Mardivirus — includes gallid alphaherpesvirus 2 (Marek's disease virus), an oncogenic virus of chickens.
  • Iltovirus — includes gallid alphaherpesvirus 1, the cause of infectious laryngotracheitis in poultry.
  • Scutavirus — includes viruses of turtles and tortoises.

Biology

Alphaherpesviruses are enveloped, double-stranded DNA viruses with relatively short replication cycles. After primary infection of mucosal epithelium, the virus is transported retrogradely along sensory axons to neuronal cell bodies in trigeminal or dorsal root ganglia, where the genome persists in a latent state. Periodic reactivation sends virus back down the axon to the periphery, producing recurrent lesions such as cold sores or shingles and enabling transmission to new hosts.

The neurotropism of the group has made attenuated alphaherpesviruses useful as neuronal tracers in neuroscience research (for example pseudorabies virus) and as vectors for gene delivery.

Clinical significance

In humans, HSV-1, HSV-2 and VZV are among the most prevalent viral infections worldwide. Antiviral nucleoside analogues such as aciclovir exploit viral thymidine kinase and are the mainstay of treatment. Vaccines exist for varicella-zoster virus (against chickenpox and shingles); as of the early 2020s no licensed vaccine prevented herpes simplex infection.
