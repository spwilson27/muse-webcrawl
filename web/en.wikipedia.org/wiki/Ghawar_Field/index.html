Ghawar is an oil field located in Al-Ahsa Governorate, in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia. Measuring roughly 280 km by 30 km, it is by far the largest conventional oil field in the world, and has accounted historically for more than half of Saudi Arabia's cumulative oil production. Discovered in 1948 and brought on stream in 1951, Ghawar has produced on the order of 70–100 billion barrels of oil over its lifetime — more than any other field — and for decades its output alone exceeded that of most oil-producing nations.

Geology

The field is an elongated anticline over a basement fault block. The principal reservoir is the Upper Jurassic Arab-D limestone, an exceptionally porous and permeable carbonate, sealed by anhydrite. The field is conventionally divided from north to south into the 'Ain Dar, Shedgum, Uthmaniyah, Hawiyah, and Haradh areas.

Production and significance

Ghawar's production is maintained by massive peripheral water injection — on the order of millions of barrels of treated seawater per day. Peak output of about 5.7 million barrels per day was reached in 1981. Because Saudi reserve figures were long treated as state secrets, Ghawar was the focus of intense speculation, notably Matthew Simmons's 2005 book Twilight in the Desert, which argued the field was nearing decline. Saudi Aramco's 2019 bond prospectus and IPO disclosures gave the first modern audited glimpse: Ghawar's maximum sustainable capacity was stated at about 3.8 million barrels per day, with remaining reserves of about 58 billion barrels — still the world's largest field, but producing at a lower rate than many analysts had assumed.
