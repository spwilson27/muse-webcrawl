Immigration Act of 1990

Enacted101st United States Congress
Signed29 November 1990, by President George H. W. Bush
Public lawPub. L. 101-649

The Immigration Act of 1990 is a United States federal law, signed by President George H. W. Bush on 29 November 1990, that significantly increased legal immigration to the United States. It raised the overall annual ceiling for immigration, reformed employment-based and family-based visa categories, and created the Diversity Immigrant Visa program (the "green card lottery").

Provisions

The Act increased the annual cap on worldwide immigration to 700,000 for an initial period (later 675,000). It restructured employment-based immigration into five preference categories (EB-1 through EB-5), including the EB-5 immigrant investor program. It created the diversity visa lottery making up to 55,000 visas available annually to natives of countries with low rates of immigration to the United States. The Act also created Temporary Protected Status (TPS) for nationals of countries experiencing armed conflict or natural disaster, and revised deportation and exclusion grounds, repealing ideological exclusion provisions dating from the McCarthy era.

Background

The law was the most significant overhaul of U.S. immigration law since the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 and the first major increase in legal immigration levels since the early twentieth century. Senator Ted Kennedy was a principal sponsor.

Effects

Legal immigration rose substantially in the 1990s, and the diversity lottery and expanded employment categories reshaped the sources and composition of immigration to the United States.
