Optional stopping theorem

Optional stopping theorem
FieldProbability theory
SubjectMartingales, stopping times
Key figureJoseph L. Doob

In probability theory, the optional stopping theorem (or optional sampling theorem) states that, under certain conditions, the expected value of a martingale at a stopping time is equal to its initial expected value. Since martingales can be used to model the fortune of a gambler participating in a fair game, the optional stopping theorem says that, on average, nothing can be gained by stopping play based on the information obtainable so far — that is, a strategy of betting that decides when to quit using only past outcomes cannot beat a fair game.

Statement

Let X = (Xt)t∈ℕ be a martingale with respect to a filtration, and let T be a stopping time. The theorem applies under any of the following conditions:

  • (a) The stopping time T is bounded almost surely (there is a constant c with T ≤ c almost surely);
  • (b) The expected stopping time is finite and the conditional expected absolute increments of X are bounded;
  • (c) The stopped process is dominated by an integrable random variable.

In each case: E[XT] = E[X0].

Doob's optional sampling theorem generalises the result from stopping times to totally ordered index sets and from martingales sampled at a stopping time to a martingale sampled at two comparable stopping times.

Applications

The theorem formalises why no betting system — such as the martingale doubling strategy — can turn a fair or unfavourable game into a winning one when stakes and time are bounded. In the classic counterexample, a gambler doubles stakes until the first win; the stopping time is finite almost surely but has infinite expectation and unbounded losses, so the conditions fail and the theorem does not apply. The result is also used in proofs of Wald's equation, in the analysis of random walks (showing, for instance, exit probabilities for biased intervals), and in the theory of sequential statistical tests.
