One-point compactification

The plane plus a point at infinity yields the Riemann sphere

In topology, the one-point compactification is a construction that adds a single extra point — a "point at infinity" — to a non-compact topological space, producing a compact space. It is also called the Alexandroff compactification, after the mathematician Pavel Alexandroff, who introduced it in 1924.

Definition

Let X be a non-compact, locally compact Hausdorff space. Form the set X* = X ∪ {∞}, where ∞ is a new point. Declare the open sets of X* to be: the open sets of X, together with sets of the form (X \ K) ∪ {∞}, where K ranges over the compact subsets of X. In other words, neighbourhoods of the point at infinity are the complements of compact sets — going "far enough out" in X means entering a neighbourhood of ∞. With this topology, X* is compact and Hausdorff, and X sits inside it as a dense open subspace.

Examples

  • The one-point compactification of the real line ℝ is a circle: the two ends of the line meet at the single point at infinity.
  • The one-point compactification of the plane ℝ² is the 2-sphere — realised geometrically by stereographic projection — giving the Riemann sphere of complex analysis when applied to the complex plane.
  • The one-point compactification of ℝⁿ is the n-sphere Sⁿ.
  • The one-point compactification of the natural numbers (with the discrete topology) is a convergent sequence together with its limit point.

Properties

The construction is functorial: a proper map between locally compact Hausdorff spaces extends uniquely to a continuous map between their one-point compactifications fixing ∞. The one-point compactification is the smallest possible compactification of X in the sense that any other compactification maps onto it. A continuous real-valued function on X extends continuously to X* precisely when it has a limit "at infinity." The construction fails to be Hausdorff if X is not locally compact, which is why the hypotheses are essential.
