Human cannibalism
DefinitionConsumption of human flesh by human beings
FormsEndocannibalism (funerary), exocannibalism (warfare), survival, medicinal, pathological
Health riskPrion diseases (e.g. kuru)

Human cannibalism is the act or practice of humans eating the flesh or internal organs of other human beings. A person who practices cannibalism is called a cannibal. The term derives from Caníbales, the Spanish name for the Carib people of the West Indies, whom Columbus's contemporaries accused of eating human flesh.

Anthropologists distinguish endocannibalism, eating members of one's own group (often funerary), from exocannibalism, eating outsiders (often in warfare), along with survival cannibalism in famine or disaster, medicinal cannibalism, and rare pathological cases.

Prehistory and history

Cut-marked and marrow-split human bones at sites such as Gran Dolina (Spain, ~800,000 years ago) and Gough's Cave (England, ~15,000 years ago, where skulls were shaped into cups) show cannibalism reaches deep into the human past. Documented or probable historic practices include funerary endocannibalism among the Fore people of Papua New Guinea — which spread the prion disease kuru — ritual consumption in Aztec Mexico the scale of which is debated, mortuary practices among groups in Amazonia such as the Wari', and consumption of ashes of the dead among the Yanomami. Colonial-era accusations were also routinely weaponised against indigenous peoples, making many early reports unreliable.

Survival cannibalism

Well-documented survival cases include the Donner Party (1846–47), the Franklin expedition (whose crew's remains show cut marks), the wreck of the Méduse (1816) and Essex (1820), the Uruguayan rugby team crash in the Andes (1972), and famines including the Holodomor and the siege of Leningrad. In English law, the case of the yacht Mignonette — R v Dudley and Stephens (1884) — established that necessity is no defence to murder, after the defendants killed and ate the cabin boy.

Medicine and modern cases

Medicinal cannibalism was widespread in early-modern Europe, where powdered mummy, human fat and blood were sold as pharmaceuticals. Modern documented cases are overwhelmingly connected with homicide and mental illness rather than custom. Cannibalism is not a separate offence in most legal systems; perpetrators are prosecuted for murder, desecration or related crimes — the 2001 Rotenburg case of Armin Meiwes in Germany prompted legal and ethical debate on consent. Taboos against the practice are near-universal in contemporary societies, and human flesh consumption carries severe disease risks, including prion infections.
