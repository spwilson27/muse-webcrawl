Father Brown

Father J. Brown is a fictional Roman Catholic priest and amateur detective created by English writer G. K. Chesterton. He appears in 53 short stories published between 1910 and 1936, beginning with "The Blue Cross" in the collection The Innocence of Father Brown (1911). The stories are generally regarded as classics of detective fiction, notable for pairing ingenious plots with philosophical and theological reflection.

Character

Father Brown is a short, stout, unassuming parish priest with a round face, large glasses and a shabby umbrella, modelled in part on Father John O'Connor, a priest Chesterton knew in Yorkshire. His appearance leads criminals to underestimate him. His method of detection is intuitive rather than forensic: he solves crimes by identifying with the criminal's state of mind, reasoning that his training as a priest — hearing confessions and studying human evil — has taught him how such acts are conceived. He often insists that his secret is simply that he has committed the crimes himself, in imagination.

The stories

The stories appeared in five collections: The Innocence of Father Brown (1911), The Wisdom of Father Brown (1914), The Incredulity of Father Brown (1926), The Secret of Father Brown (1927) and The Scandal of Father Brown (1935). Recurring figures include the reformed master criminal Flambeau, who begins as Brown's opponent and later becomes his friend and assistant, and various policemen who rely on the priest's insight. Well-known stories include "The Blue Cross", "The Secret Garden", "The Queer Feet", "The Flying Stars" and "The Hammer of God".

Adaptations

Father Brown has been portrayed on screen many times. Alec Guinness played him in the 1954 film Father Brown (released in the US as The Detective). Kenneth More starred in a 1974 television series, and Mark Williams has played the priest in the BBC series Father Brown (2013–present), set in the fictional Cotswold village of Kembleford in the 1950s.

Influence

Chesterton's priest-detective influenced later writers of detective fiction, and the stories are frequently cited for demonstrating that rational explanation and religious faith need not be opposed: Brown repeatedly remarks that reason and faith are not enemies, and that his reasoning is the very thing that makes him believe.
