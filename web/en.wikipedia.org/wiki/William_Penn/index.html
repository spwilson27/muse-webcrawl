William Penn

William Penn (14 October 1644 – 30 July 1718) was an English writer, religious thinker and influential Quaker who founded the Province of Pennsylvania in British North America. An advocate of democracy and religious freedom, he negotiated peace with the Lenape (Delaware) people and designed the colony's capital, Philadelphia, as a planned city.

Early life and Quakerism

Penn was the son of Admiral Sir William Penn. Expelled from Oxford for his nonconformist religious views, he travelled in France and Ireland and in 1667 converted to Quakerism, whose adherents were persecuted in England. He was imprisoned several times for his beliefs and preaching; his trial in 1670 (the Penn–Mead trial) became a landmark in establishing the independence of juries. His writings, including No Cross, No Crown (1669), made him a leading Quaker thinker.

Founding Pennsylvania

In 1681, King Charles II granted Penn a large tract of land west of the Delaware River in payment of a debt owed to Penn's father. Penn named the colony Pennsylvania ("Penn's Woods") and drafted a Frame of Government guaranteeing religious toleration, representative assembly and fair trials — radical provisions for the time. He arrived in 1682, founded Philadelphia on a grid plan, and made a treaty of friendship with the Lenape chief Tamanend at Shackamaxon, an event Voltaire later celebrated as the only treaty "never sworn to and never broken".

Later life

Penn returned to England in 1684, was caught up in the politics of the Glorious Revolution, and made a second visit to Pennsylvania in 1699–1701, issuing the Charter of Privileges of 1701. Financial troubles and a lawsuit with his steward led to a spell in debtors' prison. He died in 1718 at Ruscombe, Berkshire. Pennsylvania's democratic constitution later influenced the framers of the United States Constitution.
