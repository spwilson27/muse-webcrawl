Neopaganism

Modern paganism, also known as contemporary paganism and neopaganism, is a collective term for new religious movements influenced by or derived from the various historical pagan beliefs of pre-modern peoples. Although they share similarities, contemporary pagan religious movements are diverse, and as a result do not share a single set of beliefs, practices, or texts.

History

Neopagan movements emerged in the mid-twentieth century. Wicca, founded in England in the 1950s by Gerald Gardner and others, became the largest and most influential; other streams include Heathenry (reviving Germanic traditions), Druidry, Hellenism, Kemetism and various forms of eclectic, goddess-centred and nature-based spirituality. The 1970s environmental and feminist movements broadened their appeal.

Beliefs and practices

Many neopagans celebrate seasonal festivals tied to solstices and equinoxes (often grouped as the Wheel of the Year), practise ritual magic, honour multiple deities or a divine feminine and masculine polarity, and regard nature as sacred. Adherents number perhaps over a million worldwide, concentrated in North America and Europe.
