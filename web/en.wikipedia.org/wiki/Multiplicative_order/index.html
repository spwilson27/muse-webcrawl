Multiplicative order

In number theory, given an integer a and a positive integer n with gcd(a, n) = 1, the multiplicative order of a modulo n is the smallest positive integer k such that

ak ≡ 1 (mod n).

The order of a modulo n is often written ordn(a). It is a fundamental concept in modular arithmetic and in the study of cyclic groups, and it plays a central role in cryptography, where the difficulty of recovering the order is related to the difficulty of the discrete logarithm problem.

Properties

By Euler's theorem, aφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n), where φ is Euler's totient function. Consequently, the multiplicative order of a modulo n always divides φ(n). More generally, the order divides the value of the Carmichael function λ(n), the exponent of the multiplicative group of integers modulo n.

If the order of a modulo n equals φ(n), then a is called a primitive root modulo n; such an element generates the whole multiplicative group (Z/nZ)×, which is then cyclic of maximal order.

Example

The powers of 4 modulo 7 are 4, 2, 1, … Since 43 = 64 ≡ 1 (mod 7) and no smaller positive exponent works, the multiplicative order of 4 modulo 7 is 3.

Applications

Multiplicative orders appear in the analysis of the period of repeating decimals (the period of 1/p is the order of 10 modulo p), in Shor's algorithm for quantum factoring (which is based on order finding), and in the design of pseudorandom number generators.
