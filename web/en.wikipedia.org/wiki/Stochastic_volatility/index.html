Stochastic volatility

Stochastic volatility (SV) models are used in quantitative finance to model the fact that the volatility of a financial instrument is not constant, as assumed in the Black–Scholes model, but varies through time as a random process itself.

In a basic SV model, the asset price follows a diffusion in which the instantaneous variance follows its own stochastic process, often a mean-reverting process such as the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. The Heston model (1993) is one of the most popular: in it, variance follows a CIR-type square-root process correlated with the asset price process, and it admits a semi-analytic solution for European option prices using characteristic functions. Other well-known models include the Hull–White, Stein–Stein and SABR models.

Stochastic volatility models are used to fit the volatility smile or skew observed in options markets — the pattern by which implied volatility varies with strike and maturity — and for risk management and hedging. Because volatility is not directly observable, calibration typically uses filtering or Bayesian methods such as Markov chain Monte Carlo.
