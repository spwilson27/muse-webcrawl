Musique concrète

Musique concrète (French for "concrete music") is a type of electroacoustic music in which composers work directly with recorded sound — from the environment, instruments, or the voice — rather than with abstract musical notation. Sounds are recorded, then manipulated by editing, splicing, reversing, changing playback speed, and layering, and finally assembled into a composition fixed on tape or in digital form.

Origins

The genre was founded by the French composer and engineer Pierre Schaeffer, who began experiments at the Studio d'Essai of French radio in 1948. His first study, Étude aux chemins de fer ("Railway Study"), was assembled from recordings of trains. Schaeffer coined the term musique concrète to contrast this "concrete" approach, which starts from real sounds, with conventional "abstract" composition, which starts from a score. His collaborator Pierre Henry produced landmark works such as Symphonie pour un homme seul (1950).

Techniques and theory

Schaeffer developed the concept of the objet sonore (sound object) and proposed "reduced listening" — attending to the qualities of a sound divorced from its source. Core techniques included tape loops, reversed sounds, pitch transposition by speed change, filtering, and montage. In 1951 the Groupe de Recherche de Musique Concrète (GRMC) was founded in Paris, attracting composers such as Luc Ferrari and Iannis Xenakis, and standing in creative rivalry with the purely electronic elektronische Musik of the Cologne studio.

Legacy

Musique concrète techniques underlie modern sampling, sound design, and much electronic and popular music, from the Beatles' tape experiments on "Tomorrow Never Knows" to hip-hop production and contemporary field-recording composition.
