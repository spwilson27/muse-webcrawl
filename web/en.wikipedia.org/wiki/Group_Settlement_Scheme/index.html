Group Settlement Scheme

The Group Settlement Scheme was an assisted migration and agricultural settlement program in Western Australia during the 1920s. Initiated under Premier James Mitchell, the scheme aimed to settle British migrants on small dairy farms in the high-rainfall forests of the state's South West, thereby increasing agricultural production, populating the region, and reducing Western Australia's reliance on imported dairy products.

Operation

Under the scheme, settlers were organised into groups of about twenty families. Each group worked communally to clear a block of heavily timbered land, receiving a small sustenance wage, before the land was divided into individual farms of roughly 100 to 160 acres, which the settlers then occupied on credit terms. The government provided stock, equipment, and advances, to be repaid over many years. Around 6,000 people were settled under the scheme, and new towns and districts including Margaret River, Manjimup, Pemberton, Walpole, and Denmark grew around group settlements.

Difficulties

The scheme is generally regarded as a hardship-laden failure for many participants. Settlers, often urban workers with no farming experience, faced the enormous labour of clearing karri and jarrah forest, poor soils, isolation, falling dairy prices, and heavy debt. Many walked off their blocks. A royal commission examined the scheme in 1924–25, and it was restructured and wound back, with some groups disbanded and some settlers relocated to better land.

Legacy

Despite its difficulties, the Group Settlement Scheme permanently reshaped the South West, founding communities and a dairy industry that persist today. The preserved Group Settlement farm buildings and the experiences of the "groupies", as the settlers were known, remain an important part of Western Australian social history. A related scheme, the soldier settlement of the same era, placed returned servicemen on the land under similar arrangements.
