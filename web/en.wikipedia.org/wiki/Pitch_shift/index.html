Pitch shift

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

TypeAudio effect
Related effectsTime stretching, harmonizer, octave effect

Pitch shifting is a sound recording technique in which the original pitch of a sound is raised or lowered. Effects units that raise or lower pitch by a pre-designated musical interval (transposition) are called pitch shifters. If the pitch is altered without changing the duration (tempo), the technique should be used with time stretching.

Pitch and time compression/expansion

The simplest way to change the pitch of a recording is to change the playback speed. Playing a vinyl record or tape faster raises the pitch but also shortens the duration, and slowing it down lowers the pitch while lengthening the recording. Modern digital methods decouple pitch from tempo, so that a voice or instrument can be transposed while keeping its original timing.

Methods

Common approaches include resampling combined with time stretching, the phase vocoder, and time-domain granular methods that slice the signal into small overlapping grains and reassemble them at a different rate. The Sola and PSOLA algorithms are widely used for speech. Real-time pitch shifters are built into guitar effects pedals, harmonizers, and digital audio workstations; auto-tune software applies pitch correction, a related process, to vocals.

Uses

Pitch shifting is used to correct or embellish performances, to create harmonies, to thicken sounds through slight detuning, for octave effects on guitar and bass, and in film and voice production to characterise voices. Extreme downward shifting is a stock technique for monster voices; upward shifting produces the "chipmunk" effect familiar from novelty records.

History

Early experiments relied on variable-speed tape machines and rotating-head devices. The Eventide H910 Harmonizer of 1975 was among the first commercially successful digital pitch shifters, and pitch manipulation is now a standard, inexpensive digital audio effect.
