Hematopoiesis (from Greek αἷμα, "blood" and ποιεῖν "to make"; also spelled haematopoiesis in British English) is the formation of blood cellular components. All cellular blood components are derived from haematopoietic stem cells. In a healthy adult human, roughly ten billion to a hundred billion new blood cells are produced each day in order to maintain steady state levels in the peripheral circulation.

Sites of hematopoiesis

In developing embryos, blood formation occurs in aggregates of blood cells in the yolk sac, called blood islands. As development progresses, blood formation occurs in the spleen, liver and lymph nodes. When bone marrow develops, it eventually assumes the task of forming most of the blood cells for the entire organism. However, maturation, activation, and some proliferation of lymphoid cells occurs in the spleen, thymus, and lymph nodes. In children, haematopoiesis occurs in the marrow of the long bones such as the femur and tibia. In adults, it occurs mainly in the pelvis, cranium, vertebrae, and sternum.

In some cases, the liver, thymus, and spleen may resume their haematopoietic function if necessary. This is called extramedullary haematopoiesis. It may cause these organs to increase in size substantially. During foetal development, since bones and thus the bone marrow develop later, the liver functions as the main haematopoietic organ.

Stem cells

Haematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) reside in the medulla of the bone (bone marrow) and have the unique ability to give rise to all of the different mature blood cell types and tissues. HSCs are self-renewing cells: when they differentiate, at least some of their daughter cells remain as HSCs, so the pool of stem cells is not depleted. This phenomenon is called asymmetric division. The other daughters of HSCs (myeloid and lymphoid progenitor cells) can follow any of the other differentiation pathways that lead to the production of one or more specific types of blood cells, but cannot renew themselves.

Lineages

Traditionally, blood-cell production is divided into two major lineages arising from a common hematopoietic stem cell:

  • Myelopoiesis — the formation of myeloid cells, including erythrocytes (red blood cells), megakaryocytes (which produce platelets), monocytes and macrophages, neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, and mast cells.
  • Lymphopoiesis — the formation of lymphocytes, including T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells.

Erythropoiesis (red cell production) is regulated principally by the hormone erythropoietin, produced by the kidneys in response to low blood oxygen levels, while platelet production is regulated by thrombopoietin. Colony-stimulating factors guide the production of white blood cells.

Clinical significance

Disorders of haematopoiesis include aplastic anaemia, in which the bone marrow fails to produce sufficient cells; myelodysplastic syndromes; and the leukaemias, in which uncontrolled proliferation of abnormal white-cell precursors crowds out normal blood formation. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (bone marrow transplantation) is used to treat many of these conditions, as well as certain immune deficiencies and genetic disorders of blood cells such as thalassaemia and sickle cell disease.
