The categorical imperative is the central concept of Immanuel Kant's moral philosophy: an unconditional moral obligation that applies to all rational beings regardless of their desires or interests. It contrasts with hypothetical imperatives, which command actions as means to chosen ends ('if you want X, do Y').

Formulations

Kant gives several formulations he takes to be equivalent. The universal-law formula: 'Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it become a universal law.' The humanity formula: 'Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means to an end, but always at the same time as an end.' A third formula invokes the idea of a 'kingdom of ends', a community of rational agents legislating universal law.

The imperative is the foundation of Kantian deontology and a major influence on later ethics and political philosophy, including John Rawls. Critics from Hegel onward have charged that the universal-law test is empty or generates false positives and negatives; defenders reply that it captures the impartiality at the heart of morality.
