Edom

LocationSouthern Jordan and the Negev, east and south of the Dead Sea
PeriodLate Bronze Age – Roman period (as Idumea)
CapitalBozrah; later Petra (under the Nabataeans)
LanguageEdomite (a Canaanite language)

Edom (Hebrew: ʾĔḏōm, "red") was an ancient kingdom in Transjordan, in the region of modern southern Jordan and Israel, between the Dead Sea and the Gulf of Aqaba. Inhabited by the Edomites, it is frequently mentioned in the Hebrew Bible, which portrays its people as descendants of Esau, the brother of Jacob.

Geography and history

Edom's heartland was a rugged plateau of red sandstone east of the Arabah valley. The region was rich in copper, mined at sites such as Khirbat en-Nahas in the Wadi Faynan lowlands, which some archaeologists link to the biblical "King Solomon's mines." Egyptian sources mention the Shasu of Edom from the late Bronze Age.

By the eighth to sixth centuries BC Edom was a settled kingdom with towns including Bozrah, Teman and Sela, trading along the King's Highway and paying tribute at various times to Assyria and Babylon. After the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC, Edomites moved into southern Judah, which became known in Greek as Idumea.

Idumea and later history

The original Edomite heartland was taken over by the Nabataean Arabs, who built Petra. The Idumeans in the west were converted to Judaism by the Hasmonean ruler John Hyrcanus around 110 BC; the most famous Idumean was Herod the Great. After the Roman destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70, the Edomites disappear from history as a distinct people.

Religion

The Edomites worshipped the god Qos (Qaus), a national deity comparable to Israel's Yahweh, along with other Semitic gods. Hebrew prophets repeatedly denounced Edom, most fully in the Book of Obadiah.
