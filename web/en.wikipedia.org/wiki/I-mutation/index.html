I-mutation

I-mutation (also called i-umlaut, front mutation, palatal umlaut, or Germanic umlaut) is a type of sound change in which a back vowel is fronted or a front vowel is raised when the following syllable contains /i/, /iː/, or /j/. It is one of the most characteristic sound changes of the Germanic languages and left visible traces in modern English, German, Dutch, and the North Germanic languages.

History

The change operated in the older Germanic languages roughly between the 4th and 8th centuries CE. In Old English, a following i or j in the next syllable caused vowels to shift forward in the mouth: for example, pre-Old English *fōtiz ("feet") became fēt, contrasting with the singular *fōt > fōt ("foot"). Similar processes are reconstructed for Old High German, Old Norse, and Old Saxon.

Effects in English

Although the triggering syllables were later lost in most cases, i-mutation survives in a set of irregular English alternations:

  • Plurals: foot/feet, goose/geese, tooth/teeth, mouse/mice, man/men, woman/women
  • Derivational pairs: strong/strength, long/length, broad/breadth, deep/depth, foul/filth
  • Verbs: sit/set, lie/lay, fall/fell, drink/drench
  • Adjectives: old/elder, full/fill

In German

In German the change is called Umlaut and is marked orthographically with a diaeresis: a → ä, o → ö, u → ü, and au → äu. It remains grammatically productive in plurals (Mann/Männer), comparatives, diminutives (Haus/Häuschen), and the subjunctive.
