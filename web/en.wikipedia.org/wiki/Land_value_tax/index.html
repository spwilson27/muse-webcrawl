Land value tax

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A land value tax (LVT) is a levy on the value of land without regard to buildings, personal property, and other improvements. It differs from a standard property tax, which also taxes the value of structures and other improvements. Land value taxes are generally favoured by economists because, unlike taxes on income or sales, they do not distort economic behaviour: the supply of land is essentially fixed, so a tax on its value cannot reduce its supply.

Rationale

The value of a plot of land arises largely from its location — proximity to infrastructure, services, markets and community — rather than from anything the owner has done. Advocates argue that taxing this "unearned increment" is both fair and efficient. Because land cannot be hidden, moved abroad, or reduced in supply, the tax is difficult to avoid and creates no deadweight loss. It also discourages land speculation and vacant-land holding in city centres, encouraging owners to develop sites or sell them to those who will.

History

Ideas resembling a land tax appear in the work of the French Physiocrats (the impôt unique), Adam Smith, David Ricardo and John Stuart Mill. The modern movement stems from the American economist and social reformer Henry George, whose book Progress and Poverty (1879) proposed a "single tax" on land values to replace other taxes. Georgist movements spread widely in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, influencing policy in several countries.

Implementation

Forms of land value tax have been used in Denmark, Estonia, Lithuania, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore, and parts of Australia; in the United States, a number of Pennsylvania cities, most famously Pittsburgh, long operated a two-rate ("split-rate") property tax that taxed land more heavily than buildings. Assessment is the principal practical difficulty: valuers must separate the value of the land from the value of improvements, typically using comparable sales and residual methods. Critics raise concerns about liquidity for land-rich but cash-poor owners, transitional losses for recent purchasers, and the adequacy of revenue from land alone.
