Adjacent-channel interference

FieldRadio engineering, telecommunications
CausesImperfect filtering, out-of-band emissions, intermodulation
MitigationGuard bands, filtering, power control, channel planning

Adjacent-channel interference (ACI) is interference caused by extraneous power from a signal in an adjacent frequency channel. It arises from imperfect filtering in receivers and transmitters, which allows energy from one channel to leak into neighbouring channels, degrading the performance of communications systems that share a band.

Causes

ACI can result from inadequate filtering of unwanted modulation products in a transmitter (out-of-band emissions), from a receiver that cannot fully reject signals just outside its passband (poor adjacent-channel selectivity), and from nonlinearities producing intermodulation products that fall into adjacent channels. A strong nearby transmitter can swamp a weak desired signal two channels away, an effect sometimes described as the "near–far" problem.

Measurement

The adjacent channel power ratio (ACPR) quantifies how much power a transmitter leaks into adjacent channels. Regulators set emission masks and receiver standards to limit ACI, and network designers mitigate it with guard bands between channels, careful frequency planning, power control, and improved filtering.
