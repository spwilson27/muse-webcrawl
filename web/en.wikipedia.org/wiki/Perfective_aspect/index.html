The perfective aspect (abbreviated PFV) is a grammatical aspect that describes an action or event viewed as a complete whole — as a single, bounded occurrence without regard to its internal temporal structure. It contrasts with the imperfective aspect, which views a situation as ongoing, habitual or internally structured.

Meaning

A perfective verb form presents an event from the outside, as it were: its beginning and end are taken together as an indivisible point or unit. Because of this, perfective forms often imply completion, but completion is a consequence of the bounded viewpoint rather than the definition: what matters is that the situation is presented in its totality. In narrative, perfective forms typically carry the story line forward, while imperfectives supply background.

Expression

The perfective/imperfective distinction is central in the Slavic languages, where most verbs exist in perfective–imperfective pairs; perfectives are commonly formed by prefixation (Russian pisát' "to write" (impf.) → napisát' "to write (to completion)" (pf.)) or by suffix changes. Ancient Greek marked the distinction in its aorist versus present stems. Mandarin Chinese uses the particle le for a related perfective function. English has no dedicated perfective form, conveying the contrast periphrastically or by lexical choice.

Perfective vs. perfect

The perfective should not be confused with the perfect, which locates a prior event with current relevance ("has written"). Some traditions — particularly in Romance linguistics — use "perfective" of past tenses, but in aspectology the terms are distinct: an event can be perfective without being perfect, and vice versa.
