Antisthenes of Athens (Greek: Ἀντισθένης; c. 446 – c. 366 BC) was a Greek philosopher and a pupil of Socrates. He is regarded as the founder of Cynic philosophy, although some later accounts credit his pupil Diogenes of Sinope with founding the school. Antisthenes taught that virtue is sufficient for happiness and that it is shown through action rather than words.

Life

Antisthenes was born in Athens around 446 BC. His father was an Athenian, but his mother was said to be Thracian, a fact his enemies later used against him. He fought at the Battle of Tanagra and was a pupil of the sophist Gorgias before becoming a devoted follower of Socrates, whose discussions he walked forty stades from the Piraeus each day to attend.

After Socrates' death in 399 BC, Antisthenes set up his own school in the Cynosarges gymnasium outside Athens — a place associated with those of mixed Athenian and foreign parentage. From the name Cynosarges ("White Dog" or "Swift Dog") his followers were said to have taken the label "Cynics" (dog-like).

Philosophy

Antisthenes held that virtue is the only good and that it can be taught and, once acquired, never lost. Wealth, reputation, pleasure, and social convention he regarded as worthless or harmful. In deliberate opposition to Plato's theory of Forms, he argued that only individual things exist — famously remarking, "Plato, I see a horse, but I do not see horseness," to which Plato replied that Antisthenes had the eyes to see a horse but not the intellect to grasp horseness.

He advocated a return to nature and a life of rigorous self-sufficiency (autarkeia), practising what he preached by wearing a rough cloak and carrying only a staff and a wallet. He defined pleasure, paradoxically, as something to be avoided, saying he would rather go mad than feel pleasure.

Writings

According to Diogenes Laertius, Antisthenes wrote ten volumes of works, including dialogues such as the Heracles, the Cyrus, and works on education and interpretation of Homer. Only fragments survive, including two declamations, Ajax and Odysseus.

Legacy

Through Diogenes, Antisthenes' teachings shaped Cynicism and, through the Cynics' influence on Zeno of Citium, early Stoicism. His emphasis on virtue, self-sufficiency, and contempt for convention became central themes of both schools.
