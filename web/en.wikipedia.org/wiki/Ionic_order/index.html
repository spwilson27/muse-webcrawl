Ionic order

Ionic order
OriginIonia (western Anatolia), 6th century BC
Distinguishing featuresVolutes, fluted shaft, moulded base, egg-and-dart ornament

The Ionic order is one of the three orders of classical architecture, the others being the Doric and the Corinthian. It is most readily recognised by its capital, which is crowned with a pair of spiral scrolls called volutes. More slender and ornate than the Doric, the Ionic order was associated by ancient writers with feminine proportions, in contrast to the masculine Doric.

Anatomy

An Ionic column stands on a moulded base, unlike the Greek Doric which rests directly on the stylobate. The shaft is usually fluted with 24 flutes separated by flat fillets rather than sharp arrises. The capital comprises a cushion of mouldings — typically decorated with the egg-and-dart (ovolo) pattern — beneath the volutes, which scroll down to either side; above sits a shallow abacus. The entablature consists of an architrave divided into three horizontal bands (fasciae), a frieze which in Ionic may be continuous and sculptured, and a cornice with dentils (small tooth-like blocks).

A notorious design problem is the corner volute: because the capital's volutes form a front face, the capital at the corner of a building must be adapted, with the volutes angled out diagonally at 45 degrees. The Roman writer Vitruvius describes the order and its proportions, and later architects such as Scamozzi systematised solutions to the corner problem.

History

The Ionic order originated in the mid-6th century BC in Ionia, the Greek-settled coast of western Anatolia (modern Turkey), where its early masterpieces included the great temples of Artemis at Ephesus and of Hera at Samos. It may have had antecedents in Near Eastern and Aeolic capitals. In mainland Greece the Ionic order was used for the Temple of Athena Nike and the Erechtheion on the Athenian Acropolis (where Ionic caryatids support the south porch) and for interiors of Doric temples such as the Propylaea and the temple at Bassae.

The Romans adopted the order, placing it in their theoretical hierarchy between Doric and Corinthian in superimposed colonnades such as the Colosseum. The order was revived in Renaissance architecture after the publication of treatises by Alberti, Serlio, Palladio and Vignola codified its proportions. It was widely used in the Greek Revival of the 18th and 19th centuries for museums, libraries, universities and public buildings, and Ionic columns remain a staple of neoclassical and civic design.
