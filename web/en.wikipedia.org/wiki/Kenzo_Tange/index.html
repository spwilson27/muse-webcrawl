Kenzo Tange

Yoyogi National Gymnasium (1964), Tange's masterpiece

Kenzō Tange (丹下 健三, 4 September 1913 – 22 March 2005) was a Japanese architect and winner of the 1987 Pritzker Architecture Prize. One of the most significant architects of the 20th century, he combined traditional Japanese styles with modernism and was a leading figure of the Metabolist movement, designing major buildings on five continents.

Early career

Tange was born in Sakai and raised in Imabari, Shikoku. Influenced by Le Corbusier, he studied architecture at Tokyo Imperial University and worked for Kunio Maekawa. His competition-winning plan for the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere memorial (1942) foreshadowed his later monumental style. After the war he led the reconstruction planning of Hiroshima.

Major works

The Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum (1955) established Tange's reputation, marrying modernist concrete construction to Japanese proportions and symbolism. His Yoyogi National Gymnasium, built for the 1964 Tokyo Olympics with its dramatic suspended roof, is considered a pinnacle of structural expressionism. Other works include St. Mary's Cathedral, Tokyo (1964), the Shizuoka Press and Broadcasting Centre (1967), the Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building (1991), and plans or buildings for Skopje, Bologna, Singapore, Nigeria (Abuja master plan elements) and Nepal (Lumbini).

Metabolism and urban planning

Tange mentored the Metabolists — Kisho Kurokawa, Kiyonori Kikutake and Fumihiko Maki among them — and his 1960 Plan for Tokyo, proposing a linear city across Tokyo Bay, remains a landmark of megastructure thinking. His teaching at the University of Tokyo shaped generations of Japanese architects through the "Tange Lab".

Death

Tange died in Tokyo on 22 March 2005, aged 91. His practice continues as Tange Associates under his son Paul Noritaka Tange.
