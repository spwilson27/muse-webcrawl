Hellenistic art

Hellenistic art is the art of the Hellenistic period, generally taken to begin with the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BC and end with the conquest of the Greek world by Rome, conventionally dated to the Battle of Actium in 31 BC. It represents the final phase of ancient Greek art, characterised by increased naturalism, emotional expression, drama, and an interest in the individual, the exotic, and the everyday.

Characteristics

Where classical Greek art prized idealised balance and restraint, Hellenistic art embraced movement, pathos, and virtuoso technique. Sculptures such as the Laocoön and His Sons, the Winged Victory of Samothrace, and the Venus de Milo display swirling drapery, twisting bodies, and intense emotion. New subjects appeared: old age, drunkenness, children, grotesques, and people of many ethnicities encountered through Alexander's conquests.

Centres and patronage

Major centres included Pergamon, Alexandria, Rhodes, and Antioch. Ruling dynasties such as the Attalids of Pergamon commissioned monumental works — notably the Pergamon Altar, whose Gigantomachy frieze exemplifies the "Hellenistic baroque". Portable arts flourished too: terracotta Tanagra figurines, cameos, toreutics, and elaborate jewellery reached wide markets.

Influence

Hellenistic art strongly shaped Roman art, which copied and adapted Greek originals for villas and public spaces, and later inspired Renaissance and Baroque artists after famous pieces were excavated in Rome.
