Erectile dysfunction

Other namesImpotence
SpecialtyUrology, sexual medicine
Risk factorsCardiovascular disease, diabetes, smoking, age, certain medications
TreatmentLifestyle change, PDE5 inhibitors, vacuum devices, injections, prosthesis

Erectile dysfunction (ED), also known as impotence, is the inability to develop or maintain an erection of the penis during sexual activity. It is one of the most common male sexual health problems, and its prevalence increases with age; large surveys such as the Massachusetts Male Aging Study found some degree of ED in about half of men aged 40–70.

Causes

Erection requires coordinated vascular, neurological, hormonal and psychological function, so ED has many possible causes. Erection is fundamentally a blood-flow event: arterial inflow fills the corpora cavernosa while venous outflow is restricted. The most common organic causes are vascular — atherosclerosis, hypertension and diabetes damage penile blood vessels and endothelium — and ED is now recognized as an early warning sign of cardiovascular disease, often preceding coronary symptoms by several years. Other causes include neurological disease (multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury, pelvic surgery such as radical prostatectomy), hormonal disorders (hypogonadism, thyroid disease), medication side effects (antihypertensives, antidepressants), smoking, alcohol and recreational drugs, and psychological factors such as anxiety, depression and relationship stress, which may be primary or secondary.

Diagnosis

Evaluation centres on the medical, sexual and psychosocial history, physical examination, and blood tests including glucose or HbA1c, lipids and morning testosterone. Questionnaires such as the International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF) quantify severity. Identifying the underlying cause matters because ED can be the presenting sign of undiagnosed diabetes or cardiovascular disease.

Treatment

First steps target reversible causes: exercise, weight loss, smoking cessation and medication review can substantially improve function. The principal drug treatments are oral phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE5) inhibitors — sildenafil (Viagra), tadalafil, vardenafil and avanafil — which amplify the nitric-oxide–cGMP pathway and are effective in most men; they must not be combined with nitrates. Second-line options include vacuum erection devices, intraurethral or intracavernosal alprostadil, and psychosexual therapy. For refractory cases, inflatable penile prostheses offer high satisfaction. Testosterone replacement is appropriate when hypogonadism is confirmed.

History

Descriptions of impotence appear in ancient Egyptian and other early medical texts, and remedies — from glandular extracts to implants — proliferated in the early twentieth century. The modern era began with the demonstration of intracavernosal pharmacotherapy in the 1980s and the approval of sildenafil in 1998, which transformed both treatment and public discussion of the condition.
