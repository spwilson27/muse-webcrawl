Drag coefficient

Drag coefficient
Symbolcd, cx
TypeDimensionless quantity
Used inFluid dynamics, aerodynamics, automotive engineering

In fluid dynamics, the drag coefficient (commonly denoted cd) is a dimensionless quantity used to quantify the drag or resistance of an object in a fluid environment, such as air or water. It appears in the drag equation:

Fd = ½ ρ v² cd A

where Fd is the drag force, ρ is the density of the fluid, v is the speed of the object relative to the fluid, and A is a reference area (often the frontal area). A lower drag coefficient indicates that the object will incur less aerodynamic or hydrodynamic drag. The coefficient combines the effects of form drag, skin friction and, in some regimes, wave drag, and generally depends on the Reynolds number and, for high-speed flow, the Mach number.

Typical values

  • Streamlined body (airfoil-like): ~0.04
  • Modern passenger car: ~0.25–0.30
  • Sphere: ~0.47
  • Flat plate perpendicular to flow: ~1.28
  • Cube: ~1.05

For a sphere the drag coefficient is roughly constant over a wide range of Reynolds numbers but drops abruptly at the drag crisis, when the boundary layer becomes turbulent and the wake narrows — the effect exploited by the dimples on a golf ball.

Applications

Drag coefficients are measured in wind tunnels and computed with computational fluid dynamics. In the automotive industry, reducing cd improves fuel economy and electric-vehicle range, and manufacturers advertise figures such as 0.20 for the most aerodynamic production cars. Aircraft, ships, cyclists and even competitive swimsuits are likewise designed around minimising the coefficient.
