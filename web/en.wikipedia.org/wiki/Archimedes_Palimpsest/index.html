The Archimedes Palimpsest is a parchment codex containing a tenth-century copy of several works by the ancient Greek mathematician Archimedes, which was erased and overwritten in the thirteenth century with the text of a Christian prayer book. It is the oldest surviving manuscript of Archimedes' works in Greek and the unique source for two treatises, The Method of Mechanical Theorems and the Stomachion.

History

The underlying manuscript was copied in Constantinople around 975 AD. In 1229 the parchment leaves were unbound, scraped, and reused — folded and rebound with other recycled leaves — to make a euchologion, a prayer book for a monastery, probably in Jerusalem or the Mar Saba monastery.

The palimpsest resurfaced in Constantinople in the nineteenth century and was identified in 1906 by the Danish scholar Johan Ludvig Heiberg, who read much of the underwriting and recognised The Method. The book later disappeared during the upheavals of the early twentieth century, suffering damage from mould and from forged miniature paintings added to boost its value, before re-emerging in Paris. In October 1998 it was sold at Christie's in New York for about $2 million to an anonymous private collector (later reported to be Jeff Bezos), who placed it on deposit at the Walters Art Museum in Baltimore.

Recovery of the text

Between 1999 and 2008 a team of conservators, imaging scientists, and classicists disbound the fragile leaves and recovered the erased text using multispectral imaging and, for pages obscured by the forged paintings, X-ray fluorescence imaging at the Stanford Synchrotron Radiation Lightsource, which made the iron in the ancient ink fluoresce. The project also recovered speeches of the Athenian orator Hyperides and a commentary on Aristotle from other leaves of the same codex.

Contents

The palimpsest contains On the Equilibrium of Planes, On Spirals, Measurement of a Circle, On the Sphere and Cylinder, On Floating Bodies (whose only Greek text survives here), The Method of Mechanical Theorems, and the Stomachion, a combinatorial puzzle treatise. The Method revealed that Archimedes used mechanical reasoning with indivisibles and anticipated aspects of integral calculus.
