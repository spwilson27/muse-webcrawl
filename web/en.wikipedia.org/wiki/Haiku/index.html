Haiku

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A single brush-written haiku on a hanging scroll beside a sprig of pine, the calligraphy breathing in white space]

A haiku is a type of short-form poetry that originated in Japan, traditionally composed of seventeen on (sound units) in three phrases of 5, 7 and 5, containing a seasonal reference (kigo) and often a cutting word (kireji) that juxtaposes two images. It developed from the opening verse (hokku) of collaborative linked poetry, and was established as an independent form by Matsuo Bashō (1644–1694), its greatest master.

Bashō's frog-and-pond verse — old pond, a frog jumps, sound of water, in the usual rendering, paraphrased here from the many translations — is the world's most famous three lines, emblematic of haiku's method: perception before thought, the moment held without commentary.

The tradition

After Bashō, Yosa Buson brought a painter's eye and Kobayashi Issa a peasant's compassion (his haiku grieve for children, flies and fleas alike); Masaoka Shiki coined the name 'haiku' in the 1890s and modernised the form toward realism. In Japanese the count is of sounds, not syllables, and masters break the count deliberately — form as frame, not cage.

Haiku in the world

Exported in the early twentieth century (through translation and Imagism's debts), haiku became world poetry's most portable form — practised in dozens of languages, usually in freer counts, from Ezra Pound's two-line metro poem to the African-American haiku of Richard Wright and Sonia Sanchez. Its discipline of the observed instant fitted it to the age of the short message: it may now be the most-written poetic form on earth.
