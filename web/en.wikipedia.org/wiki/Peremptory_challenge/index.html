Peremptory challenge

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the law of jury selection in the United States and a few other jurisdictions, a peremptory challenge (or peremptory strike) is a dismissal of a prospective juror that a party may exercise without stating a reason and without judicial approval — unlike a challenge for cause, which requires shown bias and the judge's agreement. Allotted in limited numbers (varying by case type and jurisdiction), peremptories let parties shape the jury by intuition — and, historically, by stereotype.

The Batson limit and reform

Because unconstrained peremptories were used to exclude jurors by race, the Supreme Court held in Batson v. Kentucky (1986) that the Equal Protection Clause forbids race-based strikes, later extended to gender (J.E.B., 1994) and to strikes by defendants and in civil cases. The three-step Batson procedure (prima facie showing; race-neutral explanation; judicial finding on pretext) is widely criticised as easy to game, and enforcement failures (documented in cases such as Flowers v. Mississippi, 2019) fuel reform: Arizona abolished peremptories entirely in 2022, a first among US states, and other jurisdictions have adopted objective-observer standards (Washington's GR37) designed to catch implicit bias. England and Wales abolished peremptory challenge in 1988, relying on challenge for cause and judicial excusal.
