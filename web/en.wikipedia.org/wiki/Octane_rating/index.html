Octane rating

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An octane rating, or octane number, is a standard measure of a fuel's ability to withstand compression in an internal combustion engine without undergoing pre-ignition. The higher the octane number, the more compression the fuel can withstand before detonating. In broad terms, fuels with a higher octane rating are used in higher-compression gasoline engines, which may yield higher power. In contrast, fuels with lower octane numbers (but higher cetane numbers) are ideal for diesel engines, because diesel engines compress only air and then inject fuel, which ignites under the high temperature and pressure of compression.

The octane rating of plain gasoline can be increased by adding octane boosters, such as oxygenates or (historically) tetraethyllead. Using a fuel with a higher octane rating than the engine requires does not typically improve performance; it simply prevents knocking where lower-grade fuel would cause it.

Principles

Knocking

In a spark-ignition engine, the fuel–air mixture is ignited by the spark plug near the end of the compression stroke. If parts of the unburned mixture ignite spontaneously ahead of the flame front — because of heat and pressure — the resulting colliding pressure waves produce a metallic "pinging" or "knocking" sound (engine knocking), which can damage the engine. A fuel's resistance to this auto-ignition is what the octane number measures.

The reference scale

The scale is defined by two pure hydrocarbons:

  • Isooctane (2,2,4-trimethylpentane) is extremely resistant to knocking and is assigned a rating of 100.
  • n-Heptane knocks very readily and is assigned a rating of 0.

A fuel's octane number is the percentage by volume of isooctane in a mixture with n-heptane that matches the fuel's knock resistance in a standard test engine. Ratings can exceed 100 for fuels more resistant than pure isooctane (for example, ethanol has a research octane number around 108–109).

Measurement methods

Research Octane Number (RON)

The Research Octane Number is determined by running the fuel in a test engine (the Cooperative Fuel Research, or CFR, engine) at 600 rpm under controlled conditions and comparing its knock behaviour with isooctane/heptane blends. RON is the figure displayed on pumps in most of the world, including Europe, Australia and much of Asia.

Motor Octane Number (MON)

The Motor Octane Number is measured at 900 rpm with a preheated mixture and variable ignition timing, stressing the fuel more severely. MON is typically 8–12 points lower than RON for the same fuel.

Anti-Knock Index (AKI)

In the United States, Canada, Brazil and some other countries, the posted rating is the Anti-Knock Index, the average of the two:

AKI = (RON + MON) / 2

It is labelled on pumps as "(R+M)/2". Because of this averaging, a US "87" regular fuel corresponds roughly to 91–92 RON elsewhere, and US "premium" 91–93 AKI corresponds to about 95–98 RON.

Effects and use

Higher-performance engines with higher compression ratios or turbocharging require higher-octane fuel to avoid knock; modern engine computers use knock sensors to retard ignition timing when lower-octane fuel is used, at some cost in power and efficiency. The once-universal octane booster tetraethyllead ("leaded petrol") was phased out worldwide due to lead's toxicity, a process completed globally in 2021, and refiners now rely on refining processes (reforming, isomerisation, alkylation) and oxygenates such as ethanol or MTBE to reach the required ratings. Aviation gasoline (avgas) uses a separate lean/rich rating system and still contains lead in its common 100LL grade.
