Gallows

A gallows (or scaffold) is a frame or elevated structure, typically of wood, from which a rope with a noose is suspended for the execution of a person by hanging. Gallows have been used for capital punishment since ancient times and vary in form from a simple upright with a crossbeam to elaborate permanent structures with trapdoors.

Types

The simplest gallows consist of two uprights and a crossbeam, sometimes with a platform beneath. Permanent gallows in Britain, such as the "Triple Tree" at Tyburn in London, could execute many prisoners at once. Later gallows incorporated a trapdoor through which the condemned dropped — the "long drop" method, developed in the 19th century, calculated the length of the drop from the prisoner's weight so as to break the neck and cause rapid death rather than slow strangulation.

History

Hanging was a common method of execution across Europe, the Islamic world and elsewhere. Public executions at permanent gallows sites were major spectacles into the 19th century. In England, the Murder Act 1751 provided that the bodies of executed murderers could be dissected or hung in chains on gibbets after death as an additional deterrent. Gallows remain in use in the few jurisdictions that retain hanging as a method of execution.
