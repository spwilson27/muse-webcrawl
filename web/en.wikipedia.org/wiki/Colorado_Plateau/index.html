The Colorado Plateau is a physiographic and desert region of the Intermontane Plateaus, roughly centered on the Four Corners region of the southwestern United States. Covering about 337,000 km² (130,000 mi²) across southeastern Utah, northeastern Arizona, northwestern New Mexico, and southwestern Colorado, it is the largest plateau in North America, drained largely by the Colorado River and its tributaries, including the Green, San Juan, and Little Colorado rivers.

Geology

The plateau is a relatively coherent block of crust that was uplifted some 1.5–3 km during the Laramide orogeny and later tectonic episodes, while remaining comparatively undeformed internally — unlike the folded Rocky Mountains to the east and the extended Basin and Range to the west. Its strata, laid down from the Pennsylvanian through the Paleogene, are exposed in a staircase of cliffs and canyons, most famously the Grand Canyon, where the Colorado River has cut down through nearly two billion years of rock history. Differential erosion of these layers has produced the region's mesas, buttes, arches, hoodoos, and slot canyons.

Climate and ecology

Mostly high desert, the plateau receives modest precipitation, much of it as winter snow on its high plateaus and summer monsoon storms. Piñon–juniper woodland dominates mid-elevations, giving way to ponderosa pine forests on the highest surfaces (such as the Kaibab Plateau) and sagebrush and blackbrush scrub below.

Human history

The region was home to Ancestral Puebloan peoples, builders of cliff dwellings at Mesa Verde and Chaco Canyon's great houses; their descendants include the Hopi, Zuni, and Pueblo peoples, and the Navajo Nation — the largest Native American reservation in the U.S. — lies largely within the plateau. Uranium and coal mining, tourism, and water rights on the Colorado River system are central modern issues.
