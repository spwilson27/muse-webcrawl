A light machine gun (LMG) is a light-weight machine gun designed to be employed by an individual soldier, with or without an assistant, as an infantry support weapon. LMGs firing cartridges of the same caliber as the other riflemen of the same combat unit are often referred to as squad automatic weapons.

History

The Danish Madsen machine gun of 1902 was one of the first true light machine guns. During the First World War, designs such as the Lewis gun and the lightened Chauchat were issued to infantry squads to provide mobile automatic fire for troops advancing across no-man's-land. In the interwar and Second World War periods the type matured with guns such as the British Bren, the Czechoslovak ZB vz. 26, the Soviet DP-27, and the American Browning Automatic Rifle, which blurred the line between automatic rifle and LMG.

Characteristics

Light machine guns are typically equipped with a bipod, weigh roughly 7 to 12 kilograms, and are fed either from box or drum magazines or from disintegrating belts. Compared with medium and general-purpose machine guns mounted on tripods, they are optimised for portability and short bursts of suppressive fire from the bipod. Post-war practice split the role between belt-fed guns such as the FN Minimi (M249 SAW) and magazine-fed automatic-rifle derivatives such as the Soviet RPK.
