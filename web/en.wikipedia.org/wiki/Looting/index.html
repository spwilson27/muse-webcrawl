Looting

Looting is the act of stealing, or the taking of goods by force, in the midst of a military, political, or other social crisis, such as war, natural disasters (where law and civil enforcement are temporarily ineffective), or rioting. The proceeds of all these activities can be described as booty, loot, plunder, spoils, or pillage.

History

Plunder was a normal part of warfare in the ancient and medieval worlds: armies expected to live off the land and to be rewarded with the sack of a stormed city — as with the Roman sack of Jerusalem (70 AD), the Fourth Crusade's sack of Constantinople (1204), and the sack of Rome (1527). War trophies taken from defeated enemies were displayed in triumphs and temples.

Modern law

Pillage is now prohibited by international humanitarian law. The Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907 forbade the pillage of towns and the seizure of cultural property; the Fourth Geneva Convention (1949) and the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court make pillage a war crime. The 1954 Hague Convention for the Protection of Cultural Property extended protection to museums, monuments and archives.

Looting in disasters and unrest

Looting may follow hurricanes, earthquakes and floods when policing breaks down, though studies of disaster sociology often find actual looting rarer than media reports suggest. In riots and civil unrest, looting of businesses is a common form of property crime and a focus of debate about protest, policing and inequality.

Art and antiquities

The large-scale removal of cultural property — from Napoleon's confiscations, to Nazi art looting during the Second World War, to the looting of the Iraq Museum in 2003 and trafficking of antiquities during the Syrian civil war — has generated restitution claims and an illicit international market in looted antiquities.
