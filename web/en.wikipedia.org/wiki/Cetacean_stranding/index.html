Cetacean stranding

Cetacean stranding, commonly known as beaching, is a phenomenon in which whales and dolphins strand themselves on land, usually on a beach. Beached animals usually die from dehydration, from the collapse of their lungs under their own weight, or from drowning when high tide covers their blowholes. Individual strandings occur frequently, while mass strandings — involving whole pods, sometimes hundreds of animals — are rarer and concentrated in a few geographic hotspots.

Causes

No single cause explains all strandings. Proposed explanations include navigational errors in gently shelving, echolocation-confusing bays; animals following a sick or disoriented pod member ashore; disorientation from geomagnetic cues; noise disturbance, including naval sonar, which has been linked to strandings of beaked whales; disease and biotoxins; and pursuit of prey into shallow water. Many mass strandings involve highly social species, especially pilot whales, supporting the idea that strong social bonds lead healthy animals to follow stranded companions.

Mass strandings

Some locations experience repeated events: Farewell Spit in New Zealand, Cape Cod in Massachusetts, and the west coast of Tasmania, where in September 2020 about 470 long-finned pilot whales stranded at Macquarie Harbour, the largest mass stranding in Australian history. In October 2022 about 230 more pilot whales stranded on Tasmania's Ocean Beach.

Response

Trained responders stabilise stranded animals by keeping them upright, wet and shaded, and attempt refloating at high tide. Animals that cannot be saved are euthanised on welfare grounds. Strandings also provide scientists with valuable samples of otherwise inaccessible pelagic species.
