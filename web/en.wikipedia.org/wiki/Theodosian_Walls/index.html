Theodosian Walls

Theodosian Walls
Location: Istanbul, Turkey
Built: 5th century AD, under Theodosius II
Length: approximately 6.5 km (land walls)
Type: triple defensive system of moat, outer wall and inner wall
Part of: Historic Areas of Istanbul UNESCO World Heritage Site

The Theodosian Walls are the fortifications built during the fifth century AD to defend Constantinople, the capital of the Byzantine Empire, on its landward side. Stretching from the Golden Horn to the Sea of Marmara, the system of walls protected the city for a thousand years and was breached decisively only twice: by the Fourth Crusade in 1204 (from the sea walls) and by the Ottoman Turks under Mehmed II in 1453.

Construction

An earlier wall had been built under Constantine the Great, but as the city grew, the praetorian prefect Anthemius, regent for the young emperor Theodosius II, constructed a new line about 1.5 km further west, completed in the early fifth century. After an earthquake damaged the walls in 447, the prefect Cyrus (Constantine) rapidly rebuilt and strengthened them, adding the outer wall and the moat, reportedly completing the work in sixty days with the labour of the city's circus factions — just as Attila's Huns approached the Balkans.

Structure

The land walls form a triple defensive system. Outermost is a wide moat, about 20 metres across, which could be flooded. Behind it rises a low outer wall (proteichisma) with its own terrace and small towers. Finally stands the great inner wall, roughly 12 metres high and up to 5 metres thick, of alternating bands of brick and limestone rubble, reinforced by nearly two hundred towers placed at intervals of about 55 metres. Each successive line is higher than the one before, allowing defenders to fire over the heads of those in front.

The walls were pierced by a series of public and military gates, the most famous being the Golden Gate, a triumphal arch through which emperors entered in procession, later incorporated into the fortress of Yedikule.

Sieges and later history

The walls turned back a long succession of besiegers, including the Avars and Persians in 626, the Arabs in 674–678 and 717–718, Bulgars, Rus', and rebels. Their landward fortifications were never successfully stormed until 1453, when Ottoman cannon — among the largest yet built — pounded the section near the Lycus valley, and Mehmed II's army finally forced its way through after a siege of about seven weeks.

Under the Ottomans the walls were maintained for a time but eventually fell into disrepair; sections were damaged by earthquakes and by later road building. Substantial stretches survive today in Istanbul, with restored portions near the Golden Gate and along the line toward the Golden Horn, and the walls form part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing for the Historic Areas of Istanbul.
