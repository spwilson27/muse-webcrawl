Cumbric language

Cumbric
The Brittonic-speaking "Old North" (Yr Hen Ogledd)
RegionSouthern Scotland and north-west England (Cumbria)
EraEarly Middle Ages; extinct by about the 12th–13th century
FamilyIndo-European → Celtic → Brittonic
RelatedWelsh, Cornish, Breton, Pictish (possibly)
EvidencePlace names, a few words in Latin documents

Cumbric was a variety of the Common Brittonic language spoken during the Early Middle Ages in the Hen Ogledd or "Old North" — the region covering what is now southern Scotland and north-west England, including the kingdom of Strathclyde and the lands around modern Cumbria, whose name preserves the Brittonic word for "compatriots" (compare Welsh Cymru). Cumbric died out by around the 12th or 13th century as the region was absorbed into the Gaelic- and English-speaking kingdoms of Scotland and England.

Evidence

No text in Cumbric survives. Knowledge of the language rests on three sources: place names across the Old North (such as Glasgow, Carlisle, Penrith and Lanark), which show Brittonic elements like pen "head, top" and caer "fort"; three words preserved in the Latin Leges inter Brettos et Scottos, a legal text from the kingdom of Strathclyde, including galnes (blood-fine, compare Welsh galanas); and the traditional sheep-counting scores (yan, tan, tethera…) recorded in northern England into modern times, which resemble Welsh numerals.

Relationship to Welsh

Scholars debate whether Cumbric was a distinct language or a northern dialect of early Welsh. Both descend from Common Brittonic, and the surviving evidence is so scant that a firm distinction is difficult: some apparent differences, such as the retention of certain sounds in place names, may reflect the dates at which names were recorded rather than a genuine linguistic split. The bards of the Old North — Taliesin and Aneirin, authors of some of the earliest surviving Welsh poetry, including Y Gododdin — sang at Brittonic courts in this region.

Decline

The fall of the kingdom of Strathclyde in the 11th century removed Cumbric's political base. Gaelic spread from the north and west under the kingdom of Alba, while English (and later Scots) advanced from the south and east. Cumbric probably lingered longest in rural districts of Cumberland and the Clyde valley before disappearing entirely, leaving its traces in the landscape and in regional vocabulary.
