Freelancing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia · Redirected from "Freelancer". For other uses, see Freelance (disambiguation).

Freelancing is a form of self-employment in which a person — a freelancer — works for multiple clients on a per-job, per-project, or contract basis rather than as the employee of a single organisation. Common freelance fields include writing and journalism, graphic and web design, software development, photography, translation, consulting, marketing, and film and television production. The word originally described a medieval mercenary ("free lance") who offered his weapon to any lord; its modern occupational sense was popularised in the nineteenth century, notably in Walter Scott's Ivanhoe (1820).

The freelance economy

Freelancing has expanded with the growth of the internet, remote work, and online marketplaces such as Upwork, Fiverr, and Freelancer.com that match independent workers with clients worldwide. Surveys in the United States have estimated that tens of millions of Americans do some freelance work each year, contributing on the order of a trillion dollars to the economy. Freelancers typically set their own hours and rates, choose projects, and may work from home or co-working spaces, but they bear business risks employees do not: irregular income, finding their own clients, unpaid administrative work, and providing their own health insurance and retirement savings.

Legal status and debates

Freelancers are usually classified in law as independent contractors rather than employees, which excludes them from many labour protections (minimum wage, overtime, unemployment insurance, collective bargaining) but also gives them flexibility. The boundary is contested: gig-economy platforms have faced litigation and legislation — such as California's AB5 law (2019) and Proposition 22 (2020) — over whether workers they engage are misclassified employees. Tax treatment also differs: freelancers in the U.S. pay self-employment tax and receive Form 1099 rather than a W-2. Advocacy organisations such as the Freelancers Union campaign for portable benefits and prompt-payment laws, exemplified by New York City's "Freelance Isn't Free" Act (2017).
