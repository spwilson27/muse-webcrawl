Fermi liquid theory

Fermi liquid theory is a theoretical model of interacting fermions, developed by Lev Landau in 1956, that describes the normal state of most metals at sufficiently low temperatures. Despite strong interactions between the constituent particles, the system's low-energy excitations behave as weakly interacting quasiparticles in one-to-one correspondence with those of a free Fermi gas.

Key ideas

Quasiparticles carry the same charge, spin, and momentum as bare electrons but with a renormalized effective mass, and their interactions are captured by Landau parameters. The theory predicts, among other things, an electronic specific heat linear in temperature, temperature-independent Pauli susceptibility, and a resistivity varying as T². Liquid helium-3 is a classic neutral Fermi liquid.

Non-Fermi liquids

Systems that violate these predictions — including the normal state of high-temperature cuprate superconductors and materials near quantum critical points — are called non-Fermi liquids and are a major topic in condensed matter physics.
