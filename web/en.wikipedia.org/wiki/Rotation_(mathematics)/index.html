Rotation (mathematics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A rotation in the plane about a centre by angle θ

In mathematics, a rotation is a transformation of a space that preserves distances (an isometry) and orientation, and that keeps at least one point fixed. Intuitively it describes the motion of a rigid body turning about a fixed point in the plane, or about a fixed axis in three-dimensional space, without any change of shape or size.

Rotations in the plane

A rotation of the Euclidean plane is determined by its centre and its angle. Every point P is mapped to a point P′ such that the centre O, P and P′ satisfy OP = OP′ and the angle POP′ equals the given angle. In coordinates, a rotation about the origin through an angle θ (counterclockwise) is given by the rotation matrix

R(θ) = [cos θ   -sin θ;   sin θ   cos θ]

Plane rotations commute with one another, and together with the identity they form the special orthogonal group SO(2).

Rotations in three dimensions

By Euler's rotation theorem, any rotation in three dimensions can be described as a rotation by some angle about a single fixed axis through the origin. Sequences of rotations are commonly specified with Euler angles (such as yaw, pitch and roll), though this representation can suffer from gimbal lock. Axis–angle representation uses Rodrigues' rotation formula; rotation matrices belonging to SO(3) act on vectors, and unit quaternions provide an alternative, singularity-free parametrisation widely used in computer graphics and robotics.

Unlike in the plane, three-dimensional rotations do not generally commute: turning a book about two different axes in different orders gives different final orientations.

General properties

Rotations preserve lengths, angles and handedness (their matrices have determinant +1). In higher dimensions a rotation may fix a whole plane rather than a single axis. Improper rotations combine a rotation with a reflection and have determinant −1.
