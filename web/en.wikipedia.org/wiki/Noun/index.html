Noun

Part of speechWord class (lexical category)
Typical functionsSubject, object, head of a noun phrase
Common categoriesNumber, case, gender/class, definiteness
SubtypesCommon/proper, count/mass, concrete/abstract, collective

A noun is a word that represents a concrete or abstract thing, such as a person, place, object, quality or action, and that typically functions as the head of a noun phrase and as the subject or object of a clause. Along with verbs, nouns are one of the two word classes found in virtually all languages. The traditional school definition — "a person, place, thing or idea" — is semantic, but linguists identify nouns by their grammatical behaviour: where they occur, what affixes they take, and what words (articles, adjectives) accompany them.

Classification

Nouns are commonly divided into proper nouns, naming unique entities (London, Marie Curie) and written with a capital letter in English, and common nouns. Common nouns are further classified as count nouns (which can be pluralised and counted: three chairs) and mass nouns (water, information); as concrete (table) or abstract (freedom); and as collective nouns denoting groups (team, committee).

Morphology

In many languages nouns inflect for number (singular/plural, sometimes dual), grammatical case (nominative, accusative, genitive and others), and belong to gender or noun classes that trigger agreement on adjectives and verbs. English nouns mark only the plural (usually -s) and the possessive ('s), though pronouns preserve more case distinctions. German, Russian, Latin and Finnish nouns, by contrast, carry rich case systems.

Nouns across languages

The noun–verb distinction appears universal, but its boundaries vary. Some languages, such as Mandarin, lack articles and plural inflection yet still distinguish nouns distributionally. In many languages new nouns are freely created by derivation (nominalisation: decide → decision) and compounding, making the noun inventory open-ended.
