Benford's law

Benford's law, also known as the Newcomb–Benford law, the law of anomalous numbers, or the first-digit law, is an observation that in many real-life sets of numerical data, the leading digit is likely to be small. In sets that obey the law, the number 1 appears as the leading significant digit about 30% of the time, while 9 appears as the leading significant digit less than 5% of the time. If the digits were distributed uniformly, they would each occur about 11% of the time.

History

The regularity was first noted in 1881 by the astronomer Simon Newcomb, who observed that in logarithm tables the earlier pages — those for numbers beginning with 1 — were more worn than later pages. Physicist Frank Benford rediscovered and popularised the phenomenon in 1938, testing it on 20,229 numbers from diverse sources such as river areas, atomic weights, and street addresses.

Mathematical statement

The probability that the first digit is d is given by P(d) = log10(1 + 1/d), so 1 occurs with probability about 30.1%, 2 with 17.6%, decreasing to 9 with 4.6%. The law applies best to data that span several orders of magnitude and arise from multiplicative processes or mixtures of distributions; it does not hold for data that are constrained to a narrow range, assigned numbers such as telephone numbers, or quantities influenced by human psychology such as prices ending in .99.

Applications

Benford's law is used in forensic accounting and auditing to flag potentially fabricated figures — famously in analyses of financial statements and tax data — and has been applied to election statistics, macroeconomic data, and scientific fraud detection. Deviations from the law are treated as a prompt for investigation rather than proof of wrongdoing.
