Abdul Hamid II (Ottoman Turkish: عبد الحميد ثانی; 21 September 1842 – 10 February 1918) was the 34th sultan of the Ottoman Empire, reigning from 1876 until his deposition in 1909. The last Ottoman sultan to wield effective autocratic power, he oversaw a period of modernisation and centralisation combined with repression, territorial losses in Europe, and massacres of Armenians that earned him the epithet "the Red Sultan" in the European press.

Accession and constitutional episode

Abdul Hamid came to the throne after the deposition of his brother Murad V, having promised Midhat Pasha and the reformers a constitution. The first Ottoman constitution was proclaimed in December 1876 and parliament opened in 1877, but after the disastrous Russo-Turkish War of 1877–78 the sultan prorogued parliament in February 1878 and ruled personally for thirty years from the Yıldız Palace.

The Hamidian regime

From Yıldız, Abdul Hamid built an elaborate palace bureaucracy and intelligence service, censored the press, and promoted pan-Islam, emphasising his role as caliph to hold the empire's Muslim peoples together. Yet his reign also brought major development: railways (including the Hejaz Railway, financed by Muslim donations worldwide), telegraph lines, schools, hospitals and a professionalised bureaucracy, alongside continued foreign debt oversight through the Ottoman Public Debt Administration.

Armenian massacres and decline

In the 1890s, Armenian revolutionary activity and reform demands were met with the Hamidian massacres of 1894–96, in which tens of thousands — by many estimates 100,000 or more — Armenians were killed, an episode that badly damaged the sultan's reputation abroad. The 1897 war with Greece ended in Ottoman victory but European intervention denied him the fruits of it.

Deposition and death

The Young Turk Revolution of July 1908 forced him to restore the constitution. After the counter-revolutionary 31 March Incident in April 1909, the Committee of Union and Progress deposed him (27 April 1909) and exiled him to Salonica; he was brought back to Constantinople in 1912 and died at Beylerbeyi Palace in 1918.
