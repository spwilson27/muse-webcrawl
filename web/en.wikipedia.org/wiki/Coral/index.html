Corals are colonial marine invertebrates in the class Anthozoa of the phylum Cnidaria. They typically form compact colonies of many identical individual polyps. Coral species that build reefs are known as hermatypic ("hard") corals; they secrete calcium carbonate skeletons that accumulate over generations into coral reefs, among the most productive and biodiverse ecosystems on Earth.

Biology

Each coral polyp is a sac-like animal a few millimetres across, with a ring of stinging tentacles (nematocysts) around a central mouth. Most reef-building corals live in symbiosis with photosynthetic dinoflagellate algae called zooxanthellae, which supply the coral with energy-rich compounds, while the coral provides the algae with shelter and nutrients. Corals also capture plankton and dissolved organic matter. They reproduce both sexually, through mass spawning events in which eggs and sperm are released simultaneously, and asexually by budding and fragmentation.

Classification and diversity

Hard corals (order Scleractinia) number around 1,600 described species and dominate modern reefs. Soft corals (subclass Octocorallia) include sea fans, sea whips and leather corals, which lack massive limestone skeletons. Black corals and hydrozoan fire corals are also commonly called corals. Most reef corals require warm, clear, sunlit, shallow water, though deep-water corals such as Lophelia pertusa form reefs in cold darkness hundreds of metres down.

Threats

Coral reefs face severe pressure from climate change. Rising sea temperatures cause coral bleaching, when corals expel their zooxanthellae and may starve; ocean acidification reduces calcification; and disease, pollution, sedimentation and overfishing compound the damage. The Great Barrier Reef and Caribbean reefs have lost large fractions of their coral cover in recent decades, motivating research into heat-resistant corals and large-scale reef restoration.

Human uses

Precious coral (red and pink Corallium) has been carved into jewellery and ornaments since antiquity. Coral skeletons have been used as a source of lime, in bone graft substitutes, and corals remain model organisms in research on symbiosis, calcification and climate impacts.
