Cyberpunk

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cyberpunk is a subgenre of Science fiction in a dystopian, high-tech future marked by the combination of 'low life and high tech': computer hackers, megacorporations, cyberspace and body modification set against urban decay and social breakdown. It crystallised in the 1980s around William Gibson's Neuromancer (1984) and films such as Blade Runner (1982), and its aesthetics now pervade literature, cinema, anime and video games.

Origins

The term was coined by Bruce Bethke for his 1980 story 'Cyberpunk' and popularised by editor Gardner Dozois. The movement's core writers — Gibson, Bruce Sterling, Rudy Rucker, John Shirley and Pat Cadigan — reacted against both utopian SF and space opera, drawing on Film noir, hardboiled crime fiction, the New Wave and the emerging personal-computer culture. Gibson's coinage 'Cyberspace' (1982) anticipated the internet age.

Themes and style

Cyberpunk probes the social fallout of information technology: corporate power eclipsing governments, artificial intelligence, virtual reality, prosthetics and the blurring of human and machine. Its prose style is dense, information-saturated and brand-haunted; settings favour sprawling Asian-Pacific megacities, neon and rain — imagery fixed by Blade Runner and anime such as Akira (1988) and Ghost in the Shell (1995).

Legacy and derivatives

As real networks caught up with its visions, cyberpunk spawned derivatives — Steampunk, Biopunk, solarpunk — and diffused into mainstream culture, from The Matrix (1999) to the video game Cyberpunk 2077 (2020). Critics debate whether the genre remains a critique of corporate techno-capitalism or has become a nostalgic aesthetic.
