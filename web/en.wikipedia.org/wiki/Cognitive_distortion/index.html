Cognitive distortion

A cognitive distortion is an exaggerated or irrational thought pattern involved in the onset or maintenance of psychopathological states, such as depression and anxiety. Cognitive distortions are thoughts that cause individuals to perceive reality inaccurately; they are usually negative patterns of thinking about the self, the world and the future. The concept was developed within the cognitive therapy of Aaron T. Beck and popularised by David D. Burns in Feeling Good: The New Mood Therapy (1980).

Common types

Frequently listed distortions include all-or-nothing thinking (seeing things in black-and-white categories), overgeneralisation (drawing a sweeping conclusion from a single incident), mental filter (dwelling exclusively on the negative), disqualifying the positive, jumping to conclusions (including "mind reading" and "fortune telling"), magnification and minimisation (catastrophising), emotional reasoning (assuming feelings reflect reality), "should" statements, labelling and personalisation (attributing external events to oneself).

Treatment

Cognitive behavioural therapy works in part by helping patients identify their own distortions, test them against evidence, and replace them with more balanced thoughts — a process called cognitive restructuring. Decatastrophising, behavioural experiments and thought records are standard techniques.
