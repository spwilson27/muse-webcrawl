Life drawing

Life drawing, also known as figure drawing, is the practice of drawing the human figure from a live model, who is usually nude. It has been a core part of art education since the Renaissance, training artists in anatomy, proportion, observation and the rendering of form in light and shade.

Practice

A life drawing session typically begins with short poses — from thirty seconds to five minutes — for rapid gesture drawings that capture movement and proportion, followed by longer poses of twenty minutes or more for sustained studies. The model poses on a raised platform while artists sit or stand around in a circle at easels or drawing boards. Common media include charcoal, graphite, ink and sanguine chalk.

History

Drawing from nude models was institutionalised in the academies of art that spread across Europe from the 16th century; the resulting studies were called académies. Access to the life class was long restricted — women artists were largely excluded from drawing nude models until the late 19th century, a barrier fought by artists such as Laura Herford, who was admitted to the Royal Academy Schools in 1860 after signing her work with initials only.

In education

Life drawing remains a standard component of fine art, illustration, animation and design training. Animators study it to draw believable movement; medical and scientific illustrators to master anatomy. Public drop-in life drawing groups also exist in many cities for amateur artists.
