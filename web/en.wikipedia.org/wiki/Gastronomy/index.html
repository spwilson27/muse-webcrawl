Gastronomy


Gastronomy studies the relationship between food and culture.

Gastronomy is the study of the relationship between food and culture, the art of preparing and serving rich or delicate and appetizing food, the cooking styles of particular regions, and the science of good eating. One who is well versed in gastronomy is called a gastronome, while a gastronomist is one who unites theory and practice in its study. Practical gastronomy is associated with the practice and study of the preparation, production, and service of food and drink, while theoretical gastronomy underpins it with systems and process.

History

The term derives from the Greek gastēr (stomach) and nómos (law/knowledge). Gastronomic writing has ancient roots, from Archestratus' 4th-century BCE poem on Mediterranean food to Apicius' Roman cookbook. The discipline flourished in France with Jean Anthelme Brillat-Savarin's The Physiology of Taste (1825), which defined gastronomy as knowledge of all that relates to human nourishment, and with the codification of haute cuisine by Carême and Escoffier. In 2010, "the gastronomic meal of the French" was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Modern gastronomy

Contemporary gastronomy encompasses food science and movements such as molecular gastronomy, pioneered by Hervé This and popularised by chefs like Ferran Adrià and Heston Blumenthal, as well as the New Nordic and slow food movements, which emphasise locality, seasonality and sustainability.
