Cyanide poisoning

Cyanide poisoning is poisoning that results from exposure to any of a number of forms of cyanide. Early symptoms include headache, dizziness, fast heart rate, shortness of breath, and vomiting. This may then be followed by seizures, slow heart rate, low blood pressure, loss of consciousness, and cardiac arrest. Onset of symptoms usually occurs within a few minutes. Some survivors may develop long-term neurological problems.

Mechanism

Cyanide is an inhibitor of the enzyme cytochrome c oxidase (complex IV) in the mitochondrial electron transport chain. It binds to the ferric iron (Fe3+) in the enzyme, preventing cells from using oxygen to make ATP. As a result, tissues are starved of usable oxygen despite adequate oxygen delivery — a form of histotoxic hypoxia — and cells switch to anaerobic metabolism, producing lactic acidosis. The central nervous system and heart are most vulnerable. Venous blood may appear bright red because the tissues fail to extract oxygen.

Causes

Toxic cyanide-containing compounds include hydrogen cyanide gas and many cyanide salts. Poisoning is relatively common following breathing in smoke from house and industrial fires, because burning plastics and other synthetic materials release hydrogen cyanide. Other sources include certain industrial processes (metal extraction, electroplating, fumigation), the drug sodium nitroprusside given at high doses, and cyanogenic glycosides such as amygdalin found in bitter almonds, apricot kernels and (in smaller amounts) cassava and apple seeds.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is often difficult and is usually based on the clinical picture, exposure history, elevated lactate and a high anion gap metabolic acidosis; blood cyanide levels can confirm it but are rarely available in time. Treatment consists of removing the person from the source, decontamination, and supportive care including 100% oxygen and intravenous fluids. Antidotes include hydroxocobalamin (vitamin B12a), which binds cyanide to form cyanocobalamin, and sodium thiosulfate, which donates sulfur for conversion of cyanide to thiocyanate. Nitrites, which induce methaemoglobinaemia, were historically used but are now avoided where possible, particularly in smoke inhalation.

History

Hydrogen cyanide was used as a chemical weapon (under names such as Zyklon B in the Holocaust, and as a war gas in the First World War) and in judicial executions in gas chambers in some United States jurisdictions. Cyanide salts have also featured in notorious mass poisonings and individual murders and suicides.
