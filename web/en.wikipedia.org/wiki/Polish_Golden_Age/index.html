Polish Golden Age
Periodc. 1507–1572 (reigns of Sigismund I the Old and Sigismund II Augustus)
StateKingdom of Poland, later Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth
CapitalKraków
Key featuresRenaissance culture, religious tolerance, parliamentary nobility, grain trade wealth

The Polish Golden Age was the period of prosperity and cultural flourishing in the Kingdom of Poland and, from 1569, the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, roughly spanning the reigns of Sigismund I the Old (1506–1548) and Sigismund II Augustus (1548–1572), the last kings of the Jagiellonian dynasty. Poland-Lithuania was then one of Europe's largest and most populous states.

Wawel Castle in Kraków, seat of the Jagiellonian kings during the Golden Age.

Culture and learning

The royal court patronised Renaissance art and architecture — Kraków's Wawel Castle and Sigismund's Chapel are masterpieces of the style. Nicolaus Copernicus published De revolutionibus (1543), and poets such as Jan Kochanowski laid foundations for Polish literary language. The Jagiellonian University flourished, and cities like Gdańsk (Danzig) grew rich exporting grain through the Baltic.

Politics and tolerance

The period saw the consolidation of the szlachta (nobility) democracy, the Nihil novi constitution of 1505, and the Union of Lublin (1569) creating the Commonwealth. Remarkable for the era, the Warsaw Confederation of 1573 guaranteed religious tolerance, making the Commonwealth a refuge for dissenters — "a state without stakes".
