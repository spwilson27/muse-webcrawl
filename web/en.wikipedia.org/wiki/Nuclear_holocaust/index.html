Nuclear holocaust

A nuclear holocaust, also known as a nuclear apocalypse, nuclear annihilation, nuclear armageddon, or atomic holocaust, is a theoretical scenario involving widespread destruction and radioactive fallout causing the collapse of civilization, through the use of nuclear weapons. Under such a scenario, nuclear warfare causes extensive destruction of populated areas, rendering large parts of the Earth uninhabitable and potentially causing human extinction or a collapse of modern civilization.

Background

The concept emerged during the Cold War, when the United States and the Soviet Union accumulated arsenals capable of destroying each other's cities many times over — a condition described by the doctrine of mutual assured destruction (MAD). Studies in the 1980s, notably the TTAPS research, proposed that smoke from burning cities could produce a "nuclear winter", sharply cooling the global climate and devastating agriculture.

Scenarios

Scenarios range from a limited regional exchange — for example between India and Pakistan, which researchers have modeled as capable of causing global famine through climatic effects — to an all-out exchange between major nuclear powers. Even a fraction of the current global arsenal of roughly 12,000 warheads detonated over cities could kill tens or hundreds of millions of people directly and many more through fallout, infrastructure collapse, and starvation.

In culture

The nuclear holocaust has been a recurring theme in literature and film, including the novel On the Beach (1957) and its film adaptations, the television film The Day After (1983), and the British film Threads (1984). Such works influenced public and political debate about disarmament.

Prevention

Arms control treaties such as START and New START, the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, and the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (2021) aim to reduce the risk of such an event.
