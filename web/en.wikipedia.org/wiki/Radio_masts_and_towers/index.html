Radio masts and towers

Radio mast / tower
TypesGuyed mast; self-supporting tower; mast radiator
TallestWarsaw radio mast (646.4 m, 1974–1991, collapsed); KVLY-TV mast, North Dakota (628.8 m)
UsesBroadcasting (AM/FM/TV), telecommunications, radar, navigation aids

Radio masts and towers are tall structures designed to support antennas for telecommunications and broadcasting, including radio and television transmission. A mast is typically a narrow lattice structure held upright by guy wires anchored to the ground, whereas a tower is self-supporting. In many AM broadcasting installations the mast itself is the antenna — a mast radiator — insulated from the ground and fed directly with the transmitter's output.

Design and construction

Guyed masts are economical for great heights because the slender steel lattice carries mainly compression while the guy wires resist wind loads. Their disadvantage is the large land area needed for the anchors. Self-supporting lattice towers, such as those carrying FM and TV antennas on mountains and rooftops, need no guys but require far more steel. Tall structures are painted in alternating red/white or orange/white bands and fitted with aviation obstruction lights. Ice accretion and wind-induced oscillation (galloping) are major design concerns; several of the tallest masts have collapsed during maintenance or storms, most famously the 646-metre Warsaw radio mast in 1991, which remains the tallest structure ever built to have collapsed.

Height records

Since 1991 the tallest guyed mast has been the KVLY-TV mast in North Dakota, at 628.8 m (2,063 ft). The tallest free-standing tower used for broadcasting is the Tokyo Skytree (634 m), and the CN Tower in Toronto (553 m) held the free-standing record for decades.
