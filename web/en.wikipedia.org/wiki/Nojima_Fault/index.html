The Nojima Fault (Japanese: 野島断層) is a geological fault on Awaji Island in Hyōgo Prefecture, Japan. It became famous as the fault that ruptured in the Great Hanshin earthquake of 17 January 1995, producing a clearly visible surface displacement that could be traced across the northern part of the island.

The 1995 earthquake

The Great Hanshin (Kobe) earthquake, magnitude 6.9–7.2, struck at 05:46 JST on 17 January 1995. The Nojima Fault moved with predominantly right-lateral strike-slip displacement of roughly 1 to 1.5 metres, together with a vertical component of up to about 1.2 metres. The surface rupture ran for about 9 kilometres across farmland, roads, and irrigation channels, offsetting field boundaries, hedgerows, and concrete structures into a textbook example of fault displacement. The earthquake devastated the nearby city of Kobe, killing more than 6,400 people, though the fault scarp on rural Awaji Island itself became the most visible geological trace of the event.

Preservation

A section of the rupture was preserved at the Nojima Fault Preservation Museum within the Hokudan Earthquake Memorial Park in Awaji City. There, a trench and an indoor exhibit display the offset ground, a displaced road and utility pole, and a house preserved as it stood after the quake. The site, designated a natural monument of Japan, is used for disaster-prevention education.

Geology

The Nojima Fault forms part of the Rokko–Awaji fault zone associated with the northern margin of the Philippine Sea Plate's subduction and the deformation of southwest Japan. It cuts through granitic basement and Quaternary sediments; boreholes and drilling projects after 1995 sampled the fault zone to study frictional heating and fault healing after a large earthquake.
