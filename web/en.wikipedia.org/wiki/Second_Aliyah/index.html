Second Aliyah

The Second Aliyah was a major wave of Jewish immigration (aliyah) to Ottoman Palestine between 1904 and 1914, in which roughly 35,000 Jews, mainly from the Russian Empire — with a smaller notable group from Yemen — settled in the country. It followed the First Aliyah (1882–1903) and is considered formative for the culture and institutions of the future State of Israel.

Background and character

Immigration was driven by pogroms in Russia (notably Kishinev in 1903 and the wave following the failed 1905 revolution) and by socialist-Zionist ideology. Unlike the First Aliyah, whose settlers often employed cheaper Arab labour, many Second Aliyah immigrants were young, single idealists committed to the "conquest of labour" — the principle that Jews must perform manual, especially agricultural, work themselves. Though a majority of the immigrants eventually left, the minority who stayed founded much of the Yishuv's institutional framework.

Achievements

Members of the Second Aliyah founded the first kibbutz, Degania (1909/1910); the city of Tel Aviv (1909); the Hashomer self-defence organisation (1909), forerunner of the Haganah; and the labour parties and unions that later formed the Histadrut. They were also central to the revival of Hebrew as a spoken vernacular, championed by figures such as Eliezer Ben-Yehuda's successors and the writer Yosef Haim Brenner. David Ben-Gurion, Yitzhak Ben-Zvi and Berl Katznelson — the future leadership of Israel — all arrived during this aliyah. Immigration was halted by the outbreak of the First World War in 1914.
