Bingara (Aboriginal for "creek") is a small town on the Gwydir River in the New England region of New South Wales, Australia. It is the administrative centre of the Gwydir Shire and lies on the Fossickers Way. The town is known for its historic buildings, its orange trees planted as a living memorial to its war dead, and as the site of the Myall Creek massacre memorial nearby.

History

The area was originally inhabited by the Kamilaroi people. European settlement followed the exploration of the Gwydir River valley in the 1830s. In 1838, the Myall Creek massacre occurred at a station near present-day Bingara, in which 28 Aboriginal men, women and children were murdered; seven of the perpetrators were hanged, one of the few instances in Australian colonial history of settlers being executed for the killing of Aboriginal people.

Gold was discovered in the district in the 1850s, and the town grew as a service centre for miners and pastoralists. Bingara was proclaimed a municipality in 1889. Diamonds were also mined in the nearby Copera/Bingara fields in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

The living memorial

Bingara is noted for the orange trees lining its main streets. The trees were planted as a memorial to the town's soldiers killed in the two world wars; each year the harvest is ceremonially picked by local schoolchildren, and the fruit is not otherwise harvested commercially. The annual Orange Festival celebrates the memorial and the town's heritage.

Attractions

Local attractions include the restored Roxy Theatre, a 1930s cinema built by Greek immigrants, the Myall Creek Massacre Memorial, and the Gwydir River, which is popular for fishing, camping and fossicking for sapphires and gold.
