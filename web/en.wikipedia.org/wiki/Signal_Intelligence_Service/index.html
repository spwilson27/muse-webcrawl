Signal Intelligence Service

The Signal Intelligence Service (SIS) was the United States Army's codebreaking organisation from 1930 to 1945, headquartered at Arlington Hall in Virginia. It was a forerunner of the Army Security Agency and, ultimately, of the National Security Agency.

Founding

The SIS was established on 24 April 1930 within the Signal Corps under the direction of William F. Friedman, who built a small team — including Frank Rowlett, Solomon Kullback, and Abraham Sinkov — that trained in cryptanalysis and compiled codes for the Army. Forbidden by its charter from intercepting messages itself, it depended on copies supplied by the Signal Corps and other agencies.

Second World War

The service's greatest success was the solution of the Japanese diplomatic machine cipher known to the Americans as PURPLE: in September 1940 Genevieve Grotjan discovered the repeating-key structure that let the team reconstruct the machine, and its decrypts — distributed under the codename MAGIC — gave the US crucial insight into Japanese intentions throughout the war. The SIS also ran extensive work against German, Italian, and other diplomatic systems, and cooperated with the US Navy's OP-20-G and with Britain's Government Code and Cypher School at Bletchley Park.

Reorganisation

In 1943 the organisation was renamed the Signal Security Service and then the Signal Security Agency; in September 1945 it became the Army Security Agency. Its functions were absorbed into the new NSA in 1952.
