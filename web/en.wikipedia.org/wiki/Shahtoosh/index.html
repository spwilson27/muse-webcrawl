Shahtoosh

Shahtoosh (from Persian شاهتوش shāhtūsh, "king of wools") is a luxury wool obtained from the fine underfur of the chiru (Pantholops hodgsonii), the Tibetan antelope. Shawls woven from it are exceptionally light, warm and fine — legend holds that a whole shawl can pass through a finger ring. Because chiru cannot be domesticated and the finest hair was traditionally obtained by killing the animals, the shahtoosh trade drove the chiru to the brink of extinction, and trade in shahtoosh is now banned internationally.

The fibre

Shahtoosh fibres measure roughly 7–10 micrometres in diameter, among the finest of any animal hair — finer than cashmere. The wool is woven, traditionally by craftsmen in Kashmir, into shawls that weigh only about 100 grams (3.5 oz) yet provide remarkable warmth. Before the ban, fine shahtoosh shawls sold for thousands of dollars in luxury markets in India, the Middle East, Europe and the United States.

Conservation and the ban

Chiru live on the high Changtang plateau of Tibet and migrate long distances. Their population collapsed from an estimated one million in the early 20th century to as few as 65,000–75,000 by the 1990s, largely because poachers killed the animals — as many as three to five chiru for a single shawl. China and India protected the species, and the chiru was listed in Appendix I of CITES, prohibiting all international commercial trade. India banned manufacture and trade in shahtoosh under the Wildlife (Protection) Act. Enforcement actions, including high-profile seizures and prosecutions of shawl dealers in the 1990s and 2000s, sharply reduced the market, and serious chiru conservation in the Changtang and Kekexili reserves has since allowed numbers to recover somewhat.

The ban has been controversial in Jammu and Kashmir, where weavers argued the trade supported traditional craftsmanship; attempts have been made to promote collection of naturally shed hair, but verification difficulties and continued laundering of poached wool have kept the trade prohibited.
