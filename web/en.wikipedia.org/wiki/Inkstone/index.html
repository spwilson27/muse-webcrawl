Inkstone
A traditional inkstone used to grind ink sticks into liquid ink for calligraphy and brush painting.

An inkstone is a stone mortar used in Chinese, Korean, and Japanese calligraphy and painting for grinding a solid ink stick against its surface with a small quantity of water to produce liquid ink. It is one of the "Four Treasures of the Study" (wénfáng sìbǎo), alongside the brush, paper, and ink stick itself.

History

Inkstones have been used in China since at least the Han dynasty, when ink was prepared from ground pigments and soot. Early inkstones were simple flat stones, but by the Tang and Song dynasties they had become highly refined art objects, carved from prized stones and decorated with inscriptions and relief sculpture. Famous inkstone materials include Duan stone from Guangdong, She stone from Anhui and Jiangxi, Tao stone from Gansu, and Chengni clay-based stones.

Use

To prepare ink, the user places a few drops of water in the shallow well of the inkstone and grinds the ink stick in a circular or back-and-forth motion, holding the stick vertically. The resulting ink collects in the deeper reservoir of the stone. The consistency of the ink is controlled by the amount of water added and the duration of grinding; freshly ground ink is considered superior to bottled ink by many calligraphers because of its particle fineness and luster.

Materials and care

A good inkstone has a fine, slightly abrasive texture that grinds ink efficiently without scratching the stick, and it does not absorb water too quickly. After use, the inkstone should be rinsed clean, as dried ink left on the stone damages its surface and dulls subsequent grinding. Collectors prize antique inkstones bearing the marks of famous calligraphers and carvers.
