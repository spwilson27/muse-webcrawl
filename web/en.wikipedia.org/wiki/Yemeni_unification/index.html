Yemeni unification

Yemeni unification took place on 22 May 1990, when the Yemen Arab Republic (North Yemen) and the People's Democratic Republic of Yemen (South Yemen) merged to form the Republic of Yemen, with Sanaa as its capital. It was the only successful peaceful unification of two Arab states in the modern era.

Background

Yemen had been divided since the 19th century between the Ottoman-influenced north and the British-controlled Aden Protectorate in the south. North Yemen gained independence after the First World War and became a republic in 1962 after a civil war; South Yemen won independence from Britain in 1967 under the National Liberation Front and adopted a Marxist government. Repeated unity talks and two border wars (1972 and 1979) punctuated the following decades, along with agreements that were never implemented.

The road to unity

The January 1986 civil war in the South, the weakening of Soviet support during perestroika, joint oil exploration along the undemarcated border, and economic pressures on both states revived unification talks under North Yemeni president Ali Abdullah Saleh and the southern leadership. The Taiz summit of November 1989 produced a draft unity constitution, and the Aden Agreement set the merger in motion.

Unification and aftermath

On 22 May 1990 the Republic of Yemen was proclaimed: Saleh became president, southern leader Ali Salem al-Beidh vice-president, and a transitional parliament combined the two legislatures. The first multiparty elections followed in 1993. However, incomplete military integration, disputes over power-sharing, and southern grievances over marginalization culminated in the 1994 civil war, when southern leaders attempted to secede; northern forces prevailed and unity was preserved by force. Southern resentment later fuelled the Hirak (Southern Movement) from 2007, and the fragmentation of Yemen during the civil war that began in 2015 has once again put the unity of the state in question.
