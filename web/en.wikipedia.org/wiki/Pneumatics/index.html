Pneumatics

Pneumatics (from the Greek πνεῦμα, pneuma, meaning "breath" or "wind") is a branch of engineering that makes use of gas or pressurised air as a medium for transmitting and controlling energy. Pneumatic systems used in industry typically employ compressed air — inert gases are used only for special applications — delivered by a compressor and controlled by valves to operate actuators such as cylinders and rotary motors. Pneumatics is one of the three main fluid-power technologies, alongside hydraulics (using liquids, usually oil) and electrical drives.

Pneumatic cylinders and directional control valves on production equipment.

Principles

A typical pneumatic installation consists of a compressor that raises air to a working pressure (commonly 6–8 bar in industrial plants), a receiver tank, treatment units that filter, dry, and regulate the air (often combined as an FRL unit: filter, regulator, lubricator), distribution pipework, directional control valves, and actuators. Because air is compressible, pneumatic actuators are "springy": they move quickly and can stall against a load without damage, but precise mid-stroke positioning is difficult compared with hydraulics or servomotors. The exhaust air is discharged to atmosphere, sometimes through silencers.

Comparison with hydraulics

Pneumatic systems are generally cheaper, cleaner, faster, and simpler to maintain than hydraulic systems, and leaks of air are harmless — advantages that make them dominant in factory automation, food processing, and packaging. Hydraulics, by contrast, operate at much higher pressures (often 70–350 bar) and deliver far greater forces in a compact package, so they are preferred for heavy machinery, presses, and earth-moving equipment. Pneumatic tools, such as impact wrenches, nail guns, and drills, are likewise lighter relative to their power than their electric counterparts in some trades.

Applications

  • Manufacturing automation: clamping, ejecting, sorting, pick-and-place, and valve actuation.
  • Transport: air brakes on trains and heavy trucks, bus and train doors, and aircraft systems.
  • Construction tools: jackhammers, nail guns, and riveters.
  • Medicine and dentistry: dental drills and some surgical and ventilator equipment.
  • Pneumatic tubes: capsule pipelines historically used for mail and documents, still used in hospitals and banks.
  • Robotics: including soft robotics, where inflatable structures provide compliant gripping.

History

The ancient Greek engineer Ctesibius of Alexandria (3rd century BCE) worked with compressed air in pumps and catapults, and Hero of Alexandria described pneumatic devices. Systematic study began in the seventeenth century with the gas laws of Boyle and others. Industrial pneumatics grew in the nineteenth century: compressed-air rock drills drove tunnel projects such as the Mont Cenis Tunnel, George Westinghouse's air brake (1869) transformed railway safety, and Paris operated a large municipal compressed-air power network from 1888.
