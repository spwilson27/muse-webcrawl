Chamber drama
Also calledKammerspiel (German)
OriginEarly 20th-century theatre and cinema
Key figuresMax Reinhardt, Lupu Pick, August Strindberg

A chamber drama (German: Kammerspiel, "chamber play") is a drama — in theatre or film — characterized by a small cast, an intimate setting, and a focus on psychological development rather than spectacle or action. The term is modelled on "chamber music", written for a small ensemble.

Theatre

The form was advanced by the Swedish playwright August Strindberg, whose late "chamber plays" (e.g. The Ghost Sonata, 1907) were written for his Intimate Theatre in Stockholm. In Germany, director Max Reinhardt's Kammerspiele productions from 1906 emphasised psychological realism and small-scale staging.

Film

In Weimar cinema, the Kammerspielfilm — exemplified by Lupu Pick's Shattered (1921) and F. W. Murnau's The Last Laugh (1924), with scripts by Carl Mayer — favoured few characters, confined settings, and visual storytelling with minimal intertitles. Later directors such as Ingmar Bergman carried the chamber tradition into modern cinema with films like Persona and Scenes from a Marriage.
