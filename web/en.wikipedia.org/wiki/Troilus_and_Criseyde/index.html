Troilus and Criseyde

Troilus and Criseyde is an epic poem by Geoffrey Chaucer, written in the mid-1380s, which re-tells in Middle English the tragic story of the lovers Troilus and Criseyde set against a backdrop of war during the siege of Troy. It is written in rhyme royal, a seven-line stanza form, and is widely regarded as Chaucer's finest long poem and one of the greatest works of English literature before Shakespeare.

Sources

Chaucer's principal source was Giovanni Boccaccio's Il Filostrato, itself drawing on the medieval Troy tradition rather than on Homer. Chaucer greatly expanded the roles of Criseyde and of Pandarus, Criseyde's uncle, who becomes the lovers' go-between and one of the most psychologically vivid characters in medieval literature. Elements from Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy, which Chaucer also translated, shape the poem's treatment of Fortune and free will.

Synopsis

Troilus, a young Trojan prince, mocks love until he sees Criseyde, a widow whose father has defected to the Greeks, in the temple and falls desperately in love. With Pandarus's scheming, the pair become lovers. When Criseyde is exchanged to the Greek camp for the prisoner Antenor, she promises to return in ten days but instead accepts the Greek warrior Diomede as her lover. Troilus, heartbroken, is eventually killed in battle by Achilles; his spirit ascends to the eighth sphere and laughs at the folly of earthly love and grief.

Legacy

The poem influenced Henryson's Testament of Cresseid, which continues Criseyde's story, and Shakespeare's play Troilus and Cressida. Its ending, in which Chaucer addresses his book and dedicates it to "moral Gower" and "philosophical Strode", is among the most discussed passages in Middle English poetry.
