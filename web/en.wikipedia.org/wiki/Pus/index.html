Pus

Pus is an exudate, typically white-yellow, yellow, or yellow-brown, formed at the site of inflammation during bacterial or fungal infection. It consists of a thin, protein-rich fluid known as liquor puris together with dead leukocytes — mainly neutrophils — dead and living bacteria, and cellular debris. An accumulation of pus in an enclosed tissue space is called an abscess, whereas an accumulation within a body cavity is an empyema.

Composition and formation

When the immune system detects pyogenic (pus-forming) bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus or Streptococcus pyogenes, it recruits large numbers of neutrophils to the site by chemotaxis. The neutrophils engulf and kill bacteria, then die and disintegrate, releasing enzymes that liquefy surrounding tissue; the resulting mixture is pus. Its color and odor vary with the organism involved: staphylococcal pus is typically thick and creamy, while pus from Pseudomonas aeruginosa may be blue-green owing to the pigment pyocyanin.

Clinical significance

The presence of pus is a cardinal sign of bacterial infection. Conditions involving pus include abscesses, pustules and boils of the skin, purulent conjunctivitis, empyema of the chest, and purulent meningitis. The classic surgical principle ubi pus, ibi evacua — "where there is pus, evacuate it" — reflects the fact that drainage is often essential to cure, alongside antibiotics. Sterile pus, in which no organisms are found, occurs in some inflammatory conditions such as reactive arthritis.

History

For centuries physicians debated the meaning of pus; the Galenic notion of "laudable pus" held that suppuration was a necessary and healthy stage of wound healing. This idea persisted until the 19th century, when antiseptic and then aseptic surgery showed that pus signals infection and that wounds heal best without it.
