Red Power activists demanded sovereignty, treaty rights, and an end to the federal policy of termination.

The Red Power movement was a social and political movement of Native Americans in the United States during the 1960s and 1970s. Modelled in part on the civil rights and Black Power movements, it demanded tribal sovereignty, the honouring of treaty rights, self-determination in place of the federal policies of termination and assimilation, and the return of land. The phrase "Red Power" was popularised by the Standing Rock Sioux writer Vine Deloria Jr., whose 1969 book Custer Died for Your Sins: An Indian Manifesto became the movement's best-known text.

Background

From the 1940s the United States pursued "termination" — ending federal recognition of tribes, dissolving reservations, and relocating Native people to cities. The policy produced poverty and cultural dislocation, but the urban Indian communities it created also produced a pan-Indian political consciousness. The National Indian Youth Council, founded in 1961, introduced direct-action tactics, staging "fish-ins" in the Pacific Northwest to defend treaty fishing rights.

Major actions

American Indian Movement

The American Indian Movement (AIM) was founded in Minneapolis in 1968, initially to combat police brutality against urban Indians, and grew into the movement's most militant organisation, led by figures including Dennis Banks and Russell Means.

Occupation of Alcatraz

From November 1969 to June 1971, activists of "Indians of All Tribes" occupied Alcatraz Island in San Francisco Bay, claiming it under a treaty provision for surplus federal land. The occupation drew national attention to Native issues and helped end the termination policy.

Trail of Broken Treaties and Wounded Knee

In 1972 the Trail of Broken Treaties caravan brought thousands of activists to Washington, D.C., where they occupied the Bureau of Indian Affairs headquarters. In 1973 AIM members and Oglala Lakota occupied the hamlet of Wounded Knee on the Pine Ridge Reservation for 71 days in an armed standoff with federal authorities, on the site of the 1890 massacre.

Legacy

The movement shifted federal policy toward self-determination, expressed in the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975, the Indian Child Welfare Act of 1978, and expanded recognition of treaty rights, tribal gaming, and cultural and religious freedoms. Its tactics and rhetoric also fed into later Indigenous activism in the United States and Canada.
