Lex Salica

The Lex Salica (Salic law) is the ancient Frankish civil law code compiled around 500 AD by Clovis I, first king of the Franks. One of the oldest surviving law codes of early medieval Europe, it governed the Salian Franks and became a foundational legal tradition in France and the Low Countries for a thousand years.

Contents

The code is written in Latin (with Frankish glosses known as the Malberg glosses) and consists largely of tariffed penalties: fines and wergild for theft, assault, property damage, and homicide, graded by the status and sex of the victim. Killing a free Frank cost 200 solidi, while a Roman cost half that; the code also covers inheritance, procedure, summons, and migration. A famous clause excluded women from inheriting "Salic land," later interpreted as allodial land.

Recensions

The law was repeatedly reissued and expanded — under Childebert and Chlothar, in Dagobert's 7th-century recension, and in Charlemagne's Lex Salica Karolina of about 802–803. Some 80 manuscripts survive, divided by scholars into several families of texts reflecting these revisions.

The succession rule

In the 14th century, French jurists revived the inheritance clause to argue that the French crown could not pass to a woman or through a woman — the so-called Salic law of succession used to bar Edward III of England's claim in 1328, precipitating the Hundred Years' War. The principle was invoked again in succession disputes in 16th-century France and in 19th-century Spain (the Carlist Wars). Several European monarchies long applied semi-Salic succession; most have since adopted absolute primogeniture.
