Dogū

CultureJōmon period, Japan
Datec. 14,000 – 400 BCE
MediumEarthenware (clay)
Numbers knownOver 15,000

Dogū (Japanese: 土偶, "clay figures") are small humanoid and animal figurines made during the later part of the Jōmon period (14,000–400 BCE) of prehistoric Japan. Dogū come exclusively from the Jōmon period; by the following Yayoi period they were no longer made. They are typically between 10 and 30 cm high, made of clay, and most appear to represent female figures with large eyes, small waists and wide hips.

Types

Scholars distinguish several styles, including the "heart-shaped" (or horned-owl) dogū, the "goggle-eyed" shakōki-dogū with enormous circular eyes resembling snow goggles, the "pregnant woman" type, and the "horned owl" type. Many dogū are found deliberately broken, which has led to theories that they were used in rituals in which illness or misfortune was transferred to the figure, which was then destroyed. Other theories associate them with fertility rites, toys, or funerary practices.

Significance

Dogū are among the most famous artifacts of prehistoric Japan; several are designated National Treasures, including the "Jōmon Venus" from Chino, Nagano. A celebrated hollow dogū from Hokkaido, nicknamed Kakkū, is also a National Treasure. Along with Jōmon pottery — among the oldest ceramic traditions in the world — the figurines exemplify the elaborate symbolic culture of Japan's prehistoric hunter-gatherers.
