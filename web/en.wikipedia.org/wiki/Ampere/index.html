The ampere (symbol: A), often shortened to "amp", is the unit of electric current in the International System of Units (SI). One ampere is equal to the flow of one coulomb of charge per second. It is named after the French physicist and mathematician André-Marie Ampère (1775–1836), one of the founders of the science of electromagnetism.

Definition

Since the 2019 revision of the SI, the ampere has been defined by fixing the numerical value of the elementary charge e to 1.602176634×10−19 coulombs. One ampere is therefore the current produced by the flow of exactly 1/(1.602176634×10−19) elementary charges per second — roughly 6.241509074×1018 electrons per second.

Before 2019, the ampere was defined as the constant current that, maintained in two straight parallel conductors of infinite length placed one metre apart in vacuum, would produce a force of 2×10−7 newtons per metre of length between the conductors. That definition rested on Ampère's force law and proved difficult to realise precisely, which motivated the change to a definition based on a fundamental constant.

History

The ampere was originally defined within the centimetre–gram–second system and was adopted internationally at the International Electrical Congress in Chicago in 1893. It became one of the seven SI base units when the SI was established in 1960. For much of the twentieth century it was realised in practice using devices such as the silver voltameter and later the Kibble (watt) balance, by reference to the volt and the ohm maintained through the Josephson and quantum Hall effects.

Examples of typical currents

Everyday currents range widely: a typical LED draws about 0.02 A; a smartphone charging may draw 1–3 A; a household kettle on a 230 V supply draws around 10 A; an electric car charger may supply 32 A or more; and a lightning strike can carry a peak current of roughly 30,000 A.

Multiples

Common submultiples and multiples include the milliampere (mA, 10−3 A), the microampere (µA, 10−6 A), and the kiloampere (kA, 103 A). Related units include the volt (electric potential), the ohm (resistance), and the watt (power), connected by Ohm's law.
