Runaway climate change is a hypothesised scenario in which human-caused global warming triggers self-reinforcing feedbacks in the Earth system that push the climate past tipping points, producing further warming that continues even if human greenhouse-gas emissions cease. The term is associated with fears that, beyond a threshold — often discussed around 2 °C of warming above pre-industrial levels — climate change could become effectively unstoppable on human timescales.

Feedback mechanisms

Several positive (amplifying) feedbacks are proposed as potential drivers:

  • Ice–albedo feedback: melting snow and sea ice expose darker land and ocean surfaces that absorb more sunlight.
  • Permafrost carbon feedback: thawing permafrost releases stored carbon dioxide and methane, a potent greenhouse gas.
  • Methane clathrates: warming oceans could destabilise methane hydrate deposits on continental shelves (the "clathrate gun hypothesis").
  • Forest dieback: drought and fire could convert the Amazon rainforest and boreal forests from carbon sinks into carbon sources.
  • Water vapour feedback: warmer air holds more water vapour, itself a greenhouse gas.

Tipping points

Research on tipping elements — including the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, tropical coral reefs, permafrost and major ocean circulation systems such as the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation — suggests some may be destabilised at relatively low levels of warming, with cascading effects between them. The 2018 "Hothouse Earth" paper popularised the idea that a cascade of tipping points could lock the planet into a much warmer state.

Scientific assessment

Climate scientists emphasise that a true Venus-style runaway greenhouse effect — in which the oceans evaporate entirely — is not considered a plausible outcome of human emissions, because Earth receives less solar radiation than Venus and the required carbon release exceeds available fossil-fuel reserves. However, severe, self-sustaining warming sufficient to render large regions uninhabitable and to commit the planet to many metres of sea-level rise is treated as a genuine risk, which underpins international efforts to limit warming to well below 2 °C under the Paris Agreement.
