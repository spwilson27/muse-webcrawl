Permanent income hypothesis

The permanent income hypothesis (PIH) is an economic theory proposed by Milton Friedman in A Theory of the Consumption Function (1957), holding that people's consumption depends on their long-run expected ("permanent") income rather than current income.

Permanent income hypothesis
Proposed by: Milton Friedman, 1957
Field: Macroeconomics, consumption theory
Related: Life-cycle hypothesis

Theory

Observed income is the sum of permanent income (expected lifetime earnings) and transitory income (windfalls or temporary losses). Consumers smooth consumption: they save most of a transitory windfall and borrow against temporary shortfalls, so the marginal propensity to consume out of transitory income is low. This reconciled Keynesian cross-section findings with stable long-run consumption ratios (the Kuznets paradox).

Influence and criticism

PIH, with Modigliani's life-cycle hypothesis, founded modern consumption theory and underlies the rational-expectations version formalized by Robert Hall (1978), in which consumption follows a random walk. Empirical tests find "excess sensitivity" to predictable income changes, attributed to liquidity constraints and myopia.
