Civil War token

Civil War tokens are token coins that were privately minted and distributed in the United States between 1861 and 1864. They were used mainly in the Northeast and Midwest. The widespread use of the tokens was a result of the scarcity of government-issued cents during the American Civil War, as the public hoarded coins — even base-metal cents — for their intrinsic metal value.

Period of use1861–1864
DenominationUsually 1 cent
MaterialsCopper, brass, lead, rubber, other metals
Suppressed byAct of Congress, April 22, 1864

Types

Civil War tokens are generally divided into three categories: patriotic tokens, bearing slogans and emblems such as "The Union Must and Shall Be Preserved"; store cards, which advertised the issuing merchant's business and place of trade; and sutler tokens, issued for use at the stores serving army regiments. Most were the size of the contemporary cent and were struck in copper or brass, though rarer pieces were made in lead, zinc, rubber, and even pressed leather. An estimated 25 million or more tokens were struck in thousands of varieties.

End of the tokens

Congress ended the practice with legislation in 1864: the Coinage Act of April 22, 1864 prohibited private one- and two-cent pieces, and a further act of June 8, 1864 forbade all private coinage. The introduction of the federal two-cent piece and bronze cent in 1864 restored confidence in small change. The tokens are now popular collectibles, catalogued by specialists such as George and Melvin Fuld.
