Inherit the Wind

Written byJerome Lawrence and Robert E. Lee
Premiere21 April 1955, Broadway
SubjectScopes "Monkey" Trial of 1925
GenreDrama

Inherit the Wind is an American play by Jerome Lawrence and Robert Edwin Lee, first performed in 1955. The story fictionalizes the 1925 Scopes "Monkey" Trial as a means to discuss the then-current wave of McCarthyism in intellectual freedom. The play's title comes from Proverbs 11:29: "He that troubleth his own house shall inherit the wind."

Plot

In the small town of Hillsboro, teacher Bertram Cates is arrested for teaching Charles Darwin's theory of evolution to his science class, in violation of a state law prohibiting the teaching of anything contradicting the biblical account of creation. The prosecution is led by Matthew Harrison Brady, a famous orator and three-time presidential candidate modelled on William Jennings Bryan, while Cates is defended by Henry Drummond, modelled on Clarence Darrow. The journalist E. K. Hornbeck, modelled on H. L. Mencken, covers the trial with cynical wit.

The climax comes when Drummond, barred from calling scientific witnesses, calls Brady himself to the stand as an expert on the Bible and questions him until Brady's literalist position collapses under its own contradictions. Cates is found guilty and fined $100, but Brady, humiliated and overwrought, dies shortly after the verdict — a fictional departure from the real Bryan, who died five days after the actual trial. The play ends with Drummond weighing the Bible and Darwin's On the Origin of Species in his hands and leaving with both books in his briefcase.

Productions and adaptations

The play premiered in Dallas in January 1955 and opened on Broadway in April 1955, running for 806 performances with Paul Muni, Ed Begley and Tony Randall. It was adapted into the acclaimed 1960 film directed by Stanley Kramer, starring Spencer Tracy as Drummond and Fredric March as Brady, and has been remade for television several times. The playwrights insisted the work was not history: characters were renamed and events altered deliberately, and the play has been criticised for its inaccurate portrait of Bryan, but it remains one of the most frequently performed American plays, regularly revived in debates over creationism in schools.
