Reproductive strategy

A reproductive strategy is the pattern by which an organism allocates resources to mating effort and parental effort across its lifetime: how many offspring it produces, how large they are, how often it breeds, and how much care it gives them. Reproductive strategies evolve by natural selection and reflect trade-offs, because energy spent on one offspring or one breeding attempt cannot be spent on another.

r/K selection

The classic framework, proposed by Robert MacArthur and E. O. Wilson and developed by Eric Pianka, contrasts r-selected species — small, fast-maturing, producing many cheaply made offspring in unstable environments (many insects, annual plants, oysters) — with K-selected species — large, long-lived, producing few offspring with heavy parental investment near the carrying capacity of stable environments (elephants, whales, humans). Modern life-history theory treats this as one axis of a continuum rather than a strict dichotomy, and adds dimensions such as age at maturity and reproductive lifespan.

Key trade-offs

  • Number versus size of offspring: seed and egg sizes vary predictably with the risks facing juveniles.
  • Current versus future reproduction: breeding hard now may reduce survival and later fecundity; some species are semelparous (breeding once, then dying, like Pacific salmon), while most are iteroparous (breeding repeatedly).
  • Mating versus parenting: in most animals females invest more in offspring, shaping sexual selection; in some fish and birds males provide most care.

Alternative strategies

Within a species, individuals may pursue alternative reproductive tactics — dominant territorial males versus sneaker males that mimic females, for instance — maintained by frequency-dependent selection when each tactic pays off best when rare.
