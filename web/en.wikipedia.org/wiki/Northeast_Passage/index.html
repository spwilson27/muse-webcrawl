The Northeast Passage is a shipping route between the Atlantic and Pacific oceans that runs along the Arctic coasts of Norway and Russia, through the Barents, Kara, Laptev, East Siberian and Chukchi seas to the Bering Strait. For centuries European merchants sought it as a shorter route to China and India than the long voyage around Africa. The portion within Russian territorial waters is administered by Russia as the Northern Sea Route.

History of exploration

English expeditions under the Company of Merchant Adventurers, including the 1553 voyage of Hugh Willoughby and Richard Chancellor, opened trade with Russia through the White Sea but failed to find a way east. Dutch navigator Willem Barentsz made three voyages in the 1590s, discovering Bear Island and Spitsbergen and wintering on Novaya Zemlya, where he died in 1597.

Russian Pomors and Cossack explorers gradually worked along the Siberian coast. Semyon Dezhnev sailed through the Bering Strait in 1648, and Vitus Bering's Great Northern Expedition (1733–1743) charted most of the Arctic coast. The first complete transit of the passage was made by the Finnish-Swedish explorer Adolf Erik Nordenskiöld aboard the Vega in 1878–79. Roald Amundsen completed the first single-expedition transit in 1918–20 with the Maud.

Modern use

The Soviet Union developed the Northern Sea Route with icebreakers to supply Arctic settlements and move Siberian resources. Melting sea ice in the 21st century has revived commercial interest: the route between Northern Europe and East Asia is roughly 40 per cent shorter than via the Suez Canal. Traffic remains seasonal and modest compared with the southern routes, and most voyages require ice-class vessels and Russian permits.
