The British Nationality Act 1948 was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that created the status of "Citizen of the United Kingdom and Colonies" (CUKC), the national citizenship of the United Kingdom and its colonies. It established a single common citizenship for the UK and the Empire while preserving the common status of British subject / Commonwealth citizen for citizens of all Commonwealth countries.

Background and provisions

Before 1949 British nationality rested on the common-law status of British subject. After Canada created its own citizenship in 1946, a Commonwealth conference agreed that each member would enact its own citizenship law beneath the shared umbrella of British subject status. The 1948 Act accordingly created CUKC status, acquired principally by birth in the UK or a colony, by descent, by registration or by naturalisation.

Crucially, the Act placed no restrictions on the movement of Commonwealth citizens: all British subjects retained the right to enter, live and work in the United Kingdom freely. This framework underpinned the post-war migration from the Caribbean, South Asia and Africa — symbolised by the arrival of the Empire Windrush in June 1948 — and the growth of multi-ethnic Britain.

Restriction and replacement

From the Commonwealth Immigrants Act 1962 onwards, successive statutes restricted the free entry that the 1948 settlement had preserved, linking the right of abode progressively to ancestry and birth in the UK. The British Nationality Act 1981, in force from 1983, repealed the 1948 Act and replaced CUKC with three categories: British citizenship, British Dependent Territories citizenship and British Overseas citizenship. Issues over the status of the Windrush generation under the old law resurfaced in the Windrush scandal of 2018.
