Precisionism

Precisionism was the first indigenous modern art movement in the United States, flourishing during the 1920s and 1930s. Influenced by Cubism, Purism, and Futurism, Precisionist artists reduced their subjects to simple geometric shapes and applied paint in smooth, unbroken planes, eliminating visible brushwork and detail. Their characteristic subjects celebrated the new American landscape of industry and technology: factories, smokestacks, bridges, dams, skyscrapers, silos, and farm machinery, rendered with a cool, almost abstract clarity.

Characteristics

Precisionist works are marked by sharply defined forms, immaculate surfaces, and an absence of human figures; light is clear and even, compositions are rigorously ordered, and the mood is often detached or ambiguous — both celebrating and quietly questioning American industrial progress. The movement's name, applied retrospectively by critics and the Museum of Modern Art, reflects this "precision" of execution.

Major artists and works

Charles Demuth's My Egypt (1927) and his poster portraits, and Charles Sheeler's paintings and photographs of the Ford River Rouge plant (1927–32) and American Landscape (1930), are canonical. Sheeler's dual career in painting and photography epitomised the movement's machine aesthetic. Other figures include Niles Spencer, Preston Dickinson, Louis Lozowick, Elsie Driggs — whose Pittsburgh (1927) shows steel mills as stark geometry — and Ralston Crawford.

Legacy

Precisionism fed into American Scene painting and anticipated elements of Pop art and Photorealism in its cool treatment of industrial and commercial imagery. Major retrospectives, notably at the Whitney Museum in 1960 and subsequent exhibitions, established it as a distinctively American contribution to interwar modernism.
