Maqam

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Instruments of the traditional Arabic takht ensemble.

Maqam (Arabic: مقام, plural مقامات maqāmāt) is the system of melodic modes used in Arabic music. A maqam is not merely a scale: it is a set of conventions governing pitch content, characteristic melodic phrases, points of emphasis, cadences and customary routes of modulation. The maqam underlies both composed and improvised genres, above all the vocal and instrumental improvisation known as taqsim.

Structure

Each maqam is built from smaller melodic units called ajnas (singular jins), trichords, tetrachords or pentachords joined together. The lower jins, built on the tonic, gives the maqam its essential character; the upper jins, usually beginning on the dominant (the ghammaz), determines favourite routes of modulation. Melodies typically begin by exploring the lower register around the tonic, rise gradually to the upper jins, and descend to a final cadence called qafla.

Tuning

Arabic music divides the octave into 24 equal quarter-tones in modern theory, though in practice intonation is flexible and regionally varied. Many maqamat contain neutral intervals — seconds and thirds lying between the Western semitone steps — which are played, for example, on the fretless oud. Important families include Hijaz, with its characteristic augmented second; Rast, considered a foundational maqam on natural notes with a half-flat third and seventh; Bayati; Saba, noted for its melancholy colour; and Sikah, which begins on a half-flat degree.

Modulation and performance

Skilled performers modulate (intiqal) between related maqamat according to well-understood affinities of shared ajnas, tracing a long melodic journey before returning to the home maqam. The listener's emotional response, culminating in the ecstatic state called tarab, depends heavily on this play of expectation and resolution. The maqam system is documented in treatises from al-Farabi and Safi al-Din al-Urmawi onward, and was systematised in the twentieth century, notably at the 1932 Cairo Congress of Arab Music.

Related systems

Related modal frameworks across the region include the Turkish makam, the Persian dastgah, the Azerbaijani mugham and the Central Asian shashmaqam. Although these systems share historical roots and some modal names, the Arabic maqam remains distinct in its jins-based theory, its melodic conventions and its repertoire.
