Lysosome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A lysosome is a membrane-bound organelle in animal cells that contains digestive enzymes capable of breaking down proteins, nucleic acids, lipids and carbohydrates. Sometimes called the cell's waste-disposal and recycling system, it maintains an acidic interior (about pH 4.5–5) at which its enzymes work best. Lysosomes were discovered by Christian de Duve in the 1950s.

Function

Lysosomes fuse with vesicles carrying worn-out organelles (autophagy), material taken in by endocytosis, and engulfed microbes (in phagocytes), digesting the contents so the building blocks can be reused. The enzymes, acid hydrolases, are made in the Endoplasmic reticulum, tagged in the Golgi apparatus with mannose-6-phosphate, and delivered to lysosomes.

Disease

Inherited defects of single lysosomal enzymes cause the lysosomal storage diseases — Tay–Sachs, Gaucher and Pompe disease among them — in which undegraded material accumulates in cells. Lysosomal dysfunction is also implicated in ageing and neurodegeneration.
