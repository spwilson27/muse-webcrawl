Helen Keller

Helen Adams Keller (June 27, 1880 – June 1, 1968) was an American author, disability rights advocate, political activist and lecturer. Born in Tuscumbia, Alabama, she lost her sight and hearing after an illness at the age of nineteen months. She then communicated primarily through home signs until the age of seven, when she met her first teacher and life-long companion Anne Sullivan, who taught her language, including reading and writing.

Keller became the first deafblind person to earn a Bachelor of Arts degree.

Early life and education

At the age of seven, Keller's frustration at being unable to communicate led her family to seek help from the Perkins School for the Blind, which sent Anne Sullivan, herself visually impaired. The famous breakthrough came at the water pump in April 1887, when Sullivan spelled "w-a-t-e-r" into one hand while running water over the other, and Keller grasped that the finger movements named things. Keller attended schools in Boston and New York and entered Radcliffe College, graduating cum laude in 1904 — the first deafblind person to earn a Bachelor of Arts.

Career and activism

Keller's autobiography The Story of My Life (1903), written while she was still a student, made her internationally famous; it was later dramatised as William Gibson's play and film The Miracle Worker. She wrote a dozen books and numerous essays, lectured around the world, and co-founded the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) in 1920. Keller was a socialist and an advocate for women's suffrage, labour rights and birth control; she worked for the American Foundation for the Blind for over forty years and campaigned tirelessly for the education and employment of disabled people.

Legacy

Keller received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1964. Her birthplace, Ivy Green in Tuscumbia, is a museum, and statues of Keller — including one depicting the water-pump moment — stand in the U.S. Capitol's National Statuary Hall collection representing Alabama.
