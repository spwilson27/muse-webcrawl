Goldwater–Nichols Act

The Goldwater–Nichols Department of Defense Reorganization Act of 1986 (Pub. L. 99–433) made the most sweeping changes to the United States Department of Defense since the department was established in 1947. Named for Senator Barry Goldwater and Representative William Flynt "Bill" Nichols, it was enacted on October 1, 1986, and greatly increased the powers of the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff and of the commanders of the unified combatant commands.

Background

The act responded to operational failures attributed to interservice rivalry and fragmented command, including the failed Iran hostage rescue mission of 1980 and coordination problems during the 1983 invasion of Grenada. Studies through the early 1980s argued that the military services planned and fought largely separately, weakening joint operations.

Provisions

  • Made the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff the principal military adviser to the President, the National Security Council, and the Secretary of Defense.
  • Streamlined the operational chain of command from the President through the Secretary of Defense directly to the combatant commanders, who gained authority over all assigned forces.
  • Created the position of Vice Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff.
  • Reformed officer personnel policy to reward joint duty and to require joint experience for promotion to general or flag rank.

Effects

The act is widely credited with making joint operations the norm in the U.S. military, evident in the coalition campaigns of the Gulf War (1991) onward. Later debates have considered whether the joint system it entrenched has grown overly bureaucratic.
