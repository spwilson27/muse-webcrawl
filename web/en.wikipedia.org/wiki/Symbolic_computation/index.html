Symbolic computation

Symbolic computation, also known as symbolic manipulation, algebraic computation or computer algebra, is the use of computers to manipulate mathematical expressions in symbolic form rather than as numeric approximations. The symbols can represent numbers, variables, or arbitrary mathematical objects, and the operations include simplification, differentiation, integration, equation solving, factorisation and exact arithmetic with integers, rationals and algebraic numbers.

Overview

Whereas numerical computation works with approximate floating-point values, symbolic computation aims at exact, closed-form results: differentiating x² yields 2x, not an approximate slope. This makes it invaluable in mathematics, physics and engineering, though intermediate expressions can grow enormously ("expression swell"), and some problems, such as general symbolic integration, are only partially solvable.

Computer algebra systems

The field began in the 1960s with systems such as Macsyma at MIT and Reduce. Modern general-purpose computer algebra systems include Mathematica, Maple, SageMath, Maxima, Axiom, and libraries such as SymPy for Python. Specialised systems address areas such as number theory (PARI/GP), commutative algebra and algebraic geometry (Singular, Macaulay2) and group theory (GAP).

Methods

Core algorithms include greatest-common-divisor computation for polynomials, Gröbner bases for systems of polynomial equations, the Risch algorithm for indefinite integration, and algorithms for factorisation over finite fields and the integers.
