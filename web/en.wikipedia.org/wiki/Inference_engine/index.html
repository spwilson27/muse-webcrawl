Inference engine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Structure of a rule-based inference engine.

In computer science, an inference engine is a component of a computer system that applies logical rules to a knowledge base to deduce new information. Inference engines are a core component of expert systems, in which the knowledge base typically consists of facts and if–then rules supplied by domain specialists. The inference engine interprets and evaluates the facts in the knowledge base in order to provide an answer, prediction, or decision.

Architecture

A classic inference engine operates over two components: the knowledge base (facts and rules) and working memory (assertions derived during a run). The engine cycles through matching rules whose conditions are satisfied by working memory, selecting among conflicting matches (conflict resolution), and firing the chosen rule, which adds new assertions or triggers actions. Efficient pattern matching is provided by algorithms such as Rete, developed by Charles Forgy for the OPS5 production system.

Chaining strategies

Forward chaining

Forward chaining is data-driven: the engine starts from known facts and applies rules to derive everything that follows, until a goal statement is derived or no rules can fire. It suits monitoring, configuration, and classification tasks where all data is present at the outset.

Backward chaining

Backward chaining is goal-driven: the engine starts from a hypothesis and works backwards, looking for rules that conclude the hypothesis and then trying to establish their premises, asking the user for facts it cannot derive. It is the strategy used by the logic programming language Prolog and suits diagnostic and consultation tasks.

Applications

Inference engines powered landmark expert systems such as MYCIN (medical diagnosis), DENDRAL (chemical analysis), and XCON/R1 (computer configuration at DEC). Modern descendants include business-rules engines (such as Drools), production-rule systems (such as CLIPS, developed at NASA), and the reasoners used in the semantic web to infer class memberships and relationships from ontologies expressed in RDF and OWL. Description-logic reasoners classify large terminologies in medicine and biology.

Relation to theorem proving

Inference engines overlap with automated theorem provers, but are typically optimised for practical rule sets and explanation facilities rather than completeness over arbitrary logical theories. Many provide an explanation subsystem that can show which rules fired, answering "why" and "how" questions from users — a feature considered essential for trust in advisory systems.
