Tenecteplase

Trade namesTNKase, Metalyse
TypeThrombolytic agent (tissue plasminogen activator)
RoutesIntravenous (single bolus)

DeveloperGenentech

Tenecteplase (sold under the trade names TNKase and Metalyse) is a thrombolytic drug used to dissolve blood clots in acute myocardial infarction (heart attack) and, increasingly, in acute ischaemic stroke. It is an engineered variant of tissue plasminogen activator (tPA), produced by recombinant DNA technology in Chinese hamster ovary cells.

Design

Tenecteplase differs from the native enzyme alteplase by six amino-acid substitutions at three sites, from which its abbreviation TNK derives: a threonine (T) replaced by asparagine at position 103, an asparagine (N) replaced by glutamine at position 117, and a tetra-alanine substitution (K) at positions 296–299. These changes give the molecule a longer half-life, markedly greater fibrin specificity, and increased resistance to plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) compared with alteplase.

Clinical use

Like other plasminogen activators, tenecteplase converts plasminogen to plasmin, which degrades the fibrin mesh of a thrombus. Because of its longer half-life it can be given as a single weight-adjusted intravenous bolus over about five seconds, rather than the prolonged infusion required for alteplase — a practical advantage in emergency and pre-hospital care.

Tenecteplase was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2000 for reducing mortality associated with acute myocardial infarction. The ASSENT-2 trial found it equivalent to alteplase in 30-day mortality with fewer non-cerebral bleeding complications. From the 2010s onward, trials such as NOR-TEST, EXTEND-IA TNK, and AcT established its role in ischaemic stroke thrombolysis, where its ease of administration — particularly before interhospital transfer for thrombectomy ("drip and ship") — led many stroke centres to adopt it in preference to alteplase; the FDA approved it for acute ischaemic stroke in 2025.

Adverse effects

The principal risk is bleeding, including haemorrhagic transformation of an infarct and intracranial haemorrhage. As with all thrombolytics, treatment requires careful screening for contraindications such as recent surgery, active bleeding, or uncontrolled hypertension.
