Jacob Riis

Jacob August Riis (May 3, 1849 – May 26, 1914) was a Danish-American social reformer, "muck-raking" journalist, and social documentary photographer. He is known for using his photographic and journalistic talents to help the impoverished in New York City. His best-known work, How the Other Half Lives (1890), documented the squalid living conditions in New York City slums and prompted reforms of tenement housing.

Early life

Riis was born in Ribe, Denmark, in 1849, the third of fifteen children. He emigrated to the United States in 1870 with little money, arriving in New York and working odd jobs, experiencing the poverty he would later document. After nearly returning to Denmark, he found work as a carpenter and later as a police reporter for the New-York Tribune, where he covered the city's slums and tenements.

Career

As a police reporter, Riis saw firsthand the conditions of the tenements and became determined to publicize them. Recognizing that words alone had limited impact, he taught himself photography, using the new flash powder technology to photograph the dark interiors of tenements, lodging houses, and sweatshops. He gave lantern-slide lectures illustrated with his photographs, and in 1890 published How the Other Half Lives: Studies Among the Tenements of New York.

The book's vivid descriptions and photographs shocked middle-class readers and spurred reform: tenement laws were strengthened, sweatshops inspected, and the notorious Mulberry Bend slum was demolished and replaced by a park. Riis went on to publish other books, including The Children of the Poor (1892) and his autobiography, The Making of an American (1901).

Legacy

Riis is considered a pioneer of documentary photography and photojournalism. He maintained a friendship with Theodore Roosevelt, whom he considered "the most useful citizen of New York." Places named after him include Jacob Riis Park in Queens and a housing project in Manhattan. He died in 1914 in Barre, Massachusetts.
