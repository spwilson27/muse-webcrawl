A film format is a technical definition of a set of standard characteristics regarding image capture on photographic film, for either stills or filmmaking. It can also apply to projected film, slide photography, or digital image sensors. The primary feature of a film format is its size and shape.

Still photography formats

Formats are conventionally grouped into small, medium, and large categories. The most widely used still format is 135 film (35 mm), introduced by Leica and popularised through the 20th century; smaller formats include 110 and the Advanced Photo System (APS). Medium format uses 120 or 220 roll film, producing negatives of 6×4.5 cm up to 6×17 cm depending on the camera. Large format uses individual sheets of film, commonly 4×5 inches and 8×10 inches, in view cameras, giving very high image quality and camera movements for perspective control.

Motion picture formats

Cinematography historically used 35 mm film as its professional standard, with 16 mm for lower-budget and television production and 65/70 mm for high-resolution exhibition formats. Image area on the film varies with aspect ratio and the space reserved for soundtracks; anamorphic formats squeeze a wide image onto the film using cylindrical lenses.

Characteristics

Larger formats record more detail and shallower depth of field at a given aperture, at the cost of size, cost, and slower operation. In digital photography, sensor sizes are frequently named by analogy with film formats, such as "full frame" (equivalent to the 135 frame) and "medium format".
