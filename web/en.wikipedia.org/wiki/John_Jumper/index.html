John M. Jumper (born 1985) is an American chemist and computer scientist who directs work on protein structure prediction at Google DeepMind. He led the team that developed AlphaFold 2, the artificial intelligence system that in 2020 effectively solved the fifty-year-old "protein folding problem" by predicting the three-dimensional structures of proteins from their amino acid sequences with near-experimental accuracy. In 2024 he shared one half of the Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Demis Hassabis "for protein structure prediction," the other half going to David Baker for computational protein design.

Education and early career

Jumper grew up in Arkansas and earned a B.S. in physics and mathematics from Vanderbilt University in 2007. He won a Marshall Scholarship to the University of Cambridge, where he took a master's degree in theoretical condensed matter physics, then completed a Ph.D. in theoretical chemistry at the University of Chicago in 2017. There, and during work at D. E. Shaw Research in New York and at the University of Chicago, he developed computational methods for simulating protein dynamics and enhanced sampling of molecular systems.

AlphaFold

Jumper joined DeepMind in 2017 and soon took scientific leadership of the AlphaFold project. The team's AlphaFold 1 won the CASP13 assessment in 2018; the redesigned AlphaFold 2, unveiled at CASP14 in November–December 2020, achieved a median backbone accuracy competitive with experimental methods for the great majority of targets, a result hailed as revolutionary for structural biology. In 2021 DeepMind released the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database with EMBL-EBI, making predicted structures freely available — growing to over 200 million proteins. Jumper led further development of AlphaFold 3 (2024), which models interactions between proteins and other molecules such as DNA, RNA and small-molecule ligands.

Recognition

Among many honours, Jumper received the Breakthrough Prize in Life Sciences (2023), the Lasker Award for Basic Medical Research (2023, with Hassabis), the Canada Gairdner International Award, and election to the National Academy of Sciences. The 2024 Nobel Prize made him one of the youngest chemistry laureates and recognised AI-driven prediction as a fundamental scientific achievement.
