Homomorphism

Homomorphism
A homomorphism maps one algebraic structure to another while preserving its operations
FieldAbstract algebra, category theory
Related conceptsIsomorphism, endomorphism, automorphism, morphism

In algebra, a homomorphism is a structure-preserving map between two algebraic structures of the same type (such as two groups, two rings, or two vector spaces). The word homomorphism comes from the ancient Greek words ὁμός (homos) meaning "same" and μορφή (morphē) meaning "form" or "shape".

A homomorphism is a map between two algebraic structures of the same type that preserves the operations of the structures. This means a map f : A → B between two sets A, B equipped with the same structure such that, if ∗ is an operation of the structure, then:

f(x ∗ y) = f(x) ∗ f(y)

for every pair x, y of elements of A. One says often that f preserves the operation, or is compatible with the operation.

Examples

The exponential function is a homomorphism from the additive group of real numbers to the multiplicative group of positive real numbers, since exp(x + y) = exp(x) · exp(y). The determinant is a homomorphism from the group of invertible matrices under multiplication to the multiplicative group of non-zero scalars. A linear map between vector spaces is a homomorphism of vector spaces, preserving both addition and scalar multiplication. Reduction of integers modulo n is a ring homomorphism from ℤ onto ℤ/nℤ.

Special homomorphisms

  • Isomorphism — a bijective homomorphism whose inverse is also a homomorphism; the two structures are then essentially the same.
  • Endomorphism — a homomorphism from a structure to itself.
  • Automorphism — an endomorphism that is also an isomorphism.
  • Monomorphism / epimorphism — injective and surjective homomorphisms respectively (with category-theoretic generalisations).

Kernel and image

Every homomorphism has a kernel — the set of elements mapped to the identity (or zero) element — and an image, the set of elements actually attained. The first isomorphism theorem states that the quotient of the domain by the kernel is isomorphic to the image, a result that underpins much of abstract algebra. In category theory the notion generalises: homomorphisms are the morphisms within a concrete category of structured sets.

History

The notion crystallised in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as algebra shifted from computation to the study of structures, in the work of Dedekind, Noether, and the founders of modern group and ring theory.
