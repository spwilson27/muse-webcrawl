Madame Bovary

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Emma Bovary at the ball at Vaubyessard, in an early illustration: the provincial doctor's wife glimpsing the glittering world she craves]

Madame Bovary: Provincial Manners is the debut novel of Gustave Flaubert, published in book form in 1857 after serialisation in the Revue de Paris (1856) — and prosecuted, unsuccessfully, for offence to public morality. It is a cornerstone of literary realism and of the modern novel's art: the story of Emma Bovary, a farmer's daughter married to a dull provincial doctor, who seeks escape from the boredom of Yonville in adulteries and purchases, and finds debt and ruin.

Flaubert's fanatical pursuit of le mot juste and his impersonal, free-indirect style — sliding between narrator and character's mind within a sentence — made the book the model for the novel as an art of prose. His reported remark that 'Madame Bovary is me' is among literature's most quoted authorial confessions.

The trial and the style

Serialised with cuts the editors insisted on, the novel was prosecuted in January 1857 for its supposed glorification of adultery; Flaubert was acquitted, and the publicity made the book. Flaubert had drafted it over five years of agony, reading sentences aloud in his study at Croisset to test their rhythm — the famous gueuloir. Its set pieces — the agricultural show, where Rodolphe's seduction is intercut with prize announcements — are textbooks of ironic counterpoint.

Influence

'Bovarysme' — the romantic dissatisfaction Emma embodies — entered French as a common noun. James, Proust, Nabokov and Vargas Llosa (whose The Perpetual Orgy is a book-length homage) testify to its formative power; it is the ancestor of the modern adultery novel and of realism's claim that style itself is a moral instrument.
