457 plan

A 457 plan lets public-sector employees defer income into a tax-advantaged retirement account.

A 457 plan is a type of non-qualified, tax-advantaged deferred compensation retirement plan available for governmental and certain non-governmental employers in the United States. It takes its name from section 457 of the Internal Revenue Code. Employees of state and local governments, school districts, public universities, hospitals, and certain tax-exempt organizations may defer part of their salary into the plan, reducing current taxable income.

Types of plans

The most common form is the 457(b) or "eligible" plan, which must comply with statutory requirements on contribution limits, distributions, and funding. Governmental 457(b) assets must be held in trust for the exclusive benefit of participants, and may be rolled over to IRAs and other plans. Assets of a non-governmental (tax-exempt employer's) 457(b) remain the property of the employer and are subject to its creditors, and participants are generally limited to highly compensated employees. 457(f) or "ineligible" plans are used by tax-exempt organizations to provide supplemental benefits to executives, with amounts taxable when the substantial risk of forfeiture lapses.

Contributions and distributions

Elective deferrals are limited to an annual dollar cap set by the IRS (the same basic limit as for 401(k) and 403(b) plans), with additional catch-up contributions permitted near retirement — 457(b) plans have long offered a special catch-up allowing up to double the normal limit in the last three years before normal retirement age. Many governmental plans also accept designated Roth contributions. Unlike 401(k) plans, distributions from a governmental 457(b) after separation from service are not subject to the 10% early-withdrawal penalty, a feature often valued by public-safety employees who retire early.

History

Section 457 was enacted by the Revenue Act of 1978, largely to codify the tax treatment of deferred compensation for state and local government employees. The Tax Reform Act of 1986 extended it to tax-exempt organizations, and the Small Business Job Protection Act of 1996 required governmental plans to hold assets in trust.
