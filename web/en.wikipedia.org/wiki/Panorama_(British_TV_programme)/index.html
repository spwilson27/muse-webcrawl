Panorama is a British current affairs documentary television programme broadcast by the BBC. First broadcast in 1953, it is the world's longest-running television news programme and the BBC's flagship investigative strand, shown in a prime-time evening slot on BBC One. Panorama films have covered wars, politics, organised crime, health and science, and its reports have repeatedly influenced public debate and policy in the United Kingdom.

History

Panorama began on 11 November 1953 as a magazine-style programme. It found its enduring identity under presenter Richard Dimbleby, whose authoritative interviewing and reporting from the mid-1950s established its reputation; his son David Dimbleby later also fronted the programme. An April Fools' Day edition in 1957 — the famous "spaghetti-tree hoax", a mock report on the Swiss spaghetti harvest — became one of the most celebrated broadcasts in British television history.

Landmark editions over the decades have included interviews with heads of government and royalty (including the 1995 interview with Diana, Princess of Wales, watched by more than 20 million people in the UK), undercover investigations into football corruption, care-home abuse and racism, and extensive reporting from war zones including Vietnam, Northern Ireland, Iraq and Afghanistan.

Format

Modern Panorama consists of single-subject films, usually about 30 minutes (occasionally an hour), fronted by a reporter rather than a studio host. Reporters associated with the strand include John Ware, Jane Corbin, Richard Bilton and Darragh MacIntyre. The programme has won numerous BAFTA and Royal Television Society awards for investigative journalism, though some editions have attracted controversy, including disputes over editing and over undercover methods.
