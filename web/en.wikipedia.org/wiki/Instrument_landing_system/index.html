FunctionPrecision approach guidance

An instrument landing system (ILS) is a precision radio navigation system that provides short-range guidance to aircraft, enabling them to approach a runway at night or in bad weather. It provides both horizontal and vertical guidance: a localizer signal indicates alignment with the runway centerline, and a glide slope (glide path) signal indicates the correct descent angle, typically 3 degrees.

Principle of operation

The localizer transmitter, located beyond the far end of the runway, emits signals in the 108.10–111.95 MHz band that are modulated at 90 and 150 Hz; the difference in depth of modulation tells the receiver how far left or right the aircraft is from the centerline. The glide slope station beside the touchdown zone transmits in the 329–335 MHz band with a similar modulation scheme to mark the descent path. Marker beacons (outer, middle, and inner) historically signaled distance from the threshold, though these are increasingly replaced or supplemented by distance-measuring equipment (DME). Cockpit instruments display deviations on a course deviation indicator or flight director, and modern airliners can fly fully coupled autoland approaches.

Categories

ILS approaches are classified by decision height and visibility minima: Category I (decision height not lower than 200 ft, runway visual range at least 550 m), Category II (100 ft / 300 m), and Category III — subdivided a, b, and c — permitting operations down to essentially zero visibility with autoland and rollout guidance. Higher categories demand redundant airborne equipment, specially trained crews, protected signal areas, and specific ground infrastructure such as touchdown-zone and centerline lighting.

History and alternatives

ILS was developed in the 1930s–40s and standardized internationally by ICAO in 1949; it remains the most common precision approach aid worldwide. Satellite-based augmentation (GBAS/GLS) and performance-based navigation approaches increasingly supplement or replace ILS at some airports, though ILS remains the backbone of all-weather operations.
