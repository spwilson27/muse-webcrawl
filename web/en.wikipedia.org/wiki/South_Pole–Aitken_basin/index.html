South Pole–Aitken basin

The South Pole–Aitken basin (SPA Basin) is an immense impact crater on the far side of the Moon. At roughly 2,500 kilometres (1,600 miles) in diameter and between 6.2 and 8.2 kilometres deep, it is the largest, deepest, and one of the oldest known impact structures on the Moon, and one of the largest known impact craters in the Solar System. It is named for the two features on its extremes: the lunar south pole at one end and the crater Aitken at the other.

Discovery and characteristics

The basin's existence was inferred from early photographs of the lunar far side and confirmed by data from missions including Lunar Orbiter, Clementine, and Lunar Prospector. Clementine's altimetry showed the dramatic depth variation between the basin floor and the surrounding highlands. Because the crater is so large relative to the Moon, its rim and structure are not obvious from the surface and are best appreciated from orbital data.

The basin is thought to have formed about 4.2 to 4.3 billion years ago during the heavy bombardment of the early Solar System. The impact may have excavated material from deep in the lunar crust, and possibly the upper mantle, making samples from the basin scientifically valuable for understanding the Moon's interior and early history. Compositional data suggest the basin interior has a distinctive iron-rich signature compared with other lunar regions.

Exploration

In January 2019, China's Chang'e 4 lander and Yutu-2 rover made the first soft landing on the far side of the Moon, touching down in Von Kármán crater within the South Pole–Aitken basin. In 2024, Chang'e 6 returned the first samples collected from the basin region to Earth. The region around the lunar south pole is also a focus for future crewed exploration because permanently shadowed craters there harbour water ice, while nearby peaks receive near-constant sunlight.
