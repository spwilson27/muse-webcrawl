Virchow's triad

Virchow's triad describes the three broad categories of factors that contribute to thrombosis — the formation of blood clots within blood vessels — as identified from the work of the German physician Rudolf Virchow (1821–1902).

The three components

The triad consists of:

  • Endothelial injury or dysfunction — damage to the vessel wall, for example from trauma, surgery, inflammation, or atherosclerosis, which exposes procoagulant surfaces.
  • Stasis or abnormal blood flow — sluggish or turbulent flow, as in prolonged immobility, long-haul travel, or atrial fibrillation, allowing clotting factors to accumulate.
  • Hypercoagulability — inherited or acquired changes in blood composition that increase clotting tendency, such as Factor V Leiden, antiphospholipid syndrome, pregnancy, malignancy, or oestrogen therapy.

History and use

Although Virchow described the relevant phenomena in his work on thrombosis and embolism in the 1850s, he never set them out as a single named triad; the eponym and three-part formulation were coined by later writers and became standard in medical teaching. The triad remains a useful framework for understanding risks of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.
