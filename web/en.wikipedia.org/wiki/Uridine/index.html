Uridine

IUPAC name1-[(2R,3R,4S,5R)-3,4-dihydroxy-5-(hydroxymethyl)oxolan-2-yl]pyrimidine-2,4-dione
FormulaC9H12N2O6
Molar mass244.20 g/mol
ComponentsUracil + ribose

Uridine is a glycosylated pyrimidine analogue containing the nucleobase uracil attached to a ribose sugar ring via a β-N1-glycosidic bond. It is one of the five standard nucleosides that make up nucleic acids, and is the characteristic nucleoside of RNA, in which it pairs with adenosine through two hydrogen bonds. The corresponding DNA component is thymidine, which bears a methylated base.

Biochemistry

Uridine is phosphorylated by uridine-cytidine kinases to uridine monophosphate (UMP), the precursor of all other pyrimidine nucleotides. Addition of phosphates yields UDP and UTP; UDP-sugars such as UDP-glucose and UDP-galactose are central to glycogen synthesis and glycosylation reactions. In RNA, uridine residues are frequently modified post-transcriptionally: isomerisation yields pseudouridine (Ψ), the most abundant modified nucleoside, whose stabilising properties made modified nucleosides such as N1-methylpseudouridine valuable in mRNA vaccine technology.

Sources and uses

Dietary uridine occurs in RNA-rich foods such as organ meats, certain vegetables, beer, and brewer's yeast, though most is catabolised in the gut and liver. Pharmacologically, uridine and its prodrug uridine triacetate are used to treat hereditary orotic aciduria and as an antidote to fluorouracil toxicity and overdose. Uridine has also been investigated, alone and combined with omega-3 fatty acids and choline, for effects on cognition and mood, though evidence remains preliminary.
