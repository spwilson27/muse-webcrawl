Mettā (Pali: mettā; Sanskrit: maitrī) is a Buddhist concept meaning benevolence, loving-kindness, friendliness, amity, goodwill, and active interest in others. It is one of the ten pāramīs of Theravāda Buddhism and the first of the four sublime states, or brahmavihārās, alongside compassion (karuṇā), sympathetic joy (muditā), and equanimity (upekkhā). The cultivation of mettā, known as mettā bhāvanā, is a popular form of Buddhist meditation.

Mettā meditation

In mettā bhāvanā, the practitioner directs well-wishing silently toward a sequence of recipients: first oneself, then a benefactor, then a neutral person, then a difficult person, and finally all beings without exception. A common formula uses phrases such as "May I be happy; may I be free from suffering," extended outward to the other recipients. The practice is described in the Visuddhimagga, Buddhaghosa's fifth-century manual of meditation, and is rooted in canonical texts such as the Karaniya Metta Sutta, one of the most widely recited discourses in Theravāda Buddhism.

Canonical sources

The Metta Sutta urges that one's mind of goodwill be "boundless," comparing it to a mother's love for her only child. The Buddha is recorded as teaching that the release of the heart through loving-kindness brings eleven benefits, including sleeping and waking in comfort, being dear to humans and non-humans, and protection from fire, poison, and weapons. In the discourses, mettā is also presented as an antidote to ill will (vyāpāda), one of the five hindrances to meditation.

Mettā and maitrī in other traditions

The Sanskrit cognate maitrī appears in Mahāyāna Buddhism as one of the four immeasurables and is personified in Maitreya, the future Buddha, whose name derives from it. Related concepts appear in Hindu and Jain practice: Patañjali's Yoga Sūtras list maitrī among the attitudes that calm the mind, and Jain texts commend friendliness toward all living beings as part of right conduct.

Modern research

Loving-kindness meditation has been studied in clinical and contemplative neuroscience research, with studies reporting associations with increased positive emotion, reduced symptoms of chronic pain and post-traumatic stress, and greater social connectedness. It forms a component of several secular mindfulness-based programmes.
