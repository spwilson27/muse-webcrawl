Building insulation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Purpose: Reduce heat transfer and energy use
Measured by: R-value (thermal resistance)

Building insulation is material used in a building to reduce the flow of thermal energy and provide resistance to heat flow. Insulation in buildings is an important factor in achieving thermal comfort for occupants while reducing energy consumption for heating and cooling, and it also contributes to sound control and moisture management.

Principles

Most insulation works by trapping still air or another low-conductivity gas in pockets within a fibrous or cellular material, greatly reducing conduction and convection. Reflective barriers instead reduce radiant heat transfer. Performance is quantified by the R-value: the higher the R-value per unit thickness, the better the insulation. Correct installation matters—gaps, compression and air leakage can sharply reduce real-world performance.

Materials

  • Fibreglass — batts, rolls and loose fill; the most common residential insulation.
  • Mineral wool — rock or slag wool; fire-resistant and sound-absorbing.
  • Cellulose — recycled paper fibre, usually blown in.
  • Foam boards — polystyrene (EPS/XPS) and polyisocyanurate rigid panels.
  • Spray polyurethane foam — open-cell and closed-cell, also seals air leaks.
  • Natural materials — sheep's wool, cork, hemp and straw.

Applications

Insulation is installed in walls, roofs and attics, floors and foundations. Building codes in most developed countries specify minimum insulation levels, and retrofitting insulation is among the most cost-effective measures for improving the energy efficiency of existing buildings.
