Submarine landslide

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Submarine landslides are marine landslides that transport large volumes of sediment across the continental shelf and down the continental slope into the deep ocean. They range from small slumps to some of the largest mass movements on Earth, involving hundreds of cubic kilometres of material and run-out distances of hundreds of kilometres across the abyssal plain. As deposits break up and mix with water, they commonly evolve into turbidity currents, which carve submarine canyons and build deep-sea fans.

Causes

Submarine landslides are typically triggered when sediment accumulates faster than it can consolidate — for example at river deltas and glacial margins — leaving weak, over-pressured layers. Earthquakes are a frequent trigger; others include storm-wave loading, rapid sea-level change, gas hydrate dissociation, and volcanic flank collapse. The resulting failures can occur on slopes of only one or two degrees.

Notable examples and hazards

The prehistoric Storegga Slide off Norway, about 8,100 years ago, moved an estimated 2,400–3,200 km³ of sediment and generated a tsunami that inundated coastlines around the North Atlantic, leaving deposits in Scotland and the Faroe Islands. In 1929, an earthquake off Newfoundland triggered the Grand Banks landslide and turbidity current, which severed transatlantic telegraph cables in sequence and generated a tsunami that killed 28 people in Newfoundland. In 1998, a submarine landslide off Papua New Guinea generated a tsunami that killed more than 2,100 people. Submarine landslides also pose risks to offshore oil and gas infrastructure, pipelines, and seafloor communication cables.
