Secretin is a peptide hormone of 27 amino acids produced by the S cells of the duodenum, which are located in the intestinal glands. It regulates water homeostasis throughout the body and influences the environment of the duodenum by regulating secretions in the stomach, pancreas, and liver. Secretin was the first hormone to be identified: in 1902 William Bayliss and Ernest Starling showed that acid in the duodenum caused the pancreas to secrete fluid even when all nerves to the organ were cut, demonstrating chemical (rather than purely nervous) control; Starling coined the word "hormone" for such messengers in 1905.

Function

Secretin is released when acidic chyme from the stomach lowers duodenal pH below about 4.5. Its principal actions are to stimulate the pancreas to secrete a bicarbonate-rich fluid that neutralises gastric acid, to stimulate bile production and bicarbonate secretion by the liver, and to inhibit gastric acid secretion and gastric motility, thereby protecting the duodenal mucosa and creating suitable conditions for intestinal enzymes. Secretin also has effects on osmoregulation, acting on the hypothalamus, pituitary and kidney.

Structure and synthesis

Human secretin is encoded by the SCT gene and is synthesised as part of a larger precursor, preprosecretin, which is processed to the mature 27-amino-acid peptide. Secretin belongs to the secretin/glucagon family of peptides, which also includes glucagon, vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), and gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP). It acts through the secretin receptor, a class B G protein-coupled receptor that raises intracellular cyclic AMP.

Clinical significance

Synthetic secretin is used diagnostically, for example in the secretin stimulation test for gastrinoma (Zollinger–Ellison syndrome) and in imaging and function tests of the pancreas. In the late 1990s secretin was controversially promoted as a treatment for autism on the basis of anecdotal reports, but controlled trials found no benefit over placebo.
