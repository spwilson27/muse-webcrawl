History of animation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Early animation relied on thousands of hand-drawn frames.

The history of animation stretches from pre-cinematic optical toys — the phenakistiscope (1832), zoetrope and Reynaud's praxinoscope — through the hand-drawn films of the silent era, the golden age of American studio cartoons, the rise of Japanese anime and television animation, to computer animation, which from Pixar's Toy Story (1995) became the dominant commercial form.

Origins and silent era

Émile Reynaud projected painted image bands to audiences from 1892 with his Théâtre Optique. J. Stuart Blackton's Humorous Phases of Funny Faces (1906) and Émile Cohl's Fantasmagorie (1908) pioneered drawn film animation; Winsor McCay's Gertie the Dinosaur (1914) showed its expressive potential. Earl Hurd's cel patent (1914) and the Bray studio's assembly-line methods industrialised production, and Felix the Cat became the first animated star of the 1920s.

Golden age and television

Disney's synchronised-sound Steamboat Willie (1928), the three-strip Technicolor Flowers and Trees (1932) and Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937) defined the medium's ambitions; Warner Bros., MGM and UPA developed contrasting styles. Fleischer Studios contributed rotoscoping and Popeye. After the war, limited animation for television (Hanna-Barbera) cut costs, while in Japan Osamu Tezuka's Astro Boy (1963) launched the anime industry. National film boards — notably Norman McLaren in Canada — sustained experimental animation.

Computer animation

Computer graphics entered features with Tron (1982) and brief CGI sequences; Pixar's fully computer-animated Toy Story (1995) began a shift that displaced hand-drawn features in Hollywood by the mid-2000s, even as stop-motion (Aardman, Laika) and hand-drawn anime continued. Motion capture, real-time game engines and streaming-era adult animation further diversified the field in the 21st century.
