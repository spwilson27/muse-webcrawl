Isle of Portland

Isle of Portland

Location: Dorset, England; English Channel

Length: about 6 km (4 miles)

Population: about 13,000

Settlements: Fortuneswell, Easton, Southwell, Wakeham

Known for: Portland stone, Portland Bill lighthouse

The Isle of Portland is a tied island in the English Channel off the coast of Dorset, England, connected to the mainland at Abbotsbury by Chesil Beach, a long shingle tombolo. About 6 kilometres long, Portland is famous worldwide as the source of Portland stone, the white-grey limestone used in St Paul's Cathedral, Buckingham Palace, the United Nations headquarters in New York and countless other landmark buildings.

Geography

The island slopes from a height of about 150 metres at the northern Verne to sea level at Portland Bill in the south. Its coastline is rocky and indented, with old quarry workings along the cliffs; the disused quarries are now important for wildlife and climbing. Off the Bill, tidal races produce the dangerous stretch of water known as the Portland Race. Portland Harbour, formed by breakwaters completed in the 19th century and expanded for the Royal Navy, is one of the largest artificial harbours in the world; together with Weymouth it hosted the sailing events of the 2012 Olympic Games, based at the Weymouth and Portland National Sailing Academy.

History

Portland has been inhabited since at least Mesolithic times. Henry VIII built Portland Castle at Castletown in 1539–41 to guard the anchorage. Quarrying of Portland stone expanded enormously after the Great Fire of London (1666), when Christopher Wren chose it for rebuilding St Paul's; Wren, who was MP for Weymouth, supervised the quarries himself. Convict labour from Portland Prison, established in 1848, worked the great Admiralty quarries. In the 19th century the island's population grew rapidly, and a famous local superstition holds that the word "rabbit" is bad luck on Portland, a taboo often linked to quarrymen's fears of burrow-caused landslides — the animals are traditionally referred to as "underground mutton" or "long-eared things".

Local custom also forbids outsiders calling islanders anything but Portlanders; the traditional (now rare) insult for islanders from the mainland is "Kimberlin". A notable natural feature is the fossil forest and the raised beach at Portland Bill, evidence of past sea levels.
