Mark Snyder is an American social psychologist best known for developing the concept of self-monitoring and the Self-Monitoring Scale, a widely used measure of individual differences in the extent to which people monitor and control their self-presentation and expressive behaviour. He has been a professor of psychology at the University of Minnesota, where he holds the McKnight Land-Grant Professorship.

Self-monitoring

In a landmark 1974 paper, "Self-monitoring of expressive behavior," Snyder proposed that people differ substantially in how much they observe and regulate their expressive behaviour and verbal self-presentation. High self-monitors are pragmatic and highly sensitive to social cues of situational appropriateness, adjusting their behaviour to fit the situation; low self-monitors are guided more by their inner feelings, attitudes and dispositions, and behave more consistently across situations.

The original Self-Monitoring Scale consisted of 25 true–false items; a shortened 18-item version was later developed. The construct generated an extensive research literature on friendship formation, romantic relationships, consumer behaviour, leadership and attitude–behaviour consistency.

Other research

Snyder's research has also addressed the behavioural confirmation of social stereotypes and expectations — the processes by which perceivers' beliefs elicit behaviour from targets that confirms those beliefs — as well as the psychology of volunteerism and helping, including the personal and social motivations that sustain volunteer activity. With Allen M. Omoto, he studied volunteers working with people living with HIV/AIDS.

His books include Public Appearances, Private Realities: The Psychology of Self-Monitoring (1987).

Education and career

Snyder received his undergraduate education at McGill University and his doctorate in psychology from Stanford University. He joined the faculty of the University of Minnesota, where he has spent most of his career and has been associated with the Center for the Study of the Individual and Society.
