Tsar (also spelled czar, tzar, or csar) is a title historically used by Slavic monarchs. The term is derived from Caesar, the title of Roman emperors. In Russia, "tsar" was the official title of the ruler from the coronation of Ivan IV in 1547 until the abdication of Nicholas II in 1917, although Peter the Great formally replaced it with "Emperor of all Russia" in 1721. The title was also used by monarchs of Bulgaria (from Simeon I in the 10th century, and again 1908–1946) and Serbia (1346–1371).

Usage in Russia

Ivan III had used "tsar" informally in correspondence, but Ivan IV (the Terrible) was the first Russian ruler formally crowned as Tsar of all Russia in 1547. The coronation asserted Moscow's claim to be the heir of both Kievan Rus' and the Byzantine Empire — the "Third Rome" doctrine. Peter I assumed the title Imperator in 1721 after victory in the Great Northern War, and "emperor" became the official style thereafter; nevertheless "tsar" remained in universal popular use, both in Russia and abroad, down to 1917.

Other uses

In modern English, "tsar" (or "czar") is used figuratively for an official appointed to coordinate policy in a particular area — for example a "drug czar" in United States government usage.
