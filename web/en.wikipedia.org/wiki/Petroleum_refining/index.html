Petroleum refining processes

Petroleum refining processes are the chemical engineering and other processes used in petroleum refineries (also referred to as oil refineries) to transform crude oil into useful products such as liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), gasoline or petrol, kerosene, jet fuel, diesel oil and fuel oils.

Crude oils are composed of hydrocarbon compounds and other organic compounds containing nitrogen, oxygen, sulfur, and metals such as nickel or vanadium. Petroleum refining processes separate these fractions by taking advantage of differences in the volatility of components in continuous distillation columns.

Primary processes

The first step in refining is fractional distillation, in which crude oil is heated and separated into fractions according to boiling point. Lighter fractions such as gasoline-range hydrocarbons vaporize and rise to the top of the fractionating column, while heavier fractions such as bitumen remain near the bottom. Desalting precedes distillation to remove mineral salts, and the crude is often treated to remove water, sulfur compounds, and other impurities.

Conversion processes

Because market demand for light products such as gasoline exceeds what distillation alone provides, refineries use conversion processes to break large molecules into smaller ones. Catalytic cracking uses a catalyst to break heavy gas oil into gasoline and lighter products. Hydrocracking accomplishes a similar conversion in the presence of hydrogen under high pressure. Coking processes handle the heaviest residuals, producing petroleum coke. Other conversion processes include alkylation, which combines isobutane with light olefins to make high-octane blending components, polymerization, isomerization, and catalytic reforming, which rearranges naphtha molecules to increase octane number and produces hydrogen as a by-product.

Treating processes

Treating processes such as hydrotreating remove sulfur, nitrogen, and metals from petroleum fractions. Hydrodesulfurization is widely used to produce low-sulfur diesel and gasoline that comply with environmental regulations. Other treating methods include caustic treating, acid treating, clay contacting, and deasphalting.

Blending and supporting processes

Finished products are made by blending intermediate streams to meet specifications for octane, volatility, sulfur content, and other properties. Supporting operations include hydrogen production (typically by steam reforming of natural gas), sulfur recovery in Claus units, wastewater treatment, and utilities such as steam and power generation.

Environmental and economic aspects

Refining is a capital-intensive industry subject to substantial environmental regulation, including limits on air emissions, wastewater discharges, and the composition of fuels. Refinery configurations vary from simple topping and hydroskimming plants to complex deep-conversion refineries equipped with cokers and hydrocrackers that maximize yields of light, high-value products.
