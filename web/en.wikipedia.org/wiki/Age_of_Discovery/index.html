Age of Discovery

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A Portuguese carrack of the kind that sailed the India route]

The Age of Discovery (or Age of Exploration) was the period, roughly from the early 15th to the 17th century, when European powers — first Portugal and Spain, then the Netherlands, England and France — explored, mapped and colonised much of the rest of the world. Portuguese sailors rounded Africa to reach India (1498); Columbus reached the Americas for Spain (1492); Magellan's expedition circumnavigated the globe (1519–1522). Driven by trade, Christianity, precious metals and rivalry, the voyages produced the Columbian exchange of plants, animals, peoples and diseases, the first global trading system — and colonisation whose violence and demographic catastrophe transformed, and in places destroyed, the societies encountered.

Exploration

Portugal pioneered systematic Atlantic exploration — Madeira, the Azores, the Guinea coast, and with Dias (1488) the Cape of Good Hope and da Gama (1498) the sea route to India, building a fortified trading-post empire from Brazil to Malacca and Macau. Spain, after Columbus' four voyages, conquered the Aztec (1519–21) and Inca (1532–33) empires and opened the Pacific (the Manila galleon linked Acapulco to the Philippines from 1565). Later entrants — Dutch, English, French — chartered companies (VOC 1602, EIC 1600) and planted colonies in North America, the Caribbean and Asia.

Consequences

The exchange ran both ways: American maize, potatoes, tomatoes and tobacco spread worldwide; Eurasian wheat, horses and smallpox reached the Americas, where epidemic disease killed a majority of the indigenous population (estimates of total mortality run to tens of millions). African slavery was industrialised to work American plantations; American silver flooded world markets, inflating prices from Seville to Ming China. The modern world system — its maps, diets, empires and inequalities — dates in large part from these two centuries.
