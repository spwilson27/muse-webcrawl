Charles Alexandre de Calonne

Charles Alexandre de Calonne (20 January 1734 – 30 October 1802) was a French statesman who served as Controller-General of Finances under Louis XVI from 1783 to 1787. His attempt to reform the kingdom's finances by taxing the privileged orders led to the summoning of the Assembly of Notables in 1787 and helped set in motion the crisis that produced the French Revolution.

Early career

Born in Douai to a family of magistrates, Calonne studied law and rose through the parlements, serving as intendant of Metz and later of Lille. He gained a reputation as an able and loyal administrator, though he was implicated in the harsh prosecution of the Breton magistrate La Chalotais.

Controller-General

Appointed Controller-General in November 1783, Calonne initially pursued a policy of conspicuous spending and borrowing to restore confidence in royal credit. By 1786 he concluded that the deficit could only be closed by fundamental reform: he proposed a universal land tax (the subvention territoriale) payable by all landowners without exemption, a stamp tax, the conversion of the corvée into a money payment, and the removal of internal customs barriers.

Assembly of Notables and fall

Knowing the Parlement of Paris would resist registering the new taxes, Calonne persuaded the king to summon an Assembly of Notables, which met at Versailles in February 1787. The Notables refused to approve his programme and demanded economies and the disclosure of the accounts. Calonne was dismissed in April 1787 and exiled to Lorraine; he fled to England, becoming one of the first émigrés.

Later life

During the Revolution Calonne worked in exile for the princes, organizing counter-revolutionary efforts at Coblenz. He was permitted to return to France in 1802 and died in Paris the same year.
