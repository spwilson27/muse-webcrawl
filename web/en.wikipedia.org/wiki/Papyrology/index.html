Papyrology is the study of ancient literature, correspondence, legal texts and other documents preserved on papyrus, the principal writing material of the ancient Mediterranean world. Papyrologists transcribe, translate, date and interpret these mostly fragmentary texts, which survive in large numbers mainly where conditions are extremely dry — above all in Egypt.

Sources and discoveries

The great age of discovery began in the late 19th century with excavations in the Fayum and, most famously, at Oxyrhynchus, where Bernard Grenfell and Arthur Hunt recovered hundreds of thousands of fragments from ancient rubbish mounds from 1896 onward. The Oxyrhynchus Papyri have yielded lost works of Sappho, Pindar, Sophocles, Euripides and Menander, early Christian texts, and a mass of everyday documents — tax receipts, census returns, private letters, contracts — that transformed the social and economic history of Greco-Roman Egypt. Other major finds include the carbonised scrolls of the Villa of the Papyri at Herculaneum, the Zenon archive, and the Nag Hammadi codices (on parchment and papyrus).

Methods

Papyrology combines palaeography, linguistics, codicology and ancient history. Modern techniques include multispectral imaging and X-ray tomography, which since 2023 have allowed "virtual unwrapping" and reading of unopened Herculaneum scrolls, with machine learning used to detect ink on scans. Texts are published in standard corpora and increasingly in open digital databases such as Papyri.info.
