The Plains Cree (Plains Cree: Nēhiyawak) are a division of the Cree, one of the largest Indigenous peoples of North America. Historically they occupied the northern Great Plains and parkland belt of what is now Alberta, Saskatchewan, and Manitoba, with related bands extending into Montana. Their culture was one of the classic horse-and-buffalo cultures of the Plains.

History

Originally a woodland people west of Hudson Bay, Cree groups moved westward during the fur trade era of the 17th and 18th centuries, acting as traders and middlemen between Europeans and western nations, and gradually adopted a plains way of life centred on the communal buffalo hunt, the pound, and later horse-mounted hunting. Plains Cree bands were organised into loose regional groupings — including the House People, River People, and Parklands People — each of many independent bands led by chiefs.

During the 19th century the Plains Cree were key partners in the fur trade but suffered from epidemics, the collapse of the buffalo herds in the 1870s, and famine. Many bands entered the Numbered Treaties with Canada: Treaty 4 (1874) and Treaty 6 (1876) covered most Plains Cree territory. In 1885 some Cree, notably followers of Big Bear and Poundmaker, became involved in the North-West Resistance, after which several leaders were imprisoned or executed and bands were dispersed onto reserves.

Culture

Traditional life revolved around the seasonal round of buffalo hunting, fishing, and gathering, and around ceremonies such as the Sun Dance (Thirst Dance), the sweat lodge, and the pipe ceremony. Social organisation was egalitarian and band-based, with warrior societies and extensive kinship networks. Notable historical Plains Cree leaders include Poundmaker (Pîhtokahanapiwiyin), Big Bear (Mistahimaskwa), and Sweetgrass. Today Plains Cree First Nations are numerous across the Prairies, and there are ongoing language and cultural revitalisation efforts.
