Chinese sovereign

An emperor of the Ming dynasty

The Chinese sovereign was the ruler of China for much of its history from the founding of the first dynasty until the fall of the Qing in 1912. Over the centuries, sovereigns bore different titles: wang (王, "king") in the early dynasties, and after Qin Shi Huang unified China in 221 BC, huangdi (皇帝, "emperor"), a title combining the legendary "Three Sovereigns" (huang) and "Five Emperors" (di).

Ideology and power

The sovereign ruled under the Mandate of Heaven (tianming), the doctrine that Heaven granted the right to rule to a virtuous dynasty and could withdraw it—evidenced by natural disasters, rebellion, or misgovernment—legitimizing dynastic change. The emperor was styled the Son of Heaven (tianzi) and stood at the apex of a bureaucratic state administered through the imperial examination system and a hierarchy of officials.

Titles and names

A Chinese sovereign is typically referred to by several names: a personal name (taboo during his lifetime), an era name (by which Ming and Qing emperors are commonly known), a temple name (such as Taizong or Gaozu), and a posthumous name. Succession was normally hereditary within the imperial clan, though emperors could also take power through usurpation, abdication, or conquest.

End of the institution

The last emperor, Puyi, abdicated on February 12, 1912, following the Xinhai Revolution, ending over two thousand years of imperial rule. A brief restoration attempt in 1917 lasted only days.
