Gay-Lussac's law

TypeGas law
Formulated byJoseph Louis Gay-Lussac
Published1809 (pressure–temperature law); 1808 (combining volumes)

For a fixed mass and volume of gas, pressure is proportional to absolute temperature

Gay-Lussac's law usually refers to the law of Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac stating that the pressure of a given mass of gas, held at constant volume, varies directly with its absolute temperature: P ∝ T, or P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂. It is a special case of the ideal gas law.

History and related laws

The pressure–temperature relationship had been described by Guillaume Amontons around 1700, so the law is sometimes credited to Amontons; Gay-Lussac, publishing in 1809, acknowledged earlier unpublished work by Jacques Charles. Gay-Lussac's name is also attached to the law of combining volumes (1808), which states that gases react in simple whole-number ratios by volume — a discovery that supported Avogadro's hypothesis. Together with Boyle's law and Charles's law, these relations combine into the ideal gas law.

Explanation

Kinetically, raising a gas's temperature increases the average speed of its molecules, so they strike the container walls more often and more forcefully, raising pressure when volume cannot expand. The law is exact only for ideal gases but is a good approximation for real gases at moderate pressures.
