Earth system model

An Earth system model (ESM) is a climate model that additionally simulates the global carbon cycle and other biogeochemical cycles, allowing feedbacks between climate change and the cycles in which chemical elements — particularly carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus — move between the atmosphere, oceans, land, and sediments. Earth system models extend general circulation models (GCMs) of the atmosphere and ocean by adding interactive representations of land and ocean ecology, atmospheric chemistry, ice sheets, and aerosols.


Components coupled in a typical Earth system model

Components

A typical ESM couples: an atmospheric general circulation model; an ocean circulation model with marine biogeochemistry; a land-surface model with dynamic vegetation and a terrestrial carbon cycle; sea-ice and (in some models) ice-sheet components; and atmospheric chemistry including aerosols and ozone. These components exchange fluxes of energy, water, momentum, and carbon through a coupler at regular time steps. Because carbon dioxide concentration can be computed rather than prescribed, ESMs can run "emissions-driven" simulations in which climate responds interactively to the carbon cycle.

Uses

Earth system models are central to the assessments of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) and to the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP), whose phases CMIP5 and CMIP6 coordinated ESM experiments internationally. They are used to project future climate under emissions scenarios, to attribute observed changes, to study past climates, and to evaluate Earth-system feedbacks such as permafrost carbon release and forest dieback. Well-known ESMs include the UK Earth System Model (UKESM), the Community Earth System Model (CESM) in the United States, and the Max Planck Institute Earth System Model (MPI-ESM).
