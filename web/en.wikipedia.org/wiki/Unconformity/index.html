Unconformity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An unconformity is a buried erosional or non-depositional surface separating two rock masses or strata of different ages, indicating that sediment deposition was not continuous. In general, the older layer was exposed to erosion for an interval of time before deposition of the younger, but the term is used to describe any break in the sedimentary geologic record.

Recognition and significance

Unconformities were recognised by James Hutton, who found examples at Jedburgh (1787) and Siccar Point (1788). At Siccar Point, vertical Silurian greywackes are overlain by gently dipping Devonian Old Red Sandstone — a juxtaposition Hutton understood to require immense spans of time for deposition, uplift, tilting, erosion and renewed deposition. Hutton's insight, popularised by John Playfair's remark that "the mind seemed to grow giddy by looking so far into the abyss of time", became a cornerstone of uniformitarian geology and of the concept of deep time.

Types

  • Angular unconformity — younger sediments rest on tilted or folded eroded older rocks.
  • Disconformity — a break between parallel layers of sedimentary rock, representing erosion or non-deposition.
  • Paraconformity — strata are parallel with no obvious erosion, but a significant time gap separates them.
  • Nonconformity — sedimentary rock overlies eroded igneous or metamorphic rock.
  • Blended unconformity — partly disconformity and partly nonconformity, as in the Great Unconformity of the Grand Canyon region.

The Great Unconformity

The Great Unconformity, named by Clarence Dutton in the Grand Canyon, separates Cambrian strata (~525 million years old) from underlying rocks as old as 1.7 billion years (Vishnu Schist), representing a gap of well over a billion years in places. Similar gaps worldwide have been linked to global erosion during the Neoproterozoic "Snowball Earth" glaciations.
