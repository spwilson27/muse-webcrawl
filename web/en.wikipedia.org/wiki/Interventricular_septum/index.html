Interventricular septum

The interventricular septum (IVS), or ventricular septum, is the stout wall separating the ventricles, the lower chambers of the heart, from one another. It is directed obliquely backward to the right and bulges with its convexity toward the cavity of the right ventricle, so that in cross-section the left ventricle appears circular and the right crescentic. The septum is largely muscular and contracts as part of both ventricles during systole, contributing substantially to ejection of blood, especially from the left ventricle.

Structure

The septum has two parts. The muscular part forms the great majority of the wall and is as thick as the free wall of the left ventricle. The membranous part is a small, thin, fibrous area at the top of the septum, just below the aortic valve; its thinness makes it the most common site of congenital ventricular septal defects. The right side of the septum bears the septal papillary muscles of the tricuspid valve and the moderator band, while the bundle of His and its branches — part of the heart's electrical conduction system — run within and along the septum, which is why septal disease or surgery can cause heart block.

Blood supply and development

The septum is supplied mainly by septal perforating branches of the left anterior descending artery, with the posterior part supplied by the posterior descending artery. In development, the muscular septum grows up from the floor of the primitive ventricle while cushions and the membranous portion close the remaining gap; failure of closure produces a ventricular septal defect, the most common congenital heart defect. Septal thickness is measured by echocardiography, and abnormal thickening is a hallmark of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, while thinning and rupture can complicate myocardial infarction.
