Children of Paradise

Children of Paradise (French: Les Enfants du Paradis) is a French romantic drama film directed by Marcel Carné, written by Jacques Prévert, and released in two parts in 1945 during the German occupation of France. Set among the theatrical demimonde of the Boulevard du Crime in 1820s–1830s Paris, it is widely regarded as one of the greatest films of French cinema and of all time.

Plot

In the Paris of the 1820s, the courtesan Garance (Arletty) is loved by four men: the mime Baptiste Deburau (Jean-Louis Barrault), the actor Frédérick Lemaître (Pierre Brasseur), the criminal Pierre-François Lacenaire (Marcel Herrand), and the aristocratic Count Édouard de Montray (Louis Salou). The film follows their intertwined lives across two parts — Le Boulevard du crime and L'Homme blanc — as Garance moves between the worlds of the theatre, crime and high society, while Baptiste's silent, unrequited love forms the emotional heart of the story. The title refers to the "children of paradise": the rowdy working-class audience in the highest, cheapest seats of the theatre — the paradis, or "gods".

Production

The film was shot at the Victorine Studios in Nice and in Paris under the difficult conditions of the Occupation, beginning in August 1943. Production was repeatedly interrupted — by the Italian armistice, by Allied landings, and by Carné's deliberate delays in the hope of completing the film after liberation. The enormous Boulevard du Crime set, designed by Alexandre Trauner, and costumes by Mayo anchored the film's recreation of nineteenth-century Paris. Trauner and composer Joseph Kosma, both Jewish, worked in hiding and were officially uncredited. At over three hours, the film was released in two parts in March 1945, shortly before the end of the war in Europe.

Reception and legacy

The film was an immense popular and critical success on release and has only grown in stature. In 1995, French film professionals voted it the greatest French film of the century. It is frequently compared to Gone with the Wind in scale and ambition, and its screenplay by Prévert is celebrated for its wit and poetry. Jean-Louis Barrault's performance as Baptiste, modelled on the real nineteenth-century mime Jean-Gaspard Deburau, is regarded as one of the finest in cinema.
