Khyber Pass

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Khyber Pass
Location: Spin Ghar range, Pakistan–Afghanistan border
Length: about 53 km
Elevation: 1,070 m at Landi Kotal

The Khyber Pass is a mountain pass in the Spin Ghar range connecting Peshawar (Pakistan) with Jalalabad and Kabul (Afghanistan), the most famous and historically important pass between Central/South Asia's worlds. For millennia it has been the invasion route of choice into the Indian subcontinent and a lifeline of trade on the southern Silk Road.

History

Through the Khyber came, by tradition and record, Indo-Aryan migrations, Alexander the Great's successors' armies, the White Huns, Mahmud of Ghazni, Timur, Babur (who wrote lovingly of it) and Nader Shah — the making of empires from Darius to the Mughals. Britain, fearing Russia, fought three Anglo-Afghan wars partly for control of it, and held the pass with Khyber Rifles and fortified pickets while never subduing the Afridi and Shinwari tribes of the hills, whose code (Pashtunwali) still governs.

Modern pass

Today the Torkham crossing at the pass's foot is one of the busiest border points between Pakistan and Afghanistan — a choke-point for trade, refugees and geopolitics from the Soviet war through the American war (when NATO supply convoys climbed it under ambush) to the Taliban's return. The pass railway, completed in 1925 through dozens of tunnels and bridges, was one of the great feats of colonial engineering; steam excursions long outlived its strategic freight. Few roads carry as much history per kilometre — the gateway of a continent, as St. Louis is of another.
