Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA)
LocationChajnantor plateau, Atacama Desert, Chile (~5,000 m elevation)
Antennas66 high-precision antennas (54 × 12 m, 12 × 7 m)
Wavelengths0.3–9.6 mm (31–1,000 GHz)

PartnersESO (Europe), NRAO/NSF (North America), NAOJ (East Asia), Republic of Chile

The Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) is an astronomical interferometer of 66 radio antennas in the Atacama Desert of northern Chile. Because it observes at millimetre and submillimetre wavelengths, ALMA can study the coldest and most distant objects in the Universe — the gas and dust from which stars and planets form, and galaxies in the very early cosmos. It is the largest and most expensive ground-based astronomical project of its era, costing roughly US$1.4 billion.

Site and design

ALMA sits on the Chajnantor plateau at about 5,000 metres (16,400 ft) above sea level, one of the driest places on Earth, where the thin, arid air minimally absorbs the faint submillimetre signals. The antennas work together as an interferometer: their signals are combined by a supercomputer correlator to synthesise the resolution of a single telescope up to 16 km across. Giant transporter vehicles move the antennas between configurations on specially built pads.

Science

ALMA's results include the sharpest images of protoplanetary discs — famously the concentric rings of HL Tauri, interpreted as the imprint of forming planets — detailed views of starburst galaxies seen when the Universe was a fraction of its present age, the detection of complex organic molecules in star-forming regions, and a key contribution (as part of the Event Horizon Telescope) to the first images of black hole shadows in M87 and the Milky Way. Operations are coordinated from the Operations Support Facility at 2,900 m and the Joint ALMA Observatory in Santiago.
