Divisionism

Divisionism
Art movement, late 19th century
Also called: Chromoluminarism
Key figures: Georges Seurat, Paul Signac, Giovanni Segantini

Divisionism, also called chromoluminarism, is the characteristic style in Neo-Impressionism painting defined by the separation of colours into individual dots or patches which interact optically in the viewer's eye.

Principles

By requiring the viewer to combine the colours optically instead of physically mixing pigments on the canvas, divisionists believed they could achieve a greater luminosity and scientific accuracy. The theory drew on the colour studies of Michel Eugène Chevreul, Ogden Rood and Charles Henry.

History

The technique was developed by Georges Seurat and Paul Signac from 1884, exemplified by Seurat's A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte (1884–1886). In Italy, Giovanni Segantini, Gaetano Previati and Pellizza da Volpedo adapted divisionism to symbolic and social subjects.

Divisionism vs Pointillism

Pointillism is often used interchangeably, but pointillism properly refers to the use of small dots, whereas divisionism covers a broader range of separated brushstrokes, including longer, mosaic-like strokes used by the Italian divisionists.

Legacy

Divisionism influenced Fauvism, early modernism, and later colour theory in painting. Its systematic approach marked a shift toward art grounded in perceptual science.
