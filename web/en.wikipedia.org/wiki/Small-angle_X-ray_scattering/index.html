Small-angle X-ray scattering

Small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) is a small-angle scattering technique in which the elastic scattering of X-rays (wavelength 0.1–0.2 nm) by a sample showing inhomogeneities at the nanometre scale is recorded at very low angles, typically 0.1–10°. In this angular range, information about the shape and size of macromolecules, characteristic distances of partially ordered materials, pore sizes, and other nanostructural features can be obtained. SAXS is capable of delivering structural information on structures between 1 and 100 nm, and up to 150 nm for partially ordered systems.

Principle

The technique is closely related to small-angle neutron scattering (SANS) and shares its theoretical basis. The scattering intensity I(q) is measured as a function of the scattering vector q, whose magnitude depends on the scattering angle and the wavelength. For dilute solutions of particles, a Guinier plot of the low-q data yields the radius of gyration (Rg) and the forward scattering intensity, from which molecular weight can be estimated. Fourier transformation of the scattering curve gives the pair-distance distribution function P(r), which characterizes the particle's shape and maximum dimension.

Applications

SAXS is used for the structural characterization of biological macromolecules in solution (proteins, nucleic acids, complexes, viruses), polymers, colloids, nanoparticles, surfactants, porous materials, and alloys. It can determine size distributions of particles, surface-to-volume ratios, and — for biological samples — low-resolution three-dimensional envelopes reconstructed ab initio from one-dimensional scattering data. Because samples need not be crystallized or frozen, SAXS complements crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy, and time-resolved SAXS can follow structural changes on millisecond timescales.

Instrumentation

Measurements are made on laboratory instruments with X-ray tubes or rotating anodes, or at synchrotron beamlines, which provide the high flux needed for weakly scattering biological samples and fast kinetic studies. Grazing-incidence SAXS (GISAXS) extends the method to thin films and surfaces.

History

The method was developed in the 1930s, notably by André Guinier, whose 1937 work on scattering from alloys led to the Guinier approximation, and was extended by Günther Porod (Porod's law) and by Otto Kratky, inventor of the Kratky camera, widely used for polymer and protein studies.
