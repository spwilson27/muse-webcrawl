Zidovudine

Zidovudine (ZDV), also known as azidothymidine (AZT), was the first antiretroviral drug approved to treat HIV infection. Sold under the brand name Retrovir, it is a nucleoside analogue reverse-transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI). It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

History

Zidovudine was first synthesised in 1964 by Jerome Horwitz at the Barbara Ann Karmanos Cancer Institute as a potential anticancer drug, but it proved ineffective and was shelved. In 1984, after HIV was identified as the cause of AIDS, scientists at Burroughs Wellcome (including Janet Rideout, with screening by the US National Cancer Institute team of Samuel Broder and Hiroaki Mitsuya) found it suppressed HIV in vitro. In March 1987 it became the first drug approved by the US FDA for AIDS, in a then-record-fast approval process. Its initially high price — about $10,000 per patient per year — made it a focus of activism by groups such as ACT UP.

Medical uses

Zidovudine is used in combination antiretroviral therapy (ART) and in prevention of mother-to-child transmission of HIV, where trials showed it greatly reduced transmission to newborns. Once the mainstay of treatment, it has largely been replaced in first-line regimens by better-tolerated NRTIs such as tenofovir, though it remains an alternative and is combined with lamivudine in the fixed-dose tablet Combivir.

Mechanism and side effects

Zidovudine mimics the nucleoside thymidine. After phosphorylation inside cells, it is incorporated into growing viral DNA by HIV's reverse transcriptase, where its azide group prevents further chain elongation, terminating the DNA chain. Common side effects include nausea, headache and fatigue; more serious toxicities include anaemia, neutropenia, myopathy and lactic acidosis, reflecting effects on human mitochondrial DNA polymerase.
