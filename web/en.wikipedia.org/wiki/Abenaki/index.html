Abenaki

The Abenaki (Abenaki: Wôbanakiak, "People of the Dawnlands") are Indigenous peoples of the Northeastern Woodlands, speaking languages of the Eastern Algonquian family. Their traditional homeland, Ndakinna ("our land"), spans present-day Vermont, New Hampshire, western Maine, northern Massachusetts and parts of Quebec. Together with the Mi'kmaq, Wolastoqiyik (Maliseet), Passamaquoddy and Penobscot, they form the Wabanaki Confederacy.

Branches and language

The Abenaki comprise two main divisions speaking closely related but distinct languages: the Eastern Abenaki of Maine (including the Kennebec, Androscoggin and Penobscot kin groups) and the Western Abenaki of Vermont, New Hampshire and Quebec. Both languages are endangered but subject to active revitalisation, including immersion programs and dictionaries; the last fully fluent first-language speakers of Eastern Abenaki died in the 1990s.

History

Before European contact the Abenaki practised maize-bean-squash horticulture supplemented by fishing, hunting and gathering. From the 17th century they were drawn into the fur trade and French colonial alliances, becoming largely Catholic under French missionary influence. Raids and wars — King Philip's War, Dummer's War and the French and Indian War — displaced much of the population to mission communities in Canada such as Odanak (St. Francis) and Wôlinak in Quebec. Grey Lock, a Western Abenaki leader based at Missisquoi, resisted English expansion in the 1720s.

Present day

Today Abenaki communities include the federally recognised Abenaki Nation at Missisquoi and state-recognised tribes in Vermont (including the Nulhegan, Elnu and Koasek bands), the Odanak and Wôlinak First Nations in Quebec, and Abenaki descendants among the Penobscot Nation in Maine. Debates over tribal recognition and cultural identity, including criticism of some Vermont groups' claims by Quebec Abenaki leadership, have been prominent in recent years.
