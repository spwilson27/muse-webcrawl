Qubit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A Bloch sphere with a state vector arrow pointing to a spot on its surface]

A qubit (quantum bit) is the basic unit of information in a quantum computer: a two-level quantum system whose state is a combination (superposition) of the basis states usually written |0⟩ and |1⟩, with complex weights whose squared magnitudes give measurement probabilities.

Unlike a classical bit, a qubit before measurement is not secretly 0 or 1 — its state is a point on the Bloch sphere — and several qubits can be entangled, correlating their states in ways with no classical analogue. These features are what quantum algorithms exploit.

Realisation and limits

Physical qubits are built from superconducting circuits, trapped ions, photons and other systems; all are fragile, losing their state through decoherence, so practical quantum computers need extensive error correction with many physical qubits per reliable logical qubit.

The word 'qubit' appeared in a 1995 paper by Benjamin Schumacher, by analogy with the ancient cubit, according to the recollection of William Wootters, who said he coined it in conversation.
