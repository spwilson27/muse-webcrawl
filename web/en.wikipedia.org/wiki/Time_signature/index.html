Time signature

A time signature (also known as a meter signature) is a notational convention used in Western musical notation to specify how many beats are contained in each bar and which note value is equivalent to a beat. It appears at the beginning of a piece, after the clef and key signature, as two numbers stacked vertically: the lower number indicates the note value of one beat (for example, 4 for a quarter note/crotchet), and the upper number indicates how many such beats there are in each bar.

Common time signatures

The most frequent time signature in Western popular and classical music is 4/4, often called "common time" and sometimes abbreviated by a C-like symbol; it contains four quarter-note beats per bar. 3/4 — "waltz time" — has three quarter-note beats per bar. 2/4 suits polkas and marches, while 2/2 ("cut time", alla breve) halves the felt beat. Compound meters such as 6/8, 9/8, and 12/8 group beats into threes: 6/8 contains two dotted-quarter beats, each divided into three eighth notes, producing the lilting rhythm of jigs and many ballads.

Simple, compound, and complex meters

Meters whose beats divide into two are simple; those whose beats divide into three are compound. Signatures with upper numbers of 5, 7, or other combinations are sometimes called complex or irregular meters — for example 5/4, used in Dave Brubeck's "Take Five" and the Mission: Impossible theme, and 7/8, common in Balkan folk music and progressive rock. Additive meters notate groupings such as 3+2+3.

History

Time signatures evolved from the mensuration signs of medieval and Renaissance notation, which indicated the proportional relationships between note values under systems such as tempus perfectum (division into three, symbolised by a full circle) and tempus imperfectum (division into two, a broken circle — the ancestor of the "C" for common time). Numeric signatures became standard during the Baroque era.
