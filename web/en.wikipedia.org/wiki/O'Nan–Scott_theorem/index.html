O'Nan–Scott theorem

In group theory, the O'Nan–Scott theorem is a fundamental structure theorem for finite primitive permutation groups. It classifies maximal subgroups of the symmetric and alternating groups and describes all finite primitive permutation groups in terms of a small number of types, organised according to the structure of their socles — the product of their minimal normal subgroups.

Statement and history

The theorem originated in independent work by Michael O'Nan and Leonard Scott in the 1970s; Scott's 1980 analysis of maximal subgroups of symmetric groups, combined with the classification of finite simple groups, made the result a central tool in permutation group theory. In its common modern form, a finite primitive permutation group falls into one of the following types:

  • Affine — the socle is an elementary abelian regular normal subgroup, and the group is a subgroup of an affine general linear group.
  • Almost simple — the socle is a single non-abelian simple group.
  • Simple diagonal — the socle is a direct power of a simple group acting diagonally.
  • Product action — the group preserves a product structure on the domain.
  • Twisted wreath — the socle is a regular non-abelian group built from a wreath-type construction.

Different formulations divide these cases differently (for example into as many as eight types), but all serve the same purpose: reducing questions about primitive groups — notably the description of the maximal subgroups of symmetric and alternating groups — to questions about almost simple and affine groups, which can then be attacked using the classification of finite simple groups. The theorem is sometimes called the O'Nan–Scott–Aschbacher theorem in recognition of Michael Aschbacher's related classification work.
