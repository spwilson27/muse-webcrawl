Audio filter

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An audio filter is a frequency-dependent amplifier circuit or digital signal processing algorithm that emphasises, de-emphasises or removes components of a signal within (and beyond) the audio frequency range. Filters are fundamental to equalisation, crossover networks, synthesis and noise reduction.

Types

  • Low-pass filter — passes frequencies below its cutoff frequency and attenuates those above; used to remove hiss and as the basis of subtractive synthesis.
  • High-pass filter — passes frequencies above cutoff, attenuating rumble and low-frequency noise.
  • Band-pass filter — passes a band of frequencies around a centre frequency.
  • Band-stop (notch) filter — rejects a narrow band, e.g. mains hum.
  • Shelving and peaking filters — the building blocks of equalisers, boosting or cutting broad bands or a selected region.

Characteristics

Filters are described by their cutoff or centre frequency, slope — expressed in decibels per octave, increasing in 6 dB steps per filter order ("pole") — and resonance or Q, the emphasis at the cutoff point. Classic analogue designs include RC filters, the Sallen–Key topology and the resonant ladder filter of Moog synthesisers; digital implementations use FIR and IIR structures such as the biquad.

Applications

In loudspeaker crossovers, filters divide the spectrum between drivers. In recording, high-pass filters clean up tracks, while in electronic music the voltage-controlled filter is a central expressive tool, sculpting timbre in real time.
