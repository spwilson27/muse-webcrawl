Seed bank (soil)

The soil seed bank is the natural store of seeds, dormant and viable, present in the soil of an ecosystem. Seeds enter the bank when they are shed by plants and buried by litter, animals, frost action, or soil disturbance; they leave it by germinating, being eaten, or dying. Seed banks are a form of ecological memory, allowing plant populations to persist through unfavourable years and to recolonise after fire, flood, or clearing.

Types

Ecologists following Thompson and Grime distinguish transient seed banks, whose seeds remain viable for less than a year, from persistent seed banks, in which at least some seeds survive for many years — in documented cases, centuries. Seeds of many weeds, wetland plants, and fire-following species are notably long-lived; lotus seeds over a thousand years old have germinated.

Ecological significance

Because germination is spread over time, seed banks buffer populations against catastrophic seasons (a bet-hedging strategy) and store genetic variation. They strongly influence vegetation dynamics: after disturbance, the species that return often come from the bank rather than from new seed rain, which is why weed seed banks are a persistent problem in agriculture — and why restoration projects sometimes find that a degraded site's original flora can regenerate from its buried seeds. Seed banks decline when seed predators, fungal decay, or deep burial intervene, and their composition is studied by germinating soil samples in glasshouses.
