The Menin Gate in Ypres, Belgium

The Menin Gate Memorial to the Missing is a war memorial in Ypres, Belgium, dedicated to the British and Commonwealth soldiers who were killed in the Ypres Salient of the First World War and whose graves are unknown. The memorial is located on the eastern edge of the town, on the road that Allied soldiers took toward the front line — the road to Menin (Menen) — from which it takes its name.

The memorial, designed by Sir Reginald Blomfield and built by the Imperial War Graves Commission (now the Commonwealth War Graves Commission), bears the names of more than 54,000 officers and men from the United Kingdom and Commonwealth forces who died in the Salient before 16 August 1917 and have no known grave. Those who died after that date and remain missing are commemorated at the Tyne Cot Memorial.

History

Ypres occupied a strategic position during the First World War because it stood in the path of the German army's planned sweep across the rest of Belgium and northern France. The town was almost constantly under artillery fire, and by the time of the Third Battle of Ypres in 1917 it had been reduced to ruins. The Menin Gate was built on the site of one of the old city's fortified gates, through which hundreds of thousands of soldiers marched on their way to the battlefields.

The memorial was unveiled on 24 July 1927 by Field Marshal Herbert Plumer, 1st Baron Plumer, in the presence of King Albert I of Belgium. Its inauguration began the tradition of the Last Post ceremony, held beneath the arch every evening at 8 p.m. since 1928, interrupted only during the German occupation in the Second World War.

Design

The Menin Gate is a large triumphal arch built of Portland stone. A lion, symbolising both Britain and Flanders, stands atop the arch. The interior is a Hall of Memory whose walls are covered with Portland stone panels inscribed with the names of the missing, arranged by regiment. An inscription by Rudyard Kipling, who lost his son John at the Battle of Loos, reads in part: "Here are recorded names of officers and men who fell in Ypres Salient, but to whom the fortune of war denied the known and honoured burial given to their comrades in death."

The Last Post ceremony

Since 1928, buglers from the local fire brigade have sounded the Last Post under the gate every evening at 20:00. The ceremony was suspended during the Second World War but resumed on the evening of Ypres's liberation in September 1944, even as fighting continued nearby. The 30,000th ceremony was held on 9 July 2015.
