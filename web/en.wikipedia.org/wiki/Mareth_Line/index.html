The Mareth Line (French: Ligne Mareth) was a line of fortifications built by France in southern Tunisia in the 1930s to defend against a possible invasion from Italian Libya. Running roughly 35 kilometres from the Mediterranean coast near Zarat inland to the Matmata Hills, it was sometimes called the "Maginot Line of the desert". The line became famous as the scene of a major battle in March 1943 during the Tunisia Campaign of the Second World War.

Construction

Constructed between 1936 and 1940 amid rising tensions with Fascist Italy, the Mareth Line consisted of concrete bunkers, casemates, artillery positions, anti-tank ditches and minefields, anchored on the Wadi Zigzaou, a deep natural anti-tank obstacle. Its inland flank rested on the rugged Matmata Hills, which the French assumed to be impassable to armour.

Second World War

After the French armistice of 1940 the line was demilitarised, but following Operation Torch and the Axis occupation of Tunisia in late 1942, German and Italian forces reoccupied and strengthened it. In March 1943, General Bernard Montgomery's British Eighth Army advanced against the line, held by the Axis First Army under Giovanni Messe. In the Battle of the Mareth Line (16–31 March 1943), a frontal attack (Operation Pugilist) made limited progress, but a flanking "left hook" through the Tebaga Gap by the New Zealand Corps under Lieutenant-General Brian Horrocks — made possible by reconnaissance of the supposedly impassable hills — outflanked the position. The Axis forces withdrew to the Wadi Akarit line, and the Mareth Line fell to the Allies.

Today

Many of the line's bunkers survive, and a small museum near Mareth commemorates the battle.
