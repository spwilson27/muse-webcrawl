Smith normal form

In mathematics, the Smith normal form (named after Henry John Stephen Smith) is a normal form that can be defined for a matrix over a principal ideal domain (PID), such as the ring of integers or a polynomial ring over a field. The Smith form of an m×n matrix is a diagonal matrix obtained by multiplying the original matrix on the left and right by invertible (unimodular) matrices over the same ring.

Definition

For any matrix A over a PID R, there exist invertible matrices S and T over R such that SAT is diagonal, with diagonal entries d1, d2, …, dr (the invariant factors), satisfying the divisibility chain d1 | d2 | … | dr, followed by zeros. The invariant factors are unique up to multiplication by units. The form is computed by an algorithm of elementary row and column operations — swapping, negating, and adding multiples of one row or column to another — using gcd computations analogous to Gaussian elimination.

Applications

The Smith normal form is a fundamental tool in algebra. Over the integers it classifies finitely generated abelian groups: the cokernel of an integer matrix decomposes as a direct sum of cyclic groups whose orders are the invariant factors. It is used in the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID, from which both the classification of finite abelian groups and the Jordan and rational canonical forms for matrices follow. In algebraic topology, the Smith form computes the homology groups of a chain complex by diagonalizing boundary matrices. It also appears in control theory, for manipulating polynomial matrices and characterizing system equivalence.

History

Henry John Stephen Smith introduced the normal form for integer matrices in 1861 in work on systems of linear congruences. The result was later extended to matrices over arbitrary principal ideal domains and elementary divisor rings, and constructive algorithms — with attention to controlling coefficient growth — remain an active topic in computational algebra.
