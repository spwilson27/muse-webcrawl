Epigram

Epigram
TypeShort poem / witty saying
OriginGreek epigramma, 'inscription'
Notable writersMartial, Simonides, Alexander Pope, Oscar Wilde, Dorothy Parker

An epigram is a brief, interesting, memorable, sometimes surprising or satirical statement. The word derives from the Greek epigramma, 'inscription', from epigraphein, 'to write on, to inscribe'. The Greek tradition of epigrams began as poems inscribed on votive offerings, tombs, and statues.

History

The Greek Anthology preserves thousands of epigrams, from Simonides' famous epitaph for the Spartans at Thermopylae onward. The Roman poet Martial (1st century AD) transformed the form into short, sharp, often obscene satire, establishing the model of the epigram as a poem ending in a witty sting. In English, John Donne, Ben Jonson, Alexander Pope, and Samuel Taylor Coleridge — who defined the form in verse: 'What is an Epigram? A dwarfish whole, / Its body brevity, and wit its soul' — practised it, and Oscar Wilde made the epigrammatic paradox a hallmark of his prose and plays.

Characteristics

An epigram aims at compression, antithesis, and a final turn of wit. In prose it overlaps with the aphorism and the maxim, as in the works of La Rochefoucauld. Writers such as Dorothy Parker and Ambrose Bierce carried the tradition into the twentieth century.
