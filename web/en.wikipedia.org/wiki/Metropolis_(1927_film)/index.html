Metropolis (1927 film)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The city of Metropolis: towers and aerial highways of the year 2026, the film's monumental Art Deco future skyline]

Metropolis is a 1927 German expressionist science-fiction film directed by Fritz Lang and written by Thea von Harbou, produced by UFA. It is the most famous film of Weimar cinema and a founding work of science fiction on screen: in the city of Metropolis in 2026, the sons of the ruling class sport in pleasure gardens above while workers slave in the machine halls below, until the inventor Rotwang's robot double of the saintly Maria incites both revolution and witch-hunt.

Shot over 17 months with some 25,000 extras and a budget that nearly ruined UFA (over five million Reichsmarks), Metropolis premiered in Berlin in January 1927, was cut by distributors abroad, and for decades circulated in mutilated versions. Restorations — notably the 2001 UNESCO Memory of the World-inscribed version and the near-complete 2010 restoration from a print found in Buenos Aires — recovered its monumental design: the Schüfftan process miniatures, the robot (the Maschinenmensch, cinema's first great android), and Brigitte Helm's double performance.

Themes and design

Lang said the film's moral — the mediator between head and hands must be the heart, in the film's famous formulation (its intertitle is among the most quoted in silent cinema, paraphrased here) — was naive even as he made it; what endures is the image-system: Babylonian towers, the Moloch-machine devouring workers, Art Deco and Gothic colliding. Its influence on everything from Blade Runner to Batman's Gotham to music videos is incalculable.

Music and versions

Gottfried Huppertz's original score anchors the restorations; Giorgio Moroder's 1984 rock-scored version, though derided, began the film's modern revival and colour-tinted its imagery for a generation. The 2010 restoration premiered simultaneously in Berlin and on television — a silent film as national event.
