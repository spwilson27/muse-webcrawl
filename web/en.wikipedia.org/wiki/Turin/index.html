Turin (Italian: Torino) is a city in northwestern Italy, capital of the Piedmont region and of the Metropolitan City of Turin, situated on the western bank of the Po River beneath the Alpine foothills. Long the capital of the House of Savoy and, from 1861 to 1865, the first capital of the unified Kingdom of Italy, Turin became one of Italy's principal industrial cities as the home of Fiat and the Italian automobile industry.

History

Founded as a Roman colony, Augusta Taurinorum, on the site of a settlement of the Taurini, the city passed to the dukes of Savoy in the 11th century and grew into their capital, acquiring baroque palaces, grand avenues and royal residences — many now part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing of Savoy residences. Turin was the political heart of the Risorgimento: Count Cavour edited his newspaper there and the first Italian parliament sat in Palazzo Carignano. Industrialisation under Fiat (founded 1899 by Giovanni Agnelli) drew hundreds of thousands of migrants from southern Italy in the post-war decades.

Landmarks and culture

The city's symbol is the Mole Antonelliana, a soaring 19th-century spire now housing the National Museum of Cinema. Turin Cathedral preserves the Shroud of Turin, the linen cloth venerated as the burial shroud of Jesus. Other major institutions include the Egyptian Museum — one of the world's finest collections of Egyptian antiquities — the Royal Palace, Juventus Stadium and the Politecnico di Torino university. Turin hosted the 2006 Winter Olympics. The city is also famous for chocolate (including gianduja and the bicerin drink), vermouth, and its association with the Slow Food movement founded nearby.
