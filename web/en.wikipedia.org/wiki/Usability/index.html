Usability

Usability can be described as the capacity of a system to provide a condition for its users to perform tasks safely, effectively, and efficiently while enjoying the experience. In software engineering, usability is the degree to which a software can be used by specified consumers to achieve quantified objectives with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction in a quantified context of use.

Usability testing: a moderator observes a user attempting representative tasks.

Definition

The international standard ISO 9241-11 defines usability as "the extent to which a product can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction in a specified context of use." Jakob Nielsen's widely used model breaks usability into five quality components: learnability, efficiency, memorability, errors (low error rate and easy recovery), and satisfaction.

History

Usability emerged from human factors and ergonomics research in aviation and industry, and matured with the rise of human–computer interaction in the 1970s and 1980s. Xerox PARC, Apple, and IBM established early usability laboratories; Don Norman's The Design of Everyday Things (1988) and Nielsen's discount usability engineering popularised inexpensive methods such as heuristic evaluation and thinking-aloud testing.

Evaluation methods

Usability testing

In usability testing, representative users attempt typical tasks while observers watch, listen, and take notes. Even small samples — famously, around five users — uncover a large fraction of severe problems. Metrics include task success, time on task, error counts, and standardised satisfaction questionnaires such as the System Usability Scale (SUS).

Inspection methods

Expert-based methods include heuristic evaluation against principles such as Nielsen's ten heuristics, cognitive walkthroughs, and accessibility audits against guidelines such as the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG).

Relationship to user experience

Usability is a component of the broader user experience, which also encompasses aesthetics, emotion, brand, and content. A product can be usable yet provide a poor overall experience, and vice versa; modern practice treats usability as necessary but not sufficient.
