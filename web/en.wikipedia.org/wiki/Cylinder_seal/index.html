Cylinder seal

A cylinder seal is a small cylinder engraved with a design in intaglio, used in ancient times to roll an impression onto a two-dimensional surface, generally wet clay. Cylinder seals were invented around 3500 BC in the Near East, in early Mesopotamia and southwestern Iran, and were used for over three thousand years.

Use

Rolled across clay tablets, jar stoppers, and the clay "bullae" sealing doors and containers, the seal's impression served as a signature and a guarantee: it identified the owner, marked property, and showed whether a sealed item had been tampered with. Seals were typically pierced lengthwise so they could be worn on a string or pin, and they functioned also as amulets and jewellery.

Materials and imagery

Seals were carved from stones ranging from soft steatite and limestone to hard hematite, lapis lazuli, carnelian, and rock crystal. Their imagery is a major source for ancient art and religion: presentation scenes before gods and kings, banquets, contests between heroes and animals, hunts, and mythological episodes. Styles changed distinctively over time — Uruk, Akkadian, Old Babylonian, Assyrian, and Persian periods each have characteristic seal styles used by archaeologists for dating.

Decline

As writing spread onto papyrus and parchment, which cannot take a rolled impression, cylinder seals were gradually displaced by stamp seals and signet rings in the first millennium BC, though they persisted in some regions into the Achaemenid Persian period and beyond.

Collections

Major collections are held by the British Museum, the Louvre, the Morgan Library, and the Vorderasiatisches Museum in Berlin, and catalogued corpora of seals remain fundamental references for Near Eastern archaeology.
