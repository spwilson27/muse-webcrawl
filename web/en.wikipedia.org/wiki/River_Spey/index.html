The River Spey (Scottish Gaelic: Uisge Spè) is a river in north-east Scotland. At 172 kilometres it is the eighth longest river in the United Kingdom and the second longest in Scotland after the Tay; it is also reputedly the fastest-flowing large river in Scotland at its mouth. It rises at Loch Spey in the Monadhliath Mountains, some 300 metres above sea level, flows east and north-east through Strathspey past Newtonmore, Kingussie, Aviemore, Grantown-on-Spey, Aberlour and Craigellachie, and enters the Moray Firth at Spey Bay.

Salmon and whisky

The Spey is one of Scotland's great Atlantic salmon rivers, famous for spring salmon fishing and for lending its name to the Spey cast, a single-handed fly-casting technique developed on its wide, tree-lined pools. Its valley, Strathspey, is equally famous as the heart of the Speyside whisky region: more than half of Scotland's malt whisky distilleries — including Glenfiddich, The Macallan, Glenlivet and Aberlour — stand within the catchment, drawing on its waters and forming the Malt Whisky Trail. The river's tributaries include the Fiddich, Avon (pronounced A'an), Feshie and Truim.

History and ecology

In the 18th and 19th centuries the Spey was used to float timber rafts from the great Caledonian pinewoods of Rothiemurchus and Abernethy to shipyards at Garmouth and Kingston, and the Spey salmon fishery at the river mouth was once among the most valuable in Scotland. The lower river and Insh Marshes, near Kingussie, are major nature reserves; the Spey is designated for its salmon, otter, freshwater pearl mussel and sea lamprey populations. Because the river's course across its shingle floodplain shifts constantly, the village of Garmouth sits where channels have wandered dramatically over centuries. The Speyside Way long-distance trail follows the river from Buckie to Aviemore.
