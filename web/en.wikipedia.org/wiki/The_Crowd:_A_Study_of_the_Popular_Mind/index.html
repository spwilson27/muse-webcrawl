The Crowd: A Study of the Popular Mind

The Crowd: A Study of the Popular Mind (French: Psychologie des foules, literally "Psychology of Crowds") is a work of crowd psychology by the French polymath Gustave Le Bon, published in 1895. It is one of the most influential, and most controversial, early works of social psychology.

Argument

Le Bon argued that individuals in a crowd undergo a transformation: anonymity, contagion and suggestibility produce a "psychological crowd" with a collective mind in which individuals feel invincible, shed personal responsibility, and become capable of acts contrary to their individual character. Crowds, he claimed, think in images, are swayed by emotion and prestige rather than reason, and are easily led by leaders who use affirmation, repetition and contagion. He classified crowds into types and analysed criminal crowds, juries, electoral crowds and parliamentary assemblies.

Influence and criticism

The book was translated into many languages and read by statesmen, propagandists and revolutionaries of the twentieth century; it influenced Freud's Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego (1921) and theories of propaganda and public relations. Later psychologists criticised Le Bon's theories as elitist, empirically weak and rooted in the anxieties of his class and era, though his book remains a foundational historical text in the study of collective behaviour and deindividuation.
