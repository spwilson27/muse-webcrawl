Cement clinker is a solid nodular material produced in a cement kiln during the manufacture of Portland cement. It is formed by heating a carefully proportioned mixture of limestone and clay (or similar aluminosilicate materials) to about 1450 °C, at which partial melting (sintering) occurs. The resulting clinker is cooled and ground with a few percent of gypsum to produce cement powder.

Composition

Typical Portland cement clinker contains four main mineral phases, described in cement chemist notation:

  • Alite (tricalcium silicate, C3S), about 50–70% — responsible for early strength.
  • Belite (dicalcium silicate, C2S), about 15–30% — contributes to later strength.
  • Tricalcium aluminate (C3A), about 5–10% — reacts rapidly and affects setting.
  • Ferrite (tetracalcium aluminoferrite, C4AF), about 5–15% — gives clinker its grey colour.

Small amounts of free lime, periclase, and alkali sulfates are also present.

Production

Raw meal is first calcined in a preheater tower, where calcium carbonate decomposes to calcium oxide and carbon dioxide, then enters the rotary kiln, where the burning zone temperature of roughly 1450 °C forms alite. The clinker is rapidly cooled in a grate or planetary cooler to preserve its reactive phases. Clinkering is energy- and emissions-intensive: calcination and fuel combustion together make cement production responsible for roughly 8% of global carbon dioxide emissions, motivating supplementary cementitious materials and clinker substitution.

Use

Clinker is an internationally traded commodity, shipped to grinding plants where it is milled with gypsum (to control setting) and, in blended cements, with slag, fly ash, or limestone.
