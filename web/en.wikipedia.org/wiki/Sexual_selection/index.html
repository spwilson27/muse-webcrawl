Sexual selection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sexual selection is a mode of natural selection in which members of one sex choose mates of the other sex (mate choice, or intersexual selection) and compete with members of the same sex for access to mates (intrasexual selection). Proposed by Charles Darwin in The Descent of Man (1871), it explains traits that seem to hinder survival — most famously the peacock's extravagant train — because they increase reproductive success.

Mechanisms

Choosiness, usually by females, can favour ornaments as honest signals of health and good genes, or through a self-reinforcing 'runaway' process described by R. A. Fisher. Competition ranges from direct combat (antlers, horns) to displays and sperm competition. Sexual selection also drives sexual dimorphism, differing sizes and colours of the sexes, and can contribute to speciation when preferences diverge between populations.

Evidence and debate

Experiments on guppies, stalk-eyed flies and birds confirm that preferences shape ornament evolution, though which explanation dominates — good genes, sensory bias, or runaway — varies by species. The concept languished for decades after Darwin, partly from Victorian discomfort with female choice, before being revived in the 1970s.
