Observational astronomy

Observatories collect electromagnetic radiation — and, increasingly, particles and gravitational waves — from astronomical sources.

Observational astronomy is a division of astronomy that is concerned with recording data about the observable universe, in contrast with theoretical astronomy, which is mainly concerned with calculating the measurable implications of physical models. It is the practice of observing celestial objects by using telescopes and other astronomical apparatus.

Methods

Historically, observational astronomy was limited to the visible spectrum and the naked eye. The invention of the telescope in the early 17th century transformed the field. Modern observers work across the whole electromagnetic spectrum: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, and gamma-ray astronomy each use specialised instruments, many of them space-based to avoid atmospheric absorption and distortion.

Techniques

Key techniques include photometry (measuring brightness), spectroscopy (dispersing light to reveal composition, temperature, and motion), astrometry (precise positions and motions), and imaging with CCD and CMOS detectors. Interferometry combines signals from separated telescopes to achieve the resolution of a much larger instrument. Adaptive optics corrects for atmospheric turbulence in real time.

New messengers

Since the late 20th century, "multi-messenger" astronomy has added neutrino detectors (such as IceCube), cosmic-ray observatories, and gravitational-wave interferometers (LIGO, Virgo, KAGRA), whose first detection of merging black holes in 2015 opened an entirely new observational window on the universe.

Amateur astronomy

Amateurs continue to contribute, monitoring variable stars, discovering comets and supernovae, and tracking near-Earth asteroids, often coordinating through organisations such as the AAVSO.
