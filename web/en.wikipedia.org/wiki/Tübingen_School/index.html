Tübingen School

The Tübingen School (German: Tübinger Schule) refers to two related movements in German Protestant theology centred on the University of Tübingen. The Older Tübingen School comprised the "supranaturalist" theologians of the late 18th and early 19th centuries around Gottlob Christian Storr. The Younger or New Tübingen School, which is what the term usually denotes, was founded by Ferdinand Christian Baur (1792–1860) and became the most influential school of historical-critical study of the New Testament in the 19th century.

Baur's theory

Baur applied a Hegelian dialectical scheme to early church history. He argued that primitive Christianity was split between a Jewish-Christian (Petrine) party, holding to the law and a particularist gospel, and a Gentile (Pauline) party, preaching universal grace; the early Catholic Church arose as the synthesis reconciling the two. On this thesis, only four of the letters attributed to Paul — Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, and Galatians — were accepted as genuine, and most other New Testament writings were dated to the 2nd century as "tendency writings" (Tendenzschriften) composed to advance one party's position.

Members and influence

Baur's pupils and followers included Albert Schwegler, Eduard Zeller, Adolf Hilgenfeld and, in a younger generation, Albrecht Ritschl, who later broke away to found his own school. David Friedrich Strauss, whose sensational Life of Jesus Critically Examined (1835) treated the Gospel miracles as myth, was associated with the circle though not formally Baur's student.

The school's specific conclusions — especially its late datings — were progressively overturned by later scholarship and by the study of the Apostolic Fathers and early papyri, and by the late 19th century the Tübingen synthesis was largely abandoned. Its lasting legacy is methodological: it permanently established the historical-critical method, the demand that New Testament documents be dated and interpreted by their historical setting, and the recognition of diversity and conflict within earliest Christianity.
