Lie algebra

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Lie algebra is a vector space 𝔤 over a field, together with a bilinear operation called the Lie bracket, written [x, y], satisfying two conditions: it is alternating (equivalently, in characteristic other than 2, anticommutative: [x, y] = −[y, x] and [x, x] = 0), and it obeys the Jacobi identity: [x, [y, z]] + [y, [z, x]] + [z, [x, y]] = 0. Lie algebras are named after Sophus Lie, who introduced them in the 1870s to study the symmetries of differential equations.

Relation to Lie groups

Every Lie group has an associated Lie algebra: the tangent space at the identity element, with the bracket arising from the commutator of left-invariant vector fields (or, for matrix groups, simply [x, y] = xy − yx). The algebra captures the group's infinitesimal structure, and the exponential map links the two. Conversely, every finite-dimensional real Lie algebra is the algebra of some Lie group (Lie's third theorem).

Examples and classification

An abelian Lie algebra has all brackets zero. The algebra 𝔰𝔬(3) of rotations, with the cross product as bracket, and the special linear algebras 𝔰𝔩(n) of trace-zero matrices are standard examples. Semisimple Lie algebras over the complex numbers were classified by Killing and Cartan into the four infinite families An, Bn, Cn, Dn and five exceptional types (G₂, F₄, E₆, E₇, E₈) — a classification encoded in Dynkin diagrams. Lie algebras are fundamental in modern geometry and in theoretical physics, where they describe the symmetries underlying gauge theories and particle physics.
