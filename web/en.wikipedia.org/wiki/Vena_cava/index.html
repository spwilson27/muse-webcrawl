Venae cavae

The venae cavae (from Latin venae cavae, "hollow veins") are two large veins (superior and inferior vena cava) that return deoxygenated blood from the body into the heart. They both empty into the right atrium of the heart. The inferior vena cava (IVC) is the largest vein in the human body, formed by the joining of the common iliac veins; the superior vena cava (SVC) is formed by the joining of the brachiocephalic veins.

Superior vena cava

The superior vena cava is a short valveless vein, about 7 cm long, that receives blood from the upper half of the body — the head, neck, upper limbs and thorax — and delivers it to the right atrium. It is formed by the union of the left and right brachiocephalic veins behind the lower border of the first costal cartilage. The azygos vein joins it just before it enters the heart.

Inferior vena cava

The inferior vena cava returns blood from the lower half of the body, including the abdomen, pelvis and lower limbs. It ascends on the right side of the vertebral column, passes through the diaphragm at the level of the eighth thoracic vertebra, and enters the right atrium. Along its course it receives the common iliac, lumbar, renal, hepatic and other tributary veins. The Eustachian valve at its opening into the atrium is a remnant of fetal circulation.

Clinical significance

Obstruction of the superior vena cava, often by malignancy, produces superior vena cava syndrome, with swelling of the face and arms and distended neck veins. The inferior vena cava may be compressed in pregnancy or obstructed by thrombosis; filters are sometimes placed within it to prevent pulmonary embolism. Congenital anomalies of the venae cavae include duplication and a left-sided inferior vena cava.
