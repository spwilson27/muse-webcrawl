Code duello

A code duello is a set of rules for one-on-one combat, or duelling, that governed affairs of honour among gentlemen in Europe and the Americas from the Renaissance until duelling's decline in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The rules prescribed how a challenge was to be issued, the role of seconds, the choice of weapons, and the conditions on which an apology or a drawn duel could restore honour without bloodshed.

Irish Code Duello

The most famous codification is the Irish Code Duello, adopted at the Clonmel assizes in 1777 by delegates from several Irish counties. Its twenty-six rules covered everything from the classification of offences and the order of apology, to the duties of seconds in loading pistols and measuring ground, and the rule that firing was not to begin before the signal. It was widely reprinted and adopted, with variations, in Britain and the United States.

American codes

In the United States, former South Carolina governor John Lyde Wilson published The Code of Honor in 1838, which became the standard reference in the South. Duels under these codes were usually fought with smoothbore pistols at a set number of paces. Famous duels fought under such conventions include the Burr–Hamilton duel of 1804.

Decline

As duelling was outlawed and fell into disrepute, the codes became historical documents, though they continued to be invoked in some European fencing and student duelling traditions into the twentieth century.
