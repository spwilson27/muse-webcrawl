Penning trap


Type: Charged particle trap
Named after: Frans Michel Penning

A Penning trap is a device for storing charged particles using a homogeneous magnetic field and an inhomogeneous quadrupole electric field. It is named after the Dutch physicist Frans Michel Penning, who built an early vacuum gauge based on related principles in the 1930s, and was developed into a storage device by Hans Georg Dehmelt and others in the 1950s and 1960s.

Principle of operation

The magnetic field confines particles radially by forcing them into cyclotron orbits, while the electric field—produced by hyperbolic electrodes or a stack of cylindrical ring electrodes—confines them along the magnetic field axis. A trapped ion executes three characteristic motions: the fast modified cyclotron motion, the slow magnetron drift, and the axial oscillation. By measuring the frequencies of these motions, the mass-to-charge ratio of the stored particle can be determined with extraordinary precision.

Applications

Penning traps are used for precision mass spectrometry of stable and radioactive ions, measurements of fundamental constants such as the electron magnetic moment, tests of quantum electrodynamics, and as a platform for quantum computing with trapped ions. The celebrated measurement of the electron's g-factor by Dehmelt's group held a single electron in a Penning trap for months at a time, earning Dehmelt a share of the 1989 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Comparison with Paul traps

Unlike the Paul trap, which uses radio-frequency fields, the Penning trap uses static fields only. Penning traps offer deeper trapping potentials for single particles, while Paul traps are often more convenient for large ion clouds and laser cooling experiments.
