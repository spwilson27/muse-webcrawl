The Jesuits (officially the Society of Jesus; Latin: Societas Iesu, abbreviated SJ) are a religious order of clerks regular of pontifical right for men in the Catholic Church, founded in 1540 by Ignatius of Loyola and six companions, including Francis Xavier and Peter Faber. Jesuits take vows of poverty, chastity, and obedience, plus a special fourth vow of obedience to the Pope regarding missions. Known for their work in education, scholarship, missions, and cultural pursuits, the Jesuits have operated schools, colleges, and universities on every continent and played a major role in the Counter-Reformation.

The order was suppressed by Pope Clement XIV in 1773 under pressure from European Catholic monarchies, surviving principally in the Russian Empire, and was restored universally by Pope Pius VII in 1814. In 2013, Jorge Mario Bergoglio became Pope Francis, the first Jesuit pope. The Jesuits are the largest male religious order in the Catholic Church.

Foundation and early history

Ignatius of Loyola, a Basque soldier converted during convalescence after the Battle of Pamplona (1521), composed the Spiritual Exercises and gathered companions at the University of Paris. On 15 August 1534, Ignatius and six companions — Francis Xavier, Peter Faber, Diego Laínez, Alfonso Salmerón, Nicolás Bobadilla, and Simão Rodrigues — took vows at Montmartre in Paris. Pope Paul III approved the order in 1540, and Ignatius was elected first Superior General in 1541, serving until his death in 1556. The Constitutions (1558) organised the order on disciplined, mobile, and centralised lines, owing loyalty directly to the Pope.

Jesuits quickly became prominent confessors to Catholic monarchs, theologians of the Counter-Reformation, and founders of colleges across Europe; their Ratio Studiorum (1599) standardised Jesuit education for centuries.

Missions

Francis Xavier reached India (1542) and Japan (1549) and died off the coast of China (1552); Matteo Ricci entered China (1582) and won scholarly respect at the Ming court through mathematics, astronomy, and cultural accommodation — a policy later condemned in the Chinese Rites controversy. Jesuits such as the martyrs of Canada (Jean de Brébeuf and companions) evangelised in New France; José de Anchieta worked in Brazil; and in Paraguay the Jesuit reducciones (reductions) organised Guaraní communities famed for their music and crafts and protected — imperfectly — against slavers, until the order's expulsion from Spanish territory in 1767.

Jesuit missionaries were also significant ethnographers, cartographers, and linguists, documenting languages and cultures in Asia, Africa, and the Americas.

Education and scholarship

Jesuit institutions include Georgetown, Boston College, Fordham, and Loyola universities in the United States; Sophia University in Tokyo; the Pontifical Gregorian University in Rome; and a worldwide network of secondary schools whose alumni include numerous heads of state and scientists. Jesuits made major contributions to astronomy (Christoph Clavius and the Gregorian calendar, Giovanni Battista Riccioli, Angelo Secchi), mathematics, seismology ("Jesuit science"), printing in Asia, and sinology. The Bollandists produce the Acta Sanctorum; Jesuit publications include La Civiltà Cattolica and America magazine.

Suppression and restoration

Accused by enlightened absolutist governments of political interference, commerce, and excessive influence, the Jesuits were expelled from Portugal (1759), France (1764), and Spain and its empire (1767); in 1773 Clement XIV suppressed the order entirely with the brief Dominus ac Redemptor. Catherine II of Russia refused to promulgate the brief, so the Society survived in White Russia and was tacitly tolerated elsewhere. Pius VII restored the Society universally in 1814 with the bull Sollicitudo omnium ecclesiarum.

Modern era

The restored Society rebuilt its schools and missions, navigated 19th-century anticlericalism, and in the 20th century produced theologians central to the Second Vatican Council (Karl Rahner, John Courtney Murray, Henri de Lubac). The 32nd General Congregation (1974–75) under Pedro Arrupe committed the order to "the service of faith and the promotion of justice," drawing Jesuits into social activism, sometimes at deadly cost, as in the 1989 murder of six Jesuits at the Central American University in El Salvador. Pierre Teilhard de Chardin's evolutionary theology is among the Society's best-known intellectual legacies. Membership peaked around 36,000 in 1965 and has since declined to roughly 14,000–15,000.
