Latin America during World War II

During World War II, the countries of Latin America were drawn into the conflict politically, economically, and in some cases militarily. Although most remained formally neutral for much of the war, the region supplied strategic raw materials to the Allies, hosted intelligence struggles between the Axis and Allied powers, and ultimately saw nearly every Latin American state declare war on the Axis.

Neutrality and the road to war

After the outbreak of war in 1939, Latin American governments, meeting at Panama, declared a neutrality zone around the Americas. Following the attack on Pearl Harbor, most Central American and Caribbean states declared war on the Axis promptly. The Battle of the River Plate (1939) and German U-boat attacks on Brazilian and Mexican shipping pushed the larger countries toward war: Mexico declared war in May 1942 after its oil tankers were sunk, and Brazil followed in August 1942.

Military contributions

Brazil made the largest direct contribution, sending the Brazilian Expeditionary Force — some 25,000 troops — to fight in Italy in 1944–45, and its navy and air force patrolled the South Atlantic. Mexico contributed the 201st Fighter Squadron ("Aztec Eagles"), which flew missions in the Philippines in 1945. Other nations provided bases, notably in the Galápagos, Brazil's northeast bulge, and the Panama Canal Zone.

Home front and aftermath

The war stimulated import-substitution industrialisation as manufactured imports dried up, and Allied demand raised prices for rubber, oil, tin, and foodstuffs. Axis espionage networks in Argentina, Chile, and Brazil were largely rolled up by 1944. Argentina, which had maintained neutrality longest, declared war in March 1945. The war strengthened United States influence in the hemisphere and helped lead to the Inter-American Treaty of Reciprocal Assistance (Rio Treaty) of 1947.
