Trichophyton is a genus of fungi that includes the parasitic varieties causing dermatophytosis — the infections of skin, hair, and nails commonly known as ringworm, athlete's foot, and jock itch. Along with Microsporum and Epidermophyton, Trichophyton is one of the three classic genera of dermatophytes, fungi that feed on keratin.

Species and disease

The genus contains several dozen species, classified by their primary habitat as anthropophilic (adapted to humans), zoophilic (adapted to animals), or geophilic (soil-dwelling). Important pathogens include:

  • Trichophyton rubrum — the commonest cause of athlete's foot (tinea pedis), onychomycosis, and chronic ringworm worldwide.
  • Trichophyton interdigitale and T. mentagrophytes — causes of tinea pedis, tinea corporis, and inflammatory animal-transmitted infections.
  • Trichophyton tonsurans — the leading cause of tinea capitis (scalp ringworm) in the Americas.
  • Trichophyton schoenleinii — the cause of favus, a severe chronic scalp infection.
  • Trichophyton violaceum and T. verrucosum — the latter associated with cattle ringworm in farmers.

Identification and treatment

In culture, colonies of Trichophyton range from white and powdery to pigmented, and species are identified by colony morphology, microscopic features — smooth, thin-walled macroconidia and characteristic microconidia — and increasingly by DNA sequencing. Infections are diagnosed by microscopic examination of skin scrapings in potassium hydroxide and by culture. Treatment uses topical antifungals for limited skin infections and oral agents such as terbinafine or itraconazole for nail, scalp, or extensive disease. Emerging terbinafine-resistant strains, notably Trichophyton indotineae associated with widespread recalcitrant dermatophytosis in South Asia, have become a significant clinical concern.
