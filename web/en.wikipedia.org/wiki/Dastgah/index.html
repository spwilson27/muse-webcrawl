Dastgah

Dastgāh (Persian: دستگاه, pronounced [dæstˈɡɒːh]) is the standard musical system in Persian art music — a modal system that provides the basis for improvisation and composition. A dastgah is neither a scale nor a single melody, but a collection of melodic models (gushehs) organized around characteristic phrases, pivotal notes, and customary paths of movement, which the performer weaves into an improvised performance.

Definition

The word dastgah means "system" or "apparatus" and also relates to the positioning of the fingers on the neck of a stringed instrument. Each dastgah comprises an ordered set of gushehs — short, named melodic units — beginning with an introductory daramad and proceeding through pieces of increasing intensity before returning to the modal center. Notes may include microtones (koron, lowered by a quarter tone; sori, raised), which give Persian modes their distinctive intervals.

The radif

The canonical repertoire, the radif, was assembled in the 19th century by Mirza Abdollah and his brother Aqa Hossein-Qoli, court musicians of Qajar Persia, and transmitted orally from master to student. Several versions of the radif have been notated and recorded; in 2009 the Iranian radif was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

The twelve dastgahs

The system conventionally comprises seven dastgahs proper — Shur, Mahur, Homayun, Segah, Chahargah, Nava, and Rast-Panjgah — and five derivative avaz (Bayat-e Tork, Bayat-e Esfahan, Abu'ata, Afshari, and Dashti), most of them derived from Shur. Shur is considered the mother dastgah, generating the largest family.

Performance

A performance traditionally unfolds as a suite: pishdaramad (prelude), daramad, improvised avaz sections on the gushehs, tasnif (a composed song), and reng (a lively closing dance). Instruments include the tar, setar, santur, kamancheh, ney, and tombak; vocalists improvise on classical Persian poetry by poets such as Hafez and Rumi.
