The Trobriand Islands are an archipelago of low-lying coral atolls off the eastern tip of New Guinea, in Milne Bay Province, Papua New Guinea. The largest island is Kiriwina; others include Kitava, Vakuta, and Kaileuna. They are world-famous in anthropology through the fieldwork of Bronisław Malinowski, whose Argonauts of the Western Pacific (1922) made the islands a classic case study of economic and kinship organisation.

Society and culture

Trobriand society is matrilineal: descent, clan membership, and inheritance of land and magic pass through women, while men hold most formal political authority as chiefs and garden magicians. Yams are the prestige staple—harvests are displayed in ornate yam houses and presented in great quantities to a man's sister's husband (urigubu payments), underpinning chiefly power. Elaborate mortuary feasts (sagali) redistribute food and valuables. A famous feature of Trobriand life is the relative sexual freedom of adolescents, documented by Malinowski in The Sexual Life of Savages (1929); the islands were nicknamed the "Islands of Love".

The Kula ring

The Trobriands are the centre of the Kula ring, a ceremonial exchange system spanning the Massim archipelagos: red shell necklaces (soulava) circulate clockwise and white shell armbands (mwali) counter-clockwise between lifelong partners on different islands, carried on dangerous open-sea voyages in outrigger canoes. The valuables themselves are not useful goods; their prestige lies in their histories and in the fame their temporary possession confers. Alongside Kula, ordinary barter (gimwali) takes place.

Later history

In the Second World War the islands hosted Allied airfields and a major base (Operation Cartwheel). Cricket, introduced by missionaries to channel inter-village rivalry, evolved into a distinctive Trobriand version with political and ritual overtones, famously filmed in Trobriand Cricket (1975). Annette Weiner's restudy in the 1970s corrected Malinowski by documenting women's wealth in banana-leaf bundles and skirts. The islands today face pressures from population growth, gardening land limits, and mission Christianity.
