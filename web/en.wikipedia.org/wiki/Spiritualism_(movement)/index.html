Spiritualism (movement)

Spiritualism (or modern Spiritualism) is a religious movement based on the belief that the spirits of the dead exist and have both the ability and the inclination to communicate with the living. The movement is conventionally dated from 31 March 1848, when the Fox sisters of Hydesville, New York, claimed contact with a spirit through mysterious rapping sounds. It grew rapidly in the United States and United Kingdom through the 19th century, flourishing especially after the mass bereavements of the American Civil War and the First World War.

Beliefs and practice

Spiritualists hold that the soul survives physical death and continues to evolve in a spirit world, and that communication is possible through mediums — by clairvoyance, trance speaking, automatic writing, or (more controversially) physical phenomena such as table-turning and materializations. Services typically combine hymns, prayer, an address, healing, and demonstrations of mediumship. The movement famously lacked a fixed creed, though bodies such as the British Spiritualists' National Union adopted Seven Principles, including the fatherhood of God, the brotherhood of man, and eternal progress open to every soul.

History

Influenced by Swedenborg and Mesmerism, the movement attracted millions of adherents and prominent supporters — among them Arthur Conan Doyle — as well as committed investigators and debunkers such as Harry Houdini. Fraud scandals, including the Fox sisters' 1888 confession (later partly retracted), damaged its credibility, but Spiritualist churches persist today, notably at camps such as Lily Dale in New York and Cassadaga in Florida. In France and Brazil, Allan Kardec's Spiritism developed as a related but distinct doctrine.
