Track gauge

In rail transport, track gauge is the distance between the two rails of a railway track, measured between the inner faces of the rail heads. All vehicles on a network must have running gear compatible with the gauge, and the gauge is one of the basic parameters determining interoperability. Gauges wider than standard are called broad gauge, narrower ones narrow gauge.

Track gauge is measured between the inner faces of the rails.

Standard gauge

The most widely used gauge is standard gauge, 1,435 mm (4 ft 8½ in), used on roughly 55–60% of the world's railways, including most high-speed lines and the networks of North America, most of Europe, China, and Australia (in part). It originated in the coal tramways of northern England and was promoted by George Stephenson; Britain made it the legal standard with the Railway Regulation (Gauge) Act 1846.

Break of gauge

Where lines of different gauges meet, a break of gauge occurs: passengers must change trains and freight must be transshipped, or vehicles must change bogies or use variable-gauge axles. Breaks of gauge persist at the borders of the former Soviet Union, between Spain and France (now mitigated by standard-gauge high-speed lines), and within Australia, where the colonial states adopted three different gauges. Dual-gauge track, with three or four rails, is used at some junctions and along shared corridors.

Choice of gauge

Broader gauges allow greater stability and larger, heavier trains, while narrower gauges permit tighter curves and cheaper construction in mountainous terrain. Once a network is built, conversion is expensive, so early decisions — often made for local or even accidental reasons — have persisted for more than a century.
