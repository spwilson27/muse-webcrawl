Literary journalism

Tools of the literary journalist's trade

Literary journalism, also known as narrative nonfiction or creative nonfiction, is a form of journalism that applies the techniques of literature — scene-by-scene construction, dialogue, point of view, and immersion reporting — to factual reporting. Rather than the inverted pyramid of news writing, literary journalism tells true stories with the structure and voice of the novel or short story.

History

Antecedents reach back to nineteenth-century writers such as Daniel Defoe (earlier still) and to figures like Stephen Crane and Jack London, who blended reportage with narrative. The form came to prominence in the 1960s and 1970s as the "New Journalism", a term popularised by Tom Wolfe's 1973 anthology of the same name. Leading practitioners included Wolfe himself, Gay Talese, Joan Didion, Norman Mailer, and Hunter S. Thompson, whose "gonzo" style made the reporter a character in the story.

Landmark works include Truman Capote's In Cold Blood (1966), describing a Kansas murder case and marketed as a "nonfiction novel"; Mailer's The Armies of the Night (1968) and The Executioner's Song (1979); Talese's profiles of Frank Sinatra and Joe DiMaggio; and John Hersey's earlier Hiroshima (1946), often cited as a foundational text.

Characteristics

Literary journalism typically involves extended immersion in a subject's world, extensive interviews, and attention to status details of everyday life. Writers aim for accuracy of fact combined with narrative shaping: scenes, dialogue (reconstructed from interviews and notes), interior perspectives, and symbolic detail. Because it relies on techniques associated with fiction, the genre has periodically faced controversies over composite characters, reconstructed dialogue, and embellishment.

Modern practitioners

Contemporary literary journalists include Katherine Boo (Behind the Beautiful Forevers), Adrian Nicole LeBlanc (Random Family), David Grann, Patrick Radden Keefe (Say Nothing), and Isabel Wilkerson. Magazines such as The New Yorker, Harper's, and The Atlantic have been central venues for the form.
