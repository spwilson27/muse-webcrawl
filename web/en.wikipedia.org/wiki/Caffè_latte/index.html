Caffè latte (Italian for "milk coffee"), often shortened to latte, is a coffee drink made with espresso and steamed milk, topped with a thin layer of milk foam. A typical latte uses one or two shots of espresso with roughly three parts steamed milk to one part foam, making it milkier and milder than a cappuccino.

History

Combining coffee and milk is centuries old in Europe, but the modern latte was largely developed in the United States: Berkeley, California's Caffe Mediterraneum claims its co-owner Lino Meiorin introduced the latte in its present form in the 1950s, catering to customers who found cappuccinos too strong. From there it spread through Seattle's coffee culture and worldwide via coffeehouse chains.

Variations and latte art

The flat white, from Australia and New Zealand, uses velvet-textured steamed milk with less volume. Flavoured lattes add syrups such as vanilla or caramel; the pumpkin spice latte became a seasonal cultural phenomenon in the 2000s. Baristas pour steamed milk to form patterns — hearts, rosettas, tulips — on the surface, a craft known as latte art.
