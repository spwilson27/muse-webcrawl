Meroë (Meroitic: Medewi) was an ancient city on the east bank of the Nile in present-day Sudan, the southern capital and principal royal residence of the Kingdom of Kush for nearly a millennium. It is famous above all for its royal pyramid fields — more pyramids were built at Meroë and its associated cemeteries than in all of Egypt — and for the undeciphered Meroitic language in which its inscriptions are written. Together with the nearby sites of Musawwarat es-Sufra and Naqa, it forms the UNESCO World Heritage Site "Island of Meroe" (the "island" being the seasonally water-bound Butana plain between the Nile and Atbara rivers).

History

Kushite kings — the same dynasty that ruled Egypt as its Twenty-fifth ("Nubian") Dynasty in the 8th–7th centuries BC — long had twin centres: Napata, near the holy mountain of Jebel Barkal, and Meroë in the south. After the Egyptian pharaoh Psamtik II's campaign against Kush (593 BC), royal burials and power shifted decisively south to Meroë, which remained the capital while Napata kept its religious prestige. The kingdom prospered on the trade of the Nile and the Butana — exporting gold, ivory, ebony, slaves, and iron — and dealt with Ptolemaic and then Roman Egypt as a sometimes-rival, sometimes-partner. A celebrated confrontation came in the 20s BC, when Queen (Kandake) Amanirenas resisted Augustus's forces after the Roman annexation of Egypt; the ensuing treaty fixed a favourable border for Kush, and a bronze head of Augustus, looted from Egypt, was buried beneath a temple steps at Meroë where worshippers would tread on it — it is now in the British Museum. Meroë declined in the 3rd–4th centuries CE, perhaps through over-exploitation of its environment, shifting trade, and pressure from the rising kingdom of Aksum; an inscription of the Aksumite king Ezana is often read as claiming a campaign into Kush around 350 CE, by which time Meroitic civilisation had collapsed.

The city and the pyramids

The royal city included a palace quarter, temples of Amun and Apedemak (the indigenous lion god), baths in Roman style, sun-temple complexes, and vast slag heaps from iron smelting that led early scholars to call Meroë the "Birmingham of ancient Africa" — a label modern archaeologists treat with caution about scale. Kings and queens were buried in three cemeteries at Begrawiya, a few kilometres away: the Western, Southern, and (grandest) Northern cemeteries contain in total more than 200 pyramids. Meroitic pyramids are much smaller than Egypt's — typically 10–30 metres high, steeply angled, built of stone blocks over a rubble core, each with a small offering chapel on its eastern face decorated with reliefs of the monarch before the gods. Many were plundered in antiquity, and in the 1830s the Italian treasure-hunter Giuseppe Ferlini demolished several pyramid tops searching for gold, finding the treasure of Queen Amanishakhete.

Meroitic script

The Meroitic language replaced Egyptian for royal inscriptions from about the 2nd century BC and was written in two scripts, hieroglyphic and cursive, whose letter values were deciphered in the early 20th century by Francis Llewellyn Griffith. The language itself remains essentially untranslated — we can read the words aloud without securely knowing what they mean — making Meroë one of the great unsolved problems of ancient history. Queens regnant, titled Kandake (the origin of the name "Candace" in classical sources), were strikingly prominent at Meroë, ruling in their own right in a way unusual for the ancient world.
