Borough

TypeAdministrative division / municipality
Found inUnited Kingdom, United States, Canada, Australia, and others
EtymologyOld English burh, "fortified place"

A borough is an administrative division used in various English-speaking countries. In principle, the term designates a self-governing walled town, although in practice the formal meaning varies considerably between jurisdictions. The word derives from the Old English burh, meaning a fortified settlement.

United Kingdom

In England, boroughs historically were towns granted a royal charter, giving them a degree of self-government, a mayor, and the right to send members to Parliament. The Municipal Corporations Act 1835 reformed the system, and later legislation reorganized local government into county boroughs and metropolitan boroughs. Greater London is divided into 32 London boroughs, each with its own council. Borough status in England, Wales, and Northern Ireland is now largely honorary at the district level, conferred by the Crown.

United States

In the United States the term has several meanings. In Pennsylvania, a borough is a type of municipality similar to a town. In Alaska, boroughs are county equivalents. In New York City, each of the city's five counties is also a borough — Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx, and Staten Island — a usage dating to the 1898 consolidation.

Elsewhere

In Canada, boroughs have been used as subdivisions of cities such as Montreal (arrondissements in French). In Australia the term survives in the Borough of Queenscliffe in Victoria. Several other countries use cognates or borrowings of the term for local government units.
