Côte-d'Or

Côte-d'Or (French pronunciation: [kot dɔʁ], literally "Golden Slope") is a department in the Bourgogne-Franche-Comté region of northeastern France. It is named after the Côte d'Or escarpment, the limestone ridge west of Dijon whose autumn vineyards turn golden. In 2019 it had a population of about 533,000. Its prefecture is Dijon, the regional capital, famed for mustard, gingerbread and the Dukes of Burgundy.

Geography and history

Created on 4 March 1790 during the French Revolution from part of the province of Burgundy, the department combines the Dijon plain and Saône valley, the limestone Côte, the forested Morvan foothills and the Auxois — where Julius Caesar defeated Vercingetorix at Alesia (52 BC), a site now marked by the MuséoParc Alésia. The Canal de Bourgogne links the Saône to the Yonne.

Wine

The Côte d'Or escarpment forms the heartland of Burgundy wine, divided into the Côte de Nuits (north) and Côte de Beaune (south). Its villages produce many of the world's most expensive wines from Pinot noir and Chardonnay — Romanée-Conti, Chambertin, Montrachet, Pommard and Meursault among them. The climats of the Côte were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2015. Dijon is also the home of crème de cassis, used in the Kir apéritif.
