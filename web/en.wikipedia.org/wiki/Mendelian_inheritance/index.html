Mendelian inheritance (also called Mendelism) is a set of principles of heredity derived from the experiments of the Augustinian friar Gregor Johann Mendel, who crossed pea plants (Pisum sativum) at St Thomas's Abbey in Brno and published his results in 1866. Mendel showed that discrete traits are inherited as units — later called genes — that segregate and assort when passed from parents to offspring, rather than blending together.

Mendel's experiments

Between 1856 and 1863 Mendel cultivated and tested some 28,000 pea plants, tracking seven contrasting traits (seed shape and colour, flower colour and position, pod shape and colour, and stem length). In monohybrid crosses, the first filial (F1) generation displayed only one of the two traits (the dominant), while the F2 generation recovered the hidden (recessive) trait in a consistent ratio of approximately 3:1. Dihybrid crosses produced the characteristic 9:3:3:1 ratio.

The laws

Law of Segregation: the two copies (alleles) of a gene in an individual separate during gamete formation, so each gamete carries only one allele. Law of Independent Assortment: genes for different traits are inherited independently of one another (true in general for genes on different chromosomes; genes close together on the same chromosome are linked, a later refinement). Dominance: when two different alleles are present, one may mask the expression of the other, though phenomena such as incomplete dominance and codominance extend the simple picture.

Rediscovery and legacy

Mendel's paper was largely ignored until 1900, when Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns and Erich von Tschermak independently obtained similar results and recognised his priority. William Bateson championed the work in English and coined the term "genetics". Mendelian principles, combined with the chromosome theory of inheritance, became the foundation of classical genetics, and "Mendelian" disorders — single-gene conditions such as cystic fibrosis and Huntington's disease — are still contrasted with complex, multigenic traits. Exceptions and extensions include linkage, epistasis, pleiotropy and non-Mendelian inheritance (mitochondrial, epigenetic, and others).
