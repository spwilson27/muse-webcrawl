Neural tube

In the developing chordate embryo, the neural tube is the embryonic precursor to the central nervous system, which is made up of the brain and spinal cord. The neural groove gradually deepens as the neural folds become elevated, and ultimately the folds close to form the neural tube in a process called neurulation.

Development

The neural tube develops in two ways. In primary neurulation, cells surrounding the neural plate proliferate, invaginate, and pinch off from the surface ectoderm to form a hollow tube. In secondary neurulation, the tube arises from a solid cord of cells that cavitates. In humans, the spinal cord from about the sacral level downwards forms by secondary neurulation, while the brain and most of the cord form by primary neurulation.

Closure begins near the middle of the embryo (at the level of the fourth somite) and proceeds both cranially and caudally, so that for a time the tube is open at the anterior and posterior neuropores; these normally close around days 25 and 27 respectively. The lumen of the tube becomes the ventricular system of the brain and the central canal of the spinal cord.

Structure and patterning

The wall of the early tube consists of a single layer of pseudostratified neuroepithelial cells. Along the dorsoventral axis, the floor plate and roof plate organise patterning through morphogens such as sonic hedgehog (ventrally) and BMPs (dorsally), specifying motor neurons ventrally and sensory interneurons dorsally. Cranially, the tube expands and folds into the primary and then secondary brain vesicles.

Neural tube defects

Failure of closure leads to neural tube defects: craniorachischisis (complete failure), anencephaly (failure at the cranial end), and spina bifida (failure in the spinal region). These are among the most common serious congenital malformations, and periconceptional folic acid supplementation substantially reduces their incidence.
