The Battle of Khe Sanh was fought in early 1968 around the United States Marine Corps combat base at Khe Sanh in northwestern Quảng Trị Province, South Vietnam, near the Laotian border and the DMZ. Two divisions of the People's Army of Vietnam (PAVN) besieged the base for 77 days, in one of the longest and most publicised engagements of the Vietnam War.

Background

Khe Sanh guarded Route 9, an approach toward the coast, and formed the western anchor of the strongpoints below the DMZ. US commander General William Westmoreland valued it as a potential jump-off point against the Ho Chi Minh Trail in Laos and refused to abandon it. In January 1968, as the Tet Offensive erupted across South Vietnam, PAVN forces cut Route 9 and surrounded the base, recalling for many Americans the French defeat at Dien Bien Phu in 1954.

The siege

From 21 January 1968 the garrison — chiefly the 26th Marine Regiment under Colonel David E. Lownds — endured daily artillery, mortar and rocket fire. The base was supplied entirely by air; cargo aircraft landing under fire used daring low-altitude extraction techniques, and parachute drops filled the gaps. In support, the US Air Force mounted Operation Niagara, one of the heaviest aerial bombardments of the war, dropping more than 100,000 tons of bombs around the base, with B-52 strikes directed close to the perimeter. On the night of 6–7 February the PAVN overran the nearby Special Forces camp at Lang Vei with Soviet-supplied PT-76 tanks — the first use of armour by North Vietnam in the war — but the main base was never stormed. In April, Operation Pegasus reopened Route 9 and relieved the siege.

Aftermath

In June–July 1968 the Americans demolished and quietly abandoned Khe Sanh as part of a new mobile strategy, a decision both sides presented as vindication. Historians still debate whether the siege was a genuine attempt to take the base or a diversion for Tet, and whether Westmoreland was correspondingly distracted from the cities. The battle fixed itself in American memory through intense television and press coverage, and President Johnson famously followed it on a terrain model in the White House.
