Cycling in the Netherlands

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cycling in the Netherlands
Modal shareRoughly 27% of all trips nationwide; over 35% in Amsterdam
Bicycles~23 million for ~17.5 million residents
Infrastructure~35,000 km of segregated cycle paths
Typical bicycleUpright "omafiets" (city roadster)

Cycling is the dominant mode of transport in the Netherlands: there are more bicycles than people, and approximately a quarter of all journeys nationwide are made by bike, rising to more than a third in cities such as Amsterdam and Utrecht. The Dutch cycle on an extensive network of physically separated cycle tracks, with dedicated traffic lights, parking facilities, and priority over motor vehicles in many settings.

History

Cycling was popular in the Netherlands from the late 19th century. Car ownership grew after the Second World War and cycling collapsed in the 1950s–60s, but in the 1970s campaigns such as Stop de Kindermoord ("Stop the Child Murder"), protesting road deaths of children, together with the 1973 oil crisis, pushed governments to rebuild cities around the bicycle. Car-free Sundays and sustained infrastructure investment reversed the decline.

Infrastructure

The country has tens of thousands of kilometres of separated cycle paths (fietspaden), numbered junction networks for touring, cycle "motorways" (snelfietsroutes) between cities, and vast bicycle-parking garages — Utrecht Centraal's facility holds over 12,000 bikes, among the largest in the world. Children typically cycle to school independently from a young age.

Culture

Cycling is treated as ordinary transport rather than sport: riders wear everyday clothes, helmets are rare for adults, and the upright black roadster — the omafiets ("grandma bike") — is ubiquitous. Cycle tourism and professional racing are also popular; Dutch riders have prominent records in road and cyclo-cross racing.
