Victor Saul Miller (born 3 March 1947) is an American mathematician and computer scientist. He is best known, together with Neal Koblitz, for independently proposing the use of elliptic curves in public-key cryptography in 1985, one of the foundational ideas of modern cryptographic practice.

Career

Miller received his bachelor's degree from Columbia University and his PhD in mathematics from Harvard University in 1974, with a thesis in number theory. He worked for many years at the IBM Thomas J. Watson Research Center in Yorktown Heights, New York, and later at the Center for Communications Research (IDA/CCR) in Princeton, New Jersey. His research interests include computational number theory, combinatorics, data compression, and cryptography.

Elliptic-curve cryptography

In 1985 Miller published "Use of Elliptic Curves in Cryptography", proposing cryptosystems whose security rests on the difficulty of the discrete logarithm problem in the group of points on an elliptic curve over a finite field. Koblitz proposed the same idea independently in the same period. Elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC) offers security comparable to RSA with much smaller key sizes, and it is now ubiquitous in protocols such as TLS. Miller also devised Miller's algorithm, the standard method for computing the Weil and Tate pairings on elliptic curves, which underlies pairing-based cryptography. Victor Miller's other namesake contributions include work in data compression, where he and Mark Wegman developed the LZRW family of algorithms at IBM in the 1980s.

Recognition

For his role in inventing elliptic-curve cryptography, Miller has received major honours including the Levchin Prize for real-world cryptography and induction into cryptographic halls of fame; the Certicom ECC Challenge and industry awards have recognized ECC's inventors collectively. His 1985 paper remains one of the most cited in applied cryptography.
