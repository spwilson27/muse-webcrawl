Aleksandr Lyapunov

Aleksandr Mikhailovich Lyapunov (Russian: Александр Михайлович Ляпунов; 6 June 1857 – 3 November 1918) was a Russian mathematician, mechanician and physicist. He is known for his development of the stability theory of a dynamical system, as well as for many contributions to mathematical physics and probability theory.

Early life and education

Lyapunov was born in Yaroslavl, Russian Empire, into a family of scholars: his father Mikhail was an astronomer and director of the Demidov Lyceum, and his brothers were the composer Sergei Lyapunov and the linguist Boris Lyapunov. He studied at the University of St. Petersburg, where he was a student of Pafnuty Chebyshev, graduating in 1880.

Work on stability

His 1892 doctoral dissertation, The General Problem of the Stability of Motion, founded the modern theory of stability. Lyapunov introduced two approaches now known as the first method (based on power-series expansions and characteristic exponents — Lyapunov exponents) and the second, or direct, method (based on constructing Lyapunov functions, scalar functions that decrease along trajectories to prove stability without solving the equations of motion). These ideas are fundamental in control theory, dynamical systems, and engineering.

Other contributions

Lyapunov made major contributions to the theory of equilibrium figures of rotating fluids, relevant to astronomy; to potential theory; and to probability, where he proved a form of the central limit theorem under general conditions (the Lyapunov condition) in 1901. He taught at Kharkov University from 1885, and in 1901 was elected to the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences and moved to the capital.

Death

Amid the turmoil of the Russian Civil War, Lyapunov moved to Odessa in 1918. After his wife's death from tuberculosis, he shot himself and died on 3 November 1918.
