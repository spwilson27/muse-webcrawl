Pituitary apoplexy

Pituitary apoplexy

SymptomsSudden severe headache, visual loss, ophthalmoplegia, vomiting, altered consciousness

Pituitary apoplexy is an acute clinical syndrome caused by haemorrhage or infarction of the pituitary gland, almost always occurring within a pituitary adenoma. It is a medical and potentially neurosurgical emergency that can cause sudden headache, visual impairment, and acute pituitary failure.

Signs and symptoms

The classic presentation is the abrupt onset of a severe ("thunderclap") headache, often with vomiting and meningism. Expansion of the gland compresses nearby structures: the optic chiasm, producing bitemporal visual field loss and reduced acuity; and the cranial nerves in the cavernous sinus (III, IV, VI), causing double vision and ophthalmoplegia. Acute ACTH deficiency may lead to hypotension and hypoadrenal crisis.

Causes and risk factors

Apoplexy occurs in an estimated 2–12% of pituitary adenomas, in many cases previously undiagnosed. Reported precipitating factors include hypertension, major surgery, anticoagulation, pregnancy, head trauma, and dynamic pituitary function testing.

Diagnosis

MRI of the pituitary is the investigation of choice, showing haemorrhage or infarction within an enlarged gland; CT may be used when MRI is unavailable. Urgent endocrine assessment is required because of the risk of secondary adrenal insufficiency.

Management

Initial treatment is stabilisation, high-dose hydrocortisone, and correction of fluid and electrolyte abnormalities. Patients with severe or deteriorating visual loss or reduced consciousness are usually offered urgent transsphenoidal surgical decompression; others may be managed conservatively with close monitoring. Long-term pituitary hormone replacement is often required.
