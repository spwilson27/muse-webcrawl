The Dutch Republic — officially the Republic of the Seven United Netherlands — was a confederal republic in northwestern Europe that existed from 1581 to 1795. It arose from the Dutch Revolt against Spanish Habsburg rule and comprised seven provinces: Holland, Zeeland, Utrecht, Gelderland, Overijssel, Friesland, and Groningen. During the 17th century "Dutch Golden Age" it became a great power in trade, science, art, and finance, despite its small size.

Government

Sovereignty rested with the provincial States, which sent delegates to the States General in The Hague. The province of Holland, dominated by Amsterdam, contributed the majority of the budget. Two offices shaped politics: the stadtholder, usually a prince of Orange, who commanded the army and navy; and the Grand Pensionary of Holland, the republic's leading statesman. The tension between republican regents and the Orangists defined Dutch politics for two centuries.

Golden Age

The Dutch East India Company (VOC, founded 1602) and West India Company built a global trading empire from Indonesia to the Caribbean and New Amsterdam. Amsterdam became Europe's financial centre, with the first modern stock exchange and the Bank of Amsterdam. Dutch painting flourished with Rembrandt, Vermeer, and Frans Hals; science with Huygens, Leeuwenhoek, and Grotius in law. The republic fought three Anglo-Dutch Wars with England and survived the catastrophic invasion year 1672 (the Rampjaar) against France, England, and German allies.

Decline and fall

In the 18th century the republic stagnated economically and politically. Reformist Patriots clashed with Orangists; French revolutionary armies invaded in 1795 and the republic was replaced by the Batavian Republic.
