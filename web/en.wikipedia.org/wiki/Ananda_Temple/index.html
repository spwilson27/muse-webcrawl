The Ananda Temple (Burmese: အာနန္ဒာဘုရား) is a Buddhist temple built in 1105 CE during the reign of King Kyansittha of the Pagan Dynasty. Located in Bagan, Myanmar, it is regarded as one of the most beautiful and best-preserved temples of the Bagan plains and a masterpiece of Burmese architecture, blending Mon and Indian (Nagara) design. The temple's name derives from Ananda, the Buddha's cousin and devoted attendant, and it is sometimes called the "Westminster Abbey of Burma".

Architecture

The temple is laid out on a cruciform (Greek cross) plan with several terraces leading to a small pagoda topped by a gilded umbrella (hti). The structure is built of brick covered in stucco, pierced by dormer windows that admit light into its two concentric ambulatories. Four huge standing Buddha images, nearly 9.5 metres tall and made of gilded teak, face the four cardinal directions in the central sanctuary; two of them are originals of the Pagan period. The corridors are lined with stone reliefs and glazed terracotta plaques depicting scenes from the Jataka tales and the life of the Buddha.

Legend

According to legend, eight Indian monks described to King Kyansittha the legendary Nandamula Cave in the Himalayas, where they had meditated; the king invited them to convey its cool atmosphere, and the temple's deep, well-ventilated corridors were designed in response. Legend further claims the king had the architects executed after completion so that the design could never be duplicated — a story historians treat as folklore.

Festival and restoration

The Ananda Temple festival, held around the full moon of Pyatho (roughly January), draws thousands of pilgrims and continues for weeks, with villagers arriving by oxcart to camp on the temple grounds. The temple was damaged in the 1975 Bagan earthquake and has been carefully restored; in 1990 its spire and walls were gilded and whitewashed to mark the 900th anniversary of its construction.
