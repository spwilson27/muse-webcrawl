Birch bark letter

Birch bark manuscripts are documents written on pieces of birch bark, once widespread in northern Eurasia. In medieval Novgorod and other East Slavic towns, hundreds of birch bark letters (berestyanye gramoty) have been excavated, revealing everyday literacy.

Birch bark letters
Region: Novgorod and other Rus' towns
Period: 11th–15th centuries
First found: 26 July 1951, Novgorod (excavations led by Artemiy Artsikhovsky)
Language: Old Novgorodian dialect

Discovery

The first letter was found in Novgorod in 1951 during excavations directed by Artemiy Artsikhovsky; waterlogged clay preserved the bark. Over a thousand letters have since been found in Novgorod and others in Staraya Russa, Smolensk, Torzhok, Moscow and elsewhere.

Content

Text was scratched with a stylus without ink. Letters cover debts, orders, family matters, complaints and school exercises — famously those of a boy named Onfim (13th century), whose doodles and alphabet practice survive. They show literacy beyond clergy and elites, including women and children, and preserve the Old Novgorodian dialect.

Other traditions

Birch bark was also used for Buddhist manuscripts in Gandhara (among the oldest surviving Buddhist texts) and in Finland, Scandinavia and North America for various records.
