Taxicab geometry

Taxicab geometry, also known as Manhattan geometry or Manhattan distance, is a form of geometry in which the usual metric of Euclidean geometry is replaced by a new metric in which the distance between two points is the sum of the absolute differences of their Cartesian coordinates. The taxicab metric is also called the L1 metric or Manhattan distance, because a taxicab in a grid street layout such as that of Manhattan borough in New York City must travel along axis-parallel streets, and the shortest such route has this length.

Definition

The taxicab distance between two points p = (p1, p2) and q = (q1, q2) in a plane with fixed Cartesian coordinates is defined as |p1 − q1| + |p2 − q2|. In n-dimensional space the distance generalizes to the sum of absolute coordinate differences over all dimensions.

History

The L1 metric was used in regression analysis as early as the 18th century, and the geometry was systematically studied in the 19th century by Hermann Minkowski and others. The name "taxicab geometry" was popularized by Karl Menger and later writers.

Properties

A circle in taxicab geometry is the set of points at a fixed distance from a center; unlike Euclidean circles, it is a square rotated 45 degrees, with vertices on the coordinate axes. The value of pi in taxicab geometry is 4. Taxicab geometry satisfies all the axioms of a metric space and is used in applications such as chess (the minimum number of moves for a king), coding theory, and urban planning.
