Omega Centauri (ω Cen, NGC 5139, or Caldwell 80) is a globular cluster in the constellation of Centaurus that was first identified as a non-stellar object by Edmond Halley in 1677. Located at a distance of about 17,370 light-years (5,330 parsecs), it is the largest known globular cluster in the Milky Way at a diameter of roughly 150 light-years.

Characteristics

Omega Centauri is estimated to contain approximately ten million stars and a total mass equivalent to 4 million solar masses, making it the most massive known globular cluster in the Milky Way. It is so distinctive from the other galactic globular clusters that it is thought to be the core remnant of a disrupted dwarf galaxy: unlike most globular clusters, it contains several distinct stellar populations with a range of metallicities and ages, and its stars show retrograde orbits suggesting it was captured by the Milky Way.

Visible to the naked eye from southern latitudes as a fuzzy star-like patch, it was listed by Ptolemy in his catalogue of stars and given the Bayer designation "Omega" by Johann Bayer in 1603, long before its true nature was recognised. Studies of its central region have suggested the presence of an intermediate-mass black hole.
