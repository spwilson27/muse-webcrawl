Microelectromechanical systems

Microelectromechanical systems (MEMS), also written as micro-electro-mechanical systems, are miniature devices that combine electrical and mechanical components at the micrometre scale. MEMS are made using modified semiconductor fabrication techniques — photolithography, etching, and deposition — to carve moving structures such as beams, membranes, gears, and mirrors out of silicon and other materials, integrated with electronic circuits on the same chip. In Japan the field is often called micromachines, and in Europe, microsystems technology.

Applications

MEMS devices are ubiquitous. MEMS accelerometers and gyroscopes detect motion in smartphones, game controllers, vehicle airbag systems, and drones. MEMS pressure sensors monitor tyre pressure and altitude; MEMS microphones are standard in phones; and arrays of millions of microscopic mirrors form the digital micromirror devices used in DLP projectors. Other applications include inkjet printer heads, microfluidic lab-on-a-chip systems, optical switches, and radio-frequency switches and resonators.

Fabrication

Two broad approaches dominate. In bulk micromachining, structures are etched directly into a silicon wafer. In surface micromachining, thin films are deposited and patterned layer by layer, with sacrificial layers dissolved away to free the moving parts. Processes such as deep reactive-ion etching allow tall, narrow structures with near-vertical walls. Because MEMS borrow the batch-fabrication economics of the semiconductor industry, devices can be produced in very large quantities at low unit cost.

Actuation and sensing

MEMS sense by converting mechanical motion into electrical signals, most often changes in capacitance or piezoresistance. Actuation is commonly electrostatic, but thermal, piezoelectric, and magnetic actuation are also used. At the microscale, surface forces such as stiction and friction dominate over gravity and inertia, which strongly shapes MEMS design.
