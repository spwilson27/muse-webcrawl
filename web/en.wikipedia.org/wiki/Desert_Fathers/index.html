The Desert Fathers (along with the Desert Mothers) were early Christian hermits and ascetics who lived mainly in the Scetis desert of Egypt beginning around the third century AD. Their pursuit of solitude (hesychia), prayer and manual labour laid the foundations of Christian monasticism.

Origins

When persecution of Christians ended in the early 4th century, many believers sought a new form of the martyrdom they called "white martyrdom" — a lifelong ascetic struggle in the desert. Anthony the Great (c. 251–356), whose biography by Athanasius of Alexandria became a bestseller of late antiquity, withdrew progressively deeper into the Eastern Desert. Others, such as Pachomius, organised communal (cenobitic) monasteries with a rule of life, while settlements at Nitria, Kellia and Scetis housed thousands of semi-anchorite monks living in individual cells under the guidance of an elder (abba or amma).

Spirituality and sayings

The Fathers' teaching survives chiefly in collections of short sayings and stories, the Apophthegmata Patrum ("Sayings of the Desert Fathers"), arranged by author and by theme. Their spirituality emphasised humility, watchfulness over thoughts (nepsis), ceaseless prayer — including early forms of the Jesus Prayer — hospitality, and non-judgement of others. Visitors travelled great distances simply to ask an elder, "Give me a word."

Influence

Through translations into Latin and the writings of John Cassian, the Desert Fathers shaped Western monasticism, including the Rule of St Benedict, and remain central to Eastern Orthodox spirituality and the Philokalia.
