American School for the Deaf

The American School for the Deaf (ASD), in West Hartford, Connecticut, is the oldest permanent school for the deaf in the United States and the birthplace of American Sign Language (ASL). It was founded on 15 April 1817 in Hartford by Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet, Laurent Clerc, and Mason Cogswell, and was first known as the Connecticut Asylum for the Education and Instruction of Deaf and Dumb Persons.

History

Mason Cogswell, a Hartford physician whose daughter Alice was deaf, enlisted his neighbour Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet to learn European deaf-education methods. In Paris, Gallaudet recruited Laurent Clerc, a deaf teacher from the Institut National des Jeunes Sourds, and the two returned to Hartford to open the school with seven pupils, including Alice Cogswell. The blend of French Sign Language with local sign systems and home signs used by the pupils developed into American Sign Language.

The school moved to its present campus in West Hartford in 1921. ASD served as the model and training ground for deaf education across the United States: many of its graduates and teachers went on to found schools in other states, and its approach influenced the founding of Gallaudet University in 1864.
