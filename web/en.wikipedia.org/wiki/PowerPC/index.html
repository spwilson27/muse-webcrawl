PowerPC


Labelled image placeholder: a PowerPC microprocessor

PowerPC (short for Performance Optimization With Enhanced RISC – Performance Computing, sometimes abbreviated as PPC) is a reduced instruction set computer (RISC) instruction set architecture created by the 1991 Apple–IBM–Motorola alliance, known as the AIM alliance.

History

PowerPC grew out of IBM's earlier POWER architecture, used in the RS/6000 workstation line. The first implementations, the PowerPC 601 (1993), were followed by the 603, 604, the G3 (750), the G4 (74xx) with its AltiVec vector unit, and the 64-bit G5 (970). PowerPC processors powered Apple's Macintosh computers from 1994 until the Mac transition to Intel processors was announced in 2005 and completed in 2006. They were also used in IBM servers and workstations, in embedded systems, and in all three major seventh-generation video game consoles: the Nintendo GameCube and Wii, Microsoft's Xbox 360, and Sony's PlayStation 3 (with the related Cell processor).

Architecture

The architecture is a load/store RISC design with 32 general-purpose registers, a large set of floating-point registers, and support for both big-endian and little-endian operation. Later extensions added 64-bit addressing, virtualisation features, and vector instructions. The specification was opened through the OpenPOWER Foundation, founded in 2013, and in 2019 IBM released the instruction set architecture under an open licence managed by the OpenPOWER community.

Successors and usage

IBM's POWER series (POWER8, POWER9, Power10) continues the lineage in high-end servers, while Freescale/NXP PowerPC derivatives (e500, QorIQ) persist in networking and automotive embedded applications.
