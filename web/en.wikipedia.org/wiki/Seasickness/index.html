Seasickness

Rough seas are the classic trigger of seasickness.

Seasickness is a form of motion sickness characterised by nausea, vomiting, dizziness and cold sweating, brought on by the rocking motion of a vessel at sea. It results from conflicting signals between the vestibular system of the inner ear, the eyes and proprioceptive sensors when the body is moved passively by waves.

Specialty: Neurology, otolaryngology

Symptoms: Nausea, vomiting, dizziness, pallor, cold sweat, headache

Causes: Sensory conflict from vessel motion

Risk factors: Migraine, pregnancy, reading below deck, poor ventilation

Treatment: Fresh air, horizon gazing, antihistamines, scopolamine, acclimatisation

Mechanism

The prevailing explanation is the sensory-conflict (neural mismatch) theory: the brain compares actual vestibular, visual and proprioceptive input with the pattern expected from past experience, and a sustained mismatch — for example, the inner ear sensing motion while the eyes see a stationary cabin — triggers symptoms. Vertical oscillation at frequencies around 0.2 Hz, typical of ships, is especially provocative.

Course

Most sufferers adapt within two to three days at sea as the brain recalibrates ("getting one's sea legs"). A small minority remain susceptible, and symptoms resolve quickly once back on stable ground, though a lingering sensation of rocking, mal de débarquement, occasionally persists.

Prevention and treatment

Being on deck with a view of the horizon, facing the direction of travel, fresh air and avoiding reading reduce symptoms. Medications include scopolamine (often by skin patch), antihistamines such as dimenhydrinate and meclizine, and ginger, which has modest supporting evidence. Crews historically used acclimatisation schedules, and ship stabilisers reduce the provoking motion itself.
