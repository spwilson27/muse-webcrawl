Frank–Starling law

The Frank–Starling law of the heart (also known as the Starling law and the Maestrini heart law) represents the relationship between stroke volume and end diastolic volume. The law states that the stroke volume of the heart increases in response to an increase in the volume of blood in the ventricles, before contraction (the end diastolic volume), when all other factors remain constant. As a larger volume of blood flows into the ventricle, the blood stretches the cardiac muscle fibres, leading to an increase in the force of contraction.

History

The mechanism was described by the German physiologist Otto Frank in 1895 and the British physiologist Ernest Starling, whose experiments on the isolated heart-lung preparation, published in the early twentieth century, generalised the principle. Starling summarised it in his 1915 Linacre lecture: "the mechanical energy set free in the passage from the resting to the contracted state depends on the area of the chemical surfaces taking part in the contraction, i.e. on the initial length of the muscle fibres."

Physiology

As the ventricle fills with more blood during diastole, ventricular pressure rises and the cardiomyocytes are stretched. This stretch increases the affinity of troponin C for calcium ions and increases the overlap and cross-bridge formation between actin and myosin filaments, so the subsequent contraction is more forceful. The relationship is graphically represented by the Frank–Starling (cardiac function) curve, plotting stroke volume or cardiac output against atrial pressure or end-diastolic volume.

Clinical significance

The law allows the heart to automatically match its output to venous return, keeping the outputs of the right and left ventricles balanced and preventing blood from damming up in the pulmonary or systemic circulation. In heart failure, the curve is shifted downward and flattened: a failing ventricle needs a much higher filling pressure to achieve the same stroke volume, contributing to congestion and oedema. The principle also underlies the assessment of fluid responsiveness in critical care — a patient on the steep part of the curve will increase stroke volume after a fluid bolus, whereas one on the plateau will not.
