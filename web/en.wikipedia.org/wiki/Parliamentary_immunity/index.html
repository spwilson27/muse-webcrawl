Parliamentary immunity

Parliamentary immunity
Also called: Parliamentary privilege (in part); legislative immunity
Components: Non-liability (freedom of speech in debate); inviolability (protection from arrest/prosecution)
Purpose: Protect the independence of the legislature from executive or judicial interference

Parliamentary immunity is a system of legal protection granted to members of a parliament or legislature, designed to allow them to perform their duties without interference from the executive, the courts, or private litigants. It is found, in varying forms, in most democratic constitutions.

Forms

Two main protections are usually distinguished:

  • Non-liability (freedom of speech in parliament): members may not be prosecuted or sued for words spoken or votes cast in the exercise of their functions. In England this was asserted in Article 9 of the Bill of Rights 1689 ("the freedom of speech and debates or proceedings in Parliament ought not to be impeached or questioned in any court or place out of Parliament"); in the United States it appears as the Speech or Debate Clause of the Constitution.
  • Inviolability: members may not be arrested or prosecuted for other matters during their term without the consent of their chamber, which may vote to lift (waive) the immunity. This broader form is common in continental European systems, such as those of France, Germany, and Italy, and for members of the European Parliament.

Rationale and criticism

The doctrine originated in struggles between legislatures and monarchs, protecting representatives from politically motivated prosecution. In modern practice it is sometimes criticised when it appears to shield members from corruption or criminal investigations; several countries have narrowed inviolability or reformed lifting procedures in response. Immunity typically covers only acts connected with the mandate, or is lifted where a member is caught in the act of a serious offence.
