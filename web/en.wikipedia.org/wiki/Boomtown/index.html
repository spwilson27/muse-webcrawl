Boomtown

A boomtown is a community that undergoes sudden and rapid population and economic growth, usually driven by the discovery of a resource — gold, silver, oil — or another sudden economic opportunity nearby. The growth is typically abrupt, largely speculative, and often followed by an equally rapid decline when the resource is exhausted or the opportunity passes, leaving a ghost town.


A mining boomtown at its height

Characteristics

Boomtowns spring up where fortune-seekers converge faster than institutions can follow. Housing, food and services command inflated prices; land speculation flourishes; and saloons, gambling halls and other entertainments catering to a largely young, male, transient workforce dominate. Law enforcement and infrastructure lag behind population, so boomtowns acquired reputations for violence and disorder — sometimes exaggerated, sometimes earned. Because the economy depends on a single resource or employer, the town's fate is tied to a single commodity price.

Examples

The classic boomtowns arose from the North American mineral rushes of the 19th century: San Francisco grew from a village of some 1,000 people in 1848 to a city of 25,000 within two years of the California Gold Rush; Deadwood, Leadville, Tombstone, Cripple Creek and Dawson City (in the Klondike Gold Rush) followed the same pattern. Gold also transformed Ballarat and Bendigo in Australia and Johannesburg in South Africa. Later booms were driven by oil — Spindletop-era Beaumont, Tulsa, and the shale towns of North Dakota such as Williston — by railways and wartime industry, and in the modern era by technology and construction. A few boomtowns, like San Francisco and Johannesburg, matured into lasting cities; most shrank to villages or vanished.

The bust

When the ore pinches out or prices collapse, population drains away as quickly as it came, businesses close, and buildings are abandoned or dismantled. Surviving boomtowns often reinvent themselves through tourism, heritage preservation, or new industries.
