Linoleum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Linoleum, sometimes shortened to lino, is a floor covering made from materials such as solidified linseed oil (linoxyn), pine resin, ground cork dust, wood flour, and mineral fillers such as calcium carbonate, most commonly on a burlap or canvas backing. Pigments are often added to the materials to create the desired colour finish. Linoleum is notable for being made almost entirely from renewable natural raw materials.

History

Linoleum was invented by the Englishman Frederick Walton, who patented the process in 1863 and established the Linoleum Manufacturing Company at Staines in 1864. The town of Kirkcaldy in Fife, Scotland, became the world centre of production. Because the material is waterproof, hard-wearing and easy to clean, heavy "battleship linoleum" covered the decks and interiors of warships, hospitals, schools and kitchens through the first half of the twentieth century. Sales declined after the Second World War as cheaper PVC (vinyl) flooring spread, though linoleum has enjoyed a revival for its sustainability credentials. Walton's process is also the origin of Lincrusta, a deeply embossed wall covering, and of linocut printmaking, in which artists carve a block of soft linoleum.

Properties

Linoleum continues to oxidise and harden throughout its life, lasting 25–40 years in service; it is naturally bacteriostatic, antistatic and resistant to cigarette burns. It is often confused with vinyl flooring, but unlike PVC it contains no plasticisers or chlorine, and offcuts and old flooring are biodegradable. A faint smell of linseed oil characterises newly laid sheets.
