The Anglo-Dutch Wars were a series of four conflicts fought between England (later Great Britain) and the Dutch Republic in the mid-17th and late 18th centuries. Fought almost entirely at sea, they arose from the two nations' rivalry for dominance of global maritime trade at a time when the Dutch merchant marine was the largest in the world and England sought to displace it.

First Anglo-Dutch War (1652–1654)

The Commonwealth of England under Oliver Cromwell passed the Navigation Act of 1651, targeting Dutch carrying trade. Skirmishes escalated into open war after a clash between admirals Robert Blake and Maarten Tromp off Dover in May 1652. Massed fleet battles — Kentish Knock, Dungeness, Portland, the Gabbard, and Scheveningen, where Tromp was killed — were fought in the Channel and North Sea. The war ended with the Treaty of Westminster (1654), by which the Dutch accepted the Navigation Act and agreed to salute the English flag in British seas.

Second Anglo-Dutch War (1665–1667)

Charles II's England renewed the struggle amid colonial seizures, including the English capture of New Netherland (New York) in 1664. England won the Battle of Lowestoft (1665) but suffered plague, the Great Fire of London, and financial exhaustion. In June 1667 the Dutch under Michiel de Ruyter sailed up the Thames and Medway, burned the English fleet at its moorings at Chatham, and towed away the flagship Royal Charles — one of the worst defeats in English naval history. The Treaty of Breda (1667) confirmed England in possession of New Netherland while the Dutch kept Suriname and the nutmeg island of Run.

Third Anglo-Dutch War (1672–1674)

By the secret Treaty of Dover (1670), Charles II allied with Louis XIV's France against the Dutch. In the Dutch Rampjaar ("disaster year") of 1672, France invaded by land while England attacked at sea; De Ruyter's victories at Solebay, Schooneveld, and the Texel prevented an English landing, and the Dutch opened the dikes to save Amsterdam. Parliament forced Charles to make peace at Westminster in 1674.

Fourth Anglo-Dutch War (1780–1784)

A century later, Britain declared war over Dutch trade with the rebellious American colonies and Dutch accession to the League of Armed Neutrality. The war exposed the decay of Dutch sea power — the indecisive Battle of Dogger Bank (1781) was its only fleet action — and accelerated the Dutch Republic's decline, ending with the Treaty of Paris (1784).

Legacy

The wars shifted the balance of maritime power toward England, sowed the seeds of the British Empire's commercial supremacy, and left a legacy of rivalry in language (many English "Dutch" idioms date from this era). Paradoxically, they ended in alliance: after 1688 the Dutch stadholder William of Orange became king of England, and the two nations fought together against Louis XIV.
