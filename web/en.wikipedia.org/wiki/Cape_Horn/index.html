Cape Horn (Spanish: Cabo de Hornos) is the southernmost headland of the Tierra del Fuego archipelago of southern Chile, and marks the northern boundary of the Drake Passage, where the Atlantic and Pacific oceans meet. It is widely considered one of the most hazardous shipping routes in the world: strong westerly winds (the "Furious Fifties"), enormous waves funnelling between Antarctica and the Andes, icebergs and a rocky lee shore wrecked hundreds of ships in the age of sail.

The clipper route

From the 1850s to the opening of the Panama Canal in 1914, Cape Horn was an obligatory gateway for trade between Europe and the Pacific coasts of the Americas and for the California and Australian gold rushes. Clipper ships "rounding the Horn" westbound could battle contrary winds for weeks; sailors who completed the passage formed an elite, and the route bred a rich literature (Richard Henry Dana's Two Years Before the Mast, the novels of Joseph Conrad). Commercial sailing died with steam and the canal, but the Horn remains a rite of passage for ocean racing (the Vendée Globe and Clipper races round it) and is protected within Chile's Cabo de Hornos National Park, a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve. The cape's waters, driven by the circumpolar current, are continuous with the Southern Ocean and, far to the east, the seas around the Arabian Peninsula trade routes of an earlier age of sail.
