Police informant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A police informant (also called an informer, or in British police terminology a covert human intelligence source, CHIS) is a person who provides privileged information about a person or organization to a law-enforcement agency. The term is usually associated with the police, but informants also supply information to customs, intelligence services, and other agencies. Many informants are criminals themselves who cooperate in exchange for money, reduced sentences, or the dropping of charges; others are motivated by conscience, revenge, or financial reward.

Types and motivation

Handlers distinguish between casual sources, who pass on information incidentally, and registered confidential informants, who are formally recruited, assigned a handler, and tasked with reporting on specific criminal activity. Informants are crucial to investigating organized crime, drug trafficking, and terrorism, where conventional evidence is difficult to obtain; undercover operations and prosecutions frequently depend on their testimony. Criminologist Gary T. Marx and others have catalogued motivations ranging from financial reward and leniency to eliminating criminal rivals — a misuse sometimes called "working the police".

Reliability and controversy

Informant testimony is a leading contributor to wrongful convictions: informants who testify falsely in expectation of leniency — sometimes called "jailhouse snitches" when they claim a cellmate confessed — have been implicated in a substantial share of overturned capital convictions in the United States. Critics also note that agencies may protect informants who commit serious crimes, and that reliance on informants can distort priorities, for example in drug enforcement and in the use of informants against political groups. In the United Kingdom, the Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act 2000 formalized the authorization and handling of CHIS, and a later undercover policing inquiry examined cases in which officers formed deceptive long-term relationships with activists.

Protection

Because exposure can be fatal, informants' identities are closely guarded; in many jurisdictions courts recognize an "informer's privilege" allowing the state to withhold an informant's identity, subject to exceptions where disclosure is essential to a fair trial. Informants who testify may enter witness protection programs, such as the United States Federal Witness Security Program (WITSEC).

In culture

Informants occupy a notorious place in popular culture and criminal codes — the label "snitch", "rat", or "grass" carries strong stigma. Famous informants include Henry Hill and Sammy "the Bull" Gravano in organized crime, Whitey Bulger (whose corrupt relationship with his FBI handler became a major scandal), and Deep Throat (Mark Felt) in the Watergate scandal.
