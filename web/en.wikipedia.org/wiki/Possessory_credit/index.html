Possessory credit

A possessory credit (in French, crédit possessoire) is a form of secured lending in which the creditor takes physical possession of an asset belonging to the debtor as collateral for a loan. Because the lender holds the pledged property, the arrangement provides strong security: if the debtor defaults, the creditor may in many legal systems sell the asset to satisfy the debt.

Forms

The most familiar example is the pawnbroking loan, in which a borrower leaves a valuable item with a pawnbroker in exchange for a short-term loan. In civil-law jurisdictions, possessory pledges (such as the French gage) are a standard category of security interest, distinguished from non-possessory security such as mortgages and hypothecs, where the debtor retains the asset. Possessory arrangements are also used in commodity and inventory finance, where goods are held by a warehouse or collateral manager on the lender's behalf.

Advantages and limits

Possession reduces the lender's risk of the collateral being sold, damaged, or encumbered again, and historically made registration unnecessary because possession itself gave notice to third parties. The drawback is that the debtor loses the use of the asset, which is why modern commercial law has developed non-possessory secured transactions regimes (such as Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code) for most business lending, leaving possessory credit chiefly to pawnbroking, Lombard lending against securities, and certain trade-finance structures.
