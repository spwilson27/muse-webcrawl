Newly industrialized country

Countries commonly identified as newly industrialized economies.

The category of newly industrialized country (NIC), also called a newly industrialized economy, is a socioeconomic classification applied by political scientists and economists to countries whose economies have not yet reached the status of a developed country but have, in a macroeconomic sense, outpaced their developing counterparts. NICs are characterised by rapidly growing exports of manufactured goods, fast industrialisation, rising standards of living and an expanding middle class.

Characteristics

NICs typically combine several features: a shift of production from agriculture towards manufacturing and services, openness to foreign direct investment and multinational corporations, strong growth rates, and urbanisation. The term was coined in the 1970s with reference to the "Four Asian Tigers" — South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore and Hong Kong — several of which have since graduated to developed status.

Countries commonly listed

Analysts vary in their lists, but countries frequently classified as NICs include Brazil, China, India, Indonesia, Malaysia, Mexico, the Philippines, South Africa, Thailand and Turkey. China and India are sometimes treated separately because of their scale. Some classifications place countries such as Vietnam, Egypt and Bangladesh in a second tier of emerging or "next" NICs.

Criticism

The category is imprecise: there is no universally agreed definition, indices change over time, and the label can obscure wide differences between countries grouped under it. Critics also note that rapid industrialisation in NICs has often been associated with environmental degradation and poor labour conditions, though it has also produced dramatic reductions in poverty.
