Hávamál

Hávamál (Old Norse for "Words of the High One") is a single poem in the Poetic Edda, preserved in the 13th-century Icelandic manuscript Codex Regius. The poem presents itself as the wisdom of Odin, the "High One", and combines practical proverbial advice on conduct, hospitality, friendship, and caution with mythological narratives, most famously Odin's self-sacrifice on the world tree Yggdrasill to win the runes, and his theft of the mead of poetry.

Odin sacrifices himself upon Yggdrasill, as described in Hávamál

Structure

The poem contains about 164 stanzas and is generally divided by scholars into several originally distinct sections: the Gestaþáttr ("Guest's Part"), gnomic verses offering worldly wisdom to travellers and guests; stanzas on love, women, and the Loddfáfnir episode; the Rúnatal, in which Odin describes hanging on the tree "wounded by a spear, dedicated to Odin, myself to myself" for nine nights before seizing the runes; and the Ljóðatal, a catalogue of eighteen magical songs or charms.

Themes

The gnomic sections praise moderation, generosity to guests, careful speech, and the value of reputation after death—famously: "Cattle die, kindred die, we ourselves also die; but the fair fame never dies of him who has earned it." The poem's pragmatic and sometimes sceptical tone contrasts with the heroic ethos of other Eddic poems and has made Hávamál one of the most quoted works of Old Norse literature.

Editions and influence

Hávamál survives only in Codex Regius (c. 1270), with a single stanza also quoted by Snorri Sturluson in the Prose Edda. Its wisdom stanzas are frequently cited in modern Heathenry and in popular culture, and it has been translated many times into English, including by W. H. Auden and Jackson Crawford.
