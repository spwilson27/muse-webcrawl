Immunoassay

Immunoassay
PrincipleAntigen–antibody binding with a measurable label
Key typesRIA, ELISA, lateral flow, chemiluminescent assays
UsesClinical diagnostics, drug testing, research

An immunoassay is a biochemical test that measures the presence or concentration of a molecule (the analyte) in a solution through the use of an antibody or an antigen, exploiting the highly specific binding between them. A detectable label — radioactive, enzymatic, fluorescent, or chemiluminescent — attached to one component allows the amount of binding to be measured.

History

The first immunoassay, the radioimmunoassay (RIA), was developed in the late 1950s by Rosalyn Sussman Yalow and Solomon Berson to measure insulin; Yalow received the 1977 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the work. Enzyme-linked methods (ELISA), developed around 1971 by Peter Perlmann and Eva Engvall and by Anton Schuurs and Bauke van Weemen, largely replaced radioactivity with enzyme-generated colour signals.

Types

  • Competitive assays, in which labelled and unlabelled analyte compete for antibody sites.
  • Sandwich assays, which capture the analyte between two antibodies, improving specificity.
  • ELISA — the workhorse plate-based format for detecting antibodies and antigens (e.g. HIV and hepatitis testing).
  • Lateral flow assays — simple strip tests, such as home pregnancy tests and rapid antigen tests.

Applications

Immunoassays are fundamental to modern laboratory medicine — measuring hormones, tumour markers, infectious-disease antibodies, cardiac markers such as troponin, and drugs — as well as to food safety, environmental monitoring, and biomedical research.
