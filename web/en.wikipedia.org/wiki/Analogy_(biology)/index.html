Analogy (biology)

In biology, an analogy is a similarity in function — and often in superficial form — between structures or traits of different organisms that is not due to inheritance from a common ancestor. Analogous traits are the product of convergent evolution, in which independent lineages evolve similar solutions to similar problems. The term stands opposite to homology, similarity explained by shared descent.

Analogous body plans: shark, ichthyosaur, and dolphin

Terminology

Richard Owen distinguished "analogue" (a part in one animal having the same function as a part in a different animal) from "homologue" (the same organ under every variety of form and function). After Darwin, the distinction was reinterpreted in evolutionary terms. In modern phylogenetics, analogy is grouped under the broader term homoplasy, which also includes parallel evolution and evolutionary reversals.

Examples

  • The eyes of vertebrates and octopuses — both camera eyes, built from different developmental pathways; the octopus eye lacks the vertebrate blind spot.
  • Sweet potatoes (a root) and potatoes (a stem tuber) as storage organs.
  • The wings of birds, bats, pterosaurs, and insects.
  • Thorns of hawthorn and spines of cacti as defensive structures.

Importance in classification

Because analogous traits do not reflect common ancestry, relying on them can mislead biological classification. The realization that similarity can arise by convergence underlies modern systematic practice, which weights homologous characters and molecular data when inferring the tree of life.
