The Maison Carrée (French for "square house") is an ancient Roman temple in Nîmes, southern France; it is one of the best-preserved Roman temples to be found anywhere in the territory of the former Roman Empire. It is a mid-sized Augustan provincial temple of the Imperial cult.

History

The temple was built around 19 BC and rededicated around AD 4–5 to Gaius Caesar and Lucius Caesar, grandsons and adopted heirs of Augustus. An inscription, reconstructed in the 18th century from the holes left by bronze letters, recorded the dedication. The building dominated the forum of the Roman city of Nemausus.

Through the Middle Ages it served successively as a consular house, a church, a stable, and a storehouse — uses that, paradoxically, preserved it. In the 19th century it became a museum. Thomas Jefferson was so impressed by the Maison Carrée during his time as ambassador to France that he used it as the model for the Virginia State Capitol.

Architecture

The temple is a classic example of Vitruvian architecture. It is a hexastyle pseudoperipteral temple: a deep porch of six freestanding Corinthian columns supports the entablature, while engaged columns decorate the walls of the cella. It measures about 26.4 metres by 13.5 metres and stands on a high podium, reached by a single flight of steps.

Heritage status

In September 2023 the Maison Carrée was inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List. A new museum opposite the temple explains its history and Roman Nîmes.
