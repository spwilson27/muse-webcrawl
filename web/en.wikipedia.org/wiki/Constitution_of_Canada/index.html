Constitution of Canada

The Constitution of Canada is the supreme law in Canada, comprising a written component — principally the Constitution Act, 1867 (formerly the British North America Act) and the Constitution Act, 1982 — together with unwritten conventions, judicial decisions, and Indigenous legal traditions and treaties.

History

Confederation in 1867 united colonies under a federal system modelled partly on Britain and the United States, dividing powers between Parliament (s.91) and provincial legislatures (s.92). Amendments long required the UK Parliament until patriation in 1982 under Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau — accomplished without Quebec's assent, a lasting grievance addressed unsuccessfully by the Meech Lake and Charlottetown Accords.

Constitution Act, 1982

The 1982 Act added the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, recognition of Aboriginal and treaty rights (s.35), equalization commitments, and a domestic amending formula requiring, for most changes, Parliament plus two-thirds of provinces representing at least 50% of the population (the 7/50 rule), with unanimity for some matters.

Unwritten constitution

Responsible government, judicial independence, the rule of law, federalism, democracy, and protection of minorities were identified by the Supreme Court in the Quebec Secession Reference (1998) as underlying principles.
