Condorcet paradox

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Condorcet paradox in social choice is the finding that collective preferences can be cyclic, even if the individual preferences of the voters are not. If three voters rank candidates A, B, C in the orders ABC, BCA and CAB, then pairwise majorities prefer A over B, B over C, and C over A — a cycle. There is then no Condorcet winner (a candidate who beats every other in head-to-head contests), and the outcome of majority voting depends on the agenda: whichever candidate is voted on last can be made to win by suitable ordering.

Named for the Marquis de Condorcet, who described it in his Essay on the Application of Analysis to the Probability of Majority Decisions (1785), the paradox undermined the simple equation of majority rule with “the will of the people” and seeded social choice theory (Arrow's impossibility theorem generalises it). Mitigations include the Condorcet methods that elect the pairwise winner when one exists — and resolve cycles by defined completions (Ranked Pairs, Schulze) — and domain restrictions (single-peaked preferences). Its practical signature is agenda power: committees, legislatures and amendment procedures are designed with cycling in mind, whether or not the designers know the theorem.
