Jurisdiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jurisdiction is the legal term for the legal authority granted to a legal entity to enact justice. In federations like the United States, areas of jurisdiction apply to the areas of law at local, state and federal levels. Jurisdiction draws its substance from international law, conflict of laws, constitutional limits and the statute creating the court or body.

Types

Courts analyse jurisdiction over the subject matter (the class of cases a court may hear), over the person or property (territorial presence, domicile, consent, or long-arm statutes), and by instance — original (trial) versus appellate jurisdiction. Jurisdiction may be exclusive or concurrent; defendants may challenge it (forum non conveniens, forum shopping by plaintiffs choosing favourable courts being the mirror problem). Beyond states' courts, jurisdiction questions structure international law: the principles of territoriality, nationality, protection, passive personality and universality (asserted for piracy and grave crimes) determine when a state may prescribe and enforce law — including controversial extraterritorial assertions in competition, sanctions and internet regulation.
