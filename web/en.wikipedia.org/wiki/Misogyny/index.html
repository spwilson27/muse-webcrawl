Misogyny

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Misogyny is the hatred of, contempt for, or prejudice against women or girls. The word comes from the Greek misein ("to hate") and gynē ("woman"). It can take many forms, including discrimination, objectification, hostility, male privilege, patriarchal norms, exclusion and violence against women.

Definitions and scholarship

Feminist theorists distinguish misogyny from sexism: sexism is commonly understood as an ideology that justifies men's dominance, while misogyny functions more as an enforcement mechanism — punishing women who violate patriarchal norms and rewarding those who comply. In her book Down Girl (2017), philosopher Kate Manne argues misogyny is best understood not as individual hatred felt by particular men, but as the "law enforcement branch" of patriarchy that polices gendered expectations, which explains why it often targets particular women rather than women in general.

History

Scholars have traced misogynistic themes in ancient Greek literature (such as Hesiod's account of Pandora and Semonides' poem on women), in some religious texts and traditions, and in the writings of figures such as Aristotle, who described women as imperfect men. Medieval and early modern writers both attacked and defended women in the long-running querelle des femmes. Modern discussions address misogyny in workplaces, media, politics and online spaces, where harassment campaigns have drawn wide attention.

Related concepts

Misogyny is the counterpart of misandry (hatred of men) and is related to internalised misogyny, in which women themselves adopt hostile attitudes toward other women. Psychologists measure related attitudes with instruments such as the Ambivalent Sexism Inventory, which distinguishes hostile from "benevolent" sexism.
