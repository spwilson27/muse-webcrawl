Postsecularism

Postsecularism refers to a range of theories about the persistence, revival and renewed public significance of religion in contemporary societies, in contrast to the classical secularization thesis, which predicted that modernization would bring a steady decline of religion. A "postsecular" society, in the influential formulation of Jürgen Habermas, is one that adjusts to the continued existence of religious communities in an increasingly secularized environment, requiring both religious and secular citizens to engage in a mutual learning process.

Background

By the late 20th century, the global resurgence of religion — from the Iranian Revolution of 1979 and the rise of the religious right in the United States to the growth of Pentecostalism in the Global South and the role of religion in post-communist societies — led sociologists such as Peter Berger, once a leading theorist of secularization, to repudiate the thesis and speak instead of "desecularization". José Casanova's work on "public religions" documented how religious traditions had re-entered the public sphere around the world. Charles Taylor's A Secular Age (2007) reframed the issue, describing modern Western secularity as a changed "condition of belief" rather than simply the subtraction of religion.

Habermas and the postsecular

Habermas argued, notably in his 2001 "Faith and Knowledge" speech after the September 11 attacks and in later writings and dialogues (including with Joseph Ratzinger, the future Pope Benedict XVI), that secular citizens should not dismiss religious contributions to public debate, while religious citizens accepting democratic procedures should "translate" their reasons into a generally accessible language — the so-called translation proviso. The concept has influenced debates about religious symbols in public institutions, faith schools, and the integration of Muslim minorities in Europe.

Critics argue the term can be Eurocentric (Europe being the "exceptional case" of secularization, as Grace Davie suggests), that it exaggerates religion's novelty in public life, or that it normatively privileges established religious voices. Others use "postsecular" more loosely in literary and cultural studies to describe works that engage religious themes after, and against the background of, secular modernism.
