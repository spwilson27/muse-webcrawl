Feuilleton

A feuilleton (French: [fœjtɔ̃]; from feuillet, the leaf of a book) was originally a kind of supplement attached to the political portion of French newspapers, consisting chiefly of non-political news and gossip, literature and art criticism, a chronicle of the latest fashions, and epigrams, charades and other literary trifles. The term has since been adopted in several European languages and press traditions to denote the arts and culture section of a newspaper, as well as the short, essayistic literary form published there.

History

The feuilleton was launched in 1800 in the Journal des Débats in Paris, edited by Julien Louis Geoffroy and Bertin the Elder. It appeared at the bottom of the newspaper page, separated from the political content by a ruled line — the famous rez-de-chaussée ("ground floor") of the paper. The format proved enormously popular and was widely imitated. In the 19th century the feuilleton became the home of the serialized novel, the roman-feuilleton, through which writers such as Honoré de Balzac, Alexandre Dumas and Eugène Sue reached a mass readership.

In German-language press

In Germany and Austria the Feuilleton developed into the newspaper's cultural section, publishing essays, reviews, and literary sketches by writers including Theodor Fontane, Joseph Roth, Alfred Polgar and Karl Kraus. The form prized wit, brevity and a polished prose style, and remains a fixture of quality newspapers such as the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung and Neue Zürcher Zeitung.
