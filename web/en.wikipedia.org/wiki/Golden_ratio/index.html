Golden ratio

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A golden rectangle divided into a square and a smaller golden rectangle, with a spiral drawn through them]

In mathematics, two quantities are in the golden ratio if their ratio is the same as the ratio of their sum to the larger of the two quantities. The value is the irrational number φ = (1 + √5)/2 ≈ 1.6180339887..., known by names including golden mean, golden section (Latin: sectio aurea), and divine proportion.

Algebraically, φ is the positive solution of x² = x + 1; its continued fraction is all ones — the 'most irrational' number, slowest to approximate by rationals. The ratio of consecutive Fibonacci numbers converges to it, connecting it to phyllotaxis patterns in sunflowers and pinecones.

History and mythology

Euclid defined the 'extreme and mean ratio' in the Elements; Kepler called it a jewel of geometry. Claims that the Parthenon, the Great Pyramid, or Renaissance art systematically embody φ are mostly unsupported by measurement or documents, though some artists and architects (Le Corbusier's Modulor) used it deliberately.

The Greek letter φ was assigned in the 20th century (after Phidias, per Mark Barr), a modern touch for an ancient constant.
