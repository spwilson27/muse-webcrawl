The gravity of Earth, denoted g, is the acceleration imparted to objects at or near the planet's surface by the combined effect of gravitation (from Earth's mass distribution) and the centrifugal force arising from Earth's rotation. Its magnitude varies by about 0.5% over the surface, from approximately 9.78 m/s² at the equator to 9.83 m/s² at the poles.

Magnitude and variation

Several factors cause local and regional variation in g:

  • Latitude — Earth is an oblate spheroid, so the poles are about 21 km closer to the centre than the equator; combined with the centrifugal effect of rotation, gravity is weakest at the equator.
  • Altitude — gravity decreases with height, by roughly 0.31 mGal per metre (the free-air correction).
  • Local geology — density variations in the crust, such as ore bodies, salt domes, and mountain roots, produce measurable gravity anomalies exploited in geophysical prospecting.
  • Tides — the Moon and Sun cause periodic variations of up to about 0.3 mGal.

The largest known surface anomaly is associated with the Indian Ocean Geoid Low, while Mount Nevado Huascarán in Peru has among the lowest measured surface gravities and the Arctic Ocean surface among the highest.

Standard gravity

For engineering and metrology, standard gravity g0 was defined by the 3rd General Conference on Weights and Measures (1901) as exactly 9.80665 m/s². It is used in the definition of the kilogram-force and the standard atmosphere, and in calibrating pressure units.

Measurement

Absolute gravimeters measure g by timing the free fall of a mass in a vacuum; relative gravimeters use precision springs or superconducting levitation to map differences. Satellite missions such as GRACE and GOCE have mapped Earth's gravity field from orbit, revealing mass movements associated with ice-sheet loss, groundwater depletion, and post-glacial rebound.

Theoretical models

The International Gravity Formula and its successors (notably the model associated with the World Geodetic System, WGS 84) give gravity as a function of latitude, combining the gravitational and centrifugal terms for a rotating reference ellipsoid.
