Airspace class

Diagram of controlled and uncontrolled airspace classes

Airspace classes are a system used by aviation authorities to divide the sky into categories with different rules for aircraft operating within them. The International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) defines a set of classes, designated by the letters A through G, which member states may adopt wholly or in part. The classification determines whether air traffic control (ATC) services are provided, what separation services apply, whether an ATC clearance is required, and what weather minima and speed restrictions pilots must observe.

Class descriptions

Class A airspace generally extends from 18,000 feet above mean sea level up to flight level 600 (approximately 60,000 feet) in the United States. Only instrument flight rules (IFR) operations are permitted, and all aircraft are subject to ATC clearances and separation services. Class B surrounds the busiest airports and requires an explicit clearance to enter; both IFR and visual flight rules (VFR) traffic receive separation. Class C surrounds airports with control towers and radar approach control; two-way radio communication and a transponder are required. Class D covers smaller towered airports.

Class E is controlled airspace not classified as A, B, C or D; IFR flights are separated from each other but VFR flights receive only traffic information where available. Class F, advisory airspace, is rarely used and is not implemented in the United States. Class G is uncontrolled airspace in which ATC has no authority or responsibility, and pilots operate under "see and avoid" principles.

National variations

Although ICAO publishes standard definitions, countries adapt the classes to their own needs. Some states use only a subset of classes, impose different altitude boundaries, or add special categories for military or special-use airspace such as restricted, prohibited, warning and alert areas, which overlay the basic classification.
