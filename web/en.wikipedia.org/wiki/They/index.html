They

In Modern English, they is a third-person pronoun commonly used as a plural pronoun, historically with a plural verb, or as a gender-neutral singular pronoun, historically with a plural verb. The singular "they" has been used since the 14th century, including by major authors such as Shakespeare and Jane Austen, and its use has expanded in recent decades, particularly as a pronoun for individuals whose gender is unknown, irrelevant, unspecified, or nonbinary.

History

They entered English from Old Norse þeir during the period of Scandinavian settlement in England, gradually replacing the native Old English plural pronouns in northern dialects before spreading south. The singular use developed early: examples appear in texts from the late 1300s onward, and it remained common in speech and informal writing even while some 18th- and 19th-century prescriptive grammarians promoted generic he instead.

Singular they

The singular they is used in several situations: when the referent's gender is unknown ("Someone left their umbrella"), when gender is irrelevant, in generic statements, and as the personal pronoun of many nonbinary people. Major style guides, dictionaries, and linguistic bodies — including the Associated Press, the Chicago Manual of Style, and the American Psychological Association — now accept or endorse singular they in appropriate contexts. They was named Word of the Year by the American Dialect Society in 2015 and by Merriam-Webster in 2019.

Grammar

Singular they takes a plural verb form ("they are") even when referring to one person, much as you — originally plural — takes "you are" for a single addressee. The reflexive form for a singular referent is variously themself or themselves; themself has gained ground in recent usage.
