Biofilm

A biofilm is a syntrophic community of microorganisms in which cells stick to each other and often also to a surface. These adherent cells become embedded within a slimy extracellular matrix composed of extracellular polymeric substances (EPSs).

Formation

Formation proceeds through initial reversible attachment, irreversible adhesion, maturation into structured communities with water channels — coordinated partly by quorum sensing — and dispersal of cells to colonise new sites.

Properties

Biofilm cells differ physiologically from free-floating (planktonic) cells, exchanging genes readily and showing greatly increased resistance to antibiotics and disinfectants, which contributes to chronic infections.

Examples and importance

Biofilms form dental plaque, colonise catheters and implants, foul pipes and ship hulls, and occur naturally on river stones and in soils. They are exploited in wastewater treatment (trickling filters) and microbial fuel cells, and are among the oldest evidence of life on Earth in stromatolites.
