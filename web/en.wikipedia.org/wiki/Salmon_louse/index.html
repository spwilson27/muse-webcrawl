Salmon louse

The salmon louse (Lepeophtheirus salmonis) is a species of copepod in the family Caligidae, a parasitic "sea louse" that lives on salmonid fish in the Northern Hemisphere. It feeds on the skin, mucus and blood of its host, and heavy infestations cause lesions, stress, secondary infection and, in small juvenile fish, death. Salmon lice are the most economically damaging parasite of marine salmon farming, costing the industry hundreds of millions of dollars annually in treatment and lost growth, and they are a focus of controversy over the effects of fish farms on wild salmon.

Life cycle

Females carry two long egg strings, each containing hundreds of eggs. The life cycle has eight stages: two free-swimming nauplius stages, an infective copepodid stage that must find and attach to a host, four attached chalimus stages anchored to the host by a frontal filament, and mobile pre-adult and adult stages that can move over the fish's surface and between hosts. Development takes roughly three to nine weeks depending on temperature.

Impact and control

On farms, dense host populations allow lice to multiply far beyond natural levels, and planktonic larvae can drift to infect wild juvenile salmon migrating past farms — a mechanism linked in studies to declines of some wild pink salmon and sea trout populations. Control methods include chemotherapeutants (such as emamectin benzoate and hydrogen peroxide baths), cleaner fish (lumpfish and wrasse), freshwater and thermal treatments, lice skirts and lasers. Resistance to several chemical treatments has evolved, driving an integrated pest-management approach and, in Norway, a regulatory "traffic light" system tying farm production to measured lice levels.
