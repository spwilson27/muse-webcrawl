Supply curve

In economics, a supply curve is a graph of the relationship between the price of a good or service and the quantity that sellers are willing and able to supply at that price, holding other factors constant (ceteris paribus). By convention, price is plotted on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. The curve normally slopes upward, reflecting the law of supply: as price rises, quantity supplied increases, because higher prices cover the rising marginal cost of additional output and draw new producers into the market.

Derivation

For a competitive firm, the short-run supply curve is the portion of its marginal cost curve lying above average variable cost; the market supply curve is the horizontal sum of individual firms' supply curves. In the long run, supply also reflects entry and exit: a constant-cost industry has a horizontal long-run supply curve, an increasing-cost industry an upward-sloping one.

Movements along, and shifts of, the curve

A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the curve. Changes in other determinants — input prices, technology, taxes and subsidies, expectations, and the number of sellers — shift the whole curve. An improvement in technology, for example (see swap markets for a financial parallel of cost-driven pricing), shifts supply to the right, meaning more is supplied at every price. Together with the demand curve, the supply curve determines market equilibrium price and quantity; their interaction underlies the analysis of elasticity, tax incidence and price controls.

Special cases

Supply may be vertical (perfectly inelastic) when quantity cannot change — as with a fixed stock of land or a sold-out stadium — or, exceptionally, backward-bending, as in the labour supply curve when income effects dominate. In agricultural and commodity markets, supply responds to prices with time lags, producing cobweb cycles.
