The Great Northern Railway (reporting mark GN) was an American Class I railroad running from Saint Paul, Minnesota, to Seattle, Washington. Its main line was the northernmost transcontinental railway line in the United States, completed to the Pacific coast in 1893. It was privately financed under the leadership of James J. Hill, without the federal land grants given to other transcontinentals, and was known for its financial soundness and for promoting settlement and agriculture along its route.

History

The Great Northern grew out of the bankrupt Minnesota and Pacific Railroad, reorganized as the St. Paul and Pacific, which Hill and his partners — including the Bank of Montreal's associates and Canadian investors — acquired in the 1870s. Construction pushed west across North Dakota and Montana, crossing the Rocky Mountains at Marias Pass (discovered for the railroad in 1889 by John Frank Stevens), and reached Seattle in January 1893.

Operations

The railway's premier passenger train was the Empire Builder, inaugurated in 1929 and named for Hill, the "Empire Builder." The railroad's symbol was Rocky, a mountain goat, reflecting its route through Glacier National Park, whose creation Hill's company strongly promoted. Key engineering works included the Cascade Tunnel (opened 1929), at 7.8 miles (12.6 km) once the longest railway tunnel in the United States.

Merger

In 1970 the Great Northern merged with the Northern Pacific Railway, the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy Railroad, and the Spokane, Portland and Seattle Railway to form the Burlington Northern Railroad, a predecessor of today's BNSF Railway.
