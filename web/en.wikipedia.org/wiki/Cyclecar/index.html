Cyclecar

A cyclecar was a type of small, lightweight, and inexpensive car manufactured in Europe and the United States between 1910 and the late 1920s. Cyclecars were intended to fill the gap between the motorcycle and the full-size car, using motorcycle engines and components to keep costs and weight low.

Characteristics

Cyclecars typically weighed under 350 kg (770 lb) and used single- or twin-cylinder air-cooled motorcycle engines, often with belt or chain drive to the rear wheels. Bodies were minimal, seating two in tandem or side-by-side, and construction was deliberately simple so the vehicles could be taxed and registered in the same class as motorcycles in many countries — a major commercial advantage.

History

The cyclecar boom began around 1910 with makers such as Bédélia in France and GN and Morgan in Britain; the Morgan three-wheeler became the most enduring example. The First World War interrupted production, and there was a brief revival in the early 1920s. By the later 1920s, mass-produced light cars such as the Austin 7 offered real-car comfort at prices that erased the cyclecar's advantage, and the class faded.

Legacy

Many cyclecar manufacturers evolved into car makers, and the three-wheeler concept survived in vehicles such as the Morgan 3-Wheeler, revived in the 21st century. The cyclecar's emphasis on light weight influenced later microcars and today's quadricycle regulations in Europe.
