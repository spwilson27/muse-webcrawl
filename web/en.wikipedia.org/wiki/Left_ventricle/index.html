The left ventricle is one of the four chambers of the human heart, located in the bottom left portion. It receives oxygenated blood from the left atrium through the mitral valve and pumps it through the aortic valve into the aorta, supplying the systemic circulation. Because it must generate enough pressure to perfuse the entire body, its muscular wall is about three times as thick as that of the right ventricle.

Structure

The left ventricle is longer and more conical than the right ventricle, and in cross-section it appears circular, forming a major part of the left border and diaphragmatic surface of the heart. Its interior contains fine trabeculae carneae and two large papillary muscles — anterior and posterior — whose chordae tendineae anchor the cusps of the mitral valve and prevent their prolapse during systole. The wall is typically 8–15 mm thick in a healthy adult.

Blood enters through the inflow tract at the mitral orifice and is ejected through the outflow tract, the aortic vestibule, just below the aortic valve. The interventricular septum separating the left and right ventricles bulges into the right ventricular cavity, reflecting the higher pressures on the systemic side.

Function

During systole the left ventricle contracts and ejects a stroke volume of roughly 70 mL in a resting adult; the proportion of end-diastolic volume ejected — the ejection fraction — is normally 55–70%. During diastole the ventricle relaxes and fills. Its performance is governed by preload, afterload, and intrinsic contractility, described by the Frank–Starling law of the heart.

Clinical significance

Left ventricular hypertrophy develops in response to chronic pressure overload, as in hypertension and aortic stenosis, and is an independent risk factor for heart failure and sudden cardiac death. Dilated cardiomyopathy and myocardial infarction impair left ventricular ejection, producing systolic heart failure; impaired relaxation produces diastolic dysfunction. Left ventricular ejection fraction, measured by echocardiography or cardiac MRI, is a central parameter in cardiology guiding therapy and prognosis.
