Cynicism is a school of ancient Greek philosophy whose practitioners held that the purpose of life is to live in virtue, in agreement with nature, rejecting wealth, power, fame and conventional social values. The name derives from the Greek kynikos, "dog-like" — an insult the Cynics embraced, taking the dog's shamelessness and ability to live simply as a model.

History

The school is traced to Antisthenes, a pupil of Socrates, and its most famous figure is Diogenes of Sinope, said to have lived in a large ceramic jar in Corinth, to have carried a lantern in daylight "looking for an honest man", and to have told Alexander the Great to stand out of his sunlight. Crates of Thebes and his wife Hipparchia carried the teaching into the Hellenistic world, and Cynic street preaching influenced early Stoicism through Zeno of Citium, a pupil of Crates.

Teachings

Cynics practised askēsis (training) in enduring hardship, anaideia (shamelessness) in defying convention, and parrhēsia (frank speech). They described themselves as citizens of the world (cosmopolitans). Cynic themes — self-sufficiency (autarkeia), indifference to fortune, and contempt for luxury — recur through Stoic and later Christian ascetic thought. The modern word "cynic", meaning a person who believes people act only from self-interest, descends from the school's reputation but differs from the ancient doctrine.
