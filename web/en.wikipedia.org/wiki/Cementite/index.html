Cementite (iron carbide, Fe3C) is a metastable intermetallic compound of iron and carbon containing 6.67% carbon by weight. It is a hard, brittle ceramic-like phase that is a key constituent of steels and cast irons, and its proportion and distribution largely determine the mechanical properties of these alloys.

Structure and properties

Cementite crystallises in an orthorhombic structure with twelve iron atoms and four carbon atoms per unit cell. It is ferromagnetic below its Curie temperature of about 210 °C. Although thermodynamically metastable with respect to iron and graphite, its decomposition is extremely slow at room temperature, so it persists indefinitely in practice.

Role in steels

In slowly cooled carbon steels, cementite appears in several forms. In pearlite it forms alternating lamellae with ferrite, produced by the eutectoid reaction of austenite at 727 °C. Hypereutectoid steels contain additional (proeutectoid) cementite, often at grain boundaries. In bainite and tempered martensite, cementite precipitates as fine particles that strengthen the steel. Spheroidising heat treatments coarsen it into rounded particles for improved machinability and ductility.

Role in cast irons

In white cast iron, most carbon is present as cementite, making the material hard and wear-resistant but brittle. Where silicon and slow cooling promote graphitisation, grey cast iron forms instead. Cementite is also the principal phase in the ledeburite eutectic of high-carbon irons.

Occurrence in nature

The mineral cohenite, naturally occurring (Fe,Ni,Co)3C found in iron meteorites, is the natural analogue of cementite.
