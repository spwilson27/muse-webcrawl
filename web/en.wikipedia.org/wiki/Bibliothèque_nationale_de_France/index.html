The Bibliothèque nationale de France (BnF; "National Library of France") is the national library of France, located in Paris. It is the national repository of all that is published in France under the system of legal deposit, and one of the largest libraries in the world, holding more than 40 million items including some 15 million books and journals, manuscripts, prints, photographs, maps, coins, and audiovisual documents.

History

The library traces its origins to the royal library founded at the Louvre by Charles V in 1368 and expanded under later kings; it became a national institution during the French Revolution, enriched by confiscated collections from the church and aristocracy. For centuries its principal home was the Richelieu site on the Rue de Richelieu, whose celebrated oval reading room (Salle Ovale), designed by Henri Labrouste in the nineteenth century, remains an architectural landmark.

The François-Mitterrand site

In 1988 President François Mitterrand announced the construction of one of the largest modern libraries in the world. Designed by architect Dominique Perrault, the new Tolbiac site — often called the Bibliothèque François-Mitterrand — opened in 1996. It consists of four 79-metre glass towers shaped like open books, arranged around a sunken forest garden, with vast reading rooms below ground level. The historic Richelieu site, renovated and reopened fully in 2022, now houses the library's special collections, the Institut national d'histoire de l'art, and a museum.

Collections and Gallica

The BnF's treasures include medieval illuminated manuscripts, the Gutenberg Bible, original manuscripts of major French authors, and one of the world's great collections of coins, medals, and prints. Its digital library, Gallica, launched in 1997, provides free online access to millions of digitised books, newspapers, manuscripts, images, and maps.
