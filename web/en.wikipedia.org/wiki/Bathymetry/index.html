Bathymetry

Bathymetry (from Greek bathys, "deep", and metron, "measure") is the study of the underwater depth of ocean floors and the beds of lakes and rivers — in effect, the underwater equivalent of topography. Bathymetric charts show submarine relief using depth contours (isobaths) and colour shading, and are essential for navigation, marine science, fisheries, offshore engineering and tsunami modelling.

Methods

Historically, depth was measured by lowering weighted lines (lead lines) over a ship's side. In the 19th century, sounding leads revealed features such as the mid-Atlantic telegraphic plateau. The invention of echo sounding in the early 20th century transformed the field: sonar pulses timed the return of sound from the seafloor. Modern surveys use multibeam echosounders, which map a wide swath of seabed in high resolution, and side-scan sonar. Satellite altimetry — measuring the height of the sea surface, which bulges slightly over underwater mountains — provides global but lower-resolution gravity-derived bathymetry, pioneered by work associated with Walter Smith and David Sandwell. Airborne lidar can map shallow coastal waters.

History

Systematic ocean sounding began with Matthew Fontaine Maury's charts in the 1850s and the Challenger expedition of 1872–1876. In the 1950s, Bruce Heezen and Marie Tharp's physiographic maps of the Atlantic revealed the mid-ocean ridge system and rift valley, evidence central to the acceptance of plate tectonics.

Applications and data

Global datasets include GEBCO (General Bathymetric Chart of the Oceans). The international Seabed 2030 project aims to produce a complete map of the world ocean floor by 2030; as of the early 2020s only around a quarter of the seabed had been mapped with modern multibeam methods. Bathymetry guides safe shipping, submarine cable and pipeline routes, habitat mapping, and research into events such as the Battle of Cape Matapan's operational environment.
