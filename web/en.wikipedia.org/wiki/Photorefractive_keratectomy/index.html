Photorefractive keratectomy

Photorefractive keratectomy
SpecialtyOphthalmology
UsesCorrection of myopia, hyperopia and astigmatism
MethodExcimer laser ablation of the corneal surface

Photorefractive keratectomy (PRK) is a type of refractive surgery in which an excimer laser is used to reshape the surface of the cornea to correct myopia (short-sightedness), hyperopia and astigmatism. It was the first laser vision-correction procedure in widespread clinical use, performed from the late 1980s and approved by the US FDA in 1995.

Procedure

Unlike LASIK, PRK creates no corneal flap. The surgeon removes the corneal epithelium — mechanically, with alcohol, or with the laser itself (transPRK) — and then applies computer-guided excimer laser pulses to ablate a precise pattern of stromal tissue, flattening or steepening the cornea. A bandage contact lens is placed while the epithelium regenerates over three to five days. Antibiotic and anti-inflammatory drops are used, and mitomycin C may be applied to reduce the risk of corneal haze in higher corrections.

Advantages and disadvantages

Because no flap is created, PRK avoids flap complications and preserves more corneal thickness and biomechanical strength, making it preferable for patients with thin corneas, some corneal surface disease, or lifestyles with a high risk of eye trauma; it is often required or preferred for military aviators. Its disadvantages are slower visual recovery (days to weeks rather than a day), more postoperative discomfort, and a small risk of subepithelial haze. Long-term refractive outcomes are broadly comparable to LASIK. Related surface procedures include LASEK and Epi-LASIK.
