Real union

A real union is a political union of two or more states that, unlike a personal union, share not only a common head of state but also some institutions of government, typically including a joint legislature, ministry, or administration of common affairs such as foreign policy, defence, and finance. The constituent states nevertheless retain their own legal systems, and often considerable internal autonomy, and the union is established by a treaty or constitutional act rather than by dynastic accident alone.

A real union joins states under both a shared monarch and shared organs of state.

Characteristics

In a personal union, two crowns merely happen to rest on the same head, and the states remain entirely separate in law and administration. A real union goes further: the union itself is treated as a single subject in international relations, with common institutions handling joint affairs, while domestic matters remain with each state. The category is a classic concept of European constitutional law, distinguished both from the purely dynastic personal union and from a full federal state or unitary merger.

Historical examples

  • Austria-Hungary (1867–1918): the Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867 created a real union of the Austrian Empire and the Kingdom of Hungary, with common ministries for foreign affairs, war, and joint finance, but separate parliaments and governments.
  • Sweden–Norway (1814–1905): often classed as a real union in some respects, with a common king and foreign service but separate constitutions, parliaments, and laws.
  • Denmark–Norway and the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth (after the Union of Lublin, 1569): the Commonwealth combined a shared monarch and Sejm with separate offices, armies, and treasuries, and is frequently described as a real union.
  • Finland as a Grand Duchy within the Russian Empire (1809–1917) is sometimes analysed in similar terms, though classifications vary.
