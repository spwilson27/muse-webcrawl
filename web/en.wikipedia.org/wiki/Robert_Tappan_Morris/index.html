Robert Tappan Morris (born November 8, 1965) is an American computer scientist and entrepreneur. He is best known for creating the Morris worm in 1988 — considered the first computer worm distributed via the Internet — and later as a co-founder of the startup accelerator Y Combinator and as a professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology.

Early life and education

Morris grew up in New Jersey, the son of Robert Morris Sr., a computer security expert at Bell Labs and the NSA. He graduated from Harvard University in 1987 and enrolled as a graduate student in computer science at Cornell University.

The Morris worm

On November 2, 1988, while at Cornell, Morris released a worm program from MIT's network (to disguise its origin) designed, he said, to gauge the size of the Internet. The worm exploited vulnerabilities in Unix sendmail, finger, and rsh/rexec, and weak passwords. A design flaw in its reinfection logic caused it to spread far more aggressively than intended, infecting an estimated 6,000 machines — roughly 10 percent of the Internet at the time — and causing widespread disruption and cleanup costs.

Morris became the first person convicted under the U.S. Computer Fraud and Abuse Act of 1986. In 1990 he was sentenced to three years' probation, 400 hours of community service, and a fine of $10,050 plus supervision costs.

Later career

Morris completed his PhD at MIT in 1999. In 1995 he co-founded Viaweb with Paul Graham and Trevor Blackwell, an early web-based store builder sold to Yahoo in 1998. In 2005 Morris, Graham, Jessica Livingston, and Blackwell founded Y Combinator, the seed accelerator behind companies such as Airbnb, Dropbox, and Stripe. Morris joined MIT's faculty and is a tenured professor in the Department of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science, working in computer systems and networks research at CSAIL.
