Casablanca Conference

The Casablanca Conference (codenamed SYMBOL) was held at the Anfa Hotel in Casablanca, French Morocco, from 14 to 24 January 1943, to plan the Allied European strategy for the next phase of World War II. In attendance were United States President Franklin D. Roosevelt and British Prime Minister Winston Churchill. Soviet leader Joseph Stalin was invited but declined, pleading the demands of the Battle of Stalingrad; Free French leaders Charles de Gaulle and Henri Giraud attended for the political sessions, in an awkward encounter arranged to reconcile them. The conference is best remembered for two decisions: the Allied demand for the "unconditional surrender" of the Axis powers, and the adoption of the Combined Bomber Offensive against Germany — and for choosing Sicily, rather than an immediate cross-Channel invasion, as the next major operation of 1943.

Strategic debate

The professional discussions between the British and American chiefs of staff were hard-fought. The Americans, led by General George C. Marshall, pressed for the earliest possible invasion of France across the Channel. The British, haunted by the Somme and Dunkirk and engaged in the Mediterranean, argued that the Allies lacked the landing craft and trained divisions for 1943, and won their case: the invasion of Sicily (Operation Husky) would follow the clearing of North Africa, with a cross-Channel attack (Overlord) planned for 1944 and a build-up of American forces in Britain (Bolero) in the meantime. The British also prevailed on strategy in the air: a Combined Bomber Offensive from Britain, aiming at "the progressive destruction and dislocation of the German military, industrial and economic system", bridging RAF night area bombing and USAAF daylight precision attacks.

Unconditional surrender

At the closing press conference on 24 January, Roosevelt announced that peace could come only through "the unconditional surrender" of Germany, Italy and Japan — a phrase he associated with Ulysses S. Grant ("Unconditional Surrender" Grant) at Fort Donelson. The formula's origins and wisdom remain debated: Churchill had been consulted and the British War Cabinet approved, though British representatives later distanced themselves; critics argued the slogan stiffened Axis resistance and discouraged German opposition to Hitler, while defenders held it reassured Stalin that there would be no separate negotiated peace and kept the Allied coalition united. It became the stated political object of the war in the West and framed the later settlements at Yalta and Potsdam.

The French question

Casablanca, liberated in the Torch landings of November 1942, lay in territory administered by the French authorities the Allies had installed under Giraud, while de Gaulle's Free French controlled other colonies and claimed sole legitimacy. Roosevelt and Churchill pressed the two generals into a reluctant joint appearance — the staged photograph of the two men shaking hands before the cameras became notorious — and months of negotiation followed before the French Committee of National Liberation emerged in Algiers, from which de Gaulle progressively edged Giraud aside. Roosevelt also received the Sultan of Morocco at dinner, an encounter the Sultan's party later remembered for American anti-colonial hints that encouraged Moroccan nationalism.
