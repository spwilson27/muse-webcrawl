Stemma codicum

A stemma codicum (plural stemmata codicum; Latin for "family tree of manuscripts") is a diagram in textual criticism showing the relationships among the surviving manuscript copies of a work, traced back to their common ancestor, the archetype. Constructing a stemma is the central step of the stemmatic method (or Lachmannian method) of textual criticism.

Method

The editor first collates all surviving witnesses and records their variants (recensio). Manuscripts that share significant errors (errores coniunctivi) are grouped into families descending from a common lost intermediate copy (a hyparchetype); manuscripts that are mere copies of surviving exemplars are eliminated (eliminatio codicum descriptorum) as having no independent value. The resulting tree permits the editor to reconstruct the text of the archetype by weighing agreements among independent branches, and then to emend where the archetype itself appears corrupt (examinatio and emendatio).

History and criticism

The method is associated with Karl Lachmann's editions of the New Testament (1831) and of Lucretius (1850), though similar procedures had been used by classical and biblical scholars in the 18th century. In 1928 Joseph Bédier criticized the Lachmannian method, noting the tendency of stemmata to be two-branched and arguing for editing the single best manuscript instead; later critics, including Sebastiano Timpanaro, defended stemmatics while clarifying its limits. Contamination — copying from more than one exemplar — can make a pure tree impossible, and modern editors increasingly use computational, phylogenetic methods to explore manuscript relationships.
