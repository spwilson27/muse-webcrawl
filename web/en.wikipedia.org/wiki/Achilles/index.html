Achilles is a hero of Greek mythology, the central character of Homer's Iliad and the greatest warrior of the Achaean forces in the Trojan War. Son of the mortal king Peleus and the sea-nymph Thetis, he was raised, in some traditions, by the centaur Chiron, and chose a short life with undying fame over a long obscure one.

In the Iliad

The Iliad opens with Achilles' wrath: dishonoured when Agamemnon seized his war-prize Briseis, he withdraws from battle, and only the death of his beloved companion Patroclus — killed by the Trojan prince Hector wearing Achilles' armour — brings him back. He kills Hector in single combat, drags the body around Patroclus's tomb, and finally ransoms it to the grieving Priam in one of the poem's most famous scenes. The Iliad ends before Achilles' own death, which other traditions attribute to Paris, guided by Apollo, shooting him in the heel.

The heel and later tradition

The story that Thetis dipped the infant Achilles in the river Styx, making him invulnerable except by the heel she held, appears only in later sources (fully in Statius's unfinished Achilleid, 1st century CE), yet it gave the language "Achilles' heel" for a single fatal weakness, and anatomy its "Achilles tendon". Achilles received hero cult in antiquity, was a model for Alexander the Great, and remains a byword for martial excellence and tragic anger.
