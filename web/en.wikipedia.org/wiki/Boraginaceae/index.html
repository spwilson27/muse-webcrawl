Boraginaceae, the borage or forget-me-not family, is a family of flowering plants comprising about 150 genera and some 2,700 species. Members of the family include herbs, shrubs, and trees, and are distributed worldwide, with a center of diversity in the Mediterranean region. Well-known members include borage (Borago officinalis), forget-me-nots (Myosotis), comfrey (Symphytum), and heliotropes (Heliotropium).

Description

Plants in the Boraginaceae are typically covered in rough, bristly hairs. The leaves are usually simple and arranged alternately along the stem. The flowers are commonly borne in coiled inflorescences called scorpioid cymes, which uncoil as the flowers open. The flowers are usually radially symmetric with five fused petals, often blue, pink, or white, and many species change color as they age — a signal to pollinators that a flower has already been visited.

Taxonomy

The family is the only family in the order Boraginales in most modern classifications. The APG IV system recognizes Boraginaceae in a broad sense, though some botanists split it into several smaller families, including Cordiaceae, Ehretiaceae, and Heliotropiaceae. Subfamilies commonly recognized include Boraginoideae, Cordioideae, Ehretioideae, and Hydrophylloideae.

Uses

Several species have culinary and medicinal uses. Borage leaves have a cucumber-like flavor and are used in salads and drinks. Comfrey has a long history in herbal medicine, though it contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids that can damage the liver. Species of Cordia and other genera provide timber, and many borages are grown as ornamentals in gardens.

Ecology

Boraginaceae flowers are important nectar and pollen sources for bees and other pollinators. The color change of aging flowers helps direct pollinators toward unvisited, more rewarding blooms, increasing the efficiency of both plant reproduction and insect foraging.
