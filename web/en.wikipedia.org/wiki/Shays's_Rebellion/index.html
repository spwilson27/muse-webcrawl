Shays's Rebellion

[Image: The Springfield Armory, target of the rebels' January 1787 attack]

Shays's Rebellion was an armed uprising in Western Massachusetts and Worcester in response to a debt crisis among the citizenry and in opposition to the state government's increased efforts to collect taxes on both individuals and their trades. The fighting broke out in 1786 and was most intense between December 1786 and February 1787, led largely by Revolutionary War veterans — most famously Daniel Shays, a former captain from Pelham. The rebellion is best remembered for convincing national leaders that the Articles of Confederation were too weak and for spurring the movement that produced the United States Constitution.

Background

The post-war American economy was depressed, and Massachusetts, burdened by war debts owed largely to Boston merchants and foreign creditors, imposed heavy taxes payable in scarce hard currency. Farmers in the western counties faced foreclosure and imprisonment for debt when they could not pay. Imitating the resistance tactics of the Revolution, "Regulators" petitioned for paper money and tax relief, and when the legislature in Boston adjourned without acting, crowds began forcibly closing county courts in the autumn of 1786 to prevent foreclosure judgments.

Course of the rebellion

Daniel Shays emerged as the best-known leader of the western insurgents, alongside figures such as Luke Day and Job Shattuck. Governor James Bowdoin organised a privately financed militia under former Continental Army general Benjamin Lincoln. The crisis peaked on 25 January 1787, when Shays's force of about 1,200–1,500 men marched on the federal armory at Springfield to seize weapons; militia defending the armory fired warning shots and then cannon, killing four rebels and scattering the rest. Lincoln's army pursued the insurgents through harsh winter weather, surprising and dispersing the main body at Petersham on 4 February 1787. Small-scale violence and raids continued for months, and Shays fled to Vermont.

Aftermath

Most participants were pardoned; a handful of ringleaders were convicted of treason and sentenced to hang, but the two men actually executed, John Bly and Charles Rose, were convicted of burglary committed during the unrest. Shays himself was pardoned in 1788 and eventually received a federal pension for his Revolutionary service. In the 1787 elections, Governor Bowdoin was defeated by John Hancock, and the new legislature enacted tax relief and reforms.

Legacy

The rebellion alarmed George Washington and other nationalists — Henry Knox warned Washington that the insurgents threatened property throughout the Union — and it materially strengthened the case for the Constitutional Convention that met in Philadelphia in May 1787. Debates over the rebellion also shaped provisions of the Constitution concerning federal power to suppress insurrections and influenced the arguments between Federalists and Anti-Federalists.
