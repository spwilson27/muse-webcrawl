Bottom of the pyramid

The bottom of the pyramid, base of the pyramid, or base of the economic pyramid (BoP or BpP) is the largest but poorest socio-economic group. In global terms, this is the more than four billion people who live on less than about $2.50 per day (in purchasing power parity terms). The term is most widely identified with the work of C. K. Prahalad, whose 2004 book The Fortune at the Bottom of the Pyramid argued that multinational firms could profitably serve poor consumers while helping to alleviate poverty.

The argument

Prahalad proposed that the poor represent a vast, underserved market whose collective purchasing power is large, and that businesses could reach it by redesigning products and distribution: single-serve sachets of shampoo and detergent, low-cost consumer goods, microfinance, and pay-as-you-go services are frequently cited examples.

Criticism

Critics, including Aneel Karnani, argue that the BoP market is much smaller than claimed, that the poor are price-sensitive and often exploited rather than empowered, and that poverty reduction is better served by treating poor people as producers — raising their incomes — than as consumers. Others question the environmental impact of small-unit packaging and the evidence that BoP ventures have reduced poverty at scale.

Related concepts

The concept influenced development strategies such as inclusive business and social enterprise, and later "BoP 2.0" approaches emphasise co-creating products and businesses with local communities rather than simply selling to them.
