Münchhausen trilemma

The Münchhausen trilemma is a thought experiment in epistemology, named after Baron Münchhausen, intended to show that it is impossible to prove any truth with certainty. It argues that any attempt to justify a proposition must face one of three unsatisfactory outcomes: an infinite regress of justifications, a circular argument, or a dogmatic stopping point. The trilemma is closely related to the ancient problem of the criterion formulated by the Pyrrhonist philosopher Agrippa, and is also known as Agrippa's trilemma.

Baron Münchhausen extricating himself from a swamp by his own hair — the image behind the trilemma's name

The trilemma

If it is asked how we know that some proposition is true, a justification can be offered. But the justification itself then requires justification, and so on. According to the trilemma, this process can end in only three ways, each of which fails to provide certain knowledge:

  1. Infinite regress — the chain of justifications goes on forever, so no proposition is ever finally justified.
  2. Circular reasoning — the chain loops back on itself, so a proposition is ultimately supported by itself.
  3. Dogmatic termination — the chain is broken off at some point by appeal to a supposedly self-evident axiom or assumption that is itself unjustified.

Origin of the name

The term was introduced by the German philosopher Hans Albert in his Treatise on Critical Reason (1968). The name alludes to the tall tale in which Baron Münchhausen, stuck in a swamp, pulls himself (and his horse) out by his own hair — an apt image for the attempt to ground knowledge on itself. The underlying problem is much older: the ancient sceptic Agrippa described five modes leading to suspension of judgement, three of which correspond to regress, circularity (the reciprocal mode) and hypothesis.

Responses

Philosophers have responded in different ways. Foundationalism accepts a version of the third horn but argues that some beliefs — basic beliefs — are justified without needing further inferential support. Coherentism embraces circularity of a holistic kind, holding that beliefs are justified by their coherence with a whole system rather than by linear chains. Infinitism accepts the infinite regress but denies that it is vicious. Karl Popper and Hans Albert, following the tradition of critical rationalism, concede that ultimate justification is impossible and instead propose that knowledge claims be held open to criticism and testing; Albert argued that the trilemma's force depends on the classical ideal of justification, which should be abandoned rather than satisfied.
