Mountaineering in Japan

Mountaineering in Japan has a long history rooted in both religious practice and modern sport. Japan's mountainous terrain, which covers roughly 70 percent of the country's land area, includes the Japanese Alps, a series of ranges in central Honshu, and the iconic Mount Fuji, the nation's highest peak at 3,776 metres.

History

Mountain worship, known as shugendō, blended Shinto and Buddhist traditions, with ascetics climbing sacred peaks such as Mount Ōmine and Mount Haguro. Modern mountaineering arrived in the Meiji era, influenced by European alpinists such as William Gowland, who coined the term "Japanese Alps". The Japanese Alpine Club was founded in 1905. Walter Weston, an English missionary, popularised Japanese climbing abroad through his writings.

Major ranges and peaks

The Northern, Central, and Southern Alps form the backbone of Honshu. Notable peaks include Mount Kita (3,193 m), Mount Hotaka (3,190 m), and Mount Yari (3,180 m). Hokkaido's Daisetsuzan range and the mountains of Kyushu offer further climbing. The "100 Famous Japanese Mountains" list, compiled by Kyūya Fukada in 1964, remains a popular goal for hikers.

Modern practice

Today millions of Japanese hike and climb each year. Mountain huts, marked trails, and seasonal transport make access relatively easy, though overcrowding on popular routes and accidents among unprepared hikers are ongoing concerns.
