Morphology (folkloristics)

Morphology, in folkloristics, is the study of the structure of folklore and fairy tales: the way their component parts (motifs, functions and episodes) are combined. The term is most closely associated with the Russian formalist Vladimir Propp, whose Morphology of the Folktale (1928; English translation 1958) analysed Russian magic tales and concluded that, despite their variety, they were built from a fixed sequence of at most 31 narrative "functions" — such as interdiction, violation, departure, test by a donor, acquisition of a magical agent, struggle, victory and return — performed by a limited set of character roles (hero, villain, donor, helper, princess and her father, dispatcher, false hero).

Propp distinguished his syntagmatic approach, concerned with the sequence of functions, from the Aarne–Thompson classification of tale types and Stith Thompson's Motif-Index, which catalogue tales and motifs by content. His work influenced structuralism, including the narrative theories of Claude Lévi-Strauss and Algirdas Greimas, who reduced Propp's roles to actants in a general grammar of narrative.

Critics noted that Propp's scheme was derived from a single genre (the Russian volshebnaia skazka) and that imposing fixed sequences risks flattening meaning and context; later folklorists such as Alan Dundes applied and extended structural analysis to other traditions, including Native American tales.
