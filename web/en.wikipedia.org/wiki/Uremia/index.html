Uremia

Uremia (also spelled uraemia) is the condition of having high levels of urea and other nitrogenous waste products in the blood, together with the clinical syndrome that results from kidney failure. The term derives from Greek roots meaning "urine in the blood". Uremia develops in advanced chronic kidney disease and in severe acute kidney injury, when the kidneys can no longer adequately filter metabolic wastes, regulate electrolytes and fluid, or maintain acid–base balance.

Signs and symptoms

The uremic syndrome affects nearly every organ system. Manifestations include:

  • Fatigue, nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, and a metallic taste
  • Uremic encephalopathy — confusion, asterixis, seizures, and, untreated, coma
  • Uremic pericarditis and pleuritis
  • Platelet dysfunction and bleeding tendency
  • Pruritus (itching), restless legs, and peripheral neuropathy
  • Uremic frost — crystallised urea on the skin, now rare
  • Anaemia, metabolic acidosis, hyperkalaemia, and fluid overload

Many symptoms are attributed not to urea itself but to the accumulation of other "uremic toxins" such as guanidines, indoxyl sulfate, and p-cresyl sulfate.

Causes and diagnosis

Uremia results from prerenal, intrinsic renal, or postrenal causes of kidney failure, including diabetic and hypertensive nephropathy, glomerulonephritis, and obstructive uropathy. Diagnosis rests on clinical findings together with elevated blood urea nitrogen and creatinine, reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and associated electrolyte and acid–base abnormalities.

Treatment

Definitive treatment is kidney replacement therapy — haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, or kidney transplantation. Conservative management includes dietary protein and salt restriction, treatment of acidosis and hyperkalaemia, and management of mineral and bone disorders. Certain manifestations, notably uremic pericarditis and encephalopathy, are urgent indications for dialysis.
