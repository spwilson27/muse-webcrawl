Rotation in office

Rotation in office is a political principle, associated especially with Jacksonian democracy in the United States, according to which public offices should be held by citizens in turn for relatively short periods rather than becoming the preserve of a permanent office-holding class. It is closely related to the modern concept of term limits.

United States

President Andrew Jackson argued in his first annual message to Congress in 1829 that the duties of public office were, or could be made, "so plain and simple" that any person of intelligence might perform them, and that long tenure led to corruption and detachment from the people. Rotation was practised both as a democratic ideal and as a mechanism of the spoils system, by which incoming administrations replaced officeholders with their own supporters.

Historical examples

The idea has ancient precedents: in classical Athens many offices were filled by lot for one-year terms, and in the early American republic the Articles of Confederation limited delegates to three years in any six. The Twenty-second Amendment to the U.S. Constitution (1951) formally limited presidents to two terms.
