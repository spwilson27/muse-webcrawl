Five precepts

The five precepts (Pali: pañcasīla; Sanskrit: pañcaśīla) are the most important system of morality for Buddhist lay people. They constitute the basic code of ethics undertaken by lay followers of Buddhism, and are commitments to abstain from killing living beings, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying and intoxication.

The precepts

  1. To refrain from taking life (not killing any living being).
  2. To refrain from taking what is not given (not stealing).
  3. To refrain from the misuse of the senses (sexual misconduct).
  4. To refrain from wrong speech (lying and harmful speech).
  5. To refrain from intoxicants that cloud the mind (alcohol and intoxicating drugs).

Practice

The precepts are undertaken voluntarily, often recited after the formula of taking refuge in the Buddha, Dharma and Sangha. They are understood as practical training rules rather than commandments, and as gifts to oneself and others. In Theravada countries laypeople may renew the precepts on observance days (Uposatha); on such days some observe eight precepts, adding abstinence from eating after midday, entertainment, and luxurious beds. Monks and nuns observe a far larger set of rules in the Vinaya, and novices undertake ten precepts.

Rationale

The precepts are grounded in the principles of non-harming (ahimsa) and right action within the Noble Eightfold Path. Keeping the first precept, for example, is understood both as refraining from killing and as cultivating loving-kindness toward all beings. The fifth precept protects the others, since intoxication is seen as a cause of heedlessness.
