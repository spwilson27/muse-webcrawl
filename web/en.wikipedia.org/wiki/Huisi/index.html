Nanyue Huisi (慧思, 515–577), sometimes romanised as Hui-ssu or Hui-szu, was an eminent Chinese Buddhist monk of the Northern and Southern dynasties period, regarded as one of the most influential meditation masters of sixth-century China and as the teacher of Zhiyi, the de facto founder of the Tiantai school. Tiantai tradition honours him as its third patriarch (or, counting Huisi's teacher Huiwen, counts him second after Huiwen).

Life

Huisi was ordained at fifteen and, driven by a sense of living in the age of the Dharma's decline (mofa), pursued intensive meditation, reciting and meditating on the Lotus Sutra. His early years in the north were spent in strenuous ascetic practice — including periods of seclusion at Mount Dasu in Guangshan — through which he attained, according to his biography, the "Lotus Samadhi". To escape persecution and disorder, in 558 he withdrew south to Mount Heng ("Nanyue", the Southern Sacred Mountain), from which his name derives, and lived there for the last twenty years of his life, teaching a community of disciples and composing works including the Vows (Nanyue Huisi lishi yuanwen), in which he vowed to preserve the Dharma until the coming of Maitreya.

Teachings

Huisi's writings mark a decisive step in the Chinese systematisation of meditation. He classified the Buddha's teachings according to the "four modes of preaching" of the Nirvana Sutra, distinguished sudden and gradual doctrines, and developed practices such as the fourfold samadhi structure — constantly sitting, constantly walking (the 90-day circumambulation practice around a Buddha image), part-walking/part-sitting, and neither-walking-nor-sitting — which Zhiyi later elaborated in his Mohe Zhiguan. His emphasis on combining meditative concentration (zhi) with contemplative insight (guan) deeply shaped Tiantai practice and, through it, much of East Asian Buddhism.

Works

Surviving works attributed to Huisi include the Essentials of Peaceful and Blissful Practices (Anle xing yi), an exegesis of the epilogue chapter of the Lotus Sutra, and texts on the meaning of the Lotus and on the Diamond Sutra, several of which modern scholars consider to have been edited or partly composed by his school.
