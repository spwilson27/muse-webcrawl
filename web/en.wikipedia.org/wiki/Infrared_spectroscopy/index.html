Infrared spectroscopy

Infrared spectroscopy (IR spectroscopy) is a technique that measures the interaction of infrared radiation with matter, most commonly the absorption of infrared light by molecular vibrations. Because different chemical bonds vibrate at characteristic frequencies, an infrared spectrum acts as a molecular "fingerprint," allowing chemists to identify substances and functional groups within them.

Principle

When infrared radiation passes through a sample, molecules absorb photons at frequencies matching the energy gaps between vibrational states — stretching, bending, and other motions of their bonds. A vibration is infrared-active only if it changes the molecule's dipole moment. Spectra are usually plotted as transmittance or absorbance against wavenumber (in cm−1), typically over the mid-infrared range of 4000–400 cm−1. Characteristic absorptions — for example, a carbonyl (C=O) stretch near 1700 cm−1 or broad O–H stretching near 3300 cm−1 — reveal the functional groups present, while the complex "fingerprint region" below about 1500 cm−1 is unique to each compound.

Instrumentation

Modern instruments are almost all Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometers, which use an interferometer (typically a Michelson interferometer) to measure all frequencies simultaneously and a Fourier transform to compute the spectrum. FTIR offers speed, sensitivity, and wavelength accuracy advantages over older dispersive instruments. Samples can be measured as gases, liquids, solids (in KBr pellets or by attenuated total reflectance, ATR), or thin films.

Applications

IR spectroscopy is used throughout chemistry, pharmaceuticals, forensics, environmental monitoring, and industry for identifying unknown materials, verifying purity, monitoring reactions, and analysing polymers, gases, and contaminants. Related techniques include near-infrared spectroscopy and Raman spectroscopy, which probes the same vibrations by light scattering.
