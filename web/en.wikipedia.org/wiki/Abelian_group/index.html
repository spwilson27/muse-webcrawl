Abelian group

In mathematics, an abelian group, also called a commutative group, is a group in which the group operation is commutative: for all elements a and b, a · b = b · a. The concept is named after the Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel. Abelian groups are among the most thoroughly understood objects in algebra and form the foundation of module theory, homological algebra and algebraic topology.

Definition

A group (G, ·) is abelian if it satisfies the commutative law in addition to the group axioms: closure, associativity, existence of an identity element e, and existence of inverses. Abelian groups are often written additively, with the operation denoted +, the identity 0, and the inverse of a written −a; in this notation the commutativity law reads a + b = b + a.

Examples

  • The integers under addition, (ℤ, +).
  • The rational, real and complex numbers under addition.
  • The integers modulo n under addition, ℤ/nℤ, a cyclic group.
  • Every cyclic group is abelian, as is every direct product of abelian groups.
  • The Klein four-group, the smallest non-cyclic abelian group.

By contrast, matrix groups such as GL(n, ℝ) and permutation groups Sn for n ≥ 3 are non-abelian.

Classification

The fundamental theorem of finite abelian groups states that every finite abelian group is a direct product of cyclic groups of prime-power order, and the orders of these factors (the elementary divisors, or equivalently the invariant factors) determine the group up to isomorphism. Finitely generated abelian groups likewise decompose as a direct sum of a free part — copies of ℤ, whose number is the rank — and a finite torsion subgroup.

Properties and applications

Subgroups of abelian groups are automatically normal, quotients are well behaved, and the category of abelian groups has kernels, cokernels and exact sequences, making it the prototypical abelian category. Abelian groups appear throughout mathematics: homology and cohomology groups are always abelian, the Jacobian and Picard groups of algebraic geometry are abelian, and characters of abelian groups underlie Fourier analysis via Pontryagin duality.
