Henry George (September 2, 1839 – October 29, 1897) was an American political economist and journalist. His writing was immensely popular in 19th-century America and sparked several reform movements of the Progressive Era. He inspired the economic philosophy known as Georgism, the belief that people should own the value they produce themselves, but that the economic value of land (including natural resources) should belong equally to all members of society.

George is best known for his best-selling book Progress and Poverty (1879), which inquired into the paradox of increasing inequality and poverty amid economic and technological progress, the business cycle with its financial crises, and the use of rent capture, such as land value tax, as a remedy. It sold millions of copies worldwide, plausibly more than any other American book before the 20th century except the Bible. It helped spark the Progressive Era and a worldwide social reform movement around Georgist ideology. Journalist and friend A. J. Stead wrote that George's work had such an impact that "scarcely a reformer in England, America or Australia would be found who did not owe much to Henry George".

Life

George was born in Philadelphia to a lower-middle-class family. He left school at 14, worked as a clerk and went to sea as a foremast boy on a voyage to Melbourne and Kolkata, then settled in California, where he worked as a typesetter, gold prospector, and journalist, eventually editing the San Francisco Daily Evening Post. His travels and journalism exposed him to land speculation and monopoly in the American West — the sudden wealth of the few amid widespread poverty — which supplied the central puzzle of his life's work. In 1871 he published his first pamphlet, Our Land and Land Policy.

Progress and Poverty and the single tax

In Progress and Poverty George argued that nearly all of the wealth created by social and technological progress is captured by landowners and monopolists in the form of economic rent, while the value of wages and interest is held down. His remedy was the "single tax": a land value tax on the unimproved value of land, replacing all other taxes, which he argued would discourage speculation, raise wages, and make land available to those who wished to cultivate it — without confiscating private property in the products of labour. The book made him a worldwide figure; he lectured across Britain, Ireland (where he supported land reform and was arrested during the Land War agitation), and Australia.

Political career and death

In 1886 George ran for mayor of New York City as the candidate of the United Labor Party, coming second — ahead of the Republican candidate, the young Theodore Roosevelt. He ran again in 1897, as an independent Democrat, but died of a stroke on 29 October 1897, four days before the election, while campaigning. An estimated 100,000 people attended his funeral procession in New York, one of the largest public mourning events of the century.

Legacy

Georgist ideas influenced land value taxation in several countries, the design of the board game that became Monopoly (Elizabeth Magie's The Landlord's Game), and thinkers as diverse as Tolstoy, Einstein (who praised the book), and Sun Yat-sen. The Henry George School of Social Science, founded in New York in 1932, continues to teach his economics.
