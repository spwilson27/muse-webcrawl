Old Believers or Old Ritualists (Russian: староверы, starovery) are Eastern Orthodox Christians who maintain the liturgical and ritual practices of the Russian Orthodox Church as they existed before the reforms of Patriarch Nikon of Moscow between 1652 and 1666. Resisting the changes, they were anathematised in 1666–1667 and split from the official church in the schism known as the Raskol.

History

Nikon's reforms aligned Russian practice with contemporary Greek usage — changing, among other things, the sign of the cross from two fingers to three, the spelling of Jesus's name, and the number of alleluias. Opponents led by the archpriest Avvakum saw the changes as corruption of the faith; Avvakum was burned at the stake in 1682. Persecuted for centuries, Old Believer communities fled to the Russian frontier and abroad — to Siberia, Alaska, and later as far as Oregon, Brazil, and China. The anathemas on the old rites were lifted by the Moscow Patriarchate in 1971.

Groups and practice

Old Believers divided into priestly (Popovtsy), who retained ordained clergy, and priestless (Bezpopovtsy) groups, which concluded the priesthood had been lost. Distinctive practices include the eight-pointed cross, two-fingered sign of the cross, beards for men, lestovka prayer ropes, and znamenny chant. Major present-day bodies include the Russian Orthodox Old-Rite Church and the Lipovan churches of Romania.
