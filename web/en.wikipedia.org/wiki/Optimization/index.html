Mathematical optimization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A contour plot with a path descending to the minimum of a function]

Mathematical optimisation (or mathematical programming) is the selection of a best element, with regard to some criterion, from a set of available alternatives. Problems specify an objective function to maximise or minimise subject to constraints on the variables.

Linear programming — optimising a linear objective over linear constraints — was developed in the 1930s–40s (Kantorovich 1939; Dantzig's simplex method, 1947) and is a workhorse of logistics, economics, and engineering. George Dantzig, John von Neumann (duality theory), and Leonid Kantorovich are its founding figures.

Classes of problems

Convex optimisation problems have guaranteed global optima and efficient algorithms (interior-point methods, 1980s). Nonlinear, integer, and combinatorial optimisation (scheduling, routing, packing) are generally harder (often NP-hard), handled by heuristics and metaheuristics such as simulated annealing and genetic algorithms.

Optimisation is also the engine of machine learning: training is minimising a loss function, typically by stochastic gradient descent and its adaptive variants.

Applications

Airline scheduling, supply chains, portfolio selection, energy-grid operation, structural design, and control systems all reduce to optimisation at scale — it is among the most economically significant branches of applied mathematics.
