Ulam spiral

The Ulam spiral, or prime spiral, is a graphical depiction of the set of prime numbers, devised by the mathematician Stanisław Ulam in 1963. It is constructed by writing the positive integers in a square spiral and marking the primes; strikingly, the marked points tend to fall along diagonal lines, suggesting patterns in the distribution of the primes that remain only partly explained.

Discovery

Ulam hit on the construction while doodling during a "long and boring" scientific meeting in 1963. Working out larger examples by computer with Myron Stein and Mark Wells at Los Alamos, he found that the diagonal alignments persisted to large scales. The spiral appeared on the cover of Scientific American in March 1964 in Martin Gardner's Mathematical Games column.

Explanation

Diagonal lines in the spiral correspond to quadratic polynomials in the integers. Some diagonals are dense in primes, echoing famous prime-generating polynomials such as Euler's n² + n + 41; others are nearly empty. The Hardy–Littlewood conjectures on prime constellations predict the relative richness of these lines, but the general phenomenon of prime patterns along quadratics remains unproved and connects to deep open problems about prime distribution. Related features include horizontal and vertical lines associated with parity and modular constraints.

Variants

Variants include the Sacks spiral, which uses an Archimedean spiral, triangular and hexagonal arrangements (such as Robert Sacks's and the "hexagonal prime spiral"), and spirals highlighting composite structures or prime-rich polynomials. Ulam's simple picture remains a standard illustration that the primes, though apparently random, exhibit subtle large-scale order.
