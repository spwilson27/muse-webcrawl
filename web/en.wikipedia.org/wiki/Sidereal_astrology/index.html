Sidereal astrology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sidereal astrology is the system of astrology used in Hindu (Vedic) astrology and by some Western astrologers, in which the zodiac is anchored to the fixed stars rather than to the seasons. Sidereal signs are defined by the actual constellations of the same names, so the system accounts for the precession of the equinoxes — the slow westward shift of the March equinox point against the stars, of about one degree every 72 years.

Most Western astrology, by contrast, uses the tropical zodiac, in which 0° Aries is fixed at the March equinox regardless of the constellation behind it. When the two systems were roughly aligned in late antiquity, they coincided; today they differ by about 24 degrees. The offset between the tropical and sidereal zodiacs is called the ayanāṁśa in Indian astrology, and several values are in use, the most common being the Lahiri ayanāṁśa adopted by the Indian government calendar committee.

History

Hellenistic astrology originally used forms of both systems. Indian astrology retained the sidereal framework as it was transmitted from the Hellenistic world and developed its own techniques around it, including the lunar mansions (nakṣatra). In the West, the Irish astrologer Cyril Fagan championed a "sidereal revival" in the mid-20th century, and Western siderealists such as Donald Bradley adopted the Fagan-Bradley ayanāṁśa.
