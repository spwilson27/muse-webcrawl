Negative number

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A number line extending left below zero into negative values]

In mathematics, a negative number is a real number less than zero. Negative numbers represent opposites: movement left versus right, debt versus assets, temperatures below zero, and charges of opposite sign. Their arithmetic — above all the rule that a negative times a negative is positive — was systematised over centuries.

Chinese mathematicians used red and black counting rods for positive and negative quantities by the Han era (Nine Chapters, by about the 2nd century AD); Brahmagupta (7th century) stated explicit rules in terms of fortunes and debts. Europe resisted far longer: as late as the 17th–18th centuries, mathematicians such as Descartes dismissed negative roots as 'false', and British algebraists debated their legitimacy.

Acceptance

The number line picture (Wallis) and the needs of algebra — every equation x + a = b should have a solution — eventually made negatives unavoidable. Formally, integers are constructed as equivalence classes of pairs of natural numbers; the sign rules then follow from the axioms rather than from intuition.

Everyday life settled the matter first: thermometers, bank balances, and elevations below sea level make negative numbers the natural language of relative quantity.
