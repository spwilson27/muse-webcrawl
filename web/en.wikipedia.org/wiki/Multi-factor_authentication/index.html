Multi-factor authentication

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Photograph of a smartphone showing a six-digit one-time code next to a laptop login screen]

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) is an electronic authentication method in which a user is granted access to a website or application only after successfully presenting two or more pieces of evidence ('factors') to an authentication mechanism. The classic taxonomy: something you know (a password), something you have (a phone, a hardware key), and something you are (a fingerprint or face). Two-factor authentication (2FA) is the common two-factor subset.

MFA became mainstream as password-only security failed: banks issued hardware tokens in the 2000s, Google added 2-Step Verification in 2011, and time-based one-time codes (TOTP, from authenticator apps) and push approvals spread through the following decade with the growth of phishing and credential stuffing. Not all factors are equal — SMS codes can be intercepted or SIM-swapped, app codes can be phished in real time, while FIDO2/WebAuthn hardware keys and passkeys cryptographically bind the login to the real site and resist phishing outright.

Regulators and platforms increasingly require MFA for administrators, developers and financial accounts, and large providers have made it default-on for billions of users. It is now regarded, along with prompt patching, as the single most effective consumer security measure — blocking the overwhelming majority of automated account-takeover attacks in published studies.
