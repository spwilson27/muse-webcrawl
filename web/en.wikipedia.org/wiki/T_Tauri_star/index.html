T Tauri star

T Tauri stars are a class of variable stars more than 10 million years old at most, named after the prototype T Tauri in the constellation Taurus. They are very young, low- to intermediate-mass stars still contracting toward the main sequence, and they are found near molecular clouds in star-forming regions such as Taurus-Auriga and Orion.

Characteristics

T Tauri stars rotate relatively rapidly, have large dark starspots, strong magnetic fields and powerful chromospheres and coronae. Their spectra show unusually strong emission lines — hydrogen Hα above all — together with forbidden lines from low-density outflowing gas, and lithium absorption indicating their youth. Many drive bipolar jets and Herbig–Haro objects. Their brightness varies irregularly on timescales of days, from spots, accretion hot spots and variable obscuration by circumstellar dust.

Classical and weak-lined types

Astronomers divide the class into classical T Tauri stars, which are still accreting from a circumstellar disc (showing infrared excess, veiling and strong emission), and weak-lined T Tauri stars, whose discs have largely dispersed and whose emission lines are feeble. The discs of classical T Tauri stars are protoplanetary discs — the birthplaces of planets — and have been imaged in detail by ALMA, which has revealed rings and gaps thought to mark forming planets.

Evolution

A T Tauri star is still powered mainly by gravitational contraction (the Kelvin–Helmholtz mechanism); core temperatures are not yet sufficient for sustained hydrogen fusion, though deuterium and lithium burning may occur. After a few million to a few tens of millions of years the star settles onto the main sequence, passing through this phase as the young Sun itself once did — which is why the class is central to theories of solar-system formation.
