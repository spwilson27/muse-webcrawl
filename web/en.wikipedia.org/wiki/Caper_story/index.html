Caper story

The caper story is a subgenre of crime fiction that focuses on the planning and execution of a daring crime — very often a heist — told in a light, humorous or stylish tone, frequently from the criminals' point of view. Unlike hardboiled detective fiction, the caper treats the crime as a clever puzzle or game, with emphasis on ingenuity, teamwork and narrow escapes rather than violence or moral consequences.

Characteristics

Typical caper plots assemble a team of specialists, rehearse an intricate plan in detail, and then show the plan going partially wrong, forcing improvisation. The tone is playful: the victims are often unsympathetic (a corrupt tycoon, a casino, an insurance company), and the audience is invited to root for the thieves. In film, the genre is known as the heist film.

Examples

Classic literary capers include the Raffles stories, Donald E. Westlake's Dortmunder novels, and Elmore Leonard's crime fiction. In cinema, notable capers include Rififi (1955), The Thomas Crown Affair, The Italian Job (1969), The Sting (1973), and the Ocean's Eleven franchise (2001 onward). Television examples include Hustle and Leverage.

Relation to other genres

The caper differs from the whodunit, which centres on solving a crime, and from noir, whose tone is bleak. It overlaps with the picaresque and with the "gentleman thief" tradition exemplified by Arsène Lupin and A. J. Raffles.
