Dactyloscopy is the science and practice of identifying individuals by their fingerprints — the patterns of friction ridges on the fingers. It rests on two principles: that ridge patterns are unique to each person (remaining unchanged throughout life) and that they can be systematically classified, making fingerprints searchable in large collections.

History

Fingerprints were used as seals in ancient China and Babylon, but their forensic development began in the 19th century. William James Herschel used handprints and fingerprints administratively in Bengal from the 1850s–1870s; Henry Faulds proposed crime-scene fingerprint identification in Nature in 1880; and Francis Galton's Finger Prints (1892) established the statistical case for uniqueness and a classification by arches, loops and whorls. Juan Vucetich in Argentina built the first effective police fingerprint bureau in 1892 — fingerprints solved the Rojas murder case that year — while Edward Henry's classification system was adopted in British India (1897) and at Scotland Yard (1901) and became the basis of most national systems.

Method

Examiners compare latent prints lifted from surfaces with known prints using the ACE-V process (analysis, comparison, evaluation, verification), studying ridge flow, minutiae (ridge endings and bifurcations) and finer detail. Modern practice is supported by automated fingerprint identification systems (AFIS) that search millions of records.

Reliability debate

For most of its history fingerprint evidence was treated as infallible, but cases such as the erroneous identification of Brandon Mayfield in the 2004 Madrid bombing investigation, along with scientific reviews calling for error-rate studies and blind verification, have led to reforms in how conclusions are stated and tested in court.
