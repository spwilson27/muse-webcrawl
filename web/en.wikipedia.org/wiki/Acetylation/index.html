Acetylation

In chemistry, acetylation is a chemical reaction in which an acetyl functional group is added to a chemical compound. The opposite process is called deacetylation, the removal of an acetyl group. Acetylation is commonly achieved using acetic anhydride or acetyl chloride as the acetyl donor.

In organic synthesis

Acetylation converts alcohols and phenols to acetate esters and amines to amides. A classic example is the acetylation of salicylic acid with acetic anhydride to give acetylsalicylic acid — aspirin. Acetyl groups are also used as protecting groups for amines and alcohols during multi-step synthesis and are removed by hydrolysis.

In biology

Acetylation is an important post-translational modification of proteins. Histone acetylation, catalysed by histone acetyltransferases (HATs) using acetyl-CoA as donor, neutralises the positive charge of lysine residues, loosening chromatin structure and generally promoting gene transcription; the reverse reaction by histone deacetylases (HDACs) is associated with gene repression. N-terminal acetylation is one of the most common protein modifications in eukaryotes, affecting protein stability and interactions.

Industrial applications

The acetylation of wood with acetic anhydride increases its durability and dimensional stability. Acetylation of chitin yields chitosan-related products, and acetylated starches are used as food additives.
