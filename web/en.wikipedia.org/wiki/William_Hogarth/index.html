William Hogarth

Born: 10 November 1697, London, England
Died: 26 October 1764, London, England
Occupation: Painter, engraver, satirist
Known for: A Harlot's Progress; A Rake's Progress; Marriage A-la-Mode

William Hogarth (10 November 1697 – 26 October 1764) was an English artist, engraver, and satirist, a pioneer of sequential narrative art whose moralising picture series are regarded as a forerunner of the modern cartoon and comic strip.

Early life

Hogarth was born in London, the son of a Latin teacher and textbook writer who was imprisoned for debt when William was a boy. Apprenticed to an engraver of silver plate, he set up on his own around 1720, producing book illustrations and satirical prints before turning to painting.

The moral series

Hogarth's fame rests on series of paintings, sold widely as engravings, that satirised 18th-century London life: A Harlot's Progress (1732), A Rake's Progress (1735), Marriage A-la-Mode (1743–45), Industry and Idleness (1747), and the single images Beer Street and Gin Lane (1751). Dense with detail and incident, they function like stage plays in pictures.

Reform and theory

Hogarth campaigned for artists' copyright, helping secure the Engravers' Copyright Act of 1735 ("Hogarth's Act"), and supported the Foundling Hospital, donating his portrait of Captain Coram. His treatise The Analysis of Beauty (1753) promoted the serpentine "line of beauty".
