A ligament is the fibrous connective tissue that connects bones to other bones. It is also known as articular ligament, articular larua, fibrous ligament, or true ligament. Other ligaments in the body include the peritoneal ligaments (folds of peritoneum) and fetal remnant ligaments (remnants of fetal tubular structures). Ligaments are similar to tendons and fasciae in that they are all made of dense connective tissue; the difference is that tendons connect muscle to bone, and fasciae connect muscles to other muscles.

Structure

Ligaments are composed of dense regular connective tissue bundles encased in dense irregular connective tissue sheaths. The principal molecular building block is collagen — about 70–80% of the dry weight, mostly type I collagen — arranged in parallel bundles of fibres with a characteristic crimped (wavy) pattern that allows slight stretching before the ligament becomes taut. Fibroblasts are the predominant cell type. Ligaments also contain sensory nerve endings that contribute to proprioception, the sense of joint position.

Function

The primary mechanical function of a ligament is to stabilise a joint: it resists excessive or abnormal movement while permitting normal motion. The cruciate ligaments of the knee, the collateral ligaments of the elbow and ankle, and the ligaments of the spine are classic examples. Because ligaments are viscoelastic, they creep under sustained load and relax over time, properties that matter in both injury and rehabilitation.

Injury

A stretched or torn ligament is called a sprain, graded from I (mild stretching) to III (complete rupture). The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) of the knee and the lateral ligaments of the ankle are among the most commonly injured. Healing is slow because ligaments have a relatively poor blood supply; severe ruptures may require surgical reconstruction, often using a tendon graft.
