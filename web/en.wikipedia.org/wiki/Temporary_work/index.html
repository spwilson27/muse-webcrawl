Temporary work

Temporary work or temporary employment (also called temps) refers to an employment situation where the working arrangement is limited to a certain period of time, based on the needs of the employing organisation. Temporary employees are sometimes called "contractual", "seasonal", "interim", "casual staff", "outsourced" or "freelance" workers. In some cases, temporary or highly skilled professionals (particularly in white-collar fields such as human resources, research and development, engineering and accountancy) are called consultants.

Arrangements

The most common model is the temporary employment agency (staffing agency), which recruits and employs workers and then assigns them to client companies to supplement their workforce for a fee. In this "triangular" relationship, the agency is usually the legal employer while the client directs the worker's day-to-day tasks. Assignments range from a single day to several years. Other forms include fixed-term contracts taken directly with an employer, seasonal agricultural and retail work, on-call and zero-hours arrangements, and platform-mediated gig work.

Scale

Temporary agency work is a global industry worth hundreds of billions of dollars, dominated internationally by firms such as Adecco, Randstad and ManpowerGroup. Agency workers make up roughly 1–3% of employment in most OECD countries, with higher shares in sectors such as manufacturing, logistics, hospitality, agriculture and health care (where agency nurses and locum physicians fill roster gaps).

Advantages and disadvantages

Employers use temporary labour to adjust staffing to fluctuating demand, screen candidates before permanent hiring ("temp-to-perm"), cover absences, and obtain specialist skills for short projects. For workers, temporary jobs can provide income, experience and a route into permanent employment, and some value the flexibility. Against this, temporary workers typically earn less than comparable permanent staff, receive fewer benefits, face greater job insecurity, and in many studies experience higher rates of workplace injury, partly through unfamiliarity with workplaces and shorter training. Trade unions and labour advocates argue that widespread use of temporary labour can undercut permanent terms and conditions, while industry groups argue it supports employment and labour-market flexibility.

Regulation

Many jurisdictions regulate temporary work. The European Union's Temporary Agency Work Directive (2008/104/EC) establishes the principle of equal treatment with comparable permanent workers from day one or after a qualifying period. In the United Kingdom, the Agency Workers Regulations 2010 implement similar rights after 12 weeks. Some countries license agencies, prohibit fees charged to workers, restrict the sectors or durations in which agency labour may be used, or apply joint liability to client firms. In the United States, temporary workers are covered by general employment law, and the National Labor Relations Board and Department of Labor have periodically revised rules on joint-employer status.
