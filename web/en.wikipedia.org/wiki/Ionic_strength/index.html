Ionic strength


Symbol: I
Units: mol·kg−1 (molal) or mol·L−1

The ionic strength of a solution is a measure of the concentration of ions in that solution. Ionic compounds, when dissolved in water, dissociate into ions; the total electrolyte concentration influences properties such as solubility and chemical reactivity. The concept was introduced by Gilbert N. Lewis and Merle Randall in 1921.

Definition

The (molal) ionic strength I is defined as half the sum over all ions of the concentration of each ion multiplied by the square of its charge: I = ½ Σ ci zi2. Because charge enters squared, multivalent ions contribute disproportionately: a solution's ionic strength is dominated by highly charged ions. For a 1:1 electrolyte such as NaCl at concentration c, I = c; for CaCl2 at concentration c, I = 3c.

Importance

Ionic strength governs activity coefficients through Debye–Hückel theory: as ionic strength rises, electrostatic shielding increases and activity coefficients fall below 1. It affects the solubility of salts, the rates of ionic reactions (the kinetic salt effect), the behaviour of colloids and buffers, and protein solubility — salting in and salting out. Seawater has an ionic strength of about 0.7 mol/kg, and blood plasma around 0.15 mol/L.
