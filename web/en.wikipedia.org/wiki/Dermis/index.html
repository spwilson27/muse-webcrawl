Dermis

The dermis or cutis is a layer of skin between the epidermis (with which it makes up the cutis) and subcutaneous tissues, that primarily consists of dense irregular connective tissue and cushions the body from stress and strain. It is divided into two layers: the superficial papillary region adjacent to the epidermis, and the deeper, thicker reticular region. The dermis is tightly connected to the epidermis through a basement membrane and houses hair follicles, sweat glands, sebaceous glands, blood vessels, lymphatic vessels, and sensory receptors.

Structure

Papillary dermis

The uppermost layer is composed of loose areolar connective tissue. Its dermal papillae project into the epidermis, increasing the surface area for exchange of oxygen and nutrients and forming the ridges responsible for fingerprints. It contains fine collagen fibres, capillary loops, and Meissner's corpuscles, which sense light touch.

Reticular dermis

The lower, much thicker layer consists of dense irregular connective tissue rich in coarse collagen and elastin fibres, giving skin its strength, extensibility, and elasticity. It contains the deep roots of hair follicles, sweat and sebaceous glands, Pacinian corpuscles sensing deep pressure and vibration, and much of the skin's vasculature. As skin ages, loss and disorganisation of dermal collagen and elastin produce wrinkles and sagging.

Functions

  • Mechanical protection — cushions underlying tissues.
  • Sensation — hosts mechanoreceptors and free nerve endings.
  • Thermoregulation — blood flow through dermal vessels and sweat production regulate heat loss.
  • Support — anchors and nourishes the avascular epidermis.

When animal dermis is treated and tanned, it becomes leather.
