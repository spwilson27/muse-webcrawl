Perpetual Peace: A Philosophical Sketch

Perpetual Peace: A Philosophical Sketch (German: Zum ewigen Frieden. Ein philosophischer Entwurf) is a 1795 essay by the German philosopher Immanuel Kant. One of the most influential works in the history of international relations theory, it proposes conditions under which war between states could be permanently abolished, anticipating ideas later embodied, at least in aspiration, in the League of Nations and the United Nations.

Contents

Kant casts the essay in the form of a peace treaty. Six preliminary articles prohibit practices incompatible with lasting peace: no secret reservations for future war in treaties; no acquisition of one state by another; the gradual abolition of standing armies; no national debts contracted for external conflict; no forcible interference in another state's constitution; and no wartime acts — assassination, poisoning, breach of capitulation, incitement to treason — that would destroy the mutual trust peace requires.

Three definitive articles set out the positive foundations. First, the civil constitution of every state should be republican — founded on freedom, a common legislation, and legal equality, with a separation of executive and legislative powers; since citizens who must fight and pay for wars will hesitate to declare them, republics tend toward peace. Second, the right of nations should be based on a federation of free states (foedus pacificum) — not a world state, which Kant feared might become a global despotism, but a league that states join to renounce war. Third, cosmopolitan right should establish universal hospitality: the right of a stranger not to be treated with hostility merely for arriving in another's land, which Kant used to condemn European colonial conquest in unusually sharp terms.

Influence

The essay's republican-peace argument is a principal ancestor of democratic peace theory, and its federation of free states influenced liberal internationalism from Woodrow Wilson onward. Kant's closing argument — that nature, through the self-interest of states and the expanding spirit of commerce, "guarantees" perpetual peace even against human moral frailty — has been both admired and criticised as providential. The work remains a touchstone in debates about world government, humanitarian intervention, and the moral limits of state sovereignty.
