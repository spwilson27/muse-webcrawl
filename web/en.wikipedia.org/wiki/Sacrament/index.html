Sacrament

The Eucharist, central sacrament of Catholic worship

A sacrament is a Christian rite that conveys divine grace, blessing, or sanctity through an outward and visible sign of an inward and spiritual reality. In Augustine of Hippo's classical formulation, a sacrament is "a visible sign of a sacred thing" — a sign of grace instituted by Christ and entrusted to the Church. Christian traditions differ significantly over which rites count as sacraments and how they work: the Catholic Church recognises seven, most Protestant churches recognise two (Baptism and the Lord's Supper), and some traditions, such as the Quakers and the Salvation Army, practise none.

The seven sacraments

The Roman Catholic Church, following the medieval theologians and the Council of Trent (1545–1563), enumerates seven sacraments: Baptism, Confirmation, the Eucharist, Penance (Reconciliation), the Anointing of the Sick, Holy Orders, and Matrimony. The first three are called sacraments of initiation; Baptism, Confirmation, and Holy Orders are held to imprint an indelible spiritual character and so cannot be repeated. Catholic theology teaches that the sacraments act ex opere operato — by the work performed — so that their validity does not depend on the moral worth of the minister, a position settled against the Donatists in late antiquity. Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox churches likewise celebrate seven "holy mysteries", while generally avoiding rigid enumeration.

Protestant understandings

The Reformers restricted the term "sacrament" to rites instituted by Christ with a visible sign and a promise of grace — Baptism and the Eucharist (with penance admitted by some early Lutherans). Martin Luther defended the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist; Huldrych Zwingli taught a memorialist view in which the Supper is chiefly a sign of remembrance and communal pledge; John Calvin held an intermediate doctrine of spiritual presence. Anglican formularies call Baptism and the Supper "sacraments of the Gospel" and the other five rites "commonly called sacraments" but not of the same nature. Baptist and many free-church traditions prefer the term "ordinances", viewing them as acts of obedience rather than means of grace.

History and theology

The Latin sacramentum originally denoted a soldier's oath of allegiance and a legal deposit; Christian writers applied it to translate the Greek mystērion. Medieval scholasticism, culminating in Thomas Aquinas, systematised sacramental theology around matter and form, sign and effect. The Council of Trent anathematised denials of the sevenfold number and of ex opere operato efficacy, definitions that remain points of division but also of ecumenical dialogue: the 1982 Lima text Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry of the World Council of Churches recorded substantial convergence on baptismal theology. Many sacramental churches also recognise "sacramentals" — blessings, holy water, the sign of the cross — as lesser sacred signs distinct from the sacraments proper.
