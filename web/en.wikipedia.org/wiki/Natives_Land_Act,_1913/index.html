Natives Land Act, 1913

Long titleAn Act for regulating the acquisition of Land within the Union of South Africa and matters incidental thereto

The Natives Land Act, 1913 (Act No. 27 of 1913) was an Act of the Parliament of South Africa that severely restricted the ability of black South Africans to own or occupy land. It prohibited Africans from purchasing or leasing land outside a small set of designated "scheduled areas", which initially amounted to only about 7 percent of the country's surface area, later expanded to roughly 13.5 percent by the Native Trust and Land Act of 1936.

Background

The Act was passed three years after the creation of the Union of South Africa in 1910. It was introduced by J. W. Sauer, the Minister of Native Affairs, and followed a long history of settler dispossession of indigenous peoples dating back to the seventeenth century. White farmers feared competition from successful black peasant producers, many of whom farmed as sharecroppers or tenants on white-owned land.

Provisions and effects

The Act made it illegal for Africans to purchase, lease or otherwise acquire land outside the scheduled reserves, and also prohibited many forms of sharecropping and labour tenancy, particularly in the Orange Free State. Hundreds of families were evicted from farms, and the measure is widely regarded as a foundational statute of segregation and a precursor of apartheid. The writer and journalist Sol Plaatje documented its devastating effects in his 1916 book Native Life in South Africa, writing that the black South African "awoke on Friday morning, June 20, 1913, but found himself, in truth, not really a slave, but a pariah in the land of his birth".

Opposition and legacy

The South African Native National Congress (later the African National Congress), founded in 1912 partly in response to such measures, sent a delegation to Britain in 1914 to appeal against the Act, but the British government declined to intervene. The Act remained in force until it was repealed by the Abolition of Racially Based Land Measures Act of 1991, and land reform remains a central political issue in post-apartheid South Africa.
