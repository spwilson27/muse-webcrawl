Carbon monoxide

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a colourless, odourless, highly toxic gas formed by incomplete combustion of carbon-containing fuels. It is also a valuable industrial gas and a strong ligand in chemistry.

CO binds to haemoglobin about 200–250 times more strongly than oxygen, forming carboxyhaemoglobin and starving tissues of oxygen — poisoning from faulty heaters, car exhausts and fires causes headache, confusion and death without warning smell, hence household CO alarms. Paradoxically, the body makes tiny amounts of CO as a signalling molecule.

[Image: A carbon monoxide alarm on a wall beside a gas boiler]

Chemistry and uses

Carbon monoxide results whenever carbon burns short of oxygen (2C + O₂ → 2CO) and is interconverted with CO₂ in the water-gas shift reaction. Industrially it feeds methanol, acetic acid and phosgene manufacture and the Mond process for nickel refining; in metallurgy it reduces iron ore in the blast furnace.

Detection

Modern alarms use electrochemical sensors; firefighters and miners historically carried canaries and later electronic monitors. Blood carboxyhaemoglobin is the diagnostic measure.
