Universalism


Universalism holds that certain truths or values apply to all people.

Universalism is the philosophical and theological concept that certain ideas, values, or truths have universal application or applicability. In ethics, universalism holds that moral principles apply to all persons regardless of culture, race, religion, or nationality, and stands opposed to moral relativism. In theology, the term most often refers to the doctrine that all human beings will ultimately be saved and reconciled with God.

In religion

Christian universalism teaches that God's love and the atonement of Christ extend to all people, so that hell, if it exists, is corrective and temporary rather than eternal — a view associated historically with the Greek church father Origen's doctrine of apokatastasis. In America, Universalism became an organized denomination: the Universalist Church of America, founded in the late 18th century with figures such as John Murray and Hosea Ballou, preached universal salvation. In 1961 it merged with the American Unitarian Association to form the Unitarian Universalist Association.

Universalist beliefs also appear in other traditions: Baháʼí teaching stresses the oneness of humanity and religion; Sikh and Hindu traditions contain strong universalist strands; and scholars describe forms of universalism in Buddhism and in the salvation-of-all tendencies within Islamic theology.

In philosophy and politics

Philosophical universalists hold that universal facts exist and can be progressively discovered, as opposed to relativist positions. Political universalism underlies the idea of universal human rights, codified in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), which asserts rights belonging to every person by virtue of being human.
