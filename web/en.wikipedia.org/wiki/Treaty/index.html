Treaty

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A treaty is a formal, legally binding written agreement between actors in international law, normally sovereign states and international organisations. Treaties are one of the principal sources of international law, governing matters from peace settlements and alliances to trade, the environment, human rights and the law of the sea. They are also called agreements, protocols, conventions, covenants or exchanges of letters, without legal significance attaching to the name.

Formation and effect

Treaties are negotiated by authorised representatives, adopted and signed, and normally enter into force upon ratification according to each party's constitutional procedures (often involving legislatures). The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969) codifies the rules: pacta sunt servanda (agreements must be kept), interpretation in good faith in light of object and purpose, grounds of invalidity, reservations, and the limited circumstances for termination or withdrawal. Some treaties create institutions (the UN Charter, the Rome Statute, trade agreements) that generate continuing governance.

Scale

Tens of thousands of treaties are in force; the UN Secretary-General is depositary for more than 560 multilateral treaties. Compliance and enforcement rely largely on reciprocity, reputation and institutional mechanisms rather than central coercion — the subject of a large literature on why states keep, and break, their word.
