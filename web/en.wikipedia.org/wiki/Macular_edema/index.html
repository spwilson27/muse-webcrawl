Macular edema

Macular edema occurs when fluid and protein deposits collect on or under the macula of the eye — the central area of the retina responsible for sharp, detailed central vision — causing it to thicken and swell. The swelling may distort central vision, making straight lines appear wavy and reading difficult. Macular edema is not a disease itself but a common complication of several retinal conditions.

Causes

Macular edema results from breakdown of the blood–retinal barrier, allowing fluid to leak from retinal capillaries into the macula. The most common cause is diabetic retinopathy, in which case it is termed diabetic macular edema (DME) — a leading cause of vision loss in working-age adults. Other causes include retinal vein occlusion, intraocular inflammation (uveitis), age-related macular degeneration, and complications of cataract surgery, known as Irvine–Gass syndrome when following surgery.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually made by optical coherence tomography (OCT), a non-invasive imaging technique that measures retinal thickness and reveals cystic fluid spaces, supplemented where needed by fluorescein angiography to localise leakage. When fluid collects in cyst-like spaces it is described as cystoid macular edema.

Treatment

Treatment addresses the underlying cause and the leakage itself. Intravitreal injections of anti-VEGF agents such as ranibizumab, aflibercept and bevacizumab are first-line therapy for diabetic macular edema and edema due to vein occlusion. Corticosteroid injections or implants are used particularly in inflammatory and post-surgical cases; focal laser photocoagulation, once the mainstay, is now used more selectively.
