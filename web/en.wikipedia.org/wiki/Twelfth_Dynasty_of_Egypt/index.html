The Twelfth Dynasty of ancient Egypt (c. 1985–1773 BC) is often considered the apex of the Middle Kingdom. Founded by Amenemhat I, it ruled for roughly two centuries and is remembered for strong central government, extensive building, expansion into Nubia, and a flowering of literature and art regarded as classical by later Egyptians.

History

Amenemhat I, probably the vizier of the last Eleventh Dynasty king, seized the throne and moved the capital from Thebes to a new city, Itjtawy, near the Faiyum. His son Senusret I consolidated the kingdom, campaigned in Nubia and began large building projects, including the White Chapel at Karnak. The dynasty reached its height under Senusret III, a formidable warrior-king who annexed Nubia with a chain of massive fortresses, reorganised the administration by abolishing the hereditary provincial governorships, and was later deified there. His son Amenemhat III reigned nearly half a century, developing the Faiyum with irrigation works associated with Lake Moeris and building pyramids at Dahshur and Hawara — the latter attached to the vast mortuary complex Herodotus called the Labyrinth.

Kings

The dynasty's rulers were Amenemhat I, Senusret I, Amenemhat II, Senusret II, Senusret III, Amenemhat III, Amenemhat IV, and finally Sobekneferu, one of the few confirmed female pharaohs of Egyptian history, whose short reign ended the dynasty. Many kings ruled for a period as co-regents with their successors, a practice the dynasty pioneered to secure succession.

Culture

Twelfth Dynasty literature includes works still famous today: the Tale of Sinuhe, the Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor, the Instructions of Amenemhat and the Dispute Between a Man and His Ba. Royal portraiture under Senusret III broke with idealised tradition, showing the king aged, careworn and heavy-lidded — images of burdensome kingship unmatched elsewhere in Egyptian art.
