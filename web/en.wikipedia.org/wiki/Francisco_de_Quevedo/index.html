Francisco Gómez de Quevedo Villegas y Santibáñez Cevallos (14 September 1580 – 8 September 1645), known simply as Francisco de Quevedo, was a Spanish nobleman, politician and writer of the Baroque period. Together with his lifelong rival Luis de Góngora, he is regarded as one of the two supreme poets of the Spanish Golden Age. He was the master of conceptismo, a style built on dense wordplay, metaphor and wit, in deliberate contrast to Góngora's ornate culteranismo.

Quevedo, famously depicted wearing his round spectacles, the quevedos named after him.

Life

Quevedo was born in Madrid into a family of minor nobility serving the royal court. Educated at the Jesuit Imperial School and the University of Alcalá, he mastered theology, philosophy and several languages. He became secretary to Pedro Téllez-Girón, Duke of Osuna, following him to Sicily and Naples, and undertook secret missions on his behalf. When Osuna fell from favour, Quevedo's own fortunes suffered. His sharp political writings eventually brought him imprisonment in the monastery of San Marcos de León (1639–1643), which ruined his health; he died shortly after his release.

Works

Quevedo's prose masterpiece is the picaresque novel La vida del Buscón llamado don Pablos (1626), a savagely comic account of a swindler's rise. His Sueños (Dreams, written 1606–1622) are satirical visions of hell and judgement populated by corrupt judges, doctors, poets and officials. His poetry ranges from ferocious personal satires against Góngora to some of the most celebrated metaphysical and love sonnets in Spanish, meditating on death, time and the brevity of life — as in his famous line "polvo serán, mas polvo enamorado" ("they shall be dust, but dust in love").

Legacy

Quevedo's pince-nez spectacles became so associated with him that in Spanish such glasses are still called quevedos. His works, many circulated in manuscript and published posthumously, have kept him at the centre of the Spanish canon ever since.
