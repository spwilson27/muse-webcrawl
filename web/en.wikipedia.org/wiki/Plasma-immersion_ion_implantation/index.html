Plasma-immersion ion implantation

A plasma-immersion ion implantation system

Plasma-immersion ion implantation (PIII), also called pulsed plasma doping, is a surface modification technique in which a workpiece is immersed in a plasma and pulsed to a high negative voltage, causing positive ions from the plasma to be accelerated across the sheath and implanted into the surface. Unlike conventional beamline ion implantation, PIII does not require ion mass separation or a line-of-sight beam, so it can treat complex three-dimensional shapes and large areas in a single process.

Principle

In a typical PIII system, the component to be treated is mounted on a stage inside a vacuum chamber filled with a low-pressure process gas. A plasma is generated, often by radio-frequency or microwave discharge, surrounding the part. When short pulses of high negative voltage (typically 1–100 kV, lasting microseconds) are applied to the stage, electrons are repelled and a temporary ion sheath forms around the workpiece. Ions are accelerated across this sheath and strike the surface from all directions, embedding themselves a few tens of nanometres deep.

Applications

PIII is used to improve wear resistance, corrosion resistance and fatigue life of metals, to dope semiconductors, and to modify biomedical implants such as orthopaedic prostheses. Nitrogen implantation of stainless steels and titanium alloys is a common industrial use, hardening the surface while preserving bulk properties. Related processes include plasma-source ion implantation (PSII), developed in the late 1980s by Conrad and co-workers at the University of Wisconsin.

Advantages and limitations

The technique's main advantages are its ability to treat non-planar parts uniformly, high throughput, and relatively simple equipment. Its limitations include a lack of mass selectivity, retained dose uncertainty, heating of the workpiece during long treatments, and shallow implantation depth compared with MeV beamline systems.
