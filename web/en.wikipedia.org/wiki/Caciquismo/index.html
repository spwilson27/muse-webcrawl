Caciquismo

ConceptPolitical clientelism
RegionsSpain, Latin America
Key figureCacique (local boss)

Caciquismo is a system of political domination exercised through local bosses known as caciques, who control votes, resources, and access to government in their communities. The term is used principally in Spain and Latin America to describe clientelist networks that distort formal democratic processes.

In anthropology, 'cacique' derives from the Taíno word for the chiefs of Caribbean peoples, extended under Spanish colonial rule to indigenous leaders and later to any local strongman.

Spain

In Restoration Spain (1874–1931), caciquismo was the operating system of politics: local bosses delivered bloc votes through the turno pacífico, in which Conservative and Liberal parties alternated in power by arrangement, using fraud, patronage, and coercion managed by caciques and civil governors. The regenerationist writer Joaquín Costa denounced 'oligarchy and caciquismo' as Spain's true form of government.

Latin America

In Latin America, caciques — often landowners or merchants — mediated between rural communities and the state, exchanging protection and favours for loyalty. Caciquismo has persisted in various forms in rural Mexico, the Philippines (under Spanish and later influence), and elsewhere, adapting to democratisation.

Analysis

Scholars treat caciquismo as a form of clientelism in which formal institutions coexist with informal power. Its decline is associated with urbanisation, mass media, secret ballots, and programmatic political parties.
