Maniraptora is a clade of coelurosaurian theropod dinosaurs that includes birds and the non-avian dinosaurs most closely related to them. The name, coined by Jacques Gauthier in 1986, means "hand snatchers," referring to the group's characteristic grasping hands with long fingers and large, curved claws. Maniraptorans first appeared in the Jurassic period and are the only dinosaur lineage to survive the Cretaceous–Palaeogene extinction event, in the form of birds.

Characteristics

Defining features include elongated arms and three-fingered hands, a half-moon-shaped (semi-lunate) carpal bone in the wrist that allowed the hand to fold sideways — a motion later used in the bird flight stroke — and a backwards-pointing pubis in many lineages. Feathers are widespread in the group: filamentous down and true pennaceous feathers are documented in dromaeosaurids, troodontids, oviraptorosaurs, and therizinosaurs. Many maniraptorans were small (under a metre long), and several groups evolved enlarged brains, binocular vision, brooding behaviour, and in some cases flight.

Classification

Maniraptora is generally divided into the basal Therizinosauria and Alvarezsauria, and the Pennaraptora, which unites Oviraptorosauria with Paraves (Dromaeosauridae, Troodontidae, and Avialae). Anchiornithids and scansoriopterygids sit near the base of Paraves. The exact placement of several groups remains debated as new fossils — especially from the feathered-dinosaur Lagerstätten of Liaoning, China — continue to appear.

Evolution of flight

Because birds are maniraptorans, the clade is central to the study of flight origins. Experiments and fossils of four-winged forms such as Microraptor suggest gliding or powered flight evolved among small, tree- or ground-dwelling paravians in the Late Jurassic, culminating in the avialan lineage that produced modern birds.
