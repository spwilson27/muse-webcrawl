Ismailism (Arabic: الإسماعيلية, al-Ismāʿīliyya) is a branch of Shia Islam whose adherents, the Ismailis, accept Isma'il ibn Ja'far as the designated successor (imam) of his father Ja'far al-Sadiq, in contrast to the Twelvers, who recognise his younger brother Musa al-Kazim. Ismailis form the second-largest Shia community, with distinctive doctrines of esoteric interpretation (ta'wil) and the authoritative teaching of the living imam.

History

The movement spread through a clandestine missionary network (da'wa) in the 9th–10th centuries; its Qarmatian wing established a state in eastern Arabia, while the Fatimid branch conquered Egypt (969), founded Cairo and ruled an empire that at its height stretched from the Maghreb to Syria — an era remembered for institutions such as al-Azhar mosque-university. The succession crisis after the Fatimid caliph al-Mustansir (1094) split Ismailism into Nizari and Musta'li branches. The Nizaris of Alamut in Persia became famous (and feared, as the "Assassins" of Crusader-era legend) until the Mongol destruction of Alamut in 1256. Musta'li Islam survived principally through the Bohra communities of Yemen and Gujarat.

Branches today

The largest community, the Nizari Ismailis, follows the Aga Khan as hereditary imam and is organised through a transnational network of institutions (the Aga Khan Development Network) with communities in Central and South Asia, East Africa, Europe and North America. Musta'li Bohras — Dawoodi, Sulaymani and Alavi — are led by a chief missionary (da'i al-mutlaq) in the imam's concealment. The Druze faith emerged historically from an 11th-century Fatimid-era movement but is now generally regarded as a separate religion.
