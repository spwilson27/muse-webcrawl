Charles Francis Richter (April 26, 1900 – September 30, 1985) was an American seismologist and physicist, most famous as the inventor of the Richter magnitude scale, which, until the development of the moment magnitude scale, quantified the size of earthquakes. He spent most of his career at the California Institute of Technology in Pasadena.

Charles Richter (1900–1985), inventor of the Richter scale

Early life and education

Richter was born in Ohio and moved with his mother to Los Angeles in 1909. He studied physics at Stanford University, graduating in 1920, and began doctoral work at Caltech. Before finishing his thesis he took a post in 1927 at the Carnegie Institution's seismology laboratory in Pasadena, where he came under the influence of Beno Gutenberg; he completed his PhD in theoretical physics at Caltech in 1928.

The magnitude scale

Working with Gutenberg, Richter developed his local magnitude scale in 1935 to measure Southern California earthquakes using Wood–Anderson seismographs: magnitude is defined from the logarithm of the maximum amplitude recorded, corrected for distance. Richter and Gutenberg also established the Gutenberg–Richter law relating earthquake frequency to magnitude, and collaborated on the landmark treatise Seismicity of the Earth (1941, revised 1954). Richter joined the Caltech faculty in 1937 and became professor of seismology in 1952.

Later life

Richter was a prominent public voice on earthquake preparedness in California and an opponent of exaggerated earthquake prediction claims. He was also a noted naturist, an amateur poet, and an Esperanto enthusiast. He died in Pasadena in 1985. His name remains a byword for earthquake measurement in popular usage, even though seismologists now prefer moment magnitude.
