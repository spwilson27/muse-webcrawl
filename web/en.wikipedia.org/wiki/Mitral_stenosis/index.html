Mitral stenosis

Mitral stenosis
SpecialtyCardiology
SymptomsExertional dyspnoea, orthopnoea, haemoptysis, fatigue
CausesRheumatic heart disease (most common), congenital, annular calcification

Mitral stenosis is a valvular heart disease characterised by narrowing of the orifice of the mitral valve of the heart. The normal mitral valve area is 4–6 cm²; symptoms usually appear when it falls below about 2 cm², and severe stenosis is generally defined as an area under 1.0–1.5 cm². The obstruction impedes blood flow from the left atrium to the left ventricle during diastole, raising left atrial pressure and, in turn, pulmonary venous pressure.

Causes and pathophysiology

Almost all cases in adults result from rheumatic fever, whose scarring fuses the commissures, thickens and calcifies the leaflets and shortens the chordae, producing the classic "fish-mouth" valve. Rarer causes include congenital stenosis, severe mitral annular calcification in the elderly, carcinoid disease and connective-tissue disorders. Raised pulmonary pressures lead to breathlessness, orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, haemoptysis and eventually right heart failure; the chronically enlarged left atrium predisposes to atrial fibrillation and systemic embolism.

Diagnosis and treatment

Classic examination findings are a loud first heart sound, an opening snap, and a low-pitched mid-diastolic rumble with presystolic accentuation. Echocardiography confirms severity and valve morphology. Medical therapy with diuretics and heart-rate control relieves congestion; patients with suitable anatomy are treated by percutaneous balloon mitral valvuloplasty, and others by surgical commissurotomy or mitral valve replacement. Anticoagulation is required when atrial fibrillation or atrial thrombus is present.
