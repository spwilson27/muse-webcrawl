The Global System for Mobile Communications (GSM) is a standard developed by the European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI) to describe protocols for second-generation (2G) digital cellular networks used by mobile devices such as mobile phones and tablets. It was first deployed in Finland in July 1991 and grew into the most widely used mobile telephony system in history, at its peak serving over 90% of the global mobile market and more than five billion subscribers. As of the 2020s, many operators are shutting down GSM networks to refarm spectrum for 4G and 5G.

The acronym originally stood for Groupe Spécial Mobile, the study group created by the Conférence Européenne des Postes et Télécommunications (CEPT) in 1982 to design a pan-European mobile system.

History

In 1982, CEPT established the Groupe Spécial Mobile to specify a single digital cellular standard for Europe, replacing the patchwork of incompatible analogue systems such as NMT, TACS, and C-Netz. In 1987, thirteen operators and administrations signed a memorandum of understanding in Copenhagen committing to deploy GSM by 1991, and the first technical specifications were published in 1990. Responsibility passed to ETSI in 1989.

The world's first GSM network was launched by Radiolinja in Finland on 1 July 1991; the first GSM call was made by former Finnish prime minister Harri Holkeri. Commercial services spread rapidly through Europe and then worldwide. In 1998 the 3rd Generation Partnership Project (3GPP) was formed, taking over further development of GSM and its successors.

Technical details

GSM is a digital system using a combination of time-division and frequency-division multiple access. Carriers 200 kHz wide are each divided into eight time slots, and speech is encoded with digital codecs (originally the 13 kbit/s full-rate codec). GSM networks operate in various frequency bands, principally 900 MHz and 1800 MHz in Europe and Asia, and 850 MHz and 1900 MHz in the Americas.

A defining feature is the Subscriber Identity Module (SIM card), which stores the subscriber's identity (IMSI) and authentication key, allowing users to move their subscription between handsets and roam internationally. GSM also standardized the Short Message Service (SMS), originally a signalling-channel afterthought that became a huge cultural and commercial phenomenon, and provided circuit-switched data services.

Later enhancements added packet data: GPRS ("2.5G") and EDGE ("2.75G") increased data rates while remaining within the GSM framework.

Network architecture

A GSM network consists of the mobile station (handset and SIM), the base station subsystem (base transceiver stations and their controllers), and the network and switching subsystem, centred on the Mobile Switching Center (MSC) with its Home Location Register (HLR) and Visitor Location Register (VLR). Authentication uses the A3/A8 algorithms with a secret key held on the SIM and at the operator's authentication centre; air-interface encryption historically used the A5 family of ciphers, whose weaknesses have been extensively documented.

Legacy

GSM made mobile telephony a mass-market, interoperable, international service, and its notions of roaming, SIM-based identity, and text messaging shaped all subsequent generations. Its successors within the 3GPP family — UMTS (3G), LTE (4G), and 5G NR — have largely superseded it, but GSM persisted for machine-to-machine and fallback use long after, and remains in service in parts of the world.
