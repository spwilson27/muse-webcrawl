Abstract expressionism

Abstract expressionism is a post–World War II art movement in American painting, developed in New York City in the 1940s. Characterised by large-scale canvases, spontaneity, and an emphasis on the physical act of painting and the expression of the artist's inner state rather than the depiction of objects, it was the first specifically American movement to achieve international influence and shifted the centre of the Western art world from Paris to New York.

Development

The movement coalesced among a circle of artists — many influenced by European émigrés, Surrealist automatism, Mexican muralism and Jungian and mythic imagery — who exhibited in New York in the 1940s and came to be called the New York School. Clement Greenberg became its most influential critical champion, while Harold Rosenberg coined the term "action painting" in 1952 for the gestural wing of the movement.

Styles

Two broad tendencies are distinguished. Action painting, exemplified by Jackson Pollock's poured and dripped canvases (made with the canvas on the floor) and Willem de Kooning's slashing brushwork, treats the canvas as a record of the artist's physical gesture. Color Field painting, exemplified by Mark Rothko's hovering rectangles of colour, Barnett Newman's "zip" paintings, and Clyfford Still's jagged expanses, seeks contemplative or sublime effects through large areas of colour.

Reception and legacy

Major exhibitions, including the Museum of Modern Art's touring "The New American Painting" (1958–59), established the movement's dominance in Europe. Its promotion during the Cold War has been the subject of historical debate about cultural diplomacy. Abstract expressionism in turn provoked reactions including Pop art, Minimalism and post-painterly abstraction, but its emphasis on scale, process and individual expression remains foundational to modern art history. Pollock's No. 5, 1948 and de Kooning's Interchange have ranked among the most valuable paintings ever sold.
