Hylomorphism

Doctrine ofAristotle; later scholasticism
Key termsMatter (hylē), form (morphē / eidos), substance, act and potency
Principal textsPhysics, Metaphysics, De Anima

Hylomorphism (from Greek hylē, "wood" or "matter", and morphē, "form") is a philosophical doctrine developed by Aristotle which holds that every physical substance is a compound of two principles: matter and form. Matter is the indeterminate substrate that receives form; form is the organizing structure that makes a thing what it is. A bronze statue, for example, is bronze (matter) informed by the shape imposed by the sculptor (form).

Aristotle's theory

Aristotle developed the theory in the Physics and Metaphysics to explain change: in any change, something persists (the matter) while a form is gained or lost. He distinguished the form in the substance — the substantial form, which determines a thing's essence — from accidental forms, such as colour or size. Matter and form correlate with his further distinction between potency and act: matter is potentiality, and form is actuality. "Prime matter" is pure potentiality without any form of its own, inferred as the ultimate substrate of substantial change.

Aristotle applied the analysis to living beings in De Anima: the soul is the substantial form of a living body, its "first actuality," so that a living organism is a single substance rather than two things joined together.

Medieval development

Hylomorphism became a cornerstone of scholastic philosophy. Thomas Aquinas used it to explain the unity of the human person — soul as the form of the body — and the doctrine of transubstantiation. Medieval thinkers debated whether there is one substantial form in a substance or several (the plurality-of-forms controversy, associated with critics of Aquinas such as John Peckham and Duns Scotus), and whether matter could exist without form.

Modern discussions

With the rise of mechanistic natural philosophy in the 17th century, substantial forms fell out of favour in science. In contemporary metaphysics, however, hylomorphism has been revived by some philosophers of mind and ontologists as a middle way between dualism and reductive physicalism, and neo-Aristotelian accounts of structure and powers draw on its distinctions.
