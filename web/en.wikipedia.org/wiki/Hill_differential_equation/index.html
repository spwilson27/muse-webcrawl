Hill differential equation

In mathematics, the Hill differential equation (or Hill's equation) is a second-order linear ordinary differential equation with periodic coefficients, introduced by the astronomer George William Hill in his 1877–78 work on lunar theory, where he used it to describe the motion of the Moon's perigee.

d2w/dz2 + [ θ0 + 2 Σn≥1 θn cos(2nz) ] w = 0

Here the coefficients θn are constants and the bracketed term is a periodic function of z with period π. Because the coefficient is periodic rather than constant, the equation's solutions are not simple exponentials or sinusoids.

Special cases

When only one harmonic is retained, the equation reduces to the Mathieu equation, which governs among other things the stability of an inverted pendulum with a vibrating pivot and the behaviour of ions in a quadrupole trap. The Whittaker–Hill equation adds a further term proportional to cos(4z) and arises in the analysis of elliptical membranes.

Stability and Floquet theory

The central question for Hill's equation is whether its solutions remain bounded. By Floquet theory, solutions take the form of an exponential factor times a periodic function, and the parameter space divides into alternating stable regions (bounded, quasi-periodic solutions) and unstable regions (exponentially growing solutions). Hill himself developed an infinite-determinant method — the Hill determinant — to compute the characteristic exponent, a technique later placed on a rigorous footing by Poincaré and others. The same stability charts appear in accelerator physics, in the theory of parametric resonance, and in the band theory of solids, where Hill's equation models an electron in a periodic potential.
