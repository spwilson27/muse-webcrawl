Mesoamerican ballgame

Names: ōllamaliztli (Nahuatl), pitz (Maya)
Earliest courts: c. 1400 BCE (Paso de la Amada)
Sites: 1,300+ known ballcourts

The Mesoamerican ballgame was a sporting and ritual game played for over 3,000 years by the peoples of pre-Columbian Mesoamerica, on stone ballcourts found from Arizona to Nicaragua.

Play

Versions varied, but commonly two teams propelled a heavy solid rubber ball — using hips, and in some versions forearms or rackets — to keep it in play and, in later Maya and Aztec versions, to pass it through a high vertical stone ring for an instant win. The ball could weigh several kilograms, and players wore thick protective belts (yokes) and padding; injuries were common.

Ritual significance

The game was deeply religious: in the Maya Popol Vuh, the Hero Twins play ball against the lords of death in Xibalba, and ballcourts were seen as portals to the underworld. Association with human sacrifice — including decapitation imagery on court panels, as at the Great Ballcourt of Chichen Itza — is debated in extent but well attested iconographically.

Survival

Descendant games survive: ulama in Sinaloa, Mexico, and pelota mixteca in Oaxaca. See Chichen Itza and Popol Vuh.
