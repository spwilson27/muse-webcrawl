Referendum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A referendum is a direct vote by the electorate on a proposal, law or political question. Referendums may be mandatory (constitutionally required, e.g. for constitutional amendments in Ireland or Australia), called by governments at their discretion, or triggered by citizens through initiatives. They are a principal instrument of Direct democracy within otherwise representative systems.

Use and examples

Switzerland holds frequent referendums at federal, cantonal and communal levels; Italy uses abrogative referendums; many states have decided major questions by referendum — EU membership accessions and withdrawals (the United Kingdom's 2016 referendum on EU membership ("Brexit")), independence (Quebec 1980/1995, Scotland 2014, South Sudan 2011), constitutional change and moral legislation (Ireland's marriage-equality 2015 and abortion 2018 votes). Design questions — question wording, thresholds, double majorities, campaign finance and information — strongly affect outcomes and legitimacy.

Debate

Supporters see referendums as the purest expression of popular sovereignty and a check on unresponsive elites. Critics object that binary votes simplify complex issues, are vulnerable to campaign effects and misinformation, threaten minority rights, and let governments evade responsibility — or conversely bind successors irresponsibly. Political science studies "second-order" voting (using referendums to punish governments) and the conditions under which referendum decisions are accepted as settled.
