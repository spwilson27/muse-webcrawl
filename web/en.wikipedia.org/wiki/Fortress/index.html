Fortress

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A fortress is a military fortification, typically a large stronghold designed to be defended by a garrison and to withstand siege. Fortresses are distinguished from smaller forts and field fortifications by their size, permanence and capacity to protect a strategic place — a city, harbour, mountain pass or frontier line — over the long term. The term derives from the Latin fortis, "strong", via Old French forteresse.

History

Fortified strongholds are as old as warfare: Bronze Age citadels, Roman legionary fortresses and the Byzantine and Islamic frontier forts guarded empires for millennia. Medieval castles served as fortresses, administrative centres and noble residences combined. The advent of gunpowder artillery in the fifteenth century rendered high curtain walls vulnerable and produced the low, thick, angular trace italienne or star fort, refined by engineers such as Vauban into systems of bastions, ravelins and glacis. Vauban built or rebuilt some 300 fortresses for Louis XIV and created an iron frontier for France.

In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, rifled artillery and high explosive again transformed fortress design: detached forts of concrete and steel, such as those around Liège, Verdun and Metz, replaced continuous walls, and fortress lines — the Maginot Line being the most famous — sought to defend whole frontiers. The fall of Fort Eben-Emael in 1940 to glider-borne engineers demonstrated the vulnerability of even the strongest works to new methods, and the fixed fortress largely vanished from warfare after 1945.
