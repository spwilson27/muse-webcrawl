The murder of the Romanov family was the execution by Bolshevik revolutionaries of the deposed Russian imperial family on the night of 16–17 July 1918 in Yekaterinburg. The victims were the former Tsar Nicholas II, his wife Alexandra, their five children — Olga, Tatiana, Maria, Anastasia and the Tsarevich Alexei — and four members of their household: physician Eugene Botkin, lady-in-waiting Anna Demidova, footman Alexei Trupp and cook Ivan Kharitonov.

After Nicholas's abdication in March 1917, the family had been held under house arrest, first at Tsarskoye Selo, then at Tobolsk in Siberia, and finally from April–May 1918 at the Ipatiev House in Yekaterinburg, dubbed the "House of Special Purpose". As White forces of the Czechoslovak Legion approached the city during the Russian Civil War, the Ural Regional Soviet, acting with the sanction of the central leadership in Moscow, ordered the execution. In the early hours of 17 July the family was woken, told they were being moved for their safety, and assembled in a basement room, where a firing squad led by Yakov Yurovsky shot them; those who survived the shooting were finished off with bayonets and gunshots.

Disposal and discovery of the remains

The bodies were stripped, mutilated with acid and buried in a mineshaft at Ganina Yama, then moved to a concealed grave at Porosenkov Log. In 1979 amateur investigator Alexander Avdonin located the grave; the remains of nine victims were exhumed in 1991 and identified by DNA testing, then reburied in the Peter and Paul Cathedral in Saint Petersburg in 1998. The remains of Alexei and one of his sisters, found nearby in 2007, were confirmed by DNA analysis in 2008–2009.

Canonisation and legacy

The Russian Orthodox Church canonised Nicholas and his family as passion bearers in 2000; the Church on the Blood in Yekaterinburg now stands on the site of the Ipatiev House, which was demolished in 1977. The survival claims of various impostors, most famously Anna Anderson, were conclusively disproved by the DNA identifications.
