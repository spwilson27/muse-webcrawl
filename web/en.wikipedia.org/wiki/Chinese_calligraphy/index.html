Chinese calligraphy

Chinese calligraphy is the writing of Chinese characters as an art form, combining purely visual art and interpretation of the literary meaning. It is widely practised in China and revered in the Sinosphere generally; calligraphy is one of the four traditional arts of the Chinese scholar, alongside the guqin, the game of Go, and painting. In 2009, Chinese calligraphy was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Scripts

Five major script styles are recognised. Seal script (篆書) descends from oracle bone and bronze inscriptions and was standardised under the Qin dynasty. Clerical script (隸書) emerged in the Han dynasty with flatter, more rectilinear strokes. Regular script (楷書), still the standard for print and formal writing, matured in the late Han and Wei. The freer semi-cursive script (行書) and the highly abbreviated, expressive cursive script (草書) privilege speed and personality over legibility.

Tools and technique

The "Four Treasures of the Study" are the brush, ink stick, paper and inkstone. The calligrapher grinds ink with water on the inkstone, loads a soft animal-hair brush, and writes with controlled pressure, speed and lift, following a fixed stroke order. Mastery is judged by the vitality and balance of strokes, the structure of characters and the composition of the whole; students traditionally learn by copying model works (linmo) of past masters.

History and masters

Wang Xizhi of the Eastern Jin dynasty is honoured as the Sage of Calligraphy; his Preface to the Orchid Pavilion Collection (353 CE) is the most celebrated work in the art's history. Other towering figures include Yan Zhenqing and Liu Gongquan of the Tang, and Su Shi, Huang Tingjian and Mi Fu of the Song. Calligraphy shaped East Asian aesthetics broadly, influencing Japanese shodō and Korean seoye.
