Excessive daytime sleepiness

Excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) is a symptom characterised by persistent sleepiness and a general lack of energy during the day, even after apparently adequate or prolonged nighttime sleep. People with EDS may fall asleep involuntarily during quiet activities such as reading, watching television or meetings, and in severe cases while talking, eating or driving. EDS is sometimes called hypersomnolence, though that term also denotes the class of sleep disorders in which EDS is the main complaint.

Causes

EDS most commonly results from insufficient or fragmented sleep. Major causes include:

  • Obstructive sleep apnea — repeated upper-airway obstruction fragments sleep and is a leading medical cause of EDS.
  • Chronic sleep deprivation and irregular schedules, including shift work sleep disorder and circadian rhythm disorders.
  • Narcolepsy — a neurological disorder of sleep–wake regulation causing irresistible sleep episodes, sometimes with cataplexy.
  • Idiopathic hypersomnia — excessive sleep and severe sleep inertia despite long, unrefreshing sleep.
  • Restless legs syndrome and periodic limb movement disorder.
  • Medical and psychiatric conditions — hypothyroidism, depression, chronic pain, Parkinson's disease, and others.
  • Medications and substances — sedating drugs, antihistamines, some antidepressants, and alcohol.

Consequences

EDS impairs attention, memory, learning and mood, reduces quality of life and productivity, and substantially increases the risk of accidents — drowsy driving is implicated in a significant share of serious road crashes. It is associated with obesity, cardiovascular disease and diabetes, both as a symptom and possibly as a contributing factor.

Diagnosis

Assessment begins with a sleep history and screening questionnaires such as the Epworth Sleepiness Scale, in which patients rate their likelihood of dozing in eight everyday situations. Objective evaluation may include polysomnography (an overnight sleep study) followed by a Multiple Sleep Latency Test (MSLT), which measures how quickly a person falls asleep during daytime nap opportunities; a mean sleep latency of eight minutes or less indicates objective excessive sleepiness. Maintenance of Wakefulness Testing and actigraphy are also used.

Treatment

Treatment targets the underlying cause: continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) for sleep apnea, sleep hygiene and schedule correction for insufficient sleep, and bright-light therapy or melatonin for circadian disorders. Wake-promoting medications such as modafinil, armodafinil, solriamfetol and pitolisant, and traditional stimulants, are used in narcolepsy and idiopathic hypersomnia, along with sodium oxybate for narcolepsy.
