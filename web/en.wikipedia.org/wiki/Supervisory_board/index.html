Supervisory board

A supervisory board (in German, Aufsichtsrat) is a group of individuals chosen by shareholders to oversee the management of a corporation in a two-tier board system of corporate governance. In this model, the supervisory board appoints, supervises, and advises a separate management board (Vorstand) that runs the company day to day; members of one board may not simultaneously sit on the other.

Two-tier governance

The two-tier structure is characteristic of Germany and is also found in countries including Austria, the Netherlands, Poland, and (in modified or optional forms) France and China. It contrasts with the one-tier (unitary) board common in the United Kingdom and United States, where executive and non-executive directors sit on a single board. Advocates argue that the formal separation of management and supervision reduces conflicts of interest; critics counter that supervisory boards may lack information and engagement, and meet too infrequently to exercise real control.

Functions

The supervisory board typically appoints and dismisses management board members, sets their remuneration, approves major transactions and the annual financial statements, and oversees strategy and risk management. It does not itself manage the company, and its members owe duties of care and loyalty to the enterprise.

Codetermination

In Germany, the supervisory board is the institutional seat of employee codetermination (Mitbestimmung): under legislation of 1951, 1952, and 1976, employees elect one-third to one-half of supervisory board seats in larger companies, with shareholder representatives holding the remaining seats and, in parity systems, the chair's casting vote. This arrangement gives the workforce a formal voice in corporate oversight unique among major economies.
