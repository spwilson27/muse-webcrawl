Exosphere

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The exosphere (from Ancient Greek éxō, "outside", and sphaîra, "sphere") is the outermost layer of a planet's atmosphere, where it thins out into outer space. On Earth it lies above the thermosphere, beginning at the exobase — roughly 500 to 1,000 km above the surface, varying with solar activity — and extends outward for thousands of kilometres, with Earth's hydrogen geocorona detectable as far as 100,000 km or more. The exosphere merges imperceptibly with the interplanetary medium; there is no sharp upper boundary.

Properties

The exosphere is so rarefied that its particles rarely collide: an atom or molecule may travel on a ballistic trajectory for hundreds of kilometres, following orbits around the planet or, if it exceeds escape velocity, escaping into space. Its composition is dominated by the lightest gases — atomic hydrogen and helium, with some atomic oxygen near the base. Temperatures, defined by the average kinetic energy of these few particles, can reach 1,000 °C or more, yet the gas would feel intensely cold because it can transfer almost no heat. Most artificial satellites, including the International Space Station (which orbits in the upper thermosphere, near the exobase) and the great majority of low Earth orbit satellites, travel through the exosphere and experience a faint but real atmospheric drag that slowly decays their orbits.

Other bodies

Thin exospheres ("surface-boundary exospheres") exist on bodies with almost no atmosphere, notably the Moon and Mercury, where atoms are knocked off the surface by sunlight and the solar wind and hop across the landscape before being lost or reabsorbed. Earth's exosphere loses hydrogen to space at a rate of a few kilograms per second — the slow leak that, over geological time, shapes planetary evolution and contrasts with the tighter retention of heavier atmospheres.
