Crown Prosecution Service

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Crown Prosecution Service (CPS) is the principal public agency for conducting criminal prosecutions in England and Wales. Created by the Prosecution of Offences Act 1985 and operational from 1986, it separated the investigation of crime (the police) from the decision to prosecute and the conduct of cases in court — ending the arrangement under which the police force that investigated a case also prosecuted it. The CPS is headed by the Director of Public Prosecutions (DPP), who operates under the superintendence of the Attorney General, and is organised into regional Areas plus specialised divisions (serious fraud, counter-terrorism, organised crime).

Crown prosecutors apply the Code for Crown Prosecutors' two-stage test: an evidential stage (a realistic prospect of conviction on admissible evidence) and a public-interest stage. The service prosecutes the large majority of criminal cases in England and Wales (hundreds of thousands of magistrates'-court cases and tens of thousands of Crown Court cases a year, in round terms); it does not investigate, though it advises police in serious cases from an early stage. Persistent controversies include disclosure failures (a series of collapsed trials in 2017–18 triggered systemic reform), charging delays, and low prosecution rates in rape and sexual offences, the subject of repeated inspectorate and government reviews.
