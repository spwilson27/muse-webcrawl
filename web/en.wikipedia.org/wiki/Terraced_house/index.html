Terraced house

A terraced house (in the United States and Canada, a townhouse or row house) is a type of medium-density housing in which a row of attached dwellings share side walls with their neighbours. The houses in a terrace are typically identical or mirror-image in plan, though grander "terraces" of the Georgian era present a unified palace-like façade to the street.

History

Rows of uniform houses were built in Paris as early as the Place des Vosges (1605–1612), and in London after the Great Fire of 1666. In Georgian and Regency Britain, terraces became the standard urban house type, ranging from aristocratic crescents such as Bath's Royal Crescent to modest working-class streets in the industrial north. The Victorian era saw vast expansion: the "two-up, two-down" brick terrace housed factory workers, while back-to-back houses — built without rear access or through ventilation — were eventually condemned as unhealthy slums and largely demolished in 20th-century clearance programmes.

Characteristics

Terraced houses share both side (party) walls with adjacent properties except at the ends of the row, where an "end terrace" house has one exposed side wall. The form makes efficient use of land and materials, provides good thermal performance through shared walls, and creates a continuous street frontage. Disadvantages can include limited natural light to the middle of deep plans, noise transmission between neighbours, and restricted rear access.

Today terraced houses remain one of the most common house types in the United Kingdom, and townhouses resembling them are built worldwide, often as a higher-density alternative to detached houses.
