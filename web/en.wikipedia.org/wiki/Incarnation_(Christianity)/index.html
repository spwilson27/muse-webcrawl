Incarnation (Christianity)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: The Annunciation]

In Christian theology, the Incarnation is the belief that God the Son, the second person of the Trinity, "became flesh" by being conceived in the womb of the Virgin Mary and born as the human Jesus of Nazareth. It is a central doctrine of mainstream Christianity, expressed in the Gospel of John's statement that "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14).

Doctrine

The Councils of Nicaea (325) and Chalcedon (451) defined the doctrine against views that Christ was merely human (adoptionism), merely seemed human (docetism), or was a hybrid being. Chalcedon confessed one person in two natures, divine and human, "without confusion, change, division or separation" — the hypostatic union. In the Incarnation, God the Son assumed a complete human nature — body and rational soul — while remaining fully God.

Significance

The doctrine grounds Christian teaching on salvation: in Athanasius's formula, "He was made man that we might be made god" (theosis). It is liturgically celebrated at the Annunciation (25 March) and at Christmas. Related concepts include kenosis (the self-emptying of Philippians 2). Groups historically outside the Chalcedonian mainstream — such as Oriental Orthodox (miaphysite) churches — affirm the Incarnation with different formulas, while Unitarians and others reject it.
