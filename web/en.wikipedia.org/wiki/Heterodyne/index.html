Heterodyne

A heterodyne is a signal processing technique invented in 1901 by Canadian engineer Reginald Fessenden that creates new frequencies by combining two signals. In the process, two oscillating electrical signals are applied to a nonlinear device such as a mixer, producing output at the sum and difference of the original frequencies. When one input is the signal to be received and the other a local oscillator, the difference frequency converts the signal to a fixed intermediate frequency.

Applications

Heterodyning is the basis of the superheterodyne receiver, developed by Edwin Armstrong around 1918, which became the standard architecture for virtually all radio and television receivers: incoming stations are all converted to a single intermediate frequency where they can be filtered and amplified with high selectivity and gain. Heterodyne techniques are also used in frequency synthesizers, radar, Doppler measurement, musical theremins, and optical heterodyne detection in interferometry and telecommunications.

Principle

Mathematically, multiplying two sinusoids yields components at f1 + f2 and f1 − f2. A filter selects the desired product. Fessenden's original motivation was to make continuous-wave Morse signals audible by beating them against a local oscillation — the resulting beat note is the origin of the term "beat frequency oscillator" (BFO).
