Protein secondary structure

Protein secondary structure is the local spatial conformation of the polypeptide backbone of a protein, excluding the conformation of side chains. Secondary structure is formally defined by the pattern of hydrogen bonds between the amino hydrogen and carboxyl oxygen atoms in the peptide backbone, and the two standard elements are the alpha helix and the beta strand (which assemble into beta sheets); other regular conformations include turns and the 310 and π helices, while segments not adopting a regular pattern are often described as loops or random coil.

Alpha helix

In an alpha helix the backbone is coiled so that the carbonyl oxygen of each residue forms a hydrogen bond with the amide hydrogen of the residue four positions later (i → i+4). The helix has 3.6 residues per turn and a rise of 0.54 nm per turn; side chains project outward. Alpha helices are common in globular proteins and in membrane-spanning segments. The structure was predicted by Linus Pauling and Robert Corey in 1951.

Beta sheets

Beta strands are extended segments of polypeptide (typically 3–10 residues) that associate laterally through hydrogen bonds to form beta sheets. Strands may run in the same direction (parallel sheet) or opposite directions (antiparallel sheet), and the sheet has a pleated appearance. Beta barrels and beta propellers are common folds built from sheets.

Turns, loops and other elements

Turns (including beta turns) reverse the chain direction over a few residues and often contain glycine or proline. The shorter 310 helix (i → i+3 bonds) and the wider π helix (i → i+5) occur occasionally, often at helix ends. Regions lacking regular structure — loops and coils — are frequently functionally important, forming binding sites and flexible linkers; some are intrinsically disordered.

Determination and prediction

Secondary structure is derived experimentally from X-ray crystallography, NMR and cryo-electron microscopy structures, and assigned algorithmically by the DSSP method. Circular dichroism spectroscopy estimates helix/sheet content in solution. Prediction from sequence alone, once based on propensities (Chou–Fasman, GOR), now achieves high accuracy with deep-learning methods allied to structure prediction tools such as AlphaFold.
