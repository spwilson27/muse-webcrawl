Siege of Saint-Martin-de-Ré

The siege of Saint-Martin-de-Ré (1627) was an episode of the Huguenot rebellions in France, in which an English expeditionary force under George Villiers, 1st Duke of Buckingham, landed on the Île de Ré off the French Atlantic coast in support of the Huguenots of La Rochelle, and besieged the royal citadel of Saint-Martin-de-Ré. The siege failed; the English were forced to withdraw with heavy losses, and the way was opened for the French crown's decisive siege of La Rochelle in 1627–1628.

Background

Cardinal Richelieu, chief minister of Louis XIII, was determined to break the political and military power of the Huguenots, whose stronghold was the fortified port of La Rochelle. England, under Charles I, intervened in their support, partly through the influence of the royal favourite Buckingham. In July 1627 Buckingham landed on the Île de Ré with around 6,000–7,000 men, intending to use the island as a base to relieve pressure on La Rochelle.

The siege

After defeating French troops at the landing, the English advanced on Saint-Martin-de-Ré, whose citadel was held by the Marquis de Toiras with a small garrison. Buckingham's siege was hampered by a shortage of scaling ladders, artillery and supplies, and by French success in running small boats with provisions into the citadel at night. French relief forces gathered on the mainland, and in October 1627 reinforcements reached the island. When a final English assault on the citadel failed at the end of October, Buckingham withdrew. His retreat across the causeway and marshes to the embarkation point at Loix was harassed by French troops and degenerated into a rout, in which the English lost a large share of their force — contemporary estimates suggest only about half the expedition returned to England.

Aftermath

The failure humiliated Buckingham, who was assassinated the following year while preparing a second expedition at Portsmouth. Richelieu proceeded to besiege La Rochelle itself, which starved into submission in October 1628. The Peace of Alès (1629) stripped the Huguenots of their fortifications and political assemblies while preserving their religious rights. The citadel and harbour defences of Saint-Martin-de-Ré were later incorporated into Vauban's system of coastal fortifications.
