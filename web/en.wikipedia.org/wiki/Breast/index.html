Breast

The breast is one of two prominences located on the upper ventral region of the torso of primates. In females, it serves as the mammary gland, which produces and secretes milk to feed infants. Both males and females develop breasts from the same embryological tissues. At puberty, estrogens, in conjunction with growth hormone, promote the development of female breasts, a process largely absent in males. The female breast is a secondary sexual characteristic and plays roles in sexuality and body image as well as in infant nutrition through breastfeeding.

Structure

The breast lies over the pectoralis major muscle on the anterior chest wall, typically extending from the level of the second to the sixth rib. It is composed of glandular tissue arranged in 15–20 lobes, each drained by a lactiferous duct opening at the nipple, embedded in adipose tissue that largely determines breast size and shape. The lobes are supported by fibrous connective tissue (Cooper's ligaments). The nipple is surrounded by the pigmented areola, which contains sebaceous (Montgomery) glands. Lymphatic drainage runs principally to the axillary lymph nodes, a fact of major clinical importance in the spread of breast cancer.

Development

Breasts begin to form in the embryo along the mammary ridges ("milk lines"). At puberty in girls, usually between ages 8 and 13, rising estrogen levels trigger thelarche: ducts elongate and branch, and fat accumulates. During pregnancy, progesterone and prolactin complete the development of the secretory alveoli; after birth, prolactin drives milk production and oxytocin the milk-ejection ("let-down") reflex. After menopause, glandular tissue regresses and is replaced by fat. Male breasts normally remain rudimentary, though hormone imbalances can cause gynecomastia.

Clinical significance

The breast is subject to benign conditions such as fibrocystic change, fibroadenoma, mastitis, and cysts, and to breast cancer, the most commonly diagnosed cancer in women worldwide, with roughly 2.3 million new cases annually. Screening by mammography and clinical examination aims to detect cancers early. Surgical procedures on the breast include lumpectomy and mastectomy for cancer, reconstruction after mastectomy, and cosmetic augmentation and reduction. Male breast cancer occurs but is rare.

Society and culture

Breasts carry varied cultural meanings: symbols of femininity, fertility, and motherhood in art since prehistory, objects of sexualisation in modern media, and the focus of debates over breastfeeding in public, dress codes, and the "topfreedom" movement. Norms of coverage differ widely between cultures and eras.
