Diaspora

A diaspora is a population that is scattered across regions which are separate from its geographic place of origin. The word is used in reference to people who identify with a specific geographic location, but currently reside elsewhere.

Historically, the word was used first in the context of the dispersion of the Jews after the Babylonian captivity, and it came to be applied to the Jewish population living outside Israel. Usage has since broadened to describe the African diaspora created by the Atlantic slave trade, the Armenian, Irish, Chinese, Indian and many other diasporas.

Characteristics

Scholars commonly cite collective memory of a homeland, a troubled relationship with host societies, an ideal of eventual return, and a continuing connection with the homeland and with co-members elsewhere as characteristic features. Diasporas maintain transnational networks through remittances, media, travel, religion and culture.

Modern usage

The term is now applied loosely to migrant and exile communities of all kinds, and to professional and digital communities. Governments increasingly engage their diasporas through ministries, investment schemes and dual-citizenship arrangements; remittances from diasporas are a major source of income for many developing economies.
