Aryl halide

An aryl halide (or haloarene) is an aromatic compound in which one or more hydrogen atoms on an aromatic ring are replaced by a halogen atom — fluorine, chlorine, bromine or iodine. The simplest examples are the halobenzenes, such as chlorobenzene and bromobenzene.

Properties and reactivity

The carbon–halogen bond in aryl halides is stronger and less reactive than in alkyl halides because of partial double-bond character from conjugation with the ring, and because backside attack is blocked. Aryl halides therefore resist ordinary nucleophilic substitution, though they react readily under transition-metal catalysis or under forcing conditions (for example, the Dow process for phenol).

Reactions

Aryl halides are central substrates in cross-coupling reactions — Suzuki, Heck, Sonogashira, Buchwald–Hartwig and Negishi couplings — which form carbon–carbon and carbon–heteroatom bonds and are pillars of modern pharmaceutical synthesis. They also form Grignard reagents and undergo lithium–halogen exchange.

Preparation

They are prepared by direct electrophilic halogenation of arenes, by the Sandmeyer reaction from diazonium salts, or by decarboxylative and C–H halogenation methods.
