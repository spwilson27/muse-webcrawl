The Battle of Okinawa, codenamed Operation Iceberg, was a major battle of the Pacific War fought on the island of Okinawa between United States and Imperial Japanese forces from 1 April to 22 June 1945. It was the largest amphibious assault in the Pacific theatre and the last great battle of the Second World War. The ferocity of the fighting — kamikaze attacks at sea, mass civilian deaths, and the virtual annihilation of the defending army — convinced many American planners that an invasion of the Japanese home islands would be catastrophically costly, and forms part of the context of the decision to use atomic weapons (see Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki).

The battle

After a massive preliminary bombardment ("the typhoon of steel"), some 180,000 US Army and Marine troops landed almost unopposed on 1 April 1945 — General Mitsuru Ushijima's Thirty-Second Army had withdrawn to fortified lines in the south around Shuri Castle. In the north, Motobu Peninsula was cleared within weeks; in the south, American forces spent nearly three months grinding through successive defence lines (Kakazu Ridge, Sugar Loaf Hill, the Shuri Line) amid heavy rains. Offshore, the supporting fleet endured ten massed kamikaze attacks (kikusui), which sank some 36 ships and damaged hundreds; on 7 April the super-battleship Yamato, sent on a one-way surface sortie (Operation Ten-Go), was sunk by carrier aircraft.

Civilian toll

A huge share of Okinawa's population — estimates range from 40,000 to 150,000 people, perhaps a third of the pre-war population — died from crossfire, disease, starvation, and coerced or encouraged mass suicide as Japanese positions collapsed. General Ushijima and his chief of staff committed suicide on 22 June; organised resistance ended the same day.

Aftermath

Okinawa provided the Allies with airfields and anchorages within range of Kyushu for the planned Operation Downfall, which was rendered unnecessary by Japan's surrender in August 1945. The island remained under US administration until 1972, and American bases there remain a contentious legacy. The Cornerstone of Peace memorial in Itoman records the names of over 240,000 who died, of all nationalities.
