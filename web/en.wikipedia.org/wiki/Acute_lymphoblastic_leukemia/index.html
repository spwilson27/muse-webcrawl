Acute lymphoblastic leukemia

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), also called acute lymphocytic leukemia, is a cancer of the lymphoid line of blood cells characterized by the development of large numbers of immature lymphocytes (lymphoblasts). The malignant cells multiply rapidly in the bone marrow, crowding out normal blood cell production and spreading to the blood and other organs.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms result from bone marrow failure and organ infiltration: fatigue and pallor from anemia, infections from neutropenia, bleeding and bruising from thrombocytopenia, bone pain, enlarged lymph nodes, liver and spleen enlargement, and sometimes involvement of the central nervous system or testes.

Causes and classification

ALL arises from genetic damage to developing lymphocytes, involving chromosomal translocations and abnormal chromosome numbers (hyperdiploidy or hypodiploidy) that alter cell growth and maturation. Most cases are of B-cell precursor origin; the remainder are T-cell. Genetic subtypes such as the Philadelphia chromosome (BCR-ABL1) have major prognostic and therapeutic significance. Risk factors include certain genetic syndromes (e.g., Down syndrome) and prior radiation exposure; according to Greaves's "delayed infection" hypothesis, abnormal immune responses to common infections in childhood may trigger some cases.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is made by blood counts and bone marrow examination showing at least 20–25 percent lymphoblasts, with immunophenotyping, cytogenetics, and molecular studies used for classification and risk stratification.

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment is prolonged multi-agent chemotherapy, typically divided into induction, consolidation, and maintenance phases over two to three years, with central nervous system prophylaxis. Targeted agents such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors (for BCR-ABL1–positive ALL), monoclonal antibodies, and CAR T-cell therapy have improved outcomes, and stem cell transplantation is used for high-risk disease. ALL is the most common cancer in children, with a peak at ages 2–5; cure rates in children in developed countries now exceed 90 percent, while outcomes in adults are less favorable.
