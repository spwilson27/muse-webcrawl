Caen is a commune in northwestern France, the prefecture of the department of Calvados and the historic capital of Lower Normandy. It lies on the River Orne, about 15 km inland from the English Channel, and is connected to the sea by the Caen Canal at Ouistreham. The city is known for its medieval heritage associated with William the Conqueror and for its near-total reconstruction after the Second World War.

History

Caen rose to prominence under William, Duke of Normandy (later William the Conqueror), who made it his capital and built the Château de Caen, one of the largest medieval fortresses in Europe, as well as the Abbaye aux Hommes and Abbaye aux Dames, where William and his wife Matilda of Flanders are buried. The city was fought over during the Hundred Years' War, taken by Edward III in 1346 and held by England from 1417 to 1450.

During the Battle of Normandy in 1944, Caen was a key Allied objective after D-Day. Savage fighting and heavy bombing destroyed roughly three-quarters of the city before it was liberated in July 1944 (Operation Charnwood). The city was rebuilt in the following decades with wide avenues and buildings in pale local "Caen stone," a limestone long exported for famous buildings including the Tower of London.

Sights and culture

Major sites include the castle, the two abbey churches, the church of Saint-Pierre, and the Mémorial de Caen, a major museum and peace memorial opened in 1988. The University of Caen Normandy, founded in 1432, is among the oldest in France. Caen stone quarries and the city's marina at Ouistreham, with cross-Channel ferries to Portsmouth, remain important to its economy.
