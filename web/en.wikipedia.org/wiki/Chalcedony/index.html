Chalcedony

Chalcedony
CategoryOxide mineral; quartz group
FormulaSiO₂ (silica)
Crystal systemTrigonal (cryptocrystalline aggregate)
Hardness6–7 on the Mohs scale
LustreWaxy to vitreous
VarietiesAgate, onyx, carnelian, chrysoprase, bloodstone, jasper

Chalcedony is a cryptocrystalline form of silica, composed of very fine intergrowths of the minerals quartz and moganite. Both are silica minerals, but quartz has a trigonal crystal structure while moganite is monoclinic; their intergrowth gives chalcedony its characteristic waxy lustre, translucency, and smooth, banded or botryoidal forms.

Properties and formation

Chalcedony has a hardness of 6 to 7 on the Mohs scale, a specific gravity of about 2.59–2.61, and a waxy to dull-greasy lustre. It forms from silica-rich solutions at relatively low temperatures, depositing in cavities and fractures in volcanic and sedimentary rocks, often as the lining of geodes. Pure chalcedony is white to grey or blue-grey; trace impurities produce the many coloured varieties. The name is traditionally derived from the ancient Greek town of Chalcedon in Asia Minor.

Varieties

Many chalcedony varieties have their own names: banded material is agate; straight-banded black-and-white material is onyx; translucent red-orange chalcedony is carnelian; apple-green material coloured by nickel is chrysoprase; dark green with red spots is bloodstone (heliotrope); and opaque varieties are generally called jasper. Sard, prase, and chrome chalcedony are further named forms.

Uses

Chalcedony has been worked since prehistory — it flakes conchoidally and was used for stone tools — and it was a favourite material of ancient gem engravers for seals, intaglios, and cameos. It remains a popular ornamental and jewellery stone, cut as cabochons, beads, and carvings.
