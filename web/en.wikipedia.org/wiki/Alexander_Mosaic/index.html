The Alexander Mosaic, also called the Battle of Issus Mosaic, is an ancient Roman floor mosaic made around 100 BC. It depicts a battle between the armies of Alexander the Great and Darius III of Persia, generally identified as the Battle of Issus (333 BC), though some scholars favour the Battle of Gaugamela (331 BC). It is among the most famous works of ancient art.

Description

The mosaic measures 2.72 by 5.13 metres and is composed of roughly one and a half million tiny tesserae in the opus vermiculatum technique, arranged in curving lines that allow subtle gradations of colour. On the left, Alexander charges bareheaded on his horse Bucephalus, his spear transfixing a Persian soldier. On the right, Darius stands in his chariot, his charioteer whipping the horses to flee while the king reaches out in a gesture of alarm or appeal. Between them the composition is filled with the dense press of battle: rearing horses, fallen soldiers, and a forest of spears.

History

The mosaic decorated the exedra of the House of the Faun in Pompeii, the largest and grandest residence excavated at the site. It is generally believed to copy a lost Greek painting of the late fourth century BC, often attributed to Philoxenos of Eretria, who Pliny the Elder records painted a battle of Alexander and Darius for Cassander of Macedon.

The mosaic was discovered in 1831 during excavations at Pompeii and was moved to Naples in 1843. It is now displayed on a wall at the Museo Archeologico Nazionale in Naples, while a modern replica, completed in 2005, occupies the original location in the House of the Faun.
