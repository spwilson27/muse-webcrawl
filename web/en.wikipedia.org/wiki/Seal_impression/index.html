Seal impression

A seal impression is the mark left when a seal — an engraved stamp or cylinder — is pressed or rolled into a soft material such as clay, wax, lead or inked paper. In archaeology, seal impressions are primary evidence: for many ancient seals, the original matrix is lost and the design is known only from the impressions it left.

Bullae and clay sealings

In the ancient Near East, lumps of clay called bullae were pressed over the knots and pegs securing doors, jars and documents, then stamped or rolled with cylinder or stamp seals; if the seal was broken, tampering was evident. Fired accidentally or deliberately, thousands of such impressions survive, from Mesopotamia, Egypt and the Persian Empire, and they are catalogued in museum collections as key sources for ancient iconography, administration and personal names.

Wax and lead impressions

Medieval documents were authenticated with impressions in beeswax (later sealing wax), appended by cords or parchment tags. The papal bulla took its name from the lead seal (bulla) attached to it, showing Saints Peter and Paul on one side and the pope's name on the other. Byzantine and medieval lead seals — tens of thousands survive — form a major source for prosopography.

Study

The study of seal impressions, including their legends, imagery and use, is sigillography; impressions also serve forensics, since damage to a matrix over time can date documents.
