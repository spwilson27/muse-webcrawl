Huqin (Chinese: 胡琴) is a family of bowed string instruments used in Chinese music. The name literally means "barbarian instrument", reflecting the family's origin among peoples to China's north and west; its ancestor, the xiqin, is depicted in Song-dynasty sources. Huqin are spike fiddles: a long neck passes through a small resonator body, and the bow hair is threaded permanently between the two strings.

Construction and playing

Most huqin have two strings (historically silk, now usually steel) stretched over a sound box covered with snakeskin — traditionally python — or, for the banhu, a thin wooden face over a coconut shell. The instrument is held vertically on the left thigh, fingered with the left hand without pressing the strings to a fingerboard, and bowed with the right hand, the bow hair passing between the strings. This construction produces a characteristically nasal, expressive tone suited to both solo and operatic accompaniment.

Members of the family

  • Erhu — the best-known member, central to Chinese orchestras and solo repertoire
  • Gaohu — higher-pitched erhu developed in the 1920s by Lu Wencheng
  • Zhonghu — alto erhu
  • Jinghu — high, piercing fiddle of Peking opera
  • Banhu — coconut-bodied fiddle of northern China
  • Yuehu, tiqin, sihu and regional variants used in opera and narrative singing

Related instruments

Cognate spike fiddles spread throughout East and Southeast Asia, including the Mongolian morin khuur, the Korean haegeum and the Vietnamese đàn nhị.
