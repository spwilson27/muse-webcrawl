A line of succession is the ordered sequence of persons who are eligible to succeed to an office — most often a throne — when it becomes vacant. Lines of succession are typically defined by law or constitutional rules based on descent, and exist for monarchies as well as for elected offices: for example, the United States presidential line of succession specifies the order in which officials assume the presidency if both the president and vice president are unable to serve.

Hereditary succession

Historically the most common rule is primogeniture, under which the eldest child of the reigning monarch inherits. Variants include male-preference primogeniture (sons before daughters, used in the United Kingdom until the Succession to the Crown Act 2013 introduced absolute primogeniture for those born after 28 October 2011) and agnatic (Salic) succession, which excludes women entirely. Some monarchies use seniority among brothers, tanistry, or election by a council, as in Saudi Arabia's Allegiance Council and Malaysia's rotating elected kingship.

Presidential lines of succession

In the United States, the Presidential Succession Act of 1947 places the Speaker of the House and the president pro tempore of the Senate after the vice president, followed by the heads of the executive departments in order of their creation, beginning with the Secretary of State. Many other republics and organisations maintain similar statutory orders to ensure continuity of government.

Disputed successions

Unclear or contested lines have caused numerous succession crises and wars, including the Wars of the Roses in England and the War of the Spanish Succession (1701–1714).
