Plan 9 from Bell Labs is a distributed operating system developed at Bell Labs in the mid-1980s to early 1990s by the group that created Unix, including Ken Thompson, Rob Pike and Dennis Ritchie. Its name references the film Plan 9 from Outer Space. Plan 9 was designed as Unix's successor for a networked world: it builds a distributed system out of separate computers that users treat as a single, coherent environment.

Design

Plan 9 carries Unix's "everything is a file" idea much further. All resources — networks, processes, windows, remote machines — are accessed through hierarchical file systems communicated with the 9P protocol. Communication channels between processes ("namespaces") are private and can be composed per process, so a program can import a remote file server or a window system into its own tree. The window system is rio, and the customary shell is rc. The system's minimalism and uniformity influenced later systems; UTF-8 was invented for Plan 9 by Thompson and Pike, and features such as /proc and union directories spread to other operating systems.

History and legacy

Development began in the late 1980s; the first edition was released to universities in 1990, with general releases following through the Fourth Edition of 2002. Plan 9 never achieved commercial success, and Lucent/Bell Labs development wound down in the 2000s, but an active community maintains it, and forks and descendants include 9front, Harvey OS, and the research system NIX. In 2021 Bell Labs (Nokia) transferred Plan 9 copyrights to the Plan 9 Foundation and relicensed the historical editions under the MIT License. Technologies from Plan 9 live on widely, notably the 9P protocol and the Go programming language, created by Plan 9 veterans.
