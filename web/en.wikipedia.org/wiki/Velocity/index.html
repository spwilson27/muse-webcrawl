Velocity

The velocity of an object is the rate of change of its position with respect to a frame of reference, and is a function of time. Velocity is a physical vector quantity; both magnitude and direction are needed to define it. The scalar absolute value (magnitude) of velocity is called speed, a quantity measured in metres per second (m/s) in the SI system.

Average and instantaneous velocity

Average velocity is defined as the displacement divided by the time interval over which the displacement occurs: v = Δx / Δt. Instantaneous velocity is the limit of the average velocity as the time interval approaches zero, and equals the derivative of position with respect to time, v = dx/dt. The derivative of velocity with respect to time is acceleration.

Constant velocity

Constant velocity implies motion in a straight line at a constant speed. An object travelling at a constant speed but changing direction — for example, in uniform circular motion — does not have constant velocity, because its direction changes, and it therefore accelerates centripetally.

Relativity and reference frames

Velocity is always measured relative to a chosen frame of reference. In Newtonian mechanics velocities add simply, but in special relativity the relativistic velocity addition formula must be used, and no object's speed can exceed the speed of light in vacuum. Related concepts include relative velocity, escape velocity, terminal velocity, and group velocity for waves.
