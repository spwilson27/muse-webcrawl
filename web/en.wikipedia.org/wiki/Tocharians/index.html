Tocharians

The Tocharians were the speakers of the Tocharian languages, a branch of the Indo-European language family known from manuscripts dating from the 6th to 9th centuries CE found in the northern Tarim Basin, in present-day Xinjiang, China. They inhabited oasis city-states such as Kucha, Turfan, Karasahr (Yanqi) and Loulan. The name "Tocharian" derives from the Greek Tokharoi, applied to peoples of Bactria, though the connection between the two groups is debated.

Language

Two closely related languages are attested: Tocharian A, found mainly around Turfan and Karasahr and used largely as a liturgical language, and Tocharian B, attested across the region and clearly a living vernacular. Tocharian is remarkable as the easternmost known Indo-European language family and, unusually, is a centum language despite its eastern location.

History and culture

The Tocharian city-states were important waypoints on the northern Silk Road and played a key role in transmitting Buddhism from India into China. Tocharian translators such as Kumārajīva (of part-Tocharian heritage) helped render Buddhist scriptures into Chinese. Frescoes in cave complexes such as Bezeklik and Kizil depict red-haired, light-eyed donors in distinctive dress. After the Uyghur migration into the Tarim Basin in the 9th century, the Tocharian languages gradually died out and the population was absorbed into the Turkic-speaking Uyghurs.

Origins

The origins of the Tocharians are often linked to the earlier Afanasievo culture of Siberia and to the famous naturally mummified bodies of the Tarim Basin, some dating to around 1800 BCE, though the precise relationship between the Bronze Age population and the later manuscript culture remains a matter of scholarly debate.
