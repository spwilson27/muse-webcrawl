Local anesthesia

Local anesthesia
SpecialtyAnesthesiology, dentistry, surgery
Common agentsLidocaine, bupivacaine, articaine, prilocaine, benzocaine
MechanismReversible blockade of voltage-gated sodium channels

Local anesthesia is any technique to induce the absence of sensation in a specific part of the body, generally for the aim of inducing local analgesia, that is, local insensitivity to pain, although other local senses may be affected as well. It allows patients to undergo surgical and dental procedures with reduced pain and distress. In many situations, such as cesarean section, it is safer and therefore superior to general anesthesia. The term also covers topical anesthesia, infiltration, and local techniques distinct from regional anesthesia (which targets a larger area, such as a limb, via nerve blocks or neuraxial techniques).

Mechanism

Local anesthetic drugs reversibly block voltage-gated sodium channels in neuronal membranes, preventing the generation and propagation of action potentials in sensory nerve fibres. Small, myelinated and unmyelinated pain fibres are blocked before larger motor fibres, producing differential block. Agents are divided chemically into esters (e.g., procaine, benzocaine, tetracaine) and amides (e.g., lidocaine, bupivacaine, ropivacaine, articaine), differing in metabolism and allergy profile. Onset and duration depend on the drug's lipid solubility, protein binding and pKa, and on the site of injection; vasoconstrictors such as adrenaline are often added to prolong action and reduce bleeding.

Techniques

  • Topical anesthesia — creams, gels or sprays applied to skin or mucosa (e.g., lidocaine–prilocaine cream, benzocaine)
  • Infiltration — injection directly into the tissue to be operated on
  • Field block — injections surrounding the operative area
  • Nerve block — injection adjacent to a specific nerve or plexus (strictly a regional technique, but often grouped with local anesthesia)
  • Intravenous regional anesthesia (Bier block) for limb surgery

Applications and safety

Local anesthesia is fundamental to dentistry (e.g., inferior alveolar nerve block), minor skin surgery, ophthalmology, wound repair and many outpatient procedures. Adverse effects include injection pain, hematoma, infection, nerve injury, and — the most serious — local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST) from excessive dose or inadvertent intravascular injection, causing CNS excitation, seizures and cardiovascular collapse; it is treated with intravenous lipid emulsion. True allergy is rare and largely confined to ester agents and preservatives.

History

Cocaine, isolated in 1860 and used for eye surgery by Carl Koller in 1884, was the first effective local anesthetic, but its toxicity and addictiveness drove the search for substitutes, yielding procaine (Novocain) in 1905 and lidocaine in 1943.
