The Ethiopian Semitic languages (also Ethiosemitic or Ethiopic) are a group of Semitic languages spoken in Ethiopia and Eritrea. They include the liturgical language Ge'ez, Tigrinya and Tigre in the north, and Amharic — Ethiopia's most widely spoken language — together with Argobba, Harari and the Gurage languages in the south. Most are written in the Ge'ez script, an abugida derived from the ancient South Arabian consonantal script.

Classification

The group is conventionally divided into North Ethiopic (Ge'ez, Tigre, Tigrinya, and the extinct Dahalik) and South Ethiopic (Amharic, Argobba, Harari and the many Gurage varieties). Whether Ethiopian Semitic forms a single clade with the Modern South Arabian languages, and the exact internal relationships of the Gurage cluster, remain debated among specialists.

History

Semitic languages reached the Horn of Africa from South Arabia by the early first millennium BC. Inscriptions in the kingdom of D'mt and later the Kingdom of Aksum record an early form of Ge'ez, which became the literary and liturgical language of Ethiopian Christianity and remained the language of royal chronicles into the nineteenth century. Amharic, first attested in medieval royal songs, rose with the Amhara region and became the language of the imperial court and, eventually, the federal working language of modern Ethiopia.

Features

Ethiopian Semitic languages share characteristic features shaped by contact with Cushitic languages: verb-final (SOV) word order unusual for Semitic, the use of converbs, ejective consonants, and extensive suffixation. Like other Semitic languages they build words from consonantal roots with templatic vowel patterns, though the pattern system is reduced compared with Arabic or Hebrew.
