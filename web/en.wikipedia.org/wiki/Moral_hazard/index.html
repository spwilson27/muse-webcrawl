Moral hazard

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Moral hazard
When the costs of risk are borne by another party, incentives to avoid risk are reduced
FieldEconomics, insurance, finance
Related conceptsAdverse selection; principal–agent problem; information asymmetry
OriginInsurance industry usage, 19th century

In economics, moral hazard is a situation where an economic actor has an incentive to increase its exposure to risk because it does not bear the full costs of that risk. For example, when a corporation is insured, it may take on higher risk knowing that its insurance will pay the associated costs. A moral hazard may occur where the actions of the risk-taking party change to the detriment of the cost-bearing party after a financial transaction has taken place.

Moral hazard can occur under a type of information asymmetry where the risk-taking party to a transaction knows more about its intentions than the party paying the consequences of the risk, and has a tendency or incentive to take on too much risk from the perspective of the party with less information. The term originates in the insurance industry — 19th-century insurers used it to describe the risk that an insured person would behave recklessly or dishonestly — and was formalised by economists including Kenneth Arrow in the 1960s.

Insurance

The classic example is insurance itself: a person with full coverage may take less care to prevent a loss (ex ante moral hazard) or consume more of an insured service, such as healthcare, than they otherwise would (ex post moral hazard). Insurers counter moral hazard with deductibles, co-payments and coinsurance, which keep part of the cost with the insured; with policy exclusions and experience rating; and by monitoring and investigating claims.

Finance

Moral hazard is central to the principal–agent problem: managers (agents) may pursue growth, pay or empire-building rather than shareholder value, and lenders may be monitored imperfectly by borrowers. The concept featured prominently in explanations of the 2008 financial crisis: institutions regarded as "too big to fail" could borrow cheaply and take large risks in the expectation of public rescue, shifting losses to taxpayers. Proposed remedies include higher capital requirements, bail-in and resolution regimes that impose losses on creditors, deferred and clawback-able executive compensation, and credible commitments not to rescue failing firms.

Other domains

The concept is applied to employment (employees shirking when effort is unobservable), medicine, tenancy (tenants caring less for property than owners), international lending (IMF bailouts encouraging risky borrowing), and public policy — for example, flood insurance encouraging building in flood plains. Moral hazard is distinct from adverse selection, which concerns hidden information before a contract is signed; moral hazard concerns hidden actions after signing. Empirical economists debate its magnitude: studies of insurance markets find real but often moderate effects, and economists note that apparent risk-taking may also reflect rational responses to changed prices rather than opportunism.
