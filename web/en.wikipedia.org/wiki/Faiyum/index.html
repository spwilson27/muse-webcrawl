Faiyum (Arabic: الفيوم, el-Fayyūm) is a city in Middle Egypt and the capital of the Faiyum Governorate. It lies in the Faiyum Oasis, a large depression about 100 kilometres southwest of Cairo, irrigated by Nile water carried through the Bahr Yusuf canal. The oasis is one of the oldest agricultural regions in Egypt, with farming documented since the Neolithic, and its name derives from the Coptic Phiom, "the sea/lake," ultimately from ancient Egyptian Pa-yom, referring to nearby Lake Moeris.

History

In Pharaonic times the city, then called Shedet, was the cult centre of the crocodile god Sobek and was known to the Greeks as Crocodilopolis. Middle Kingdom kings of the 12th Dynasty, especially Amenemhat III, carried out major land-reclamation and irrigation works around Lake Moeris. Under the Ptolemies the region was extensively settled by Greek veterans and renamed Arsinoe, becoming famous for its papyri — thousands of Greek, Latin, and Coptic documents recovered from sites such as Karanis, Tebtunis, and Oxyrhynchus nearby.

The region is especially celebrated for the Faiyum mummy portraits: naturalistic painted portraits on wood, produced in Roman Egypt between the 1st and 3rd centuries CE and attached to mummies, which survive in large numbers because of the dry climate and are among the most famous works of ancient panel painting.

Geography and sights

The Faiyum depression lies below sea level and is fed by the Bahr Yusuf, a Nile branch channelled through the Lahun gap. Lake Qarun, the remnant of ancient Lake Moeris, is saline and a protected bird sanctuary. Attractions include the waterfalls of Wadi El Rayan, the pottery village of Tunis, the pyramids of Hawara and Lahun, and the UNESCO World Heritage Site of Wadi El Hitan ("Valley of the Whales") to the southwest, preserving fossils of early whales such as Basilosaurus. The city is also known for its historic waterwheels, driven by the canal system and depicted on the governorate's emblem.

Economy

Agriculture dominates the region, producing olives, figs, grapes, mangoes, and other crops on the fertile plots of the oasis. Tourism centred on antiquities, crafts, and eco-lodges has grown since the 2000s.
