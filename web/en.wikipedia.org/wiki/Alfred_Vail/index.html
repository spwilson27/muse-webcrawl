Alfred Lewis Vail (25 September 1807 – 18 January 1859) was an American machinist and inventor. A key collaborator of Samuel Morse, he played a central role in developing and commercialising the electric telegraph in the United States during the 1840s.

Collaboration with Morse

Vail met Morse at New York University in 1837 and witnessed a demonstration of his telegraph model. Impressed, Vail secured financial backing from his father, Stephen Vail, owner of the Speedwell Ironworks in Morristown, New Jersey, in return for a share of the eventual profits, and undertook to build practical working apparatus at Speedwell. His mechanical improvements — including the recording register and the familiar lever "key" and sounder — turned Morse's concept into a workable system. Historians have long debated the respective contributions of Morse and Vail to the dot-and-dash code that bears Morse's name; Vail's 1845 book The American Electro Magnetic Telegraph described the system, and his family later argued that he devised the alphabetic code.

Later life

Vail operated the early demonstration lines and the Washington–Baltimore line that carried the famous "What hath God wrought" message in May 1844. Disappointed by his returns and by Morse's lack of recognition, he left the telegraph business in 1848 and spent his last years researching his family genealogy. He died in Morristown in 1859. His papers and apparatus are preserved by the Smithsonian Institution and at Historic Speedwell.
