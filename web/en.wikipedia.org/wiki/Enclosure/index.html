Enclosure

Enclosure (or inclosure) is the legal process in England of consolidating (enclosing) small landholdings into larger farms, and fencing off or hedging previously open fields and common land. Once enclosed, the land use became restricted to the owner, and it ceased to be common land for communal use. Between the 16th and 19th centuries enclosure transformed the English landscape and rural society, replacing the medieval open-field system of scattered strips with compact hedged fields.

History

Enclosure proceeded in waves. Early Tudor enclosures, often converting arable land to sheep pasture for the profitable wool trade, provoked riots and prompted anti-enclosure legislation; Thomas More's Utopia (1516) famously protested that sheep "eat up and swallow down the very men themselves". Kett's Rebellion of 1549 was partly a protest against enclosure. A second, much larger wave came with parliamentary enclosure from about 1750: over 4,000 individual Inclosure Acts enclosed roughly a fifth of England, typically replacing strip farming with efficient rectangular fields.

Social consequences

Historians debate enclosure's effects. It is generally credited with raising agricultural productivity — enabling crop rotation, selective breeding and drainage — and contributing to the Agricultural Revolution that fed a growing industrial workforce. Against this, commoners lost customary rights of grazing, fuel gathering and gleaning; those without legal title to their strips often received little compensation. Critics from the Levellers and Diggers of the 1640s to Karl Marx, who treated enclosure as a paradigm of "primitive accumulation", saw it as expropriation that drove the rural poor into wage labour.

Landscape and legacy

Parliamentary enclosure produced the characteristic patchwork of small hedged fields across much of lowland England, with straight roads, new farmsteads in the fields, and surviving "ridge and furrow" earthworks of the old strips beneath pasture. Villages such as Laxton in Nottinghamshire, which retains its open fields, became exceptions. The Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 later granted public access to some former common land.
