Clancy of the Overflow

"Clancy of the Overflow" is a poem by Australian bush poet Banjo Paterson, first published in The Bulletin magazine on 21 December 1889. In the poem, a city clerk writes to a drover named Clancy whom he had met on a shearing trip, addressing the letter simply to "Clancy, of The Overflow" (a sheep station). The reply comes back scrawled in pencil "by a thumbnail dipped in tar", and the clerk daydreams of swapping his dreary city office for Clancy's free life in the bush. The poem is one of Paterson's best-loved works and a classic of Australian bush balladry.

Paterson's poem contrasts the drover's open life with the city clerk's office.

Background

Paterson is said to have based Clancy on Thomas Gerald Clancy, a drover he met, though the identity of the model is debated; the Overflow is a real locality in central New South Wales associated with large pastoral runs. The poem contrasts the freedom of the drover — "the drover's life has pleasures that the townsfolk never know" — with the "foetid air and gritty" city and the clerk's "dingy little office".

Legacy

Clancy became an enduring figure of the Australian bush legend. Paterson brought him back in later poems, and the character appears in the 1982 film The Man from Snowy River (played by Jack Thompson). The poem is widely anthologised, set to music by folk performers, and appears on Australian cultural memorabilia; Clancy's name is commemorated in places and institutions including the Clancy Catholic College in Sydney and locations in the Overflow area.

Text

The poem opens: "I had written him a letter which I had, for want of better / Knowledge, sent to where I met him down the Lachlan, years ago..." It runs to eight stanzas of galloping anapaestic verse typical of Paterson's ballads.
