Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem

In mathematics, the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem of Paul Erdős, Chao Ko, and Richard Rado (1961) is a theorem in extremal set theory about intersecting set families. It states that if n ≥ 2k, then any family of k-element subsets of an n-element set, all of whose members pairwise intersect, has at most C(n−1, k−1) members. This bound is attained by the "star": the family of all k-sets containing one fixed element.

Statement

Let A be a set of n elements and let F be a family of subsets of A, each of size k, such that any two members of F have a non-empty intersection. If n ≥ 2k, then |F| ≤ C(n−1, k−1). When n > 2k, equality holds only for stars (all sets through a common point); when n = 2k, the optimal families consist of choosing exactly one set from each complementary pair.

History and proofs

Erdős, Ko and Rado proved the theorem in 1938 but did not publish it until 1961. Their original proof used induction ("shifting"); a celebrated short proof by Gyula Katona (1972) used the circle method: place the n points on a circle, note that at most k of the arcs of length k can belong to an intersecting family, and average over all cyclic orderings. A linear-algebra proof was given by Philippe Frankl. The theorem launched much of extremal combinatorics, including the complete intersection theorems of Ahlswede and Khachatrian, and the Hilton–Milner theorem describing the largest intersecting families that are not stars.

Analogues

Versions of the theorem exist for permutations (Deza–Frankl), vector spaces over finite fields (the q-analogue), signed sets, and graphs, where for example the Kneser graph's independence number captures the set-family statement. The Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem should not be confused with the Erdős–Szekeres theorem on monotone subsequences and convex polygons, a different result in combinatorial geometry and Ramsey theory.
