Zapotecs

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Zapotec people

Self-name: Be'ena'a ("The People")

Region: Oaxaca, Mexico (Valley of Oaxaca, Sierra Norte, Isthmus of Tehuantepec)

Languages: Zapotec languages, Spanish

Population: several hundred thousand speakers of Zapotec languages

The Zapotecs (Zapotec: Be'ena'a, "The People"; in the Valley also Ben Zaa, "Cloud People") are an Indigenous people of Mexico, concentrated in the state of Oaxaca. Their civilization, which flourished in the Valley of Oaxaca from about 700 BCE, was one of the earliest and most influential cultures of Mesoamerica.

Prehispanic civilization

The Zapotec founded Monte Albán, built on a flattened hilltop above the Valley of Oaxaca around 500 BCE, which became one of the first true cities in Mesoamerica and the capital of a regional state. Zapotec society developed an early writing system — among the earliest in the Americas — along with a 260-day ritual calendar, monumental platform architecture, ballcourts, and elaborate tombs. Later Zapotec centers included Mitla, Lambityeco, and Zaachila. After Monte Albán's decline after about 900 CE, Zapotec city-states continued until the Spanish conquest in the 1520s.

Language

The Zapotec languages form a diverse branch of the Oto-Manguean family, with dozens of often mutually unintelligible varieties spoken in the Valley, the Sierra Norte, and the Isthmus of Tehuantepec. They are tonal languages, and collectively they have several hundred thousand speakers, making them among the most widely spoken Indigenous languages of Mexico.

Modern Zapotec communities

Today Zapotec communities maintain distinctive traditions of weaving (notably the wool rugs of Teotitlán del Valle), pottery (such as the black clay barro negro of San Bartolo Coyotepec), alebrijes, and cuisine. The most famous Zapotec in Mexican history is Benito Juárez, the 19th-century president of Mexico, born in the Sierra village of San Pablo Guelatao.
