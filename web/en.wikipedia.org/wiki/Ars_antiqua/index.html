Ars antiqua

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Early polyphonic notation from the Notre Dame school

Ars antiqua, also called ars veterum or ars vetus, is a term used by modern scholars to refer to the Medieval music of Europe during the High Middle Ages, between approximately 1170 and 1310. This covers the period of the Notre Dame school of polyphony and the subsequent years which saw the early development of the motet, a highly varied choral musical composition.

The term ars antiqua is used in opposition to ars nova (meaning "new art", "new technique" or "new style"), the period of musical activity beginning roughly in 1310 which marked the beginning of the musical Renaissance in the fourteenth century.

History

Nearly all composers of the ars antiqua are anonymous. Léonin (fl. late 12th century) and Pérotin (fl. c. 1200) are the two composers known by name from the Notre Dame school. In the subsequent period, the names of Petrus de Cruce, a composer of motets, and a number of anonymous composers represented in the Montpellier Codex are known.

In music theory, the period saw several advances over earlier practice in rhythm, harmony, and notation. The most famous music theorist of the ars antiqua was Franco of Cologne, whose treatise Ars cantus mensurabilis (c. 1280) clearly defined a system in which the values of notes could be unambiguously defined in relation to each other. Another famous theorist was Johannes de Garlandia, who wrote about the earlier Notre Dame practice in his treatise De mensurabili musica (about 1240).
