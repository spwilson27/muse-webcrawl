Buffer strip

A buffer strip is an area of land maintained in permanent vegetation that helps to control air, soil, and water quality, along with other environmental problems, dealing primarily with land used in agriculture. Buffer strips trap sediment and enhance filtration of nutrients and pesticides by slowing down surface runoff that could enter the local surface water. The root systems of the planted vegetation in these buffers hold soil particles together, counteracting soil erosion, and the vegetation provides habitat for wildlife.

Types

Common types include grassed waterways, filter strips, contour buffer strips planted along hillside contours, field borders, windbreaks and shelterbelts, and riparian buffers along streams and rivers. Riparian buffers typically combine grasses, shrubs, and trees in zones of increasing width nearest the watercourse, and are among the most effective at protecting water quality.

Functions

Buffer strips slow runoff, allowing suspended sediment to settle out before reaching streams, which reduces turbidity and the transport of sediment-bound phosphorus and pesticides. Plant roots and soil microbes absorb or break down dissolved nutrients such as nitrogen. Buffers also stabilise streambanks, moderate water temperature through shading, and create corridors and habitat for birds, insects, and aquatic organisms.

Design and management

Effectiveness depends on width, vegetation type, slope, and soil. Strips must be maintained: accumulated sediment should be removed periodically, vegetation harvested or mowed where appropriate, and concentrated flow that cuts channels through the buffer must be dispersed, since channelised flow bypasses filtration. Many governments support buffer installation through agri-environment schemes, such as the United States Conservation Reserve Program and European Union cross-compliance rules that require buffer strips along watercourses on farmland.
