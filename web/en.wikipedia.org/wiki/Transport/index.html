Transport

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transport (in British English) or transportation (in American English) is the intentional movement of humans, animals, and goods from one location to another. Modes of transport include air, land (rail and road), water, cable, pipelines, and space. The field can be divided into infrastructure, vehicles, and operations. Transport enables human trade, which is essential for the development of civilizations.

Transport infrastructure consists of both fixed installations, including roads, railways, airways, canals, and pipelines, and terminals such as airports, railway stations, bus stations, warehouses, trucking terminals, refueling depots, and seaports. Terminals may be used both for the interchange of passengers and cargo and for maintenance.

Modes

Land

Road transport is the most widely used mode for short and medium distances, offering door-to-door flexibility at the cost of congestion and relatively high per-unit emissions. Rail transport moves large volumes of freight and passengers efficiently over land, and underpins mass transit in dense cities. Animal-powered and human-powered transport — walking, cycling, rickshaws — remain important worldwide and are the least polluting modes.

Water

Shipping carries the great majority of world trade by volume: bulk carriers, tankers, and container ships move goods between seaports at the lowest cost and emissions per tonne-kilometre, but slowly. Inland waterways, ferries, and passenger ships also play regional roles.

Air

Aviation is the fastest mode and dominates long-distance passenger travel and high-value, time-sensitive freight, but it is the most energy- and emissions-intensive per passenger-kilometre.

Pipeline and other modes

Pipelines move liquids and gases — oil, natural gas, water — continuously over long distances. Cable transport, conveyors, and elevators serve specialized niches, and spaceflight moves satellites and crews beyond the atmosphere.

History

Early humans travelled on foot and domesticated animals from around 4000 BC. The wheel, sailing vessels, and paved roads transformed ancient trade; canals and then steam railways and steamships powered the first industrial revolution. The internal combustion engine and the automobile reshaped twentieth-century cities and suburbs, while the jet age after the Second World War made intercontinental travel routine. Containerization, introduced in the 1950s, slashed the cost of freight and enabled modern global supply chains.

Impacts

Transport is a major source of greenhouse gas emissions — roughly a seventh of the global total — as well as air pollution, noise, and road casualties. It also delivers large benefits: access to jobs, education, healthcare, and markets. Contemporary policy debates concern decarbonization through electrification and modal shift, congestion pricing, public transit investment, and the land-use patterns that determine how much travel people need.
