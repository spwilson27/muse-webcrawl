Compressed air

Compressed air is air kept under a pressure that is greater than atmospheric pressure. It serves many domestic and industrial purposes, and compressed air systems are sometimes described as the "fourth utility" of industry after electricity, natural gas, and water. Air is compressed by an air compressor, stored in receiver vessels, and distributed through piping to the point of use.

Uses

  • Pneumatic tools — drills, grinders, impact wrenches, nail guns, and paint sprayers.
  • Industry and automation — actuators, valves, conveying, packaging machinery, and process control.
  • Transport — air brakes on trains and heavy vehicles, tyre inflation, starting diesel engines, and experimental compressed-air vehicles.
  • Diving and breathing — filling scuba cylinders and supplying breathing air (with suitable filtration and purity standards).
  • Energy storage — compressed-air energy storage (CAES) in underground caverns, used to smooth electricity supply and demand.
  • Other — air horns, airbrushes, cleaning (with safety limits), dentistry, and HVAC controls.

System characteristics

Typical industrial systems operate at around 7 bar (100 psi). Compressed air is a comparatively expensive energy carrier: much of the electrical energy input is lost as heat during compression, so leak prevention, correct pressure settings, and heat recovery are major efficiency measures. Air quality matters for many uses; standards such as ISO 8573 define purity classes for particles, water, and oil, and delivered air is usually dried and filtered. Condensate must be drained from receivers and low points.

Safety

Compressed air can cause serious injury if misused: high-pressure jets can penetrate skin and cause air embolism, particles can damage eyes, and pressure vessels require inspection and pressure-relief protection under regulations such as the UK Pressure Systems Safety Regulations and ASME codes. Hoses should be secured against whipping if couplings fail.

History

Bellows provided small-scale compressed air from ancient times. Industrial use expanded in the 19th century with steam-driven compressors: Paris operated a municipal compressed-air power network from the 1880s, and pneumatic rock drills transformed mining and tunnelling, famously in the Mont Cenis Tunnel works. Electric-motor compressors made compressed air a standard factory utility in the 20th century.
