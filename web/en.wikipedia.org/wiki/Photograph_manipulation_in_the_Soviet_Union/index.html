Photograph manipulation in the Soviet Union

Photograph manipulation in the Soviet Union was the practice of altering photographs for political and propaganda purposes, most notoriously the removal of persons who had fallen from favour — so-called damnatio memoriae — from official images. Under Joseph Stalin especially, censors retouched, airbrushed, and recomposed photographs to rewrite the visual record of the revolution and the regime.

Famous cases

The best-known example shows Nikolai Yezhov, head of the NKVD during the Great Purge, standing beside Stalin at the Moscow Canal; after Yezhov's own arrest and execution in 1940, he was airbrushed out of the photograph entirely. Other images were altered to remove Leon Trotsky and other disgraced figures from scenes alongside Lenin, to add Stalin to events he had not attended prominently, or to delete officials such as those purged in the 1930s from group portraits. The famous flag-raising photograph over the Reichstag was also adjusted (a second watch on a soldier's wrist — suggesting looting — was removed, and smoke added).

Context

Manipulation ranged from retouching faces and removing wrinkles to wholesale recomposition of scenes. As purged individuals became "nonpersons", librarians and citizens were expected to alter books and photographs in their own possession. The practice became a widely recognised symbol of totalitarian historical revisionism and was extensively documented after the opening of Soviet archives; photographer and historian David King assembled many before-and-after pairs in his book The Commissar Vanishes (1997).
