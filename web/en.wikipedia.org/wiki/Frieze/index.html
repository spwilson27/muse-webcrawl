Frieze

In architecture, the frieze is the wide central section of an entablature, situated between the architrave below and the cornice above. In the classical orders, the frieze may be plain, as in the Tuscan and Roman Doric orders, or decorated: in the Greek Doric order it is divided into triglyphs and metopes, while in the Ionic and Corinthian orders it is often a continuous band sculpted with relief ornament, figures or foliage.

History and use

The term derives, through French and medieval Latin, from Phrygium, referring to the embroidery of Phrygia, though its association with the architectural element is indirect. The most celebrated example from antiquity is the Parthenon frieze, a roughly 160-metre-long low-relief sculpture that ran around the exterior of the cella of the Parthenon in Athens, depicting the Panathenaic procession; most of it is now displayed in the British Museum in London and the Acropolis Museum in Athens.

By extension, "frieze" is used for any broad horizontal band of decoration near the top of a wall, inside or outside a building, and for the decorated band on furniture, textiles and other objects. In interior decoration, a frieze may be painted, stencilled, plastered or made of wallpaper.
