Electrocution

Electrocution is death or severe injury by electric shock from electric current passing through the body. The word is derived from "electro" and "execution", coined in the United States around 1889 to describe the newly introduced method of capital punishment using an electric chair; it later broadened to include accidental deaths by electricity.

Causes

Fatal electric shocks are usually caused by current passing through the heart or chest, triggering ventricular fibrillation or respiratory arrest, or by severe thermal burns from high-voltage contact. Household-voltage electrocutions are relatively uncommon in countries with modern wiring standards but remain a hazard from damaged appliances, water contact, and improvised repairs; occupational contact with power lines accounts for many high-voltage deaths.

Capital punishment

The electric chair was first used in New York in 1890 for the execution of William Kemmler and was adopted by many U.S. states as a supposedly more humane alternative to hanging. It has largely been replaced by lethal injection, though it remains an option in a few states. The Philippines used the electric chair from 1926 to 1976.

Prevention

Residual-current devices, grounding, insulation standards, and public education about overhead lines and electrical safety have substantially reduced electrocution rates over the twentieth century.
