Action theory (philosophy)

Action theory is a branch of philosophy concerned with the analysis of action: what distinguishes actions from other kinds of events, how actions are caused and explained, and what intentions, reasons and agency amount to. It is closely connected with the philosophy of mind, ethics and the theory of free will.

Main issues

Action theorists ask how bodily movements become actions when they are intentional under some description; whether reasons are causes, as Donald Davidson argued in "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" (1963); how to individuate actions; and how agents deliberate and exercise control. Elizabeth Anscombe's Intention (1957) revived the concept of intention and introduced the notion of actions being known "without observation".

Schools and applications

Approaches include causal theories of action, teleological and narrative accounts, and work on shared or collective agency. Action theory also informs artificial intelligence and robotics, where formal models of goal-directed behaviour are required, and social theory, where Talcott Parsons and others developed theories of social action.
