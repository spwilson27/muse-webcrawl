Hindu philosophy

The six orthodox schools (darshanas) of Hindu philosophy

Hindu philosophy or Vedic philosophy is the set of philosophical traditions that emerged in ancient India and developed in dialogue with the religious literature of Hinduism, above all the Vedas and the Upanishads. It is conventionally divided into six orthodox (astika) schools, which accept the authority of the Vedas, and several heterodox (nastika) schools, such as Buddhism and Jainism, which do not. The six orthodox schools are Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Purva Mimamsa and Vedanta.

Shared concepts

Despite their differences, the Hindu schools share a family of concepts: dharma (duty, order), karma (action and its consequences), samsara (the cycle of rebirth), moksha (liberation from that cycle) and atman (the self or soul). Most schools also accept the four aims of human life (purusharthas): dharma, artha (prosperity), kama (pleasure) and moksha. Theories of knowledge (pramana)—perception, inference, comparison, testimony and others—are a central preoccupation, and the schools debate how many valid means of knowledge there are.

The six orthodox schools

Nyaya, founded on the Nyaya Sutras attributed to Akshapada Gautama, is a school of logic and epistemology that developed sophisticated theories of inference and debate. Vaisheshika, associated with Kanada, is an atomistic natural philosophy that classifies reality into categories (padarthas) such as substance, quality and motion. Samkhya, traditionally founded by Kapila, is a dualism of consciousness (purusha) and matter (prakriti), the latter evolving through three strands or gunas. Yoga, codified in Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, shares Samkhya metaphysics but adds meditative practice and devotion to Ishvara as the path to liberation. Purva Mimamsa, founded by Jaimini, defends the authority of Vedic ritual and a realist, pluralist metaphysics. Vedanta, the most influential school in later Hindu thought, interprets the Upanishads and produced divergent readings: Shankara's non-dualism (Advaita), Ramanuja's qualified non-dualism (Vishishtadvaita) and Madhva's dualism (Dvaita).

History

Hindu philosophy developed over more than two millennia, from Vedic speculation and the early Upanishads, through the systematising sutra literature of the first centuries CE, to the commentarial traditions of the medieval period and vigorous inter-school debate with Buddhist and Jain philosophers. In the modern era, thinkers such as Swami Vivekananda and Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan presented Vedanta to a global audience, and Hindu philosophical ideas entered comparative philosophy worldwide.
