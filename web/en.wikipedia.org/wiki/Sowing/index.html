Sowing is the process of planting seeds. An area or object in which seeds are sown can be called a seedbed. In agriculture, sowing is typically done after the soil has been prepared by ploughing or other tillage, and the timing, depth and spacing of the seed determine the success of germination and the eventual crop.

Methods

  • Broadcasting — scattering seed by hand or with a mechanical spreader over the surface of the soil. It is the oldest and simplest method but gives uneven distribution and exposes seed to birds and drying.
  • Drilling — placing seed in furrows at a controlled depth and spacing using a seed drill, a machine developed in recognisable form by Jethro Tull in 1701. Drilling uses less seed and gives more uniform stands.
  • Dibbling — placing individual seeds in holes made with a dibble or by hand, used for large seeds such as maize and beans.
  • Hill dropping and check rowing — planting seeds in groups at regular intervals to permit cross-cultivation.

Factors

Successful sowing depends on seed quality (germination percentage and vigour), sowing depth (generally two to three times the seed's diameter), soil moisture and temperature, and protection from pests. Sowing into a stale seedbed, or using precision planters that singulate seed, are refinements used in modern practice. In no-till systems, seed is drilled directly into undisturbed soil through the residue of the previous crop.

Cultural references

Sowing is a frequent metaphor in religious and literary texts, most famously the Parable of the Sower in the New Testament, and the proverb "you reap what you sow". Jean-François Millet's painting The Sower (1850) is an iconic image of peasant labour.
