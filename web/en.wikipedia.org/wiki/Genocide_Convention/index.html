Genocide Convention

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (CPPCG), known as the Genocide Convention, is an international treaty that criminalises genocide and obligates parties to enforce its prohibition. Passed by the United Nations General Assembly on 9 December 1948 — the first human rights treaty adopted by the UN, one day before the Universal Declaration — it entered into force in 1951 and has been ratified by the overwhelming majority of states (around 150).

Definition and enforcement

Article II defines genocide as acts (killing, causing serious bodily or mental harm, inflicting destructive conditions of life, preventing births, forcibly transferring children) committed "with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such" — Raphael Lemkin's concept narrowed by negotiation (political groups excluded at Soviet insistence, cultural genocide dropped). Article I's duties to prevent and punish have been litigated at the ICJ (Bosnia v. Serbia 2007, finding a prevention duty breached; Croatia v. Serbia 2015; The Gambia v. Myanmar over the Rohingya; Ukraine v. Russia; South Africa v. Israel, with provisional-measures orders from 2024) and the definition's near-verbatim adoption into the ad hoc tribunals' and Rome Statutes made it the operative legal definition worldwide — while the dolus specialis (special intent) requirement keeps most mass atrocities legally labelled crimes against humanity instead, a gap between law and language that politics exploits.
