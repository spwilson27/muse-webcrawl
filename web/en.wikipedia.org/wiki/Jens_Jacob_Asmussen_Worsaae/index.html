Worsaae, founder of modern scientific archaeology in Denmark.

Jens Jacob Asmussen Worsaae (March 14, 1821 – August 15, 1885) was a Danish archaeologist and historian, one of the founders of scientific archaeology. As assistant to Christian Jürgensen Thomsen at the Museum of Northern Antiquities in Copenhagen, he provided the stratigraphic field evidence that confirmed Thomsen's three-age system — the division of prehistory into the Stone, Bronze, and Iron Ages — and went on to direct Danish antiquarian institutions and serve briefly as Denmark's Minister of Culture.

Work and the three-age system

Worsaae began working at the museum while still a schoolboy volunteer. His first book, Danmarks Oldtid oplyst ved Oldsager og Gravhøie ("The Primeval Antiquities of Denmark," 1843), published when he was only twenty-two and soon translated into English, set out the three-age scheme on the basis of controlled excavation of burial mounds and bog finds, showing that artifacts of stone, bronze, and iron occurred in consistent, superposed strata. He argued — against romantic nationalist readings — that Denmark's earliest inhabitants had not been ethnic Danes but an earlier people, an inference from the archaeology that provoked controversy amid rising Danish-German tension over Schleswig.

Later career

With royal support Worsaae traveled widely — to Sweden, Germany, France, Britain, and Ireland — comparing collections and promoting the Danish system internationally. He excavated the great Viking ship burial at Jelling's royal mounds, studied bog bodies and shell middens (the kitchen-midden investigations with Steenstrup and Forchhammer established the Mesolithic Ertebølle culture), and campaigned successfully for state protection of ancient monuments. He became director of the Museum of Northern Antiquities in 1866 and served as Minister of Culture (Kultusminister) in 1874–1875.

Legacy

Worsaae is remembered as the first professional archaeologist to demonstrate prehistoric chronology from stratigraphy, a methodological step that made the three-age system the framework for European prehistory. His insistence that artifacts be interpreted in context, and his public-facing books, helped turn archaeology from antiquarian collecting into an evidence-based discipline.
