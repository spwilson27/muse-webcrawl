Gastritis


Endoscopic appearance of gastritis.

Gastritis is inflammation of the lining of the stomach. It may occur as a short episode (acute) or may be of long duration (chronic). Symptoms, when present, include upper abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, bloating, loss of appetite and heartburn; many cases have no symptoms. Complications may include stomach bleeding, stomach ulcers, and stomach tumours. When gastritis is due to an autoimmune problem, low red blood cells due to vitamin B12 deficiency (pernicious anaemia) may occur.

Causes

Common causes include infection with the bacterium Helicobacter pylori — the single most frequent cause worldwide — and use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and alcohol. Less common causes include autoimmune disease, bile reflux, cocaine use, and severe illness or stress (stress gastritis). H. pylori colonises the gastric mucosa and, over decades, can progress from chronic gastritis through atrophy and intestinal metaplasia to gastric cancer.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis may involve blood tests, stool tests, urea breath testing for H. pylori, and upper endoscopy with biopsy. Treatment depends on the cause: H. pylori is eradicated with combination antibiotic therapy plus a proton-pump inhibitor; NSAID-related gastritis is treated by stopping the drug and acid suppression. Avoiding alcohol and irritants, and treating underlying conditions, are also recommended.
