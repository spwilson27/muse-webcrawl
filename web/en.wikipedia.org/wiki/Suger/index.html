Suger (French: [syʒɛʁ]; c. 1081 – 13 January 1151) was a French abbot, statesman, and historian. He was one of the earliest patrons of Gothic architecture, and his rebuilding of the Basilica of Saint-Denis is widely considered to mark the beginning of the Gothic style.

Life

Of humble origins, Suger was educated at the abbey school of Saint-Denis, where he met the future King Louis VI. He became abbot of Saint-Denis in 1122 and served as a close adviser to both Louis VI and Louis VII, effectively acting as a leading minister of the French crown. He reformed the administration of the abbey's estates, greatly increasing its revenues, and wrote histories and memoirs that are major sources for the period, including a life of Louis VI.

During Louis VII's absence on the Second Crusade (1147–1149), Suger served as regent of France, managing the kingdom's affairs with notable competence and suppressing a conspiracy by the king's brother.

Saint-Denis and Gothic architecture

Between 1135 and 1144 Suger rebuilt the abbey church of Saint-Denis, the burial place of the French kings. His new west facade and, above all, the choir consecrated in 1144 employed pointed arches, ribbed vaults and large stained-glass windows in a coherent synthesis that art historians regard as the first truly Gothic structure. Suger articulated a theology of light, drawing on the writings attributed to Dionysius the Areopagite, in which material light leads the mind toward divine illumination — his concept of lux continua.

Legacy

Suger died at Saint-Denis in 1151. His writings, his administrative reforms and his architectural patronage made him one of the most consequential churchmen of 12th-century France, and Saint-Denis became the model for the great Gothic cathedrals that followed.
