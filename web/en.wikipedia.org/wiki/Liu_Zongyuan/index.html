Liu Zongyuan (773 – November 28, 819), courtesy name Zihou, was a Chinese writer, poet and government official of the Tang dynasty. Along with Han Yu, he was a founder of the Classical Prose Movement (guwen), which advocated a return to a plainer, more direct style modelled on the prose of antiquity, and he is counted among the Eight Masters of the Tang and Song. He is also one of the most celebrated landscape writers in Chinese literature.

Life

Liu passed the jinshi examination at the age of twenty and rose quickly in the capital, becoming part of the reformist circle around Wang Shuwen that briefly held power under Emperor Shunzong in 805. When the reforms collapsed, Liu was demoted and exiled first to Yongzhou in Hunan, where he spent ten years, and then, in 815, still further south to Liuzhou in Guangxi. Though the exiles blighted his career, they produced his finest writing: the "Eight Records of Yongzhou", a series of melancholy essays on the wild scenery around his place of banishment that effectively created a new genre of Chinese travel and landscape prose. His fables and allegories — such as the tales of the snake-catcher and the gardener — used small subjects to criticise corrupt government. In Liuzhou he governed benevolently, freeing slaves and promoting education, and after his death there the local people built a temple in his honour; the city still remembers him in the street name Liuhou ("Lord Liu"). His five-character poem "River Snow" (Jiangxue), describing a lone fisherman in a snowy landscape, is among the best-known short poems in the language.
