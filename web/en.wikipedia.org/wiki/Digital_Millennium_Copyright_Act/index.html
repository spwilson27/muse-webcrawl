Digital Millennium Copyright Act

Digital Millennium Copyright Act
Enacted by105th United States Congress
SignedOctober 28, 1998, by President Bill Clinton
CodificationAmends Title 17, United States Code
Key sections§512 (safe harbours), §1201 (anti-circumvention)

The Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) is a 1998 United States copyright law that implements two 1996 treaties of the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO). It criminalises production and dissemination of technology, devices, or services intended to circumvent measures that control access to copyrighted works (commonly known as digital rights management or DRM). It also increases the penalties for copyright infringement on the Internet.

Provisions

  • Title I — implements the WIPO treaties, including the §1201 anti-circumvention rules, subject to triennial exemptions granted by the Librarian of Congress (e.g. for phone unlocking and accessibility).
  • Title II — the Online Copyright Infringement Liability Limitation Act — creates §512 safe harbours shielding online service providers from liability for users' infringement if they expeditiously remove infringing material on receiving a takedown notice.
  • Titles III–V — cover matters including distance education, libraries' preservation copies, and vessel hull design protection.

Criticism

The DMCA has been criticised for chilling security research and interoperability (as in the arrest of Dmitry Sklyarov and the threat against Edward Felten's team), for takedown abuse, and for impeding repair and competition — debates that have driven repeated §1201 exemption rulemakings.
