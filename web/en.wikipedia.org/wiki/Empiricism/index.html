Empiricism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Empiricism is a theory of knowledge that holds that knowledge comes only or primarily from sensory experience: the mind is not stocked with innate ideas but built up from what the senses deliver. In the history of philosophy it is associated above all with the early-modern British empiricists — John Locke, George Berkeley and David Hume — and contrasted with the continental rationalism of Descartes, Spinoza and Leibniz. Empiricism is the philosophical ancestor of the scientific method's insistence on observation and experiment, and of later movements from positivism to logical empiricism.

The British empiricists

Locke argued in his Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1689) that the mind at birth is a blank slate (tabula rasa) on which experience writes, distinguishing simple ideas passively received from complex ideas the mind builds. Berkeley radicalised the doctrine into idealism: matter unperceived is a fiction — esse est percipi, "to be is to be perceived". Hume pushed empiricism to its sceptical limit: we have impressions and fainter copies of them (ideas); causal necessity is never observed, only constant conjunction, so induction rests on habit, not reason — the "problem of induction" that Kant said woke him from dogmatic slumber. Empiricism remains a default stance in science and a contested one in philosophy of mind and mathematics, where some truths (logic, arithmetic) seem to be known independently of experience.
