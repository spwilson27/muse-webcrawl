Adhesion railway

An adhesion railway relies on adhesion traction to move the train and is the most widely used form of rail transport. Adhesion traction means that the locomotive's driving wheels adhere to the smooth tops of the rails; the train's motion depends on the friction between steel wheels and steel rails. The term is used in contrast to railways that use additional mechanisms for traction, such as the rack railways used on steep mountain grades, funiculars and cable railways, and systems propelled by a moving cable or linear motor.

Physics of adhesion

The coefficient of friction between clean, dry steel wheel and rail is typically around 0.25–0.4 in creep conditions, but the usable coefficient of adhesion for a locomotive is usually only about 0.15–0.25 depending on conditions, because any contamination — water, oil, frost, or crushed leaves — dramatically reduces grip. The maximum tractive effort an adhesion locomotive can exert is the product of the weight on its driving wheels (the adhesive weight) and the coefficient of adhesion; this is why freight locomotives are built heavy and why designers quote a "factor of adhesion" (adhesive weight divided by tractive effort) of about 4 or more.

Limitations

Because tractive force is limited by friction, adhesion railways are restricted to relatively gentle gradients — usually no more than about 1 in 40 (2.5%) for freight and somewhat steeper for passenger lines; the steepest adhesion-worked main lines in the world reach around 1 in 14–1 in 17 only for short, lightly loaded sections. Slipping can occur when starting a heavy train, especially on contaminated rail, and is mitigated by sanding the rails, by wheel-slip control systems on modern traction units, and by railhead treatment trains in autumn "leaf fall" season, when "leaves on the line" famously disrupt services.

Comparison with other systems

Where gradients are too steep for adhesion, engineers have used rack-and-pinion systems (as on the Pilatus Railway, the steepest rack line at 48%), cable haulage, or the Fell system with a raised centre rail gripped by horizontal wheels. Despite these alternatives, the simplicity and efficiency of the plain steel wheel on a steel rail have kept the adhesion railway dominant since the early 19th century, from the first public steam railways to modern high-speed rail.
