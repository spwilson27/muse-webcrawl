International Fixed Calendar

The International Fixed Calendar (IFC) is a proposed calendar reform consisting of 13 months of 28 days each. It is a perennial calendar: the same date always falls on the same day of the week in every year, because each month is exactly four weeks long. The 13 months total 364 days; an extra day, "Year Day", is added at the end of the year and does not belong to any week or month, and in leap years a second such day, "Leap Day", is inserted at the end of June.

The additional month, placed between June and July, is called Sol. Because every month begins on a Sunday and ends on a Saturday, dates and weekdays are permanently aligned: the 1st, 8th, 15th and 22nd of every month are always Sundays, and so on.

History

The design was popularised by Moses B. Cotsworth, a British railway statistician who founded the International Fixed Calendar League in 1923 to campaign for its adoption. The proposal attracted support from business for the regularity it would bring to accounting periods and statistics. Its most prominent backer was George Eastman, founder of Eastman Kodak, whose company used a 13-month calendar internally for accounting from 1928 to 1989. The League of Nations considered the International Fixed Calendar among hundreds of reform proposals in the 1930s, but no international agreement was reached, partly because of religious objections to the "blank days" (Year Day and Leap Day) that interrupt the continuous seven-day week — a serious obstacle for Sabbath observance in Judaism, Christianity and Islam.

Advantages and disadvantages

Supporters cite the equality of months, the permanent alignment of dates with weekdays, and simplified interest and statistical calculations. Critics note that 13 is not divisible into halves, quarters or thirds, complicating quarterly reporting; that birthdays and holidays would fall on new dates; and that the blank days break the unbroken weekly cycle maintained since antiquity.
