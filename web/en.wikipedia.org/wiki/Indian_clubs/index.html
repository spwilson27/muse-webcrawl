Indian clubs

Indian clubs, or Indian pins, are a type of exercise equipment used for developing strength and mobility, consisting of bottle-shaped clubs swung in coordinated patterns. They take their name from the training of Indian soldiers (sepoys) under British officers in the 19th century; similar heavy clubs had long been used in India, Persia (the meel), and elsewhere.

History

British officers adopted club swinging from Indian regimental gymnasiums and promoted it for military training. The practice spread to the United States, where Sim D. Kehoe's 1866 manual The Indian Clubs and How to Use Them sparked a craze; clubs were ubiquitous in gymnasiums, and club swinging even appeared at the 1904 Olympic Games as part of gymnastics competition. Indian clubs were standard-issue exercise equipment for the U.S. Army into the early 20th century.

Use

Clubs are swung in arcs and circles around the body and shoulders in rhythmic routines, often performed by groups in synchrony. Modern revivalists use both light traditional clubs for mobility and shoulder health and heavier clubs for strength, related to Persian meels and the Indian mudgar. Practitioners credit the swinging patterns with improving grip, shoulder range of motion, and coordination.

Decline and revival

Club training fell out of fashion in the mid-20th century as machine and barbell training took over, but interest revived in the 2000s within the functional-fitness and historical physical culture movements.
