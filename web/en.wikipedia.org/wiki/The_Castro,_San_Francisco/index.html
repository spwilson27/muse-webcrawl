The Castro is a neighborhood in Eureka Valley in San Francisco, California. It was one of the first gay neighborhoods in the United States and remains one of the most prominent symbols of lesbian, gay, bisexual, and transgender (LGBT) activism and events in the world. The neighborhood is centered on Castro Street, named after José Castro, a Californio leader of Mexican Alta California.

History

The area was originally part of the Eureka Valley district, settled in the late 19th century largely by Scandinavian and Irish working-class families. After World War II, many residents moved to the suburbs, and the district's inexpensive Victorian houses attracted gay men in the 1960s and 1970s, following the Summer of Love and the growth of the nearby Haight-Ashbury scene. By the mid-1970s the Castro had become a center of gay life, with bars, businesses, and political organizations.

In 1977, Harvey Milk, a Castro camera-shop owner, was elected to the San Francisco Board of Supervisors, becoming one of the first openly gay elected officials in the United States. Milk and Mayor George Moscone were assassinated in 1978 by former supervisor Dan White; the lenient sentence given to White sparked the White Night riots of 1979. During the 1980s the neighborhood was deeply affected by the HIV/AIDS epidemic.

Landmarks

Landmarks include the Castro Theatre, a 1922 Spanish Colonial Revival movie palace; Harvey Milk Plaza; the rainbow flag at Market and Castro, first raised in 1978; Pink Triangle Park; and the GLBT Historical Society Museum, opened in 2011. The rainbow crosswalks at 18th and Castro were installed in 2014.
