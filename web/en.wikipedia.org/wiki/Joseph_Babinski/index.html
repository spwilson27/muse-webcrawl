Joseph Babinski

Joseph Jules François Félix Babinski (Polish: Józef Julian Franciszek Feliks Babiński; 17 November 1857 – 29 October 1932) was a French neurologist of Polish descent. He is best known for the Babinski sign (Babinski reflex), a diagnostic indicator of damage to the corticospinal tract in adults, described in 1896.

Life and career

Babinski was born in Paris to Polish émigré parents; his father had fled Poland after the failed uprisings against Russian rule. He studied medicine in Paris and became a pupil and chief assistant (chef de clinique) of Jean-Martin Charcot at the Salpêtrière Hospital. After Charcot's death, Babinski never obtained a full university chair — he failed the agrégation examination in circumstances widely regarded as unfair — and instead built his career at the Hôpital de la Pitié, where he remained for decades.

The Babinski sign

In a brief 1896 communication, Babinski reported that stroking the sole of the foot produces dorsiflexion (upward movement) of the big toe, often with fanning of the other toes, in patients with lesions of the pyramidal (corticospinal) tract, whereas the normal adult response is plantar flexion. In infants the extensor response is normal until the tract matures. The sign remains one of the most widely used bedside tests in neurology.

Other contributions

Babinski worked extensively on distinguishing organic neurological disease from hysteria, coining the term "pithiatism" for disorders produced by suggestion. His name is also attached to the Babinski–Fröhlich (adiposogenital) syndrome and the Babinski–Nageotte syndrome of medullary infarction. He mentored a generation of neurologists and helped establish neurology as a discipline based on precise clinical observation.
