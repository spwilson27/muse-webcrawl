Byrhtnoth (Old English: Byrhtnōð; died 991) was an Anglo-Saxon ealdorman of Essex who was killed leading English forces against a Viking army at the Battle of Maldon on 11 August 991. His defiance and death are celebrated in the Old English poem The Battle of Maldon, which preserves the most famous statement of the heroic ideal in Anglo-Saxon literature.

Life

Byrhtnoth rose to prominence under King Edgar and was appointed ealdorman of Essex in about 956, a post he held for some thirty-five years — an exceptionally long tenure. He was a leading magnate of the reign of Æthelred II, a generous patron of monasteries, particularly Ely Abbey, to which he and his wife Ælfflæd gave estates, treasures, and a tapestry depicting his deeds. By 991 he was an elderly man, described in later Ely tradition as tall and white-haired.

The Battle of Maldon

In August 991 a Viking fleet — the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle gives 93 ships, possibly under Olaf Tryggvason — sacked Ipswich and moved on Maldon, encamping on Northey Island in the Blackwater estuary, connected to the mainland by a tidal causeway. Byrhtnoth led the Essex fyrd to hold the mainland end. According to the poem, a Viking herald demanded tribute in exchange for peace; Byrhtnoth refused, declaring that the English would pay with spears and swords. When the tide prevented battle, the Vikings asked leave to cross the causeway, and Byrhtnoth — in the poem's word, through ofermod, overmastering pride — allowed them over so the fight could be joined. In the ensuing battle Byrhtnoth was killed, many of his household warriors died fighting around his body, and the English were defeated. King Æthelred's government subsequently paid the first recorded Danegeld, 10,000 pounds of silver.

Legacy

Byrhtnoth's body was recovered and buried at Ely, where his bones were later exhumed and measured, suggesting a man of about six feet tall. The poem fragment celebrating his stand — including the lines "Thought must be the harder, heart the keener, mood the more, as our might lessens" — has made him an enduring emblem of courage in defeat. A statue by John Doubleday, erected at Maldon in 2006, depicts him sword in hand at the end of the causeway.
