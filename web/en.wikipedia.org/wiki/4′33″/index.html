4′33″ (pronounced "four minutes, thirty-three seconds" or just "four thirty-three") is a three-movement composition by American experimental composer John Cage. Written in 1952 for any instrument or combination of instruments, its score instructs the performers not to play their instruments for the entire duration of the piece. The work's content is not silence but the sounds of the environment heard by the audience during the performance.

Composition and premiere

Cage conceived the piece after experiences in an anechoic chamber, where he heard his own nervous system and blood circulation, concluding that true silence does not exist. Influenced by Robert Rauschenberg's white paintings and by Zen Buddhism, he structured the work in three movements whose lengths in the premiere totalled 4 minutes 33 seconds. David Tudor premiered it at the Maverick Concert Hall in Woodstock, New York, marking the movements by closing and opening the piano lid.

Reception and influence

The premiere scandalised its audience, but 4′33″ became Cage's most famous and controversial work. It challenged fundamental assumptions about music and listening, arguing that any sound may constitute music, and it influenced generations of composers, artists and the Fluxus movement. In 2004 a performance by the BBC Symphony Orchestra was broadcast live on BBC Radio 3, and in 2010 a campaign pushed the piece toward the UK Christmas number one.

Legal disputes

The piece has generated disputes over its status as music, most notably a 2002 settlement in which composer Mike Batt credited Cage for a silent track on an album, demonstrating how seriously the work's copyright is treated.
