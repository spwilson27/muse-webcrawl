2013 budget sequestration


Across-the-board cuts under the 2013 sequester (placeholder image)

The budget sequestration of 2013 was a package of automatic, largely across-the-board cuts to United States federal spending that took effect on 1 March 2013. It arose from the Budget Control Act of 2011, passed during the debt-ceiling crisis, which set up "sequestration" as a penalty intended to be so unpalatable that it would force a bipartisan deficit-reduction agreement by the Joint Select Committee on Deficit Reduction (the "supercommittee").

Background and implementation

When the supercommittee failed to reach agreement in November 2011, the sequester was scheduled for January 2013; the American Taxpayer Relief Act delayed it by two months and slightly reduced its first-year size. The 2013 cuts amounted to roughly $85 billion in the first year, split approximately evenly between defence and non-defence spending, with Medicare provider payments reduced by 2 per cent and Social Security and Medicaid exempted.

Effects

Consequences included furloughs of federal employees, including air traffic controllers (whose resulting flight delays prompted a quick legislative fix), reductions in military readiness spending, cuts to research grants, unemployment benefits and public housing assistance. The Congressional Budget Office estimated the sequester would reduce employment by several hundred thousand jobs. The sequestration caps persisted, in modified form, for a decade, though subsequent budget deals in 2013, 2015, 2018 and 2019 raised the caps substantially.
