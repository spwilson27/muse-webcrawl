ADSR

The four stages of an ADSR envelope: attack, decay, sustain and release.

ADSR envelope
StagesAttack, Decay, Sustain, Release
Used inSynthesisers, samplers, effects

ADSR is a four-stage envelope model used in synthesisers and samplers to shape how a sound evolves over time. The acronym stands for Attack, Decay, Sustain and Release: when a key is pressed, the envelope rises during the attack, falls back during the decay to the sustain level, holds there while the key is down, and finally returns to zero during the release after the key is let go. The envelope most often controls amplifier gain, but the same shape is routinely applied to filter cutoff frequency or oscillator pitch.

The four stages

Attack is the time taken for the level to rise from zero to its peak after the note begins: a piano-like sound has a near-instant attack, while a swelling string pad uses a slow one. Decay is the time taken to fall from the peak to the sustain level. Sustain is unusual among the four stages in being a level rather than a time — the amplitude held while the key remains pressed; percussive sounds set sustain to zero so the envelope dies away regardless. Release is the time taken to fall from the sustain level to zero once the key is released, giving sounds anything from an abrupt stop to a long reverberant fade.

History

Envelope generators were central modules in the modular synthesisers of the 1960s, such as those of Moog and ARP, where the ADSR pattern was standardised with four knobs — a layout popularised on the Minimoog (whose simpler ADS/contour generator still exemplified the concept) and on countless later instruments. Vladimir Ussachevsky and others had earlier shaped sound envelopes on tape, but voltage-controlled ADSR generators made the technique playable in real time.

Variants

Many instruments extend or simplify the model: AHDSR adds a hold stage; DADSR adds an initial delay; multi-segment "breakpoint" envelopes in digital synths generalise ADSR to arbitrary shapes; and simple AR or AD envelopes suffice for drums and effects. In modern software synths, envelopes are usually drawn graphically and can be looped, tempo-synchronised or used as general modulation sources.
