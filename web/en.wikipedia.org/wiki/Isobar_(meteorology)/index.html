Isobar (meteorology)

An isobar (from Ancient Greek ἴσος ísos 'equal' and βάρος báros 'weight') is a line of equal or constant pressure on a graph, plot, or map. On a surface weather chart, isobars connect points at which the atmospheric pressure, usually reduced to mean sea level, is the same at a given time.

Use in weather analysis

Isobars are the principal tool of synoptic meteorology. Closed isobars encircle areas of high pressure (anticyclones, marked H) and low pressure (cyclones, marked L). The spacing of isobars indicates the pressure gradient: closely packed isobars mark a steep gradient and strong winds, while widely spaced isobars indicate light winds. Wind blows roughly parallel to the isobars, deflected toward low pressure by friction at the surface—an empirical relationship described by Buys Ballot's law.

Drawing and intervals

Pressures are plotted from station observations and reduced to sea level so that the effects of altitude are removed. On most operational charts isobars are drawn at intervals of 4 hectopascals (for example 996, 1000, 1004 hPa). Computer models generate isobaric analyses automatically, but manual analysis remains a training standard for forecasters.

Isobaric surfaces

In upper-air meteorology, meteorologists instead analyse charts of constant pressure surfaces (such as the 500 hPa level), drawing contours of the height of that surface. These height contours play a role analogous to isobars in surface analysis.
