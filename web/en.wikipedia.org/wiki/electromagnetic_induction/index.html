Electromagnetic induction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Electromagnetic induction is the production of an electromotive force (voltage) across an electrical conductor in a changing magnetic field. It was discovered by Michael Faraday in 1831 and, independently, by Joseph Henry in 1832. Faraday's experiments showed that moving a magnet through a coil of wire, or changing the current in a nearby coil, induces a current in the circuit.

Faraday's law

Faraday's law of induction states that the induced electromotive force in any closed circuit is equal to the negative of the time rate of change of the magnetic flux through the circuit:

ℰ = − dΦB / dt

where ΦB is the magnetic flux. The negative sign expresses Lenz's law: the induced current flows in a direction such that the magnetic field it creates opposes the change in flux that produced it. For a coil of N turns, the induced EMF is N times the rate of change of flux through one turn.

Maxwell–Faraday equation

In Maxwell's equations, electromagnetic induction is described by the Maxwell–Faraday equation, which states that a time-varying magnetic field is always accompanied by a spatially varying, non-conservative electric field, and vice versa. It is one of the four equations that form the foundation of classical electromagnetism.

Applications

Electromagnetic induction is the operating principle behind many electrical devices:

  • Electric generators convert mechanical energy into electrical energy by rotating coils in magnetic fields (or magnets past coils).
  • Transformers transfer alternating-current energy between circuits through mutual induction.
  • Induction motors use rotating magnetic fields to induce currents in a rotor.
  • Induction cooktops induce eddy currents in cookware, heating it directly.
  • Wireless charging and inductive sensors exploit near-field coupling between coils.

History

Faraday's breakthrough of 29 August 1831 — wrapping two wires around opposite sides of an iron ring and observing a transient current when one circuit was connected or disconnected — is often taken as the founding experiment. His subsequent work with rotating copper discs produced the first homopolar generator.
