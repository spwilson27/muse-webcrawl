Affective neuroscience

Affective neuroscience is the study of how the brain creates emotional responses. The field, which integrates psychology, neuroscience, and evolutionary biology, was given its name by Jaak Panksepp in the 1990s. It seeks to identify the neural systems underlying basic emotions and to relate them to behavior and mental health.

Core emotional systems

Panksepp proposed seven primary emotional systems conserved across mammals — SEEKING, RAGE, FEAR, LUST, CARE, PANIC/GRIEF, and PLAY — each associated with distinct subcortical circuits and neurochemistry. Other researchers emphasize structures such as the amygdala in threat processing, the insula in interoception and disgust, and prefrontal regions in emotion regulation.

Methods

Research methods include brain imaging (fMRI, PET), lesion studies, intracranial stimulation, and animal models in which the neural circuits of emotion can be studied more directly than in humans. Affective neuroscience informs treatments for depression, anxiety, and addiction, and is debated by constructionist theorists who argue that emotions are assembled from more basic psychological ingredients rather than dedicated circuits.
