Serpentine subgroup

The serpentine subgroup is a group of sheet silicate minerals (phyllosilicates) formed by the hydrothermal alteration (serpentinization) of magnesium-rich ultramafic rocks such as peridotite. The principal members are antigorite, chrysotile and lizardite, all with the approximate chemical formula Mg3Si2O5(OH)4.

Structure and properties

Serpentine minerals have a layered structure in which a sheet of linked silica tetrahedra is bonded to a sheet of magnesium-centred octahedra. Because the two sheets have slightly different dimensions, the structure accommodates the mismatch in different ways: in chrysotile the layers curl into microscopic tubes, giving it a fibrous habit; in lizardite the layers remain flat; and in antigorite the structure is corrugated or modulated.

Serpentine minerals are soft (Mohs hardness roughly 2.5–4), typically green, yellowish or brown, and have a greasy or waxy lustre, a soapy feel, and perfect cleavage in the platy varieties. They are common in metamorphosed oceanic crust and in ophiolite complexes.

Uses

Chrysotile is the most commonly used form of asbestos, formerly mined on a large scale for insulation and fireproofing; its use is now heavily restricted because inhaled fibres are hazardous to health. Massive serpentine rock is cut and polished as a decorative stone, sometimes sold as "serpentine marble" or verde antique.
