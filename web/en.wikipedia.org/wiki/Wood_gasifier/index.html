Wood gasifier

A wood gasifier is a device that converts carbonaceous material such as wood or charcoal into wood gas (producer gas), a mixture of nitrogen, carbon monoxide, hydrogen, and traces of other gases, through a thermochemical process called gasification. The resulting gas can be burned in an internal combustion engine, used to generate electricity, or burned directly for heat.

Principle

Inside the gasifier, wood undergoes a sequence of reactions in limited air: drying, pyrolysis, partial combustion (oxidation), and reduction. In the reduction zone, carbon dioxide and steam react with glowing charcoal to produce carbon monoxide and hydrogen — the combustible components of the gas. The gas is filtered and cooled before reaching the engine, since it carries tar, ash, and particulates.

History

Gasification was demonstrated in the 19th century and producer gas was used industrially for heat and lighting. Wood gas rose to prominence as vehicle fuel during the Second World War, when petroleum was rationed: roughly a million vehicles in Europe, Asia, and Australia ran on wood gas generators by the mid-1940s, including cars, trucks, buses, and tractors. The French Imbert downdraft design became the most common vehicle type.

Modern use

Wood gasifiers are used today in off-grid and biomass energy applications, in combined heat and power units, and by hobbyists and preparedness communities. The US Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) published a simplified gasifier construction manual in 1989 for emergency use. Disadvantages include reduced engine power (roughly 30–50% loss), bulky equipment, tar handling, and carbon monoxide toxicity.
