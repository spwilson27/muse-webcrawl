Plesiosaurus

Plesiosaurus (Greek: πλησίος plesios, "near to"; σαῦρος sauros, "lizard") is a genus of extinct marine sauropterygian reptile that lived during the Early Jurassic period, roughly 200 to 175 million years ago. It gave its name to the larger order Plesiosauria, the long-necked marine reptiles that flourished throughout the Mesozoic era.

Discovery

The first complete skeleton of Plesiosaurus was discovered in December 1823 by the fossil collector Mary Anning in the Lower Jurassic rocks at Lyme Regis, Dorset, England. The specimen was described by William Conybeare, who named the species Plesiosaurus dolichodeirus ("long-necked"). Its anatomy struck contemporaries as bizarre — Georges Cuvier initially suspected the fossil might be a fake because of its extraordinarily long neck of 35 vertebrae.

Description

Plesiosaurus was a relatively small plesiosaur, about 3 to 5 metres (10–16 ft) long. It had a small head, a long slender neck, a broad body, a short tail, and two pairs of large paddle-like flippers. It swam by flapping its flippers in a motion often compared to underwater flight, similar to sea turtles and penguins. Its sharp, conical teeth interlocked when the jaws closed, forming a trap for fish and cephalopods such as belemnites.

Palaeobiology

Like other plesiosaurs, Plesiosaurus was fully aquatic and could not come ashore; fossil evidence shows plesiosaurs gave birth to live young at sea. Its long neck may have been used to approach prey from below without creating a bow wave, or to probe for food in soft sediment. Gastroliths (swallowed stones) found in some specimens may have aided buoyancy control or digestion.

Classification

Plesiosaurus belongs to the family Plesiosauridae within the Plesiosauroidea. Over the years, dozens of species were assigned to the genus, but most have been moved to other genera, leaving P. dolichodeirus as the only widely accepted species.

In popular culture

As one of the first great marine reptiles known to science, Plesiosaurus became an iconic prehistoric animal; long-necked plesiosaurs are frequently proposed (without scientific support) as an explanation for the Loch Ness Monster.
