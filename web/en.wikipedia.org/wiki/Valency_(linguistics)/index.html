Valency (linguistics)

In linguistics, valency (or valence) is the number and type of arguments controlled by a predicate, principally a verb. The term borrows a metaphor from chemistry, where valency describes how atoms combine; in grammar it was systematically developed by Lucien Tesnière in his dependency grammar, who compared the verb to an atom with hooks on which a fixed number of dependents ("actants") can attach.

Valency classes

Verbs are commonly classified by the number of core arguments they take:

  • Avalent (zero arguments): weather verbs in languages like English that require a dummy subject (it rains).
  • Monovalent (one argument): intransitive verbs such as sleep in She sleeps.
  • Divalent (two arguments): typical transitive verbs such as hit in She hit the ball.
  • Trivalent (three arguments): ditransitive verbs such as give in She gave him the book.

A distinction is usually drawn between obligatory arguments and optional adjuncts: She ate the sandwich in the park has a divalent verb plus an adjunct, because in the park can be omitted freely.

Valency-changing operations

Languages have grammatical means of adjusting a verb's valency. Valency-increasing operations include causatives and applicatives, which add an argument; valency-decreasing operations include passives, antipassives, reflexives and object omission, which remove or demote one. Valency patterns are central to theories of argument structure, case marking and the syntax–semantics interface, and they differ in detail across languages — for example, the verb meaning "rain" is genuinely avalent in Spanish (llueve) but requires an expletive subject in English.
