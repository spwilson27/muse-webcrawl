Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd

Case information
Citation[1897] AC 22
CourtHouse of Lords
Decided16 November 1896
JudgesLord Halsbury LC, Lord Herschell, Lord Watson, Lord Macnaghten, Lord Morris, Lord Davey
KeywordsSeparate legal personality, incorporation, limited liability

Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] AC 22 is a landmark United Kingdom company law case decided by the House of Lords. The unanimous judgment established the principle that a duly incorporated company is a separate legal person from its shareholders and directors, even where one individual effectively owns and controls the whole enterprise. The decision remains the foundation of modern company law throughout the common law world.

Facts

Aron Salomon was a leather merchant and boot manufacturer in Whitechapel, London, who had traded successfully as a sole proprietor. In 1892 he incorporated his business as A Salomon & Co Ltd. To satisfy the requirement of the Companies Act 1862 for seven shareholders, the subscribers were Salomon himself, his wife, his daughter, and his four sons, each holding a single share while Salomon held 20,001 shares. The company purchased his business for about £39,000, a sum partly satisfied in shares and partly in debentures secured over the company's assets.

The leather trade then fell into depression. The company went into liquidation owing substantial sums to unsecured creditors, while the secured debentures held by Salomon (later transferred to a Mr Broderip, who advanced £5,000 on their security) stood to absorb the company's assets. The liquidator argued that the company was a mere agent or trustee for Salomon, that the incorporation was a device to defeat creditors, and that Salomon should indemnify the company and pay its debts personally.

Judgment

At first instance, Vaughan Williams J accepted the agency argument; the Court of Appeal went further, denouncing the incorporation as a scheme contrary to the true intent of the Companies Acts. The House of Lords reversed both courts. Lord Halsbury LC held that once a company is legally incorporated it must be treated "like any other independent person with its rights and liabilities appropriate to itself," regardless of the motives of those who formed it. Lord Macnaghten famously observed that the company is "a different person altogether" from its subscribers, and that its property is not in law the property of its members.

Significance

The case confirmed that the privileges of incorporation and limited liability are available to one-person ventures and small family businesses as much as to large enterprises. It rejected any inquiry into the adequacy of the consideration for shares or the proportion held by a dominant shareholder. Later cases, such as Macaura v Northern Assurance Co Ltd [1925] and Lee v Lee's Air Farming Ltd [1961], applied the principle, while the doctrine of lifting or piercing the corporate veil developed as a narrow exception. Salomon is conventionally the first case studied in company law courses across the Commonwealth.
