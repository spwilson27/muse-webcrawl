Strigolactone

Strigolactones are a group of chemical compounds produced by roots of plants. They are a class of plant hormones that stimulate the branching and growth of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, and also regulate shoot branching in the plant itself, acting largely by preventing bud outgrowth. Strigolactones also play a role in the germination of parasitic plants that prey on plant roots, most notably the genus Striga, from which they take their name.

History

Strigolactones were first identified in the 1960s as germination stimulants exuded by cotton roots that triggered suicidal germination of the parasitic witchweed Striga. Strigol, the first compound isolated, was characterized in 1966. Only much later, in 2005, were strigolactones shown to induce hyphal branching in arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, and in 2008 two groups independently demonstrated that they function as an endogenous plant hormone inhibiting shoot branching.

Structure and biosynthesis

Strigolactones are carotenoid-derived compounds with a characteristic tricyclic lactone (ABC rings) connected by an enol-ether bridge to a methylbutenolide (D ring), which is essential for biological activity. They are synthesized from carotenoids via carlactone, through the action of enzymes including DWARF27, CCD7 (MAX3), and CCD8 (MAX4). Signalling occurs through the α/β-hydrolase receptor DWARF14 (D14) and the F-box protein MAX2/D3.

Ecological role

Plants release strigolactones into the rhizosphere especially under phosphate starvation, attracting mycorrhizal fungi that aid nutrient uptake. The same signal is exploited by parasitic weeds such as Striga and Orobanche, a serious problem for agriculture in Africa and elsewhere, prompting research into synthetic analogues such as GR24 and "suicidal germination" control strategies.
