High-dynamic-range television

High-dynamic-range television (HDR-TV) is a technology that improves the quality of televised and display signals compared with standard dynamic range (SDR). HDR technology is used in televisions, monitors, laptops, and mobile displays, and is characterised by greater brightness, deeper blacks, a wider colour gamut, and more detailed gradations of shadow and highlight.

Overview

Conventional SDR television, based on the Rec. 709 standard, was designed around cathode-ray tube displays with peak brightness around 100 nits. HDR systems increase peak luminance to 1,000 nits or more while also lowering the black level, and use 10-bit or 12-bit colour depth to avoid banding across the wider range. The electro-optical transfer functions used, such as Perceptual Quantizer (PQ) and Hybrid Log-Gamma (HLG), are defined in the ITU-R BT.2100 recommendation.

Formats

Several competing HDR formats exist. HDR10 is an open standard using static metadata and the PQ curve. HDR10+, developed by Samsung, adds dynamic metadata that can vary scene by scene. Dolby Vision is a proprietary format supporting up to 12-bit colour and dynamic metadata. HLG, developed by the BBC and NHK, is designed for broadcast and is backward compatible with SDR displays. Technicolor Advanced HDR is another broadcast-oriented system.

History

The first HDR-capable televisions reached the consumer market in 2015 and 2016, alongside the launch of Ultra HD Blu-ray, which made HDR10 mandatory. Streaming services such as Netflix and Amazon Prime Video began offering HDR catalogues shortly afterwards. Broadcast trials followed, including the BBC's HLG coverage of major sporting events.
