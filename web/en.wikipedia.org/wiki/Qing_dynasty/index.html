Qing dynasty

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Qianlong Emperor in court dress, from an imperial portrait]

The Qing dynasty (1644–1912) was the last imperial dynasty of China, founded by the Manchu people of the north-east. At its height in the 18th century the Qing ruled the largest territory in Chinese history and perhaps the world's largest economy, with a population exceeding 400 million by 1850. Its long decline through the 19th century — Opium Wars, the Taiping Rebellion, foreign encroachment and failed reform — ended with the 1911 Revolution and the abdication of the last emperor, Puyi, in 1912.

Conquest and high Qing

Manchu forces took Beijing in 1644 as the Ming collapsed, then spent decades subduing Ming loyalists, Taiwan and the Zunghar Mongols. The Kangxi (r. 1661–1722), Yongzheng (1722–1735) and Qianlong (1735–1796) emperors presided over prosperity, territorial expansion into Tibet, Xinjiang and Mongolia, and great scholarly projects — alongside the queue hairstyle imposed on Han men as a symbol of submission and literary inquisitions against sedition.

Decline and fall

Defeat in the First Opium War (1839–1842) ceded Hong Kong to Britain and opened treaty ports; the Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864) killed perhaps 20–30 million people. Self-Strengthening reforms failed to match Japan, which humiliated China in 1895 and took Taiwan; the Boxer Uprising (1900) brought foreign occupation of Beijing. Revolutionary movements led by Sun Yat-sen overthrew the dynasty in 1911–12, ending two millennia of imperial rule.
