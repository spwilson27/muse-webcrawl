Sunny 16 rule

The sunny 16 rule estimates daylight exposure without a meter

In photography, the sunny 16 rule (also known as the sunny f/16 rule) is a simple method of estimating correct daylight exposure without using a light meter. On a sunny day, setting the aperture to f/16 and the shutter speed to the reciprocal of the film speed (or ISO setting) gives approximately correct exposure for a subject lit by bright sunlight. For example, with ISO 100 film, one uses f/16 at 1/100 second (or the nearest setting, 1/125).

Variations

The rule is adapted to other lighting conditions by opening the aperture while keeping the shutter speed at the reciprocal of the ISO:

ApertureLighting conditions
f/22Sun on snow or sand
f/16Bright sun, distinct shadows
f/11Slightly overcast, soft shadows
f/8Overcast, no shadows
f/5.6Heavily overcast
f/4Sunset, open shade

Equivalent exposures can be obtained by trading stops: at ISO 100 on a sunny day, f/11 at 1/250 or f/8 at 1/500 expose the same amount of light. A related mnemonic, the "loonyl 11 rule", applies the same logic to photographing the Moon (f/11 at the reciprocal of ISO).

Use

The rule originated in the era of mechanical film cameras and exposure guide tables printed inside film boxes. It remains useful for film photographers, for checking a malfunctioning meter, and for understanding the exposure value system: bright sunlight corresponds to EV 15 at ISO 100. Its accuracy is best for front-lit subjects at mid-latitudes near midday; it is less reliable at dawn, dusk or extreme latitudes.
