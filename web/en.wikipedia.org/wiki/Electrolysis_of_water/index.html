Electrolysis of water is the decomposition of water into hydrogen and oxygen gases using an electric current: 2H2O → 2H2 + O2. It is the principal method of producing high-purity hydrogen and, when powered by low-carbon electricity, yields "green hydrogen" for energy storage, industry and transport. The process was first demonstrated in 1800 by William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle, shortly after Volta announced his battery, and was studied quantitatively by Michael Faraday, whose laws of electrolysis relate gas volumes to charge passed.

Chemistry

Pure water conducts poorly, so an electrolyte — an acid, alkali or salt — is usually added, or an ion-conducting membrane is used. At the cathode, reduction produces hydrogen (in acid: 2H+ + 2e− → H2); at the anode, oxidation produces oxygen (2H2O → O2 + 4H+ + 4e−). Hydrogen is evolved at twice the volume of oxygen, a classic demonstration (the Hofmann voltameter) of water's 2:1 composition. The thermodynamic minimum cell voltage is 1.23 volts at standard conditions; practical cells operate at 1.5–2.0 volts because of overpotentials and resistance, giving efficiencies typically around 60–80 per cent.

Technologies and uses

Commercial systems include mature alkaline electrolysers (liquid KOH electrolyte), proton-exchange membrane (PEM) electrolysers that respond quickly and pair well with variable renewables, and high-temperature solid oxide electrolysers that use steam and waste heat for higher efficiency. Historically, electrolysis supplied hydrogen for airships, ammonia and industrial processes where cheap hydropower existed, and still provides breathing oxygen aboard submarines and the International Space Station. Interest has surged with the hydrogen economy: electrolyser capacity is scaling from megawatts to gigawatt factories as a route to decarbonise steel, chemicals and heavy transport.
