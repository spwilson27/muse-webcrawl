Proper motion

Proper motion is the astrometric measure of the observed changes in the apparent places of stars or other celestial objects in the sky, as seen from the centre of mass of the Solar System, compared to the abstract background of the more distant stars. Proper motion is the angular rate at which an object's position changes across the celestial sphere, conventionally expressed in milliarcseconds per year (mas/yr) and quoted as two components: in right ascension (μα) and in declination (μδ).

Measurement

Proper motion is measured by comparing precise positions of an object at two or more well-separated epochs. Historically this was done with photographic plates separated by decades; modern measurements come from space astrometry missions, above all ESA's Hipparcos (1989–1993) and Gaia (launched 2013), the latter providing proper motions for well over a billion stars. Combined with a parallax (distance) measurement, proper motion yields the object's transverse velocity; combined with a spectroscopically measured radial velocity, it gives the full three-dimensional space motion.

Notable values

Most stars have proper motions of a few tens of milliarcseconds per year. The record holder is Barnard's Star, a nearby red dwarf with a total proper motion of about 10.3 arcseconds per year — roughly the apparent diameter of the full Moon in 180 years. Large proper motions generally indicate either a genuinely fast space velocity or, more often, proximity to the Sun, which is why high-proper-motion surveys have historically been an effective way of finding nearby stars.

History

Proper motion was discovered by Edmond Halley in 1718, when he compared contemporary positions of Sirius, Arcturus, and Aldebaran with those recorded by Hipparchus and found displacements too large to attribute to error. The finding demolished the ancient notion of permanently "fixed stars." In 1738 Jacques Cassini and others confirmed the phenomenon, and by the nineteenth century systematic proper-motion catalogues (such as those of Argelander and the Bonner Durchmusterung tradition) underpinned studies of the Sun's own motion through the Galaxy — the solar apex — and of stellar kinematics generally.
