Native Americans in California

A reconstructed Indigenous village in California

Native Americans in California are the Indigenous peoples who have lived in the region now forming the U.S. state of California for at least 13,000 years. Before European contact, California supported one of the densest and most linguistically diverse Indigenous populations north of Mexico — an estimated 300,000 people speaking as many as 80 to 100 distinct languages.

Pre-contact cultures

California's peoples were organised mostly in small, politically autonomous groups sometimes called tribelets, rather than large confederacies. Major cultural groups included the Chumash of the Santa Barbara coast, famed for plank canoes (tomol); the Pomo, noted for fine basketry; the Yokuts of the San Joaquin Valley; the Hupa, Yurok and Karuk of the northwest coast; the Maidu, Wintu and Miwok of the Sacramento Valley and Sierra foothills; and the Cahuilla, Luiseño and Kumeyaay of the south. Acorns were a staple food across much of the state, supplemented by hunting, fishing, and controlled burning of the landscape.

Spanish and Mexican periods

From 1769 Spain established a chain of 21 missions along the coast, into which tens of thousands of Indigenous people were gathered. Disease, forced labour and cultural disruption caused catastrophic population decline. After Mexican independence in 1821, the missions were secularised in the 1830s, and much mission land passed to private ranchos.

American period and genocide

Following the Mexican–American War and the Gold Rush of 1848, California's Indigenous population collapsed from roughly 150,000 to about 30,000 by 1870 through disease, displacement, enslavement under the 1850 Act for the Government and Protection of Indians, and state-sanctioned militia violence — a catastrophe widely described by historians as the California genocide. In 2019 the Governor of California formally apologised for the state's treatment of Native peoples.

Present day

California today has more than 100 federally recognised tribes, more than any other state, as well as numerous unrecognised communities. Many tribes operate gaming enterprises following the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act 1988, and Native Californian languages and cultural practices are undergoing active revitalisation.
