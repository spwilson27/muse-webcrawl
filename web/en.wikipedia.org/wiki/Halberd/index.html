A halberd is a two-handed polearm weapon that came to prominent use from the 13th to 16th centuries. It consists of an axe blade topped with a spike mounted on a long shaft, with a hook or thorn on the back of the blade for grappling mounted combatants. The halberd combines the striking power of an axe, the thrusting reach of a spear, and a hook capable of pulling horsemen from the saddle.

Design

The head of a halberd typically features a broad axe blade balanced by a rear beak or fluke, a top spike for thrusting, and langets — long metal strips running down the shaft to strengthen it against cuts. Shafts were usually 1.5 to 2 metres long, giving infantry reach against cavalry and armoured opponents.

History

The halberd rose to fame in the hands of Swiss infantry in the 14th and 15th centuries, contributing to victories over Austrian and Burgundian cavalry at battles such as Sempach (1386). It spread across Europe and became a standard weapon of infantry formations alongside the pike. As firearms dominated battlefields in the 17th century, the halberd faded from combat use but survived as a ceremonial weapon; it remains the traditional arm of the Pontifical Swiss Guard at the Vatican.

Related weapons

Similar polearms include the bill, glaive, partisan, and the Chinese ji, an ancient halberd-like weapon combining a spear point with a crescent blade.
