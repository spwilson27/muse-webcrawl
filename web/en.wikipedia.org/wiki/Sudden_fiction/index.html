Sudden fiction

Sudden fiction is a form of short story in which the entire narrative is compressed into a very brief compass, usually around 750 words or fewer, distinguished by the fact that it still aims to deliver a complete story — with a beginning, middle, climax, and resolution — rather than a mere sketch, vignette, or prose poem. The term gained currency in the mid-1980s through a series of anthologies edited by Robert Shapard and James Thomas, beginning with Sudden Fiction: American Short-Short Stories (1986).

History

The short-short story has a long pedigree, from Aesop's fables and the parables of the New Testament through the conte tradition in France and the work of writers such as Ambrose Bierce, Anton Chekhov, O. Henry, and Kafka, whose parables and brief prose pieces often ran to only a page or two. In Latin America the microcuento tradition produced masters like Augusto Monterroso, whose story "El Dinosaurio" — seven words long — is often cited as the shortest story in Spanish literature.

The American anthologies of the 1980s, however, codified "sudden fiction" as a category distinct from the later, looser category of flash fiction. Shapard and Thomas argued that the sudden fiction writer works with the full apparatus of fiction — character, conflict, scene, and resolution — but must create a feeling of narrative completeness within extreme brevity, typically in under 2,000 words and ideally closer to 750.

Characteristics

Writers of sudden fiction emphasize economy: a single scene may stand for an entire relationship, and a few lines of dialogue may carry the weight of an entire backstory. Because there is no room for exposition, sudden fiction often begins in medias res and relies on implication, image, and compression. Unlike the sketch or vignette, which presents a static moment, sudden fiction insists on movement: something must change between the first line and the last.

Related forms

Sudden fiction overlaps with several neighboring forms: flash fiction (generally under 1,000 words), microfiction (often under 300 words), the prose poem, and the vignette. Editors and teachers differ on the boundaries; sudden fiction is generally regarded as the most formally complete of these, retaining the full architecture of the traditional short story at a fraction of the length.
