Talc

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Talc
Hardness: 1 on the Mohs scale (the reference mineral)
Composition: Hydrated magnesium silicate
Uses: Talcum powder, ceramics, paper, paint

Talc is a clay mineral composed of hydrated magnesium silicate, formed by the metamorphism of magnesium-rich rocks. It is famous as the softest known mineral — number 1, the reference point, on the Mohs scale of mineral hardness — slippery and soapy to the touch (its massive form is called soapstone).

Ground talc is talcum powder, long used in cosmetics and baby powder; concern that some talc deposits are contaminated with asbestos fibres, and litigation alleging links between talcum powder and ovarian cancer and mesothelioma, have driven manufacturers (notably Johnson & Johnson, which withdrew its talc-based baby powder worldwide by 2023) toward cornstarch alternatives. Industrial talc fills paper, plastics, paint and ceramics and insulates cables.

Soapstone's softness made it one of humanity's first carving materials — seals in the Indus Valley, pots in Scandinavia, and the Christ the Redeemer statue's mosaic skin in Rio — while talc schists mark the low-grade edges of the mountain belts surveyed in mountain range geology.
