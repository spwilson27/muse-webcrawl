Battle of Kasserine Pass

The Battle of Kasserine Pass was a series of engagements fought from 14 to 25 February 1943 in west-central Tunisia during the Tunisian Campaign of the Second World War. Axis forces under Erwin Rommel inflicted a sharp defeat on the inexperienced American II Corps — the first major battle between American and German troops in the war — before withdrawing in the face of Allied reinforcements and their own failing logistics.

Background

After the Allied landings in French North Africa (Operation Torch, November 1942) and the long Axis build-up in Tunisia, Rommel — retreating from Libya before Montgomery — turned west to strike the Americans before they could threaten his rear. Following the mauling of U.S. forces at Sidi Bou Zid (14–15 February), Rommel's panzers drove on the Kasserine Pass, a 2-mile (3.2 km) gap in the Grand Dorsal chain of the Atlas Mountains defended by American engineers, infantry and artillery under the dispersed and over-centralised command of Major General Lloyd Fredendall.

The battle

On 19 February Rommel probed the pass; on 20 February a combined German–Italian assault, spearheaded by Panzergrenadiers and tanks, burst through it after fierce fighting, routing the defenders and capturing the town of Kasserine. Axis columns fanned out toward Thala and Tébessa, but at Thala they ran into a concentrated British–American artillery line — including U.S. guns rushed up under General Harmon — and were stopped on 22 February. With his supply lines overextended, Allied air power growing, and Montgomery pressing his Mareth Line defences in the south, Rommel broke off the offensive; by 25 February Allied troops had reoccupied the pass.

Aftermath

Kasserine was a humiliation that exposed American weaknesses in leadership, training, dispersion of armour and combined-arms coordination. Fredendall was relieved and replaced by George S. Patton, who — with Omar Bradley as his deputy — imposed strict discipline and retrained II Corps; American performance improved markedly at El Guettar a month later. The shake-up is widely credited with starting the U.S. Army's transformation into an effective fighting force in Europe.
