Harry Lillis "Bing" Crosby Jr. (May 3, 1903 – October 14, 1977) was an American singer and actor. The first multimedia star, he was one of the most popular and influential musical artists of the 20th century, a leader in record sales, radio ratings, and motion picture grosses from 1926 to 1977. His intimate crooning style, made possible by the microphone, influenced later singers including Frank Sinatra, Dean Martin and Elvis Presley.

Career highlights

Crosby began with Paul Whiteman's orchestra as part of the Rhythm Boys and became a solo star in the early 1930s on radio and in films such as The Big Broadcast (1932). He starred with Bob Hope and Dorothy Lamour in the seven popular "Road to..." comedy films (1940–1962) and won the Academy Award for Best Actor as Father O'Malley in Going My Way (1944), reprising the role in The Bells of St. Mary's (1945).

His 1942 recording of Irving Berlin's "White Christmas" is the best-selling single of all time, with estimated sales of over 50 million copies worldwide. Crosby's investment in tape technology — backing the Ampex company — revolutionised radio broadcasting by allowing programs to be pre-recorded and edited.

Personal life and death

Crosby was an avid golfer and sports team owner (a part-owner of the Pittsburgh Pirates baseball team and horse-racing operations at Del Mar). He died of a heart attack on a golf course near Madrid, Spain, in October 1977, shortly after finishing 18 holes.
