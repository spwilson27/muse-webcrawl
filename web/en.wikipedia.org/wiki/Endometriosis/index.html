Endometriosis

Endometriosis is a disease in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus (the endometrium) grows outside the uterus. It most commonly involves the ovaries, fallopian tubes and the tissue lining the pelvis; rarely, endometrial-like tissue may be found beyond the pelvic organs. The displaced tissue continues to act as it normally would — it thickens, breaks down and bleeds with each menstrual cycle — but because it has no way to exit the body, it becomes trapped, causing inflammation, scarring and adhesions.

Common sites of endometriosis include the ovaries, pouch of Douglas and uterosacral ligaments.

Signs and symptoms

The primary symptoms are pelvic pain and infertility. Pain is often associated with menstrual periods (dysmenorrhoea) but may also occur during ovulation, intercourse, bowel movements or urination, and may become chronic. Other symptoms include heavy menstrual bleeding, fatigue, and gastrointestinal upset. Nearly half of affected women have chronic pelvic pain, and up to 30–50% experience infertility. The severity of symptoms correlates poorly with the extent of disease, and diagnosis is frequently delayed by several years.

Causes and mechanism

The cause is not fully understood. The leading theory is retrograde menstruation, proposed by John Sampson in the 1920s, in which menstrual blood flows backwards through the fallopian tubes into the pelvic cavity. However, retrograde menstruation occurs in most women, so additional factors — altered immunity, genetics (the condition runs in families), coelomic metaplasia, and stem-cell and hormonal influences, particularly oestrogen dependence — are thought to determine who develops lesions.

Diagnosis and treatment

Imaging with transvaginal ultrasound or MRI can identify ovarian endometriomas ("chocolate cysts") and deep infiltrating disease, but historically the definitive diagnosis required laparoscopy with biopsy. There is no cure; management focuses on symptom control. First-line treatments include nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and hormonal therapies (combined oral contraceptives, progestins, GnRH analogues and antagonists) that suppress menstruation. Surgery to excise or ablate lesions can relieve pain and improve fertility; hysterectomy with removal of the ovaries is a last resort.

Epidemiology

Endometriosis affects an estimated 10% of women and girls of reproductive age globally — about 190 million people, according to the World Health Organization. It can begin at menarche and usually improves after menopause.
