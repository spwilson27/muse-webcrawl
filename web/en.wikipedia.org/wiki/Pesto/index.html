Pesto (Italian: [ˈpesto]) is a sauce originating in Genoa, the capital city of Liguria, Italy. The name derives from the Italian verb pestare, "to pound" or "to crush," referring to the traditional method of preparation with a mortar and pestle.

Pesto alla genovese

The best-known variety, pesto alla genovese, consists of crushed garlic, pine nuts, coarse salt, and basil leaves, blended with hard cheese such as Parmigiano-Reggiano (and sometimes Pecorino Sardo or Pecorino Romano) and extra-virgin olive oil. Under a protected consortium recipe, the basil should be Genoese basil grown in Liguria. Pesto is traditionally served with trofie or trenette pasta, often with potatoes and green beans cooked in the same water (alla genovese style).

History

Pesto descends from ancient Roman sauces such as moretum, a paste of herbs, cheese, and garlic described by Virgil, and from medieval Ligurian agliata. The basil-based form is first documented in the 19th century, notably in Giovanni Battista Ratto's La Cuciniera Genovese (1863).

Variations

Numerous variations exist: Sicilian pesto alla trapanese (or pesto rosso) uses almonds and tomatoes; Calabrian pesto may include peppers and ricotta. Modern adaptations substitute walnuts, cashews, or sunflower seeds for pine nuts and use other greens such as spinach, arugula, or sun-dried tomatoes. Commercial pestos became widely available internationally in the late 20th century.
