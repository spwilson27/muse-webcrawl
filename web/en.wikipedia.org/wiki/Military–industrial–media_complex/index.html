The military–industrial–media complex is an extension of the concept of the military–industrial complex, adding mass media to the network of relationships between the armed forces and the defence industry. The term is used by critics who argue that news and entertainment media — through ownership links to defence contractors, dependence on official sources, embedded reporting, and the glorification of military themes in film and television — help legitimise high defence spending, arms sales and military interventions in public opinion.

Origins of the idea

President Dwight D. Eisenhower's 1961 farewell address warned of the "military–industrial complex". Later scholars and journalists — including analysts of wartime coverage of Vietnam, the Gulf War and the 2003 invasion of Iraq — argued that media institutions had become structurally integrated into the same system: major media owners historically held stakes in defence firms (General Electric's former ownership of NBC being a frequently cited example), while the Pentagon's public-affairs apparatus, retired officers serving as television analysts, and programmes of embedded journalism shaped war coverage toward official framing.

Entertainment media

The concept also covers entertainment: the US Department of Defense's entertainment liaison offices provide equipment, personnel and locations to film and television productions in exchange for script input, a relationship critics call "militainment". Supporters of the arrangement argue it aids recruitment and accuracy; critics contend it systematically softens depictions of war.
