Upper mantle

Earth's internal structure; the upper mantle lies beneath the crust

The upper mantle of Earth is the layer of silicate rock between the crust and the lower mantle. It extends from the base of the crust (the Moho discontinuity) down to a depth of approximately 410 kilometres, where a phase transition in the mineral olivine marks the top of the transition zone; the upper mantle is sometimes defined to include the transition zone and extend to about 660 km.

Structure

The uppermost, rigid part of the upper mantle, together with the crust, forms the lithosphere, which is broken into tectonic plates. Beneath it lies the weaker, ductile asthenosphere, whose slow convection drives plate tectonics. The upper mantle is composed chiefly of peridotite, dominated by the minerals olivine and pyroxene. With increasing depth, olivine transforms into its high-pressure polymorphs wadsleyite and ringwoodite within the transition zone.

Study

Knowledge of the upper mantle comes from seismology, from mantle xenoliths carried to the surface by volcanic eruptions, and from ophiolites — slices of oceanic lithosphere thrust onto continents.
