Vavilov center

A Vavilov center (also center of origin or centre of diversity) is a geographical area where a group of organisms, either domesticated or wild, first developed its distinctive properties. The concept was developed by the Russian botanist and geneticist Nikolai Vavilov (1887–1943), who argued that the region where a cultivated plant shows the greatest genetic diversity is likely to be its region of origin.

Vavilov's expeditions and theory

Between 1916 and 1940 Vavilov and his colleagues organised more than a hundred collecting expeditions across five continents, assembling the world's largest collection of plant seeds at the Institute of Plant Industry in Leningrad. From the distribution of diversity he identified eight principal world centres of origin of cultivated plants:

  1. The Chinese center (millet, soybean, many fruits and vegetables)
  2. The Indian center (rice, mango, citrus, sugarcane)
  3. The Central Asiatic center (wheat, peas, lentils, hemp)
  4. The Near-Eastern center (wheat, barley, rye, figs, pomegranate)
  5. The Mediterranean center (olives, cabbage, lettuce, broad beans)
  6. The Abyssinian (Ethiopian) center (coffee, sorghum, teff)
  7. The South Mexican and Central American center (maize, common bean, squash, cotton)
  8. The South American (Peruvian–Ecuadorian–Bolivian) center (potato, tomato, quinoa, tobacco)

Later developments

Later researchers, including Jack Harlan, revised and expanded Vavilov's scheme, distinguishing "centers" from "noncenters" and recognising that domestication could occur over broad regions rather than single points. Nevertheless, the Vavilov centers remain fundamental to plant geography, crop genetic resources conservation, and the establishment of gene banks and protected areas for crop wild relatives.
