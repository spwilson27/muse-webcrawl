National delimitation in the Soviet Union

When: Mainly 1924–1936
Where: Soviet Central Asia (also Caucasus)
Result: Uzbek, Turkmen, Tajik, Kazakh and Kyrgyz republics

National delimitation (Russian: национальное размежевание, natsionalnoe razmezhevanie) was the process in the Soviet Union of dividing ethnically diverse territories into national-territorial administrative units — union republics, autonomous republics and autonomous oblasts — each nominally the homeland of a recognised nationality. It was carried out most famously in Central Asia in 1924–25 and refined through the 1930s.

Central Asia

Before 1924, Soviet Central Asia consisted of the Turkestan ASSR and the formally independent Bukharan and Khorezm people's republics. In 1924–25 these were dissolved and replaced by the Uzbek and Turkmen Soviet Socialist Republics, a Tajik ASSR (raised to a union republic in 1929), and Kazakh and Kyrgyz units within the RSFSR (both becoming union republics in 1936). Borders were drawn by commissions using ethnographic and linguistic surveys, though economic factors, irrigation and politics also played a part.

Policy context and legacy

Delimitation formed part of the Bolshevik nationalities policy of korenizatsiya (indigenisation): creating national republics, alphabets, elites and institutions was intended to win non-Russian peoples to Soviet power and to undercut pan-Turkic and pan-Islamic movements. When the USSR dissolved in 1991, the union republics became independent states within essentially their Soviet-drawn borders, leaving legacies such as the enclaves of the Ferghana Valley and inter-republic water disputes.
