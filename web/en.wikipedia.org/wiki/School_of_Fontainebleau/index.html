The School of Fontainebleau (École de Fontainebleau) refers to the artists who worked at the Palace of Fontainebleau under the patronage of Francis I and his successors, in two main phases in the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. Their work introduced Italian Renaissance and Mannerist art to France and created a distinctively French court style.

First School (c. 1530–1570)

Francis I invited the Italian artists Rosso Fiorentino (from 1530) and Francesco Primaticcio (from 1532) to decorate his palace. In the Gallery of Francis I they devised a novel decorative system combining fresco painting with high-relief stucco frames — strapwork cartouches, fruit garlands, and elongated nude figures — that became the hallmark of the school. Niccolò dell'Abate and others carried the style into landscape and mythological painting, and Fontainebleau printmakers spread its imagery across Europe.

Second School (c. 1594–1610)

Under Henry IV, a second generation — including Toussaint Dubreuil, Ambroise Dubois, and Martin Fréminet — revived decoration at Fontainebleau with large mythological and dynastic cycles for the restored palace.

Style and influence

The school's art is characterised by elegant, elongated figures, complex allegory, erotic mythology, and the fusion of painting, stucco, and architecture into a unified interior. It shaped French Renaissance art and design far beyond the palace, influencing goldsmiths, tapestry designers, and printmakers across northern Europe.
