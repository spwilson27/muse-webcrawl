Georges Danton

Georges Jacques Danton (26 October 1759 – 5 April 1794) was a leading figure in the early stages of the French Revolution. A tall, burly and charismatic orator, he was the first president of the Committee of Public Safety, France's de facto executive government during the Reign of Terror's early months. His efforts helped transform France into the first French Republic.

Early life

Danton was born in Arcis-sur-Aube in Champagne, the son of a lawyer. Educated by the Oratorians at Troyes and trained in law, he bought a position as an advocate at the Conseil du Roi in Paris in 1787, financing the purchase by borrowing. He married Antoinette Gabrielle Charpentier in the same year.

The Revolution

When the Revolution began, Danton became the dominant figure in the Cordeliers district of Paris and founded the Cordeliers Club, a center of radical popular politics. A powerful speaker with a booming voice and commanding presence, he was implicated in the insurrection of 10 August 1792 that overthrew the monarchy, and became Minister of Justice in the provisional government that followed.

Faced with the Prussian invasion, Danton rallied France with speeches calling for audacity in the nation's defense:

"To defeat them, we need boldness, more boldness, always boldness, and France will be saved!"

The September Massacres of 1792, in which over a thousand prisoners were killed in Paris, occurred while he held the justice ministry; his responsibility for them remains debated by historians.

Committee of Public Safety and fall

Elected to the National Convention, Danton voted for the death of Louis XVI. In April 1793 he became the leading member of the first Committee of Public Safety, pursuing a pragmatic policy of national defense and compromise with foreign powers. Removed from the committee in July 1793 and replaced by more hard-line figures including Maximilien Robespierre, he later advocated clemency and an end to the Terror, denouncing its excesses through the newspaper Le Vieux Cordelier, edited by his friend Camille Desmoulins.

Arrested on 30 March 1794 on charges of corruption and conspiracy, Danton defended himself so forcefully before the Revolutionary Tribunal that the proceedings were curtailed by decree. He was guillotined on 5 April 1794, along with Desmoulins and other "Indulgents". On the scaffold, he is said to have told the executioner: "Show my head to the people; it is well worth the trouble."

Legacy

Danton's historical reputation has swung between patriot-hero of the Republic and corrupt opportunist. He is one of the most dramatized figures of the Revolution, notably in Georg Büchner's play Danton's Death (1835) and Andrzej Wajda's film Danton (1983).
