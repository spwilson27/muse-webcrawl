Dominical letter

Dominical letter
Letters A–G assigned to the days of a year
Letters usedA, B, C, D, E, F, G
Used forDetermining the day of the week; computing Easter

Dominical letters or Sunday letters are a method used to determine the day of the week for particular dates in the liturgical and perpetual calendars. When the days of a year are lettered A through G in a repeating cycle, beginning with A on 1 January, the dominical letter of the year is the letter that falls on all its Sundays. A common year's calendar is fully described by its single dominical letter; a leap year has two, one for the part of the year before 29 February and one for the part after.

Method

Assign the letter A to 1 January, B to 2 January, and so on, restarting after G. Every day bearing the year's dominical letter is then a Sunday. For example, if 1 January is a Sunday, the dominical letter is A; if 1 January is a Saturday, the Sundays fall on the days lettered B. In a leap year the extra day pushes the letters one step back after February, so a leap year beginning with dominical letter A continues with G (written AG).

Use in the computus

Dominical letters formed part of the traditional computus for calculating the date of Easter and other movable feasts, and they appear in medieval kalendars and in the tables printed in prayer books such as the Book of Common Prayer. Together with the golden number, the dominical letter fixes a year within the 532-year cycle of the Julian calendar's Easter tables.

Modern relevance

Because the Gregorian calendar repeats exactly every 400 years, each year in that cycle has a fixed dominical letter; the fourteen possible year-calendars (seven starting days, with and without a leap day) correspond to the fourteen combinations of dominical letters in use.
