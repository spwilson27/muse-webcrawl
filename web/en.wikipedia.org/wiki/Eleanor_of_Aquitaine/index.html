Eleanor of Aquitaine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Tomb effigy of Eleanor of Aquitaine at Fontevraud Abbey, shown reading a book]

Eleanor of Aquitaine (c. 1124 – 1 April 1204) was Duchess of Aquitaine in her own right, Queen consort of France (1137–1152) as wife of Louis VII, and Queen of England (1154–1189) as wife of Henry II — one of the wealthiest, most powerful and longest-lived women of the Middle Ages. Heiress to the great duchy of south-western France, she went on the Second Crusade, saw her first marriage annulled for consanguinity, married the young Henry of Anjou eight weeks later (joining Aquitaine to the Angevin Empire), bore eight children including Richard the Lionheart and King John, backed her sons' revolt against their father (1173–74) and was imprisoned for fifteen years, then in her sixties and seventies ruled as regent, ransomed Richard, and crossed the Pyrenees at nearly eighty to fetch a granddaughter to marry into France.

Two crowns

Eleanor inherited Aquitaine at fifteen and was married to the French heir within months (1137). The marriage — culturally and temperamentally mismatched, Eleanor's brilliant Poitevin court against Louis' piety — produced two daughters and a crusade (1147–49) marred by scandal at Antioch, where her uncle Raymond's hospitality fed rumours the chroniclers never dropped. Annulment followed in 1152; her remarriage to Henry Plantagenet, eleven years her junior and soon king of England, transferred Aquitaine's allegiance and created the Angevin Empire that hemmed in France — the strategic fact of the next fifty years.

Revolt, captivity and regency

After 1170 Eleanor's court at Poitiers, associated (how literally is debated) with the 'courts of love' of troubadour culture, became the focus of her sons' ambitions; her part in their rebellion of 1173–74 cost her liberty until Henry II's death (1189). Released by Richard, she governed England in his absence, raised his ransom after his capture (1193–94), defended John's succession against her grandson Arthur, and died at Fontevraud in 1204, outliving all but two of her ten children. Her effigy there, reading a book, remains the emblem of medieval queenship's possibilities and limits.
