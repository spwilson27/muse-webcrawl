Kerma culture

The Kerma culture or Kerma kingdom was an early civilisation centred in Kerma, Nubia (in present-day Sudan), that flourished from approximately 2500 BC to about 1500 BC. It was one of the earliest urban centres in sub-Saharan Africa and the first major indigenous state in Nubia, contemporary with, and at times a rival of, ancient Egypt.

History

Archaeological research, notably the long-running excavations of Charles Bonnet and the Swiss mission at Kerma, has shown that the settlement grew from Neolithic pastoralist communities in the fertile Dongola Reach of the Nile. By around 2500 BC a fortified city had emerged, and the culture is conventionally divided into Early, Middle and Classic phases.

During the Classic Kerma period (c. 1750–1500 BC) the kingdom reached its height. Its capital contained monumental mud-brick temples known as deffufas, royal palaces, workshops producing fine pottery and faience, and vast cemeteries with royal tumulus burials accompanied by human and animal sacrifices. Kerma controlled trade routes carrying gold, ivory, ebony and cattle between inner Africa and Egypt.

Relations with Egypt

Kerma's wealth and power made it both a trading partner and a military threat to Egypt. Egyptian texts of the Middle Kingdom describe campaigns against Kush, and the Egyptians built a chain of fortresses at the Second Cataract. During Egypt's Second Intermediate Period, Kerma allied with the Hyksos and expanded northward. Around 1500 BC the New Kingdom pharaoh Thutmose I conquered Kerma, destroying the city and incorporating Nubia into the Egyptian empire.

Material culture

Kerma pottery is renowned for its thin-walled, black-topped red ware, burnished to a high sheen. The culture also produced bronze work, inlaid furniture — most famously the funerary beds and objects recovered from royal tombs — and distinctive faience. Cattle held a central economic and ritual role, reflected in bucrania (cattle skulls) arranged around royal graves.

Legacy

The Kerma kingdom is regarded as the first in a succession of Nubian states that culminated in the later Kingdom of Kush at Napata and Meroë, whose rulers conquered Egypt as its Twenty-fifth Dynasty.
