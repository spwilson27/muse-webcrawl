Pitch (flight)

The pitch axis passes through the wings of an aircraft; rotation about it raises or lowers the nose.

Pitch is one of the three principal angles of rotation of an aircraft (or other vehicle) in flight, describing rotation about the lateral (side-to-side) axis. The other two principal rotations are roll, about the longitudinal axis, and yaw, about the vertical axis. Together the three are sometimes called the aircraft principal axes or the Euler angles of attitude.

Pitch is measured by the angle between the aircraft's longitudinal axis and the horizon. A positive pitch attitude ("nose up") causes the aircraft to climb, other things being equal, while negative pitch ("nose down") causes it to descend. In a conventional fixed-wing aircraft, pitch is controlled primarily by the elevator, a hinged surface on the horizontal stabiliser at the tail. Moving the control column or stick back raises the elevator, pushing the tail down and the nose up; pushing it forward has the opposite effect. On aircraft with a canard or in delta-wing designs without a conventional tail, pitch control is provided by elevons or canard surfaces.

Stability and control

Longitudinal static stability is the tendency of an aircraft, following a disturbance in pitch, to return to its trimmed attitude. It is strongly influenced by the position of the centre of gravity relative to the neutral point: a centre of gravity ahead of the neutral point gives positive stability. The horizontal stabiliser provides the restoring force. If the centre of gravity is too far aft, the aircraft becomes unstable in pitch and difficult or impossible to control.

The trim tab or adjustable stabiliser allows the pilot to set a hands-off pitch attitude. In many modern airliners, fly-by-wire systems automatically manage pitch stability and protect the aircraft against exceeding limits such as the stall angle of attack.

Pitch in other vehicles

The term is also used for ships and submarines, where pitch describes the rotation of the vessel about its transverse axis, contributing to the seaway motion known as pitching, and for spacecraft, where attitude control systems regulate pitch, roll and yaw relative to an orbital frame.
