Service stripe

A service stripe, commonly called a hash mark, is a diagonal stripe worn on the uniform sleeve of enlisted members of the armed forces to denote a specified period of completed service. In the United States Armed Forces the practice dates to the late 18th and early 19th centuries, and service stripes were formalised in Army uniform regulations during the 19th century.

United States usage

In the U.S. Army, an enlisted soldier is authorised one service stripe for every three years of honourable service, worn on the lower left sleeve of the Army Service Uniform. In the U.S. Navy and U.S. Coast Guard, service stripes ("hash marks") denote four years of service each and are worn on the left sleeve; after twelve years of good-conduct service the stripes and rating badges may be worn in gold instead of red. In the U.S. Marine Corps, a service stripe is awarded for every four years of service and is worn on both sleeves of the dress blue uniform by enlisted Marines.

Overseas service bars

Related but distinct, an Overseas Service Bar is a diagonal bar worn on the opposite (right) sleeve of U.S. Army uniforms to denote six months of service in a designated combat theatre, a practice dating to the First World War.
