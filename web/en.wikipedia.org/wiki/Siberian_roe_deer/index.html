Siberian roe deer

Scientific name: Capreolus pygargus
Family: Cervidae
Order: Artiodactyla
Conservation status: Least Concern (IUCN)

The Siberian roe deer, or eastern roe deer (Capreolus pygargus), is a species of roe deer found in northeastern Asia, from the Ural Mountains and the Caucasus through Siberia and Central Asia to Mongolia, northern China, and the Korean Peninsula. It was long considered a subspecies of the European roe deer (Capreolus capreolus), but genetic and morphological studies support its status as a separate species. The Siberian species is distinctly larger, with bigger antlers and a paler winter coat.

Description

Siberian roe deer stand about 85–100 cm at the shoulder and weigh 32–60 kg, roughly a third heavier than their European cousins — an adaptation, consistent with Bergmann's rule, to colder continental climates. Bucks carry antlers of up to 40 cm with widely spaced tines, shed each autumn and regrown by spring. The summer coat is bright reddish-brown; the thick winter coat is grey-brown with a conspicuous white rump patch. Like the European species, Siberian roe deer are browsers of forest edge, steppe, and riverine thickets, and are remarkable jumpers and swimmers.

Subspecies and distribution

Two subspecies are usually recognized: C. p. pygargus in the west (Siberia, the Urals, and the Tien Shan region) and C. p. tianschanicus in parts of Central Asia; some authorities separate additional forms in the Far East and Korea. In the west of its range, in the Urals and the Volga region, the Siberian roe deer meets and hybridizes with the European roe deer. Historically widespread and an important game animal, its populations declined sharply in the 19th and early 20th centuries due to overhunting and habitat change, but recovered under game management across much of Russia and Kazakhstan.

Behaviour

Siberian roe deer are most active at dawn and dusk, living singly or in small family groups in summer and forming larger herds — sometimes in the hundreds — in winter, when some populations migrate over long distances to escape deep snow. The rut takes place in late summer; uniquely among deer, roe deer exhibit embryonic diapause, delaying implantation so that fawns are born in late spring. Fawns are left hidden in vegetation for their first weeks.
