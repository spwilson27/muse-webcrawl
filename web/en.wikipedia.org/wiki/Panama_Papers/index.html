Panama Papers

The Panama Papers (Spanish: Papeles de Panamá) were 11.5 million leaked documents (or 2.6 terabytes of data) published beginning on 3 April 2016. The documents, sourced from the Panamanian law firm Mossack Fonseca, detailed financial and client-attorney information for more than 214,488 offshore entities. The leak exposed the offshore financial dealings of politicians, businesspeople, celebrities, and criminals around the world and remains the largest data leak in history.

The leak

An anonymous source using the name "John Doe" contacted the German newspaper Süddeutsche Zeitung in 2015 and offered the data, asking only for safety precautions. The newspaper shared the documents with the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists (ICIJ), which coordinated a year-long investigation by more than 370 journalists from over 100 media organizations in nearly 80 countries before simultaneous publication in April 2016.

Contents

The files — internal emails, bank records, passports, and incorporation documents dating from the 1970s to 2016 — showed how Mossack Fonseca created shell companies and foundations for clients, sometimes to evade taxes, launder money, or circumvent sanctions. Named figures included associates of Russian president Vladimir Putin, relatives of China's top leaders, and sitting heads of government in Iceland, Pakistan, Ukraine, Saudi Arabia, and the United Kingdom's then-prime minister David Cameron's late father.

Fallout

The revelations forced the resignation of Iceland's prime minister Sigmundur Davíð Gunnlaugsson within days, and Pakistan's prime minister Nawaz Sharif was later disqualified from office. Governments worldwide opened investigations, recovering more than a billion dollars in fines and back taxes in the following years. Mossack Fonseca, citing reputational damage and an "unjustified" media campaign, announced its closure in March 2018. In a manifesto published in May 2016, "John Doe" called for stronger whistleblower protections and action against global inequality.

Legality questions

Owning an offshore company is not in itself illegal, and many entities in the data had legitimate purposes; the papers' significance lay in the secrecy the structures afforded and in the numerous cases where they were used for fraud, tax evasion, money laundering, and sanctions-busting. The leak reshaped global debate about tax havens and beneficial-ownership transparency.
