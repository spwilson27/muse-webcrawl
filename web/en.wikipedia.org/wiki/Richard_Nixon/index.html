Richard Nixon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Richard Milhous Nixon (9 January 1913 – 22 April 1994) was the 37th president of the United States (1969–1974), the only president to resign the office. A California congressman and senator who made his name as an anti-communist investigator, he served as Dwight Eisenhower's vice-president (1953–1961), lost the presidency narrowly to John F. Kennedy in 1960 and the California governorship in 1962, then staged a comeback to win the presidency in 1968 and re-election in a landslide in 1972.

His administration ended the American combat role in Vietnam (“Vietnamisation” and the 1973 Paris accords), opened relations with China (his February 1972 visit), pursued détente and arms control with the Soviet Union (SALT I, the ABM Treaty), and created the Environmental Protection Agency and other domestic programmes. It also waged a “war on drugs”, impounded appropriated funds, and — fatally — covered up the break-in at the Democratic headquarters in the Watergate scandal.

Resignation and legacy

Facing certain impeachment after the Supreme Court ordered release of tapes proving his role in the cover-up (including the “smoking gun” tape of June 1972), Nixon resigned on 9 August 1974; his successor Gerald Ford pardoned him a month later. In later life he wrote and travelled as an elder statesman on foreign policy. Historians weigh his geopolitical achievements and domestic record against Watergate's damage to public trust and his administration's secret bombing of Cambodia and abuses of executive power.
