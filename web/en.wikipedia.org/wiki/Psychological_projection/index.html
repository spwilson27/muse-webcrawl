Psychological projection

Psychological projection is a defense mechanism in which a person attributes their own unacknowledged thoughts, feelings, or motives to another person. By projecting, the individual avoids confronting qualities in themselves that provoke anxiety or shame.

History

The concept was formulated by Sigmund Freud, who described it in correspondence and case studies in the 1890s; his daughter Anna Freud systematized it in The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936). Freud suggested projection underlay phenomena from everyday blaming to paranoia, in which one's own hostile impulses are perceived as persecution by others.

Forms

Common forms include attributive projection (assigning one's own motives to others), complementary projection (assuming others share one's feelings), and projection in intimate relationships, where partners accuse each other of the very behaviors they themselves exhibit. Empirically, social psychology has documented related phenomena such as the false consensus effect and defensive projection in experimental settings.

Criticism and related concepts

Projection is difficult to falsify in its classical psychoanalytic form, and critics note it can be invoked to dismiss legitimate accusations ("projection" is itself sometimes used as an ad hominem). Related concepts include projective identification in object relations theory, in which the projector also pressures the recipient to enact the projected feeling.
