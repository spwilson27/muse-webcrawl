Gloucester Cathedral


Gloucester Cathedral, showing the central tower.

Gloucester Cathedral, formally the Cathedral Church of St Peter and the Holy and Indivisible Trinity, is an Anglican cathedral in Gloucester, England. It stands on the site of an abbey dedicated to Saint Peter founded in around 679, and the present building, largely constructed between 1089 and the fifteenth century, is celebrated as one of the finest examples of English Gothic architecture and as a pioneering site of the Perpendicular style.

History

The Norman abbey church was begun in 1089 under Abbot Serlo, with a massive crypt, an apsidal eastern end and sturdy cylindrical nave piers that still survive. Following a fire and financial difficulties in the thirteenth century, rebuilding accelerated after the murder of King Edward II in 1327 at Berkeley Castle. Edward was buried in the abbey church, and his tomb rapidly became a shrine attracting pilgrims whose offerings funded the remodelling of the south transept and choir in the new Perpendicular Gothic style — among the earliest large-scale uses of that style in England. The great east window, installed around 1350, was one of the largest windows of its time and commemorates the Battle of Crécy. The cloisters, completed by about 1412, contain the earliest surviving fan vaults in England.

At the Dissolution of the Monasteries, the abbey was surrendered in 1540 and refounded by Henry VIII in 1541 as a cathedral of the new Diocese of Gloucester, with John Manstead as its last abbot and first dean.

Architecture

The building combines a Norman core with Gothic remodelling. The central tower, rising to about 69 metres (225 feet), is a landmark of the city. The Lady Chapel, the spacious eastern arm, is a masterpiece of fifteenth-century Perpendicular architecture. The tomb of Edward II lies in the north ambulatory, and the cathedral also contains the tomb of Robert Curthose, eldest son of William the Conqueror, who died in 1134.

Culture

Gloucester Cathedral is closely associated with the Three Choirs Festival, held in rotation with Hereford and Worcester since the eighteenth century; it was at Gloucester in 1910 that Ralph Vaughan Williams's Fantasia on a Theme by Thomas Tallis was first performed. The cloisters stood in for parts of Hogwarts in the Harry Potter films. The cathedral's choir and its organ, whose case dates from 1666, remain central to its musical life.
