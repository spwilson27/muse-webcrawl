Kebab

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Skewers of shish kebab grilling over charcoal with peppers and onions]

A kebab is a dish of meat cooked on skewers or a rotating spit, a tradition spanning the Middle East, the Caucasus, Central and South Asia and the Balkans. The word — from Arabic kabāb, likely via Persian and Turkish — covers a family: shish kebab (cubes on a skewer, from Turkish şiş), ground-meat kebabs such as Persia's koobideh and Turkey's Adana, the Caucasus's shashlik, South Asia's seekh kebab, and the vertical-spit branch — döner kebab and its descendants shawarma and gyros — in which seasoned meat roasts on a rotating stack and is shaved to order.

History

Grilling meat on skewers is old wherever herding cultures met open fire; medieval Persian and Arabic texts describe kebabs, and soldiers across the steppe and Anatolia cooked meat on swords and spits on campaign. The vertical rotisserie is an Ottoman 19th-century refinement, credited in Bursa to the İskenderoğlu family, whose name still marks the dish of döner over pide with tomato sauce and browned butter. In Berlin in the 1970s, Turkish guest-workers put döner into flatbread with salad and sauce — the sandwich form that conquered Europe; Kadir Nurman's claim to have invented it in 1972 is celebrated, though contested.

Styles and service

Kebabs are a grammar, not a recipe: a protein (lamb above all, then beef, chicken, offal, and in Iran, saffron-stained jujeh), a marinade or spice paste (Adana's chilli heat against Urfa's milder smoke), a fire (charcoal preferred), and breads, rice, pickles, onions with sumac, and grilled tomatoes to serve. India's kebab culture — galouti, kakori, tikka — came through Mughal kitchens; Indonesia's satay is the family's Southeast Asian member, though it keeps its own name. The through-line is smoke, fat and char.
