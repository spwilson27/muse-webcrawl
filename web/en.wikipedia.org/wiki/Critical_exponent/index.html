Critical exponent

Near a critical point, physical quantities diverge or vanish as power laws.

Critical exponents describe the behaviour of physical quantities near continuous phase transitions. They characterise the power laws with which quantities such as the order parameter, heat capacity, susceptibility and correlation length diverge or tend to zero as the system approaches its critical point, usually expressed in terms of the reduced temperature t = (T − Tc)/Tc.

Standard exponents

The order parameter below the transition vanishes as |t|β; the specific heat diverges as |t|−α; the susceptibility diverges as |t|−γ; the correlation length diverges as |t|−ν; and at the critical temperature the order parameter responds to an applied field h as h1/δ. Correlation functions are described by the anomalous dimension η. The exponents are not independent: scaling relations such as α + 2β + γ = 2 (Rushbrooke) and γ = β(δ − 1) (Widom) connect them.

Universality

The striking discovery of critical phenomena, explained by the renormalisation group, is universality: systems with the same spatial dimension and order-parameter symmetry share identical critical exponents, regardless of microscopic details. Thus a uniaxial ferromagnet and a liquid–gas critical point (the 3D Ising universality class) share exponents β ≈ 0.327, γ ≈ 1.237 and ν ≈ 0.63 — values different from the classical mean-field predictions (β = 1/2, γ = 1, ν = 1/2), which become exact only above the upper critical dimension of four.
