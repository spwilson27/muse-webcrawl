Central sulcus

The central sulcus is a prominent fold (sulcus) in the cerebral cortex of the brain. It separates the parietal lobe from the frontal lobe and divides the primary motor cortex (in the precentral gyrus, immediately in front) from the primary somatosensory cortex (in the postcentral gyrus, immediately behind). It is also called the central fissure or the fissure of Rolando, after the Italian anatomist Luigi Rolando.

Anatomy

The sulcus begins near the cerebral longitudinal fissure, slightly behind its midpoint, and runs forward and downward at an angle across the lateral surface of each hemisphere, ending just above the lateral sulcus. A characteristic "omega" or "epsilon" shaped bend in its upper-middle portion corresponds to the hand area of the motor cortex, a useful landmark in neurosurgery.

Clinical significance

Because the motor and sensory strips face each other across the central sulcus, lesions or stimulation here produce motor or sensory effects in specific body parts following the homunculus arrangement. The sulcus is a key reference in functional MRI and cortical mapping before tumour resection.
