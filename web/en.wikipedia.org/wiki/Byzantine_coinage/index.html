Periodc. 498 – 1453
Key denominationsSolidus/nomisma, follis, hyperpyron
Main mintConstantinople

Byzantine coinage, money used in the Eastern Roman Empire after the fall of the West, followed the late Roman system in its early centuries. The coinage is conventionally dated from the currency reform of Anastasius I in 498, which introduced a large bronze follis marked with its value, until the fall of Constantinople in 1453. For most of that millennium the gold solidus (in Greek, nomisma) was the standard coin of international trade, famous for its stable weight and purity.

Early coinage

Anastasius's reform created a bronze series (follis and its fractions) alongside the gold solidus and semissis and tremissis, and the silver miliaresion appeared later. Coins bore imperial portraits of increasing stylisation and, from the reign of Justinian II, the image of Christ — the first appearance of Christ on coinage. Iconoclasm in the 8th and 9th centuries replaced religious imagery with imperial family portraits and crosses.

Middle and late periods

From the 10th century the broad, cup-shaped (scyphate) fabric became characteristic of Byzantine issues. Progressive debasement of the nomisma in the 11th century led Alexios I Komnenos to reform the coinage in 1092, introducing the gold hyperpyron, the electrum and billon aspron trachy, and a new copper series. After the Fourth Crusade (1204) the successor states and, following the restoration of 1261, the Palaiologan emperors issued increasingly silver- and copper-based coinage, including the basilicon and the last gold hyperpyra; the final Byzantine coins were silver stavrata and copper issues of the 15th century.

Iconography and study

Byzantine coins are primary documents for imperial portraiture, titulature and religious history, and their inscriptions shift from Latin to Greek over the 7th century. The standard scholarly catalogues are those of the Dumbarton Oaks collection and the British Museum.
