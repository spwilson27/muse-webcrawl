Economic Espionage Act of 1996

The Economic Espionage Act of 1996 (EEA) is a United States federal law, codified at 18 U.S.C. §§ 1831–1839, that criminalises the theft or misappropriation of trade secrets. It was signed into law by President Bill Clinton on 11 October 1996 and was the first federal statute to make trade-secret theft a federal crime.

Provisions

The Act contains two principal criminal provisions. Section 1831 covers economic espionage: knowingly stealing, copying or receiving a trade secret with the intent or knowledge that the offence will benefit a foreign government, foreign instrumentality or foreign agent. Individuals convicted under this section face up to 15 years' imprisonment. Section 1832 covers the theft of trade secrets carried out for commercial or economic advantage, where the defendant knows the offence will injure the owner of the secret; individuals face up to 10 years' imprisonment. Organisations may be fined millions of dollars, and the Act also provides for forfeiture and civil proceedings brought by the Attorney General.

Amendments

The Theft of Trade Secrets Clarification Act of 2012 widened the scope of section 1832 after a federal appeals court overturned a conviction on the ground that the stolen software was not connected to a product placed in interstate commerce. The Defend Trade Secrets Act of 2016 added a federal civil cause of action for trade-secret owners, complementing the EEA's criminal provisions.

Notable cases

Prosecutions under the Act have involved the theft of source code, chemical formulas, aerospace designs and manufacturing processes, including cases involving employees who took proprietary information to competitors or to entities abroad. The statute's definition of a trade secret is broad, covering financial, business, scientific, technical, economic or engineering information that the owner has taken reasonable measures to keep secret and that derives economic value from not being generally known.
