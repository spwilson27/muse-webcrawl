Crown corporation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Crown corporation (French: société d'État) is a type of state-owned enterprise in Canada, Australia and other Commonwealth realms, owned by the monarch as the corporation sole (the Crown) through the government of the jurisdiction in question. Crown corporations are created by legislatures to pursue public policy objectives, often in fields considered natural monopolies or too important to leave entirely to private enterprise.

Canada

In Canada, Crown corporations exist at both the federal and provincial levels. They are accountable to Parliament or a provincial legislature through a responsible minister, but typically operate at arm's length from day-to-day government direction. Well-known federal examples include the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation, Canada Post, Via Rail, the Royal Canadian Mint, and Export Development Canada. Provincial examples include Hydro-Québec, the Liquor Control Board of Ontario, and ICBC in British Columbia.

Canadian Crown corporations are generally established by statute, governed by appointed boards of directors, and subject to audit by the Auditor General. Under the Financial Administration Act they are classified as parent Crown corporations, with varying degrees of financial self-sufficiency; some depend on parliamentary appropriations while others operate commercially and pay dividends to the Crown.

Australia

In Australia the equivalent entities are usually termed government business enterprises or statutory corporations; examples historically included Australia Post and the Commonwealth Bank before its privatisation in the 1990s. Similar entities exist in New Zealand (Crown entities and state-owned enterprises) and the United Kingdom, where bodies owned by the Crown include the Crown Estate — though its legal position differs from a trading Crown corporation.
