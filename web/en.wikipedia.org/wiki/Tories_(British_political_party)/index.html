Tories (British political party)

The Tories were a political faction and later party in the parliaments of England, Scotland, and the United Kingdom between the 1670s and the 1830s. Together with the Whigs, they formed the original two-party system of British politics.

Origins

The name derived from the Irish word tóraidhe (outlaw), first applied during the Exclusion Crisis (1679–1681) to those who opposed excluding the Catholic James, Duke of York, from the succession. Tories championed the divine right of kings, the Anglican establishment, and the landed interest.

18th and 19th centuries

After the Hanoverian succession of 1714, suspected Jacobite sympathies kept the Tories out of power for decades. Under William Pitt the Younger and later Robert Jenkinson, Earl of Liverpool, they returned to dominance. The party fractured over Catholic emancipation and parliamentary reform; after 1834, under Robert Peel, the Tories rebranded as the Conservative Party.

Legacy

"Tory" remains a common nickname for Conservatives in Britain and Canada today.
