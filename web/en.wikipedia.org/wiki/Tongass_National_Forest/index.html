Tongass National Forest

LocationSoutheast Alaska, United States
AreaAbout 16.7 million acres (6.8 million ha)
EstablishedSeptember 10, 1907
Governing bodyU.S. Forest Service

The Tongass National Forest in Southeast Alaska is the largest national forest in the United States, covering roughly 16.7 million acres (about 26,100 square miles). It is also the world's largest remaining intact temperate rainforest and surrounds much of the Alexander Archipelago and the fjords and glaciers of the Alaska Panhandle.

Geography and ecology

The forest stretches some 500 miles along the coast from the Dixon Entrance in the south to Yakutat Bay in the north. Its old-growth stands are dominated by Sitka spruce, western hemlock, western red cedar and Alaska yellow cedar. The Tongass provides habitat for brown and black bears, wolves, bald eagles, Sitka black-tailed deer and five species of Pacific salmon, and it stores large amounts of carbon. Communities within and around the forest include Juneau, Ketchikan, Sitka, Petersburg and Wrangell, many reachable only by boat or plane.

History and management

The forest was established by proclamation of President Theodore Roosevelt in 1907, building on the earlier Alexander Archipelago Forest Reserve of 1902. Much of the land is the traditional territory of the Tlingit, Haida and Tsimshian peoples. Management of the Tongass has long been contested between timber harvesting — historically supported by long-term pulp contracts — and conservation. The 2001 Roadless Area Conservation Rule, and its repeated application to and removal from the Tongass by successive administrations, has been a central focus of that debate.
