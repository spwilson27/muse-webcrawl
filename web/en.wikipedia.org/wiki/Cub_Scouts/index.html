Cub Scouts

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cub Scouts are members of the Cub Scout section of Scouting organisations, aimed at younger children — typically aged about 7 to 11 — as a preparation for the Scout section. The programme was created by Robert Baden-Powell, founder of the Scout movement, who published The Wolf Cub's Handbook in 1916, drawing the section's imagery from Rudyard Kipling's The Jungle Book: adult leaders take names such as Akela, and the youngest members were originally called Wolf Cubs.

Organisation

Cubs meet in packs, subdivided into small groups called sixes led by a Sixer. Activities emphasise games, outdoor skills, crafts, badges for proficiency and simple community service, delivered in a less demanding form than in the older sections. In the United Kingdom, The Scout Association admits children from 8 to 10½ years; the section's motto is "Do Your Best" and members make a Promise appropriate to their age.

United States

In the United States, Cub Scouting is a programme of the Boy Scouts of America (Scouting America) for children in kindergarten through fifth grade, organised by school grade into ranks — Lion, Tiger, Wolf, Bear, Webelos and Arrow of Light. Children are grouped in dens and packs chartered by community organisations. Since 2018 girls have been admitted to Cub Scouting in the US, and since 2019 to the renamed Scouts BSA programme. Advancement culminates in the Arrow of Light award and crossover into a Scout troop.
