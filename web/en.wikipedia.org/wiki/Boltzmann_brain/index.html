Boltzmann brain

A Boltzmann brain is a hypothesised self-aware entity that arises from random fluctuations in a universe in thermal equilibrium (heat death). The idea is named for the physicist Ludwig Boltzmann, whose statistical mechanics implied that, given eternity, even extremely improbable fluctuations of low entropy will eventually occur. The Boltzmann brain concept is used as a reductio ad absurdum argument against certain cosmological theories.

The argument

Boltzmann suggested that the observable universe might itself be a rare statistical fluctuation from a vastly larger equilibrium cosmos. Modern cosmology revived the problem: in an eternally existing, mostly empty universe, random fluctuations would produce single disembodied brains — complete with false memories of a life and of observing a universe like ours — vastly more often than they would produce entire low-entropy universes like the one we appear to inhabit. Any theory that predicts observers are overwhelmingly Boltzmann brains is therefore considered suspect, because we (presumably) are not: such theories are called "cognitively unstable" or self-undermining.

Role in cosmology

The problem sharpens for models involving eternal inflation and a multiverse, where the total number of both ordinary observers and fluctuation-observers depends sensitively on how infinities are regulated (the "measure problem"). Cosmologists such as Sean Carroll have argued that a viable theory of the universe must not predict that typical observers are Boltzmann brains. Proposed escapes include the idea that the universe has a finite lifetime, that fluctuations of an entire brain are suppressed by quantum effects, or that the vacuum itself decays before such fluctuations dominate.

History

The term was coined in the early 2000s, building on discussion by physicists including Andreas Albrecht and Lorenzo Sorbo. The underlying worry reaches back to Boltzmann's own reply to critics of his fluctuation hypothesis and to remarks by Arthur Eddington in the 1930s about the improbability of a fluctuated universe being no larger than necessary.
