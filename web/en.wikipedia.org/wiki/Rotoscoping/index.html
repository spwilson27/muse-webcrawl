Rotoscoping

Rotoscoping is an animation technique in which animators trace over motion picture footage, frame by frame, to produce realistic action. Originally, animators projected live-action film images onto a glass panel and traced the image. The technique was invented by Max Fleischer, who patented the "rotoscope" in 1917 and used it in the Out of the Inkwell series featuring Koko the Clown. Unlike computer-graphics rendering, which synthesises images from models, rotoscoping derives its imagery directly from photographed movement.

Use in animation

Fleischer Studios used rotoscoping extensively, notably for Gulliver in Gulliver's Travels (1939) and the human characters in the Superman cartoons of the 1940s. Walt Disney Studios had used live-action reference from the 1930s and traced or closely studied footage for the human figures in Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937). Ralph Bakshi made rotoscoping a signature of his adult-oriented features, including Wizards (1977), The Lord of the Rings (1978) and Fire and Ice (1983). Richard Linklater's Waking Life (2001) and A Scanner Darkly (2006) used digital interpolated rotoscoping, in which software helps propagate hand-drawn styles across traced footage.

Use in visual effects

In modern visual effects, "roto" means creating mattes by tracing outlines in footage so that elements can be separated, replaced or colour-corrected. Software tools use splines and tracking to assist the tedious manual work. Rotoscoping was famously used to create the glowing lightsaber effect in the original Star Wars films by tracing over sticks wielded by the actors.

Criticism

Purist animators have sometimes dismissed heavy rotoscoping as tracing rather than true animation, arguing it can produce stiff, sliding movement if the traced action is not reinterpreted with exaggeration and solid drawing principles.
