Institute for the Works of Religion

Native name: Istituto per le Opere di Religione (IOR)
Founded: 27 June 1942
Founder: Pope Pius XII
Headquarters: Vatican City
Common name: "Vatican Bank"

The Institute for the Works of Religion (Italian: Istituto per le Opere di Religione; IOR), commonly known as the Vatican Bank, is a financial institution situated inside Vatican City and run by a professional bank board of directors reporting directly to a supervisory commission of cardinals. Its purpose, as restated in new statutes of 2019, is "to provide for the custody and administration of assets entrusted to it by physical or juridical persons, intended for works of religion or charity".

History

The IOR was founded by Pope Pius XII on 27 June 1942, absorbing earlier papal financial administrations. Its clients are restricted to Vatican employees, religious orders, dioceses, Catholic institutions and accredited diplomats; it is not a central bank and does not serve the general public. The Institute holds assets reported at roughly €5 billion and serves tens of thousands of clients worldwide.

Controversies

The IOR's secrecy and its entanglement with Italian finance made it a focus of scandal in the late twentieth century, most notably the 1982 collapse of Banco Ambrosiano, whose chairman Roberto Calvi was found dead beneath Blackfriars Bridge in London; the IOR, Ambrosiano's principal shareholder, paid over $224 million to creditors while denying legal liability. Archbishop Paul Marcinkus, the IOR's president from 1971 to 1989, was indicted in Italy in connection with the collapse but never tried, being protected by Vatican immunity.

Reform

Under Popes Benedict XVI and Francis, the Institute underwent extensive reform: creation of the Vatican's financial intelligence authority (ASIF), anti-money-laundering legislation, external audits, publication of annual reports from 2013, and closure of thousands of accounts not meeting eligibility criteria. In 2019 Pope Francis issued new statutes clarifying the IOR's mission and governance, and in 2022 a rescript made the Institute the sole body responsible for asset management and depositary functions for the Holy See.
