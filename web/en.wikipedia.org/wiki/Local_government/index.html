Local government

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Local government is a form of public administration which, in a majority of contexts, exists as the lowest tier of administration within a given state, below national and (in federations) regional governments. Local authorities — municipalities, counties, districts, towns and parishes — deliver services such as planning, housing, local roads, waste, libraries, social care and (in many systems) policing and primary education, and are governed by elected councils, usually with a mayor or council leader.

Structures and finance

Structures range from strong-mayor systems (common in the US, France and Latin America) to council–manager and committee systems. Local government raises revenue from property taxes, local income or sales taxes, charges and central grants — the balance determining its real autonomy. Legal doctrines differ: under the US Dillon's Rule municipalities hold only granted powers, while many European systems recognise a general competence, reinforced by the European Charter of Local Self-Government (1985) and the principle of subsidiarity.

Significance

Local government is the level of democracy citizens encounter most — turnout in local elections is nonetheless often low, prompting experiments with combined authorities, directly elected metro mayors (in England since 2016), participatory budgeting (from Porto Alegre onward) and digital participation. Central–local tension over funding, mandates and amalgamation of small municipalities is a near-universal theme.
