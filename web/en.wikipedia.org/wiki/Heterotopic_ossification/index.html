Heterotopic ossification

Heterotopic ossification (HO), also known as heterotopic bone formation, is the abnormal growth of bone in the non-skeletal tissues, including muscle, tendons and other soft tissue. When the bone forms in muscle it is sometimes called myositis ossificans traumatica when associated with trauma.

Causes and risk factors

HO most commonly develops after musculoskeletal trauma, fractures and dislocations (especially around the elbow and hip), total hip arthroplasty, burns, and central nervous system injuries such as spinal cord injury and traumatic brain injury, in which it is called neurogenic heterotopic ossification. The process begins with an inflammatory response and the recruitment of mesenchymal progenitor cells, which differentiate into osteoblasts and lay down true lamellar bone in soft tissue over weeks to months.

Genetic forms

A rare, severely disabling genetic form is fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP), caused by activating mutations in the ACVR1 gene, in which skeletal muscle and connective tissue progressively ossify. Another genetic disorder, progressive osseous heteroplasia, is associated with inactivating mutations of GNAS.

Diagnosis and treatment

Early HO may present with pain, warmth, swelling and loss of joint motion. Bone scintigraphy can detect it before it is visible on plain radiographs. Management includes physiotherapy to preserve range of motion, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, bisphosphonates, and prophylactic low-dose radiotherapy in high-risk surgical patients. Surgical excision is generally deferred until the bone has matured, typically 12–18 months, to reduce recurrence.
