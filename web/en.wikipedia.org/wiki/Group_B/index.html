Group B was a set of regulations introduced by the FIA in 1982 for competition vehicles in sportscar racing and rallying. It is remembered above all for the spectacular cars it produced in the World Rally Championship between 1982 and 1986 — lightweight, four-wheel-drive machines whose turbocharged (and in one case supercharged-and-turbocharged) engines produced well over 500 horsepower in their final evolution.

Regulations and cars

Unlike the more restrictive Group A, Group B required only 200 road-going examples for homologation and imposed few limits on technology, materials or boost pressure, encouraging exotic space-frame construction and rapid development. Audi's Quattro pioneered four-wheel drive in rallying; mid-engined rivals followed in the Lancia 037 (rear-drive, the last non-4WD car to win the title, in 1983), Peugeot 205 Turbo 16, Lancia Delta S4, Ford RS200 and MG Metro 6R4. Power and speed rose so quickly that by 1986 the cars were widely judged too fast for the roads and crowds they raced among, reaching Formula One levels of performance on narrow gravel and tarmac stages lined with spectators.

Ban

The 1986 season brought a series of fatal accidents, culminating in the deaths of Henri Toivonen and his co-driver Sergio Cresto, whose Lancia Delta S4 left the road and burned during the Tour de Corse on 2 May 1986. FISA banned Group B for the following season and cancelled the planned Group S replacement; Group A became the WRC's top class from 1987.

Legacy

Group B remains the most fondly remembered era of rallying, celebrated for its engineering audacity and the skill of drivers such as Walter Röhrl, Stig Blomqvist, Juha Kankkunen, Michèle Mouton and Ari Vatanen. Surviving cars are highly prized at auction and demonstration events, and the period is often described with the phrase applied to its cars: "too fast to race".
