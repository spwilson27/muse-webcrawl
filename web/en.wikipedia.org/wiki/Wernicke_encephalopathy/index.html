Wernicke encephalopathy (WE), also known as Wernicke's encephalopathy, is an acute neurological disorder caused by deficiency of thiamine (vitamin B1). It is named after Carl Wernicke, who described it in 1881. The condition is a medical emergency: untreated, it can progress to the chronic amnesic state known as Korsakoff syndrome (together termed Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome) or to death.

Signs and symptoms

The classic clinical triad is confusion (encephalopathy), gait ataxia, and eye signs such as nystagmus and ophthalmoplegia (paralysis of eye movements, especially lateral rectus palsy). However, only a minority of patients — perhaps 10–20% — present with all three, and reliance on the full triad causes the diagnosis to be missed. Other features include hypothermia, hypotension and, in advanced cases, coma. Caine's criteria, which require two of dietary deficiency, oculomotor abnormalities, cerebellar dysfunction and altered mental state, improve diagnostic sensitivity.

Causes and pathology

Thiamine deficiency is most often associated with chronic alcohol misuse, but also occurs with malnutrition, prolonged vomiting (including hyperemesis gravidarum), eating disorders, bariatric surgery, dialysis, cancer and prolonged parenteral feeding without vitamins. Lesions characteristically affect periventricular structures of the third and fourth ventricles, the mammillary bodies, dorsomedial thalamus and periaqueductal grey matter.

Treatment

Treatment is immediate high-dose parenteral thiamine, given before or together with any glucose load (since glucose without thiamine may precipitate or worsen the condition), alongside correction of magnesium and other deficiencies. Early treatment can reverse the eye signs within hours to days, while memory and gait deficits may recover slowly and incompletely.
