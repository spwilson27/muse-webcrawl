Bipedalism

Bipedalism is a form of terrestrial locomotion in which an animal moves using only its two rear limbs. Humans are obligate bipeds; birds are bipedal when on the ground, and many other animals — kangaroos, some rodents, and many extinct dinosaurs — move bipedally at least some of the time. Habitual bipedal walking and running is one of the defining adaptations of the human lineage.

Evolution in hominins

Fossils such as Sahelanthropus, Ardipithecus, and the australopithecines — including the famous "Lucy" (Australopithecus afarensis) — show bipedal adaptations predating large brains by millions of years. The 3.6-million-year-old Laetoli footprints in Tanzania preserve direct evidence of upright walking. Proposed drivers include energy efficiency over long distances, freeing the hands to carry food and tools, improved cooling in open savanna, and feeding postures in trees.

Mechanics

Human walking works like an inverted pendulum, exchanging kinetic and potential energy with each step, while running uses a spring-like bounce in tendons such as the Achilles. Anatomical changes include knees angled inward, a non-grasping big toe aligned with the other toes, and a bowl-shaped pelvis supporting the internal organs.

Costs

Bipedalism is associated with lower-back and knee problems, a narrowed birth canal contributing to difficult childbirth, and slower maximum sprint speeds than most quadrupeds. In birds and other animals, bipedalism arose independently, often freeing the forelimbs as wings or for manipulation.
