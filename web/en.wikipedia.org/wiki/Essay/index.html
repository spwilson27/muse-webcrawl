Essay

An essay is, generally, a piece of writing that gives the author's own argument, but the definition is vague, overlapping with those of a letter, a paper, an article, a pamphlet and a short story. Essays have been sub-classified as formal and informal: formal essays are characterised by "serious purpose, dignity, logical organisation, length", whereas the informal essay is characterised by "the personal element (self-revelation, individual tastes and experiences), humour, graceful style".

History

The form takes its name from the French word essai, "attempt" or "trial", after Michel de Montaigne, whose Essais (1580) established the genre as a literary form in which the author explores a subject through his own experience and reflection. Francis Bacon's Essays (1597) introduced the form to English in a terser, more aphoristic mode. The essay flourished in the 18th-century periodical press with Joseph Addison and Richard Steele's The Spectator, and later masters include Charles Lamb, William Hazlitt, Ralph Waldo Emerson, Virginia Woolf and George Orwell, whose essays such as "Politics and the English Language" set a standard for prose argument.

Forms

Common types include the argumentative or persuasive essay, which defends a thesis; the expository essay, which explains a subject; the descriptive essay; and the narrative or personal essay. In education, the structured school essay — often organised as introduction, body paragraphs and conclusion, with the "five-paragraph essay" as a common template — is a standard exercise and examination format in many countries.
