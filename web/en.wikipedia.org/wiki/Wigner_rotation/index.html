Wigner rotation

In theoretical physics, the composition of two non-collinear Lorentz boosts results in a Lorentz transformation that is not a pure boost but the composition of a boost and a spatial rotation. The resulting rotation is called the Wigner rotation, after Eugene Wigner, who described it in 1939. It is the basis of the Thomas precession, a relativistic effect observed in the motion of spinning particles and in atomic spectra.

The composition of two non-collinear boosts is equivalent to a boost plus a rotation.

Background

Lorentz boosts in different directions do not commute. If a particle is boosted first in one direction and then in another, the result differs from applying the boosts in the reverse order; the two results are related by a spatial rotation. Mathematically, the Lorentz group is not closed under pure boosts alone — boosts in different directions generate rotations, which is why the full Lorentz group includes both.

Thomas precession

When the sequence of boosts is infinitesimal, as for a particle moving along a curved trajectory (for example, an electron orbiting a nucleus), the accumulated Wigner rotation manifests as a continuous precession of the particle's rest frame. This is the Thomas precession, which contributes a factor of one-half to the spin–orbit coupling in atomic fine structure and affects the precession of gyroscopes in orbit, an effect measured by the Gravity Probe B experiment.

Group theory

In the representation theory of the Lorentz and Poincaré groups, Wigner rotations play a central role in Wigner's classification of elementary particles: the "little group" stabilising a particle's momentum is built from these rotations, and for massless particles leads to the concept of helicity.
