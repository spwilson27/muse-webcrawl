Pinnipeds (from Latin pinna "fin" and pes, pedis "foot"), commonly known as seals, are a widely distributed and diverse clade of carnivorous, fin-footed, semiaquatic marine mammals. They comprise the extant families Odobenidae (whose only living member is the walrus), Otariidae (the eared seals: sea lions and fur seals), and Phocidae (the earless, or true, seals). There are 34 extant species of pinnipeds, and more than 50 extinct species have been described from fossils.

Anatomy and physiology

Pinnipeds have streamlined bodies and four limbs modified into flippers. They are well adapted to cold aquatic habitats, with a thick layer of blubber for insulation and energy storage. While mostly marine, they haul out onto land or ice to rest, moult, mate and give birth. Sizes range from the diminutive Baikal seal, about 1.1 m and 45 kg, to the southern elephant seal, whose males can exceed 5 m and 3,700 kg — the largest living carnivorans. Pinnipeds have sensitive whiskers (vibrissae) used to detect prey movements in the water.

Evolution

Fossil evidence indicates pinnipeds diverged from other caniform carnivorans about 50 million years ago, and became aquatic in the late Oligocene to early Miocene. Puijila darwini, a freshwater otter-like fossil from Arctic Canada, and the bear-like Enaliarctos illustrate stages of the transition to the sea. Their closest living relatives are bears and musteloids (weasels, raccoons and skunks).

Human interactions

Pinnipeds have been hunted for meat, hides and blubber for millennia, and commercial sealing in the 19th and 20th centuries drove several species close to extinction. The Caribbean monk seal was declared extinct in 2008. International protection measures, such as the Marine Mammal Protection Act in the United States, have allowed many populations to recover, though bycatch, habitat loss and climate change remain threats.
