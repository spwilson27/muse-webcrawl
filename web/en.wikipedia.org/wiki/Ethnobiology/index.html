Ethnobiology

Ethnobiology is the scientific study of the ways in which living organisms are known, classified, used and managed by human cultures. It combines methods from anthropology, biology, linguistics and ecology, and includes the subfields of ethnobotany (plants), ethnozoology (animals), ethnomycology (fungi) and ethnoecology.

History

Although travellers and naturalists had recorded indigenous uses of plants and animals for centuries, the term "ethnobotany" was coined by John William Harshberger in 1895, and "ethnobiology" came into use in the 1930s. The field developed a cognitive orientation in the 1960s, when researchers such as Brent Berlin studied how peoples classify organisms, finding that folk taxonomies worldwide share structural principles — such as a core rank of "folk genera" — with scientific taxonomy. From the 1980s onward, ethnobiology increasingly addressed applied questions: conservation, traditional resource management and the rights of knowledge holders.

Research topics

Ethnobiologists document traditional ecological knowledge, including medicinal plant use (a source of leads for pharmaceutical research), crop diversity maintained by farmers, hunting and fishing practices, and classification systems encoded in language. The discipline also examines how biological knowledge is transmitted, how it erodes under cultural change, and how it can inform biodiversity conservation; the Nagoya Protocol on access and benefit-sharing addresses the commercial use of such knowledge.

Organisations

The Society of Ethnobiology, founded in 1977, publishes the Journal of Ethnobiology; the International Society of Ethnobiology, founded in 1988, developed a code of ethics emphasising partnership with indigenous and local communities.
