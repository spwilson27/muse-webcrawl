Chronology

Chronology (from Greek chronos, time, and -logia) is the science of arranging events in their order of occurrence in time. It is fundamental to history, archaeology, astronomy, and geology, providing the framework within which events can be dated, sequenced, and compared across cultures and regions.

Calendars and eras

Chronology depends on systems for counting time: calendars (solar, lunar, and lunisolar) and eras — counts of years from a chosen epoch, such as the Christian era (Anno Domini), the Islamic Hijri era, regnal years, the Roman count from the founding of Rome (ab urbe condita), and the Olympiad system of ancient Greece. Converting dates between systems, and reconciling king lists, eclipse records, and other sources, is a central task of historical chronology.

Dating methods

Relative dating establishes sequences without absolute ages — through stratigraphy in archaeology, typology of artefacts, and, in geology, the ordering of rock layers. Absolute (chronometric) dating assigns numerical ages using physical methods such as radiocarbon dating, dendrochronology (tree-ring dating), potassium–argon dating, and thermoluminescence, as well as astronomical anchors such as recorded eclipses and retrocalculated planetary positions.

History of the discipline

Joseph Justus Scaliger's De emendatione temporum (1583) founded modern critical chronology, and Isaac Newton, among many others, attempted systematic revisions of ancient dates. Modern chronology combines textual criticism, astronomy, and scientific dating; major reference works and synchronisms link the chronologies of Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, Rome, and other civilizations into a shared timeline.

Uses

Beyond scholarship, chronological ordering structures timelines, chronicles, annals, and almanacs, and underlies concepts such as geologic time scales, evolutionary histories, and the chronology of the universe from the Big Bang to the present.
