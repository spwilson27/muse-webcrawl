Cultural studies is an interdisciplinary field that investigates how culture — understood as the meanings, practices and artefacts of everyday life — is produced, circulated and contested, and how it relates to relations of power. Drawing on literary criticism, sociology, history, Marxism, feminism, psychoanalysis and media theory, it analyses phenomena from television and pop music to shopping, sport, subcultures and digital media, treating them as worthy of the same analytical seriousness as elite art.

Origins in Britain

The field emerged in post-war Britain from the work of Richard Hoggart, whose The Uses of Literacy (1957) examined working-class culture under pressure from commercial mass culture; Raymond Williams, whose Culture and Society (1958) and The Long Revolution (1961) argued that culture should mean "a whole way of life"; and historian E. P. Thompson, author of The Making of the English Working Class (1963). In 1964 Hoggart founded the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies (CCCS) at the University of Birmingham, the institutional birthplace of the discipline.

Under Stuart Hall, CCCS director from 1968, British cultural studies took shape: it absorbed Gramsci's theory of hegemony, Althusser and semiotics, and developed Hall's influential "encoding/decoding" model of media audiences, who do not merely absorb messages but interpret them from different social positions. Landmark collective works such as Policing the Crisis (1978) analysed moral panics and the authoritarian turn in British politics.

Themes and methods

Cultural studies is defined less by a single method than by its questions. Recurring concerns include representation (how media construct race, gender, class and nation), subcultures and youth style, consumption and identity, nation and diaspora, and the politics of popular pleasure. Methods range from textual and discourse analysis to ethnography of audiences and fans. From the 1970s, feminist scholars transformed the field by insisting on gender and the domestic sphere; later work engaged postcolonial theory, queer studies and globalisation.

Spread and criticism

From the 1980s cultural studies spread widely through universities in North America, Australia and beyond, influencing media studies, literary studies and the humanities generally — and attracting criticism for eclecticism, jargon and a perceived slide from political economy into celebratory description of consumption. The CCCS itself was closed controversially in 2002. The field nevertheless remains a major framework for studying media and culture, and its core concepts — hegemony, representation, active audiences — are now standard across the social sciences and humanities.
