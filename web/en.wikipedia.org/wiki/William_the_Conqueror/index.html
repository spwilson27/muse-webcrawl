William the Conqueror

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: William the Conqueror as depicted in the Bayeux Tapestry, holding his helmet to rally his troops]

William I (c. 1028 – 9 September 1087), usually known as William the Conqueror and sometimes as William the Bastard, was the first Norman king of England, reigning from 1066 until his death. Duke of Normandy from 1035, he claimed the English crown as the designated heir of Edward the Confessor, invaded in September 1066, defeated and killed Harold Godwinson at the Battle of Hastings (14 October 1066), and was crowned on Christmas Day. His reign replaced England's ruling class wholesale, planted castles across the kingdom, and produced the Domesday Book survey of 1086 — transforming England's language, church, law and aristocracy forever.

The Conquest

William secured Normandy through a violent minority, crushed his barons at Val-ès-Dunes (1047) and expanded into Maine. When Harold took the English throne in January 1066, William — with papal blessing — assembled some 700 ships and perhaps 7,000–12,000 men, landed at Pevensey, and met Harold's exhausted army (fresh from defeating the Norwegians at Stamford Bridge) on Senlac Hill near Hastings. Harold's death — by arrow and sword in the Bayeux Tapestry's telling — broke English resistance at a stroke, though William spent five years suppressing revolts, culminating in the devastating Harrying of the North (1069–70).

Norman England

By 1086 nearly all English land was held by Normans and their allies; English bishops and abbots were replaced, cathedrals and monasteries rebuilt in Romanesque stone, and French became the language of power for three centuries. The Domesday survey (1086) recorded some 13,000 settlements' resources for taxation — an administrative feat unmatched in medieval Europe. William died at Rouen in 1087 of injuries after his horse reared at the siege of Mantes; England passed to his son William Rufus, Normandy to Robert Curthose.
