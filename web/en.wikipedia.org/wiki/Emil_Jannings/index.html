Emil Jannings

Emil Jannings (born Theodor Friedrich Emil Janenz; 23 July 1884 – 2 January 1950) was a German actor, popular in Hollywood in the 1920s. He was the first recipient of the Academy Award for Best Actor, honoured at the inaugural ceremony in 1929 for his performances in The Last Command and The Way of All Flesh. He later worked in Nazi Germany and starred in several propaganda films, which ended his career after the Second World War.

Early life

Jannings was born in Rorschach, Switzerland, to a German mother and an American father of Lithuanian descent, and grew up in Görlitz, Germany. He ran away to sea as a teenager before returning to work in the theatre, touring with provincial companies and eventually reaching Max Reinhardt's Deutsches Theater in Berlin.

Career

Jannings rose to fame in German cinema with films including Madame DuBarry (1919), Othello (1922), F. W. Murnau's The Last Laugh (1924) — in which his performance as a proud hotel doorman reduced to a lavatory attendant became a landmark of silent acting — and Variety (1925). Signed by Paramount, he moved to Hollywood in 1926 and starred in The Way of All Flesh (1927) and Josef von Sternberg's The Last Command (1928), winning the first Best Actor Oscar.

The arrival of sound films ended his Hollywood career because of his heavy German accent. Returning to Europe, he starred opposite Marlene Dietrich in The Blue Angel (1930), playing the stuffy Professor Rath destroyed by his obsession with the cabaret singer Lola Lola.

Nazi era and later life

Under the Nazi regime Jannings became a favoured artist of the state, starring in films such as Ohm Krüger (1941), an anti-British propaganda epic in which he played Paul Kruger, and was named an "Artist of the State" by Joseph Goebbels. After the war he was blacklisted by the Allies and never acted again. He retired to his estate in Strobl, Austria, where he died of liver cancer in 1950.
