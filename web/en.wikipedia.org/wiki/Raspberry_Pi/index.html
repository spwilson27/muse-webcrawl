The Raspberry Pi is a series of small, inexpensive single-board computers developed in the United Kingdom by the Raspberry Pi Foundation in association with Broadcom. Launched in 2012 to promote the teaching of basic computer science in schools and developing countries, it became far more popular than expected, selling outside its target market for uses such as robotics, home automation, media centres, and industrial embedded applications.

History

The project began at the University of Cambridge Computer Laboratory amid concern about declining programming skills among applicants; Eben Upton and colleagues founded the charity to create a cheap programmable computer. The first models sold out immediately in February 2012.

Models

The line includes the flagship boards (Pi 1 through Pi 5, 2023), the smaller cheaper Pi Zero range, the Pi Pico microcontroller (2021, using the RP2040 chip), and Compute Modules for industry. Recent models feature quad-core ARM CPUs, up to 16 GB RAM, dual 4K HDMI output, USB 3.0, and a 40-pin GPIO header.

Software

The official Raspberry Pi OS (formerly Raspbian) is a Debian-based Linux distribution optimised for the hardware and ships with educational tools such as Scratch and Python. Many other operating systems, including Ubuntu and media-centre distributions, are supported.

Reception and impact

The Raspberry Pi is the best-selling British computer line in history, with tens of millions sold; it has been credited with reviving interest in programming education, spawned a large ecosystem of accessories and magazines, and underpinned a public listing of Raspberry Pi Holdings on the London Stock Exchange in 2024.
