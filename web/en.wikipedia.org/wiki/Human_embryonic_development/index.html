Human embryonic development

Human embryonic development, or human embryogenesis, is the development and formation of the human embryo. It is characterised by the processes of cell division and cellular differentiation, and in humans it spans the first eight weeks after fertilisation, after which the embryo is termed a foetus. The embryonic period is conventionally described in 23 Carnegie stages.

First weeks

Fertilisation produces a zygote, which undergoes cleavage as it travels down the fallopian tube, forming a morula and then a blastocyst with an inner cell mass and outer trophoblast. Around day 6–7 the blastocyst implants in the endometrium. In the second week the inner cell mass forms a bilaminar disc of epiblast and hypoblast, and the amniotic cavity and yolk sac appear.

Gastrulation in the third week converts the disc into three germ layers — ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm — and establishes the body axes through the primitive streak. Neurulation follows, with the neural plate folding into the neural tube, the precursor of the central nervous system, while somites form alongside it.

Organogenesis

From the fourth to the eighth week all major organ systems are laid down: the heart begins beating and the circulation is established, limb buds appear and differentiate, the face and neck develop from the pharyngeal arches, and the gut, liver, lungs and kidneys take shape. Because organogenesis is rapid and highly coordinated, these weeks are the period of greatest vulnerability to teratogens. By the end of the eighth week the embryo is about 30 mm long and has a recognisably human appearance, though the head remains disproportionately large.
