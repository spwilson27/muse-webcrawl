Cotinine

Cotinine: C10H12N2O, molar mass 176.22 g/mol.

Cotinine is an alkaloid found in tobacco and is also the predominant metabolite of nicotine, generated in the body after nicotine consumption. The name "cotinine" is an anagram of "nicotine". Cotinine is used as a biomarker for exposure to tobacco smoke, because it remains in the body much longer than nicotine itself.

Pharmacology

Similar to nicotine, cotinine binds to, activates, and desensitizes neuronal nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, though at much lower potency. It has a half-life of approximately 20 hours in the body, compared with about two hours for nicotine, and is typically detectable in blood, urine, saliva, and hair for several days after tobacco use.

Use as a biomarker

Because of its long half-life and stability, cotinine is the preferred measure of tobacco smoke exposure in epidemiological and clinical research. Drug rehabilitation programs, insurance underwriting, and clinical trials for smoking cessation routinely test for cotinine to verify smoking status. Reference ranges distinguish active smokers, passive smokers exposed to secondhand smoke, and non-exposed nonsmokers.

Research

Cotinine has been studied for possible therapeutic effects in conditions including depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, schizophrenia, Alzheimer's disease, and Parkinson's disease, owing to its effects on nicotinic receptors and its favourable safety profile relative to nicotine. It is sold in some countries as an antidepressant under the name Scotine.
