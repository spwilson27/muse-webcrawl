The great apes or hominids (family Hominidae) are a taxonomic family of primates that includes eight extant species in four genera: orangutans (Pongo), gorillas (Gorilla), chimpanzees and bonobos (Pan), and humans (Homo). They are distinguished from the "lesser apes" (gibbons, family Hylobatidae) by larger size, greater cognitive ability, and other anatomical traits.

Characteristics

Great apes lack external tails, have relatively large brains and complex social lives, and show extended childhood development and parental care. Orangutans are largely solitary and arboreal; gorillas live in troops led by a silverback male; chimpanzees live in large fission–fusion communities and are known for tool use and coalitionary aggression; bonobos are noted for peaceful, matriarchal, sexuallymediated social behaviour. Humans diverged from the chimpanzee lineage roughly six to seven million years ago.

Conservation and rights

All non-human great ape species are listed as Endangered or Critically Endangered, threatened by habitat loss, hunting for bushmeat, and disease. Their intelligence has fuelled the great ape personhood movement: projects such as the Great Ape Project argue for basic legal rights, and some jurisdictions (such as Spain in a 2008 parliamentary resolution, and New Zealand's Animal Welfare Act) have afforded great apes special protections, particularly against invasive research.
