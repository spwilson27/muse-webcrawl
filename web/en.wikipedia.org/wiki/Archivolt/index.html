Archivolt

An archivolt (from Italian archivolto) is an ornamental moulding or band that follows the curve of the underside and face of an arch. It corresponds to the architrave of classical architecture, transferred from a straight beam to a curved opening. A series of concentric archivolts, often richly carved, frames the great doorways of Romanesque and Gothic churches.

Description

The term may denote the moulding itself or the whole set of successive bands around an arch. Archivolts project slightly from the wall face and step outward in receding planes, creating the deep, shadowed reveals characteristic of medieval portals. The innermost band meets the jamb, while the outermost may terminate in a hood mould or dripstone.

Decoration

In Romanesque architecture archivolts carried geometric ornament — chevrons, billet, beakhead, and lozenge patterns — as at Durham Cathedral and in the great west fronts of French churches. In Gothic portals the bands were filled with carved figures: angels, saints, elders of the Apocalypse, and the signs of the zodiac or labours of the months, arranged radially like spokes. Celebrated examples include the Royal Portal of Chartres Cathedral, the portals of Amiens and Reims, and the north transept doorway of Santiago de Compostela, whose Pórtico de la Gloria bears figures of the apostles and prophets on its archivolts.

Usage

Beyond churches, archivolts appear on triumphal arches, bridges, and secular buildings in Roman, Byzantine, Islamic, and revival styles wherever an arched opening is given a decorative frame. In Islamic architecture the related feature is often rendered in contrasting voussoirs (ablaq masonry) or carved bands of inscription.
