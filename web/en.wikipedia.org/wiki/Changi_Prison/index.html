Changi Prison

Changi Prison is a prison complex in Changi, in the eastern part of Singapore. Built by the British colonial government in 1936, the original prison became notorious during the Second World War, when the wider Changi area was used by the Imperial Japanese Army as a prisoner-of-war camp for Allied servicemen and civilians after the fall of Singapore in February 1942. A modern Changi Prison Complex opened in phases beginning in 2004 and remains Singapore's principal maximum-security prison.

History

Changi Prison was constructed in 1936 as a maximum-security prison with a capacity of about 600 inmates. Following the surrender of Singapore on 15 February 1942, the Japanese military interned some 3,000 civilians in the prison itself, while tens of thousands of Allied prisoners of war were held in the surrounding Changi camp area, including Selarang Barracks. Conditions were harsh, and prisoners built the Changi Chapel, whose replica and museum today commemorate the wartime experience. In October 1943, following an Allied raid on Singapore Harbour (Operation Jaywick), Japanese authorities arrested and tortured suspected saboteurs in the "Double Tenth incident."

Post-war use

After the war the prison returned to civilian use. Under Singapore law it houses long-term and death-row prisoners; executions by hanging, carried out at dawn on Fridays, take place there, as does judicial corporal punishment by caning. In 1994 British journalist and later inmates' accounts drew international attention to the flogging of American teenager Michael Fay, who was caned at Changi for vandalism.

New complex

The old prison was largely demolished, with its entrance gate, tower, and a section of wall preserved as a heritage site. The new Changi Prison Complex, one of the largest and most technologically advanced prison facilities in the region, opened in stages from 2004 and consolidates several former prisons and drug rehabilitation centres into clusters within a single perimeter.
