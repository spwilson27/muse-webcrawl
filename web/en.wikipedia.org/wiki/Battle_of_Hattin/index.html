Battle of Hattin

[Image: the Horns of Hattin, the twin-peaked hill above the Sea of Galilee where the crusader army made its last stand]

The Battle of Hattin took place on 4 July 1187 between the crusader states of the Levant and the forces of the Ayyubid sultan Saladin. It proved a decisive defeat, resulting in the capture or death of the bulk of the crusader army and directly leading to Saladin's reconquest of Jerusalem and most of the Kingdom of Jerusalem, which in turn provoked the Third Crusade.

Background

Saladin had united Egypt and Syria and, by 1187, nearly encircled the crusader states. The truce was broken by Raynald of Châtillon's attacks on Muslim caravans and shipping. When Saladin invaded and besieged Tiberias, King Guy of Lusignan — against the advice of Raymond III of Tripoli — marched the kingdom's field army, some 18,000–20,000 men, across the waterless Galilee hills in midsummer heat to relieve it.

The battle

Harassed by Saladin's horse archers and cut off from water, the crusader column was halted short of the springs of Hattin on 3 July and surrounded on a plateau between the twin hills called the Horns of Hattin. On 4 July, tormented by thirst and by smoke from fires set in the dry brush, the infantry broke toward the Horns and the cavalry's desperate charges — led with distinction by Raymond, who cut his way out — could not save the army. King Guy, Raynald, the Grand Master of the Temple and many barons were captured; the relic of the True Cross was taken. Saladin personally executed Raynald, while most captured knights were ransomed or enslaved, and the captured Templars and Hospitallers were put to death.

Aftermath

With the crusader field army destroyed, Saladin overran the kingdom's cities almost unopposed; Jerusalem surrendered on 2 October 1187 after a short siege. Only Tyre held out. The catastrophe electrified Europe and brought the kings of England, France and the Holy Roman Empire east on the Third Crusade.
