Reversible inhibition

In enzyme kinetics, reversible inhibition refers to enzyme inhibitors that bind to the enzyme non-covalently, so that the inhibition can be reversed by removing the inhibitor — in contrast to irreversible inhibitors, which typically form permanent covalent bonds with the enzyme. Reversible inhibitors generally bind through hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and ionic bonds, and dissociate readily.

Types

Competitive inhibitors resemble the substrate and compete for binding at the active site; they raise the apparent Michaelis constant (Km) without changing Vmax, and their effect can be overcome by increasing substrate concentration. Non-competitive inhibitors bind at a site distinct from the active site and reduce Vmax without affecting Km. Uncompetitive inhibitors bind only to the enzyme–substrate complex, reducing both Vmax and Km. Mixed inhibitors bind to either form of the enzyme and produce a combination of effects.

Significance

Many drugs are reversible enzyme inhibitors; classic examples include the statins, which competitively inhibit HMG-CoA reductase, and the acetylcholinesterase inhibitors used in the treatment of Alzheimer's disease. Reversibility allows enzyme activity to recover as the drug is metabolised and cleared, making dosing more controllable than with irreversible agents.
