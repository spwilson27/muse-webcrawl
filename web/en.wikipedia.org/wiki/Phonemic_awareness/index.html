Phonemic awareness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Phonemic awareness instruction in an early-years classroom.

Phonemic awareness is a subset of phonological awareness in which listeners are able to hear, identify and manipulate phonemes, the smallest mental units of sound that help to differentiate one word from another in a given language. For example, a listener with phonemic awareness can identify that the word cat consists of the three phonemes /k/, /æ/, and /t/, and can manipulate them — deleting the first sound to make at, or substituting it to make mat.

Phonemic awareness is an oral and aural skill; it does not require print. It is distinguished from phonics, which involves the relationship between sounds and their spellings in written language.

Skills

Phonemic awareness tasks are commonly grouped into several categories:

  • Phoneme isolation — recognising the individual sounds in words, e.g. "Tell me the first sound in paste" (/p/).
  • Phoneme identity — recognising the common sound in different words, e.g. "Tell me the sound that is the same in bike, boy and bell" (/b/).
  • Phoneme blending — listening to a sequence of separately spoken phonemes and combining them to form a word, e.g. /d/ /ɔ/ /ɡ/ → dog.
  • Phoneme segmentation — breaking a word into its separate sounds, e.g. counting the sounds in ship.
  • Phoneme manipulation — adding, deleting, or substituting phonemes to create new words.

Relation to reading

Research reviewed by the U.S. National Reading Panel (2000) found that teaching phonemic awareness improves children's word reading and reading comprehension, with effects that endure beyond the period of instruction. Phonemic awareness is considered one of the strongest predictors of later reading success, and difficulties with it are associated with dyslexia. Instruction is most effective when children are taught to manipulate phonemes using letters of the alphabet, bridging the oral skill into phonics.
