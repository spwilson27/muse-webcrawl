Loss leader

A loss leader (also called a leader) is a pricing strategy in which a product is sold at a price below its market cost — or even below the seller's own cost — in order to stimulate sales of other, more profitable goods or services. The strategy is a form of promotional pricing widely used in retail.

How it works

The featured item is priced to attract customers into a store (or onto a platform) in the expectation that, once there, they will also buy regular-priced items. Supermarkets classically price staples such as milk, bread, or rotisserie chickens at or below cost; electronics retailers discount a headline appliance; game consoles and printers have historically been sold near or below cost with profits made on games, cartridges, and subscriptions.

Variants and strategies

  • Traffic builders: heavily advertised specials, sometimes in strictly limited quantities.
  • Bait pricing within a platform: a subsidised device locks consumers into a profitable ecosystem of consumables or content.
  • Razor-and-blades model: the durable product is cheap and the consumables expensive.

Risks include "cherry picking" by customers who buy only the loss leader, stockpiling by small traders for resale, and retaliation by competitors.

Legal status

Some jurisdictions restrict below-cost selling: several US states have "unfair sales" statutes setting minimum mark-ups on items such as fuel and tobacco, and some countries (for example Ireland under the former Groceries Order, and France under resale-below-cost rules) have historically limited loss-leading on groceries, partly to protect small retailers. Enforcement and scope vary widely.
