Battle of White Mountain

The Battle of White Mountain (Czech: Bitva na Bílé hoře) was fought on 8 November 1620 at Bílá Hora, just west of Prague, during the Bohemian phase of the Thirty Years' War. The army of the Bohemian Protestant estates, who had rebelled against the Habsburgs and elected Frederick V (the "Winter King") as King of Bohemia, was crushed in about two hours by the combined forces of the Catholic League and the Imperial army. The battle ended the Bohemian Revolt and reshaped Czech history for centuries.

The battle

The Bohemian army held a strong position on the White Mountain ridge west of Prague but was tired, unpaid, and incompletely deployed when the Imperial and League armies attacked on a foggy morning. After initial cavalry clashes, Imperial infantry and cavalry attacks broke the Bohemian left and centre; much of the army fled toward Prague, and the battle was over within about two hours. Frederick V fled the city the next day, ending his one-winter reign.

Aftermath

The consequences for Bohemia were sweeping: twenty-seven leaders of the revolt were executed in Prague's Old Town Square in June 1621; Protestant nobles' estates were confiscated on a massive scale and the land redistributed to Catholic loyalists; the Renewed Land Ordinance of 1627 made Bohemia a hereditary Habsburg land and Catholicism the only permitted religion, driving much of the Protestant elite — including Comenius — into exile. In Czech national memory, White Mountain long symbolised the loss of independence and the beginning of a period of "darkness" (temno).
