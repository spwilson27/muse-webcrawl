La Brea Tar Pits

The La Brea Tar Pits is an active paleontological research site in urban Los Angeles. Hancock Park was formed around a group of tar pits where natural asphalt (also called asphaltum, bitumen, or pitch; Spanish brea, "resin") has seeped up from the ground for tens of thousands of years. Over many centuries, the bones of trapped animals have been preserved in the pits, making La Brea one of the world's most famous fossil localities. The George C. Page Museum, part of the Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County, presents the site's Ice Age fossils.

Fossil record

The pits have yielded millions of fossils representing thousands of species, most dating between about 11,000 and 50,000 years old. Herbivores that came to drink at water covering the asphalt became trapped; their struggles attracted predators, which were trapped in turn — an unusual predator trap that explains the site's strikingly high proportion of carnivores such as dire wolves (thousands of individuals), saber-toothed cats (Smilodon fatalis, California's state fossil), American lions, and short-faced bears, alongside Columbian mammoths, mastodons, ground sloths, camels, and bison. Microfossils of plants, insects, and small animals preserve a detailed record of the last Ice Age environment. Only one human skeleton, the partial remains of "La Brea Woman" (c. 10,000 years ago), has been found.

History

Local Tongva and Chumash people used the asphalt to waterproof baskets and canoes. Scientific excavation began in the early 20th century on land owned by the Hancock family; in 1913, George Allan Hancock granted the county exclusive excavation rights, and the excavated collections formed a cornerstone of the county museum. Excavation continues today, notably at "Project 23", a set of deposits salvaged in 2006 and still being processed.
