Sheet film

Sheet film is a photographic film format in which large individual sheets of film are used for each exposure, rather than rolls or strips of film. It is used principally in large format cameras, in which each sheet is loaded into a film holder, typically in a darkroom or by means of a changing bag, and exposed one at a time.

History

Sheet film developed from the glass plate era of photography. Early photographers coated glass plates with emulsion; as flexible film bases became available in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, individual cut sheets of film replaced plates in plate holders, using thin separators or purpose-made film holders. Film packs, in which several sheets could be pulled through a camera back in sequence, were also popular in the early to mid twentieth century.

Formats

Common sheet film sizes include 4×5 inches, 5×7 inches, and 8×10 inches, with metric sizes such as 9×12 cm also used in Europe. Larger and smaller specialty sizes exist, including 11×14 inches and whole-plate formats inherited from the glass plate era.

Characteristics

Because each sheet is separate, the photographer can process individual exposures differently, a flexibility used in the Zone System of exposure and development control developed by Ansel Adams and Fred Archer. Sheet film also permits the use of camera movements—tilts, swings, shifts, and rise and fall—in view cameras, which control perspective and the plane of focus.

Most sheet films have a notch code cut into one corner of the sheet so the photographer can identify the emulsion type and orientation by touch in total darkness. The film base is usually a thicker polyester or acetate than roll film, to keep the sheet flat in the holder.
