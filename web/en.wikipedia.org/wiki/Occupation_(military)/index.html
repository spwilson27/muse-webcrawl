Military occupation
Governed by: Hague Regulations (1907), Fourth Geneva Convention (1949)

Military occupation, also called belligerent occupation, is the temporary control of a territory by a hostile army without a claim of formal sovereignty. Territory is considered occupied when it is actually placed under the authority of the hostile army (Hague Regulations, Article 42).

Legal framework

The law of occupation is set out in the Hague Regulations of 1907 (Articles 42–56), the Fourth Geneva Convention of 1949, and Additional Protocol I of 1977. The occupant must restore and ensure public order and safety while respecting, unless absolutely prevented, the laws in force in the territory.

Rights and duties

Occupation does not transfer sovereignty; annexation of occupied territory is prohibited. The occupant may not deport or transfer parts of the population, nor transfer its own civilian population into the territory (GC IV, Article 49). Private property must be respected; the occupant administers public property only as usufructuary.

Distinctions

Occupation differs from invasion (a phase of movement), annexation (formal incorporation), and colonial administration. Prolonged occupations — such as Allied occupations of Germany and Japan after 1945, or the Israeli occupation of Palestinian territories since 1967 — raise contested questions about transformative administration and duration.
