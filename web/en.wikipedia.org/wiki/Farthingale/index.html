Farthingale

TypeHooped underskirt
OriginSpain, 15th century
In fashionc. 1500–1650

A farthingale is a hooped petticoat worn beneath a skirt to extend and shape it into a cone, bell, or drum silhouette. It was one of the most characteristic garments of sixteenth- and early seventeenth-century European court dress.

History

The farthingale originated in Spain as the verdugado, a skirt stiffened with hoops of cane, rope, or whalebone sewn into casings. It reached England by the early sixteenth century — Catherine of Aragon is often credited with introducing Spanish fashion to the Tudor court — and became essential to the dress of Elizabethan England. The Spanish farthingale produced a conical silhouette, while the French or "wheel" farthingale of the 1580s–1620s tilted the skirt into a wide drum shape at the waist, famously seen in portraits of Elizabeth I and Anne of Denmark.

Construction

Hoops were made from whalebone (baleen), cane, or bent wood inserted into channels in a linen or cotton petticoat — an early example of engineered garment structure. The garment's name derives, through French verdugalle, from the Spanish verdugo, meaning a green rod or shoot, referring to the flexible withies used for hoops.

Decline

Farthingales fell from fashion in the mid-seventeenth century as softer silhouettes prevailed, though their principle returned in later centuries as the crinoline and the hoop skirt.
