A wind instrument is a musical instrument that produces sound from a vibrating column of air, usually enclosed in a tube or resonator, set in motion by the player's breath or by a mechanical air supply such as the bellows of an organ. Pitch is controlled by changing the effective length of the air column — opening and closing holes or valves, extending slides or tubing — and by the player's technique, such as overblowing to reach higher harmonics.

Classification

In the Western orchestra, wind instruments are divided into two families. Woodwind instruments produce sound with an edge (as in the flute), a single cane reed (clarinet, saxophone), or double reeds (oboe, bassoon); the name is historical, since some are now made of metal or plastic. Brass instruments — trumpet, horn, trombone, and tuba — are sounded by the vibration of the player's lips against a mouthpiece, and are typically made of brass. In the Hornbostel–Sachs system all are classed as aerophones, subdivided by how the air is excited.

Types of excitation

  • Edge-blown (flutes): air directed against a sharp edge splits and oscillates; includes transverse flutes, recorders, whistles, and flue organ pipes.
  • Single reed: a cane reed beats against a mouthpiece (clarinet, saxophone).
  • Double reed: two reeds vibrate against each other (oboe, bassoon, bagpipe chanters).
  • Lip reed: the player's lips act as the reed (all brass instruments, plus conchs and alphorns).
  • Free reed: air passes over a flexible tongue, as in the harmonica, accordion, and reed organ — sometimes grouped separately from other wind instruments.

History

Wind instruments are among the oldest instruments known: bone and ivory flutes from the Palaeolithic, such as those from the Swabian caves of Germany, are more than 35,000 years old. Ancient civilisations developed end-blown and transverse flutes, reed pipes, and early trumpets and horns, and the modern families took shape through keywork and valve inventions of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — valves for brass around 1815, and Theobald Boehm's redesigned flute and fingering system in the 1830s–1840s.

Physics

The air column resonates at frequencies determined by its length and whether the tube behaves as open at both ends (flutes, oboes) or stopped at one end (clarinet), which affects which harmonics are available. Bore shape — cylindrical versus conical — strongly influences timbre, and the bell of brass instruments efficiently radiates the sound.
