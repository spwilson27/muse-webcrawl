LSD

Lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), commonly known as acid, is a potent psychedelic drug. It is known for its intense psychological effects, altered thinking processes, closed- and open-eye visuals, synesthesia, an altered sense of time, and spiritual experiences. It was first synthesized by Swiss chemist Albert Hofmann in 1938 from lysergic acid, a chemical derived from the hydrolysis of ergotamine, an alkaloid in the fungus ergot. Its psychedelic effects were discovered by accident in 1943.

History

Albert Hofmann first synthesized LSD at Sandoz Laboratories in Basel, Switzerland, in 1938 while researching derivatives of ergot alkaloids. Its psychoactive properties went unnoticed until 16 April 1943, when Hofmann accidentally absorbed a small amount and experienced unusual sensations. Three days later, on 19 April 1943 — remembered as "Bicycle Day" — he deliberately took 250 micrograms and rode his bicycle home while experiencing intense effects. Sandoz marketed the drug under the name Delysid from 1947.

During the 1950s and 1960s LSD was widely researched as a psychiatric tool, including by the CIA's MKUltra program. It became central to the counterculture of the 1960s, associated with figures such as Timothy Leary and Ken Kesey, before being prohibited in most countries. A renewed wave of clinical research since the 2000s has explored its potential in treating depression, anxiety, and addiction.

Effects

LSD acts primarily as an agonist at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors. Effects typically last 8–12 hours and include visual distortion, intensified colors, geometric patterns, euphoria, and profound changes in thought and perception of time. Physiological effects are modest: dilated pupils, increased heart rate and blood pressure, and elevated body temperature. LSD is considered non-addictive and has low physiological toxicity, though adverse psychological reactions ("bad trips") can occur.
