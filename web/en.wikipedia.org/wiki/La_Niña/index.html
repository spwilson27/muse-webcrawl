La Niña

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

La Niña (Spanish: [la ˈniɲa]; "The Girl") is a coupled ocean–atmosphere phenomenon that is the colder counterpart of El Niño, as part of the broader El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) climate pattern. During a La Niña event, sea surface temperatures in the eastern and central equatorial Pacific Ocean are cooler than average, and the trade winds strengthen, pushing warm water toward Asia and increasing upwelling of cold water off the Americas.

Impacts

La Niña typically brings wetter than normal conditions to eastern Australia, Southeast Asia and parts of southern Africa, and drier conditions to the southwestern United States, parts of South America and East Africa. It is associated with increased Atlantic hurricane activity because of reduced wind shear, and with colder winters in the northern United States and Canada. A rare "triple-dip" La Niña persisted from 2020 into 2023.

Definition and monitoring

Agencies such as NOAA declare a La Niña when sea surface temperatures in the Niño 3.4 region average at least 0.5 °C below normal for several overlapping three-month periods and the atmosphere responds correspondingly. La Niña events often, but not always, follow El Niño events.
