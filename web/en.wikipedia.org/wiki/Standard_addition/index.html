Standard addition

The method of standard addition is a type of quantitative analysis approach often used in analytical chemistry whereby the standard is added directly to the aliquots of the analysed sample. This method is used in situations where sample matrix also contributes to the analytical signal, a situation known as the matrix effect, thus making it unsuitable to compare the analytical signal between the sample and standard using the traditional calibration curve approach.

Procedure

A series of equal volumes of the sample solution are taken, all but one are separately spiked with known and different amounts of the analyte, and all are then diluted to the same volume. The instrument signals are then measured for all solutions and the results plotted: signal on the y-axis, added amount on the x-axis. The regression line is extrapolated to the point where the signal is zero; the magnitude of the negative x-intercept equals the amount of analyte in the unspiked sample aliquot.

Rationale

Because each solution contains the same amount of sample matrix, the matrix affects the analyte and the added standard equally. The slope of the standard addition line therefore reflects the sensitivity of the measurement in the actual sample matrix, allowing the original concentration to be determined despite matrix interference.

Applications

Standard addition is used with techniques such as atomic absorption spectroscopy, inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry, and voltammetry, particularly for complex samples such as seawater, biological fluids, and industrial effluents.
