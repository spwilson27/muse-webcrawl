Solvent extraction

Solvent extraction, also known as liquid–liquid extraction, is a method to separate compounds or metal complexes based on their relative solubilities in two different immiscible liquids, usually water (polar) and an organic solvent (non-polar). It is a widely used separation technique in chemical analysis, hydrometallurgy, pharmaceuticals, and nuclear fuel reprocessing.

A separatory funnel, the classic laboratory apparatus for liquid–liquid extraction.

Principles

When a solution containing a solute is shaken with a second immiscible solvent, the solute distributes itself between the two phases according to its partition coefficient, the ratio of its concentrations in each phase at equilibrium. Extraction is most efficient when performed repeatedly with small portions of solvent rather than once with a large volume. In metal extraction, extractants dissolved in the organic phase selectively bind target metal ions; common extractants include organophosphorus compounds such as tributyl phosphate (TBP) and hydroxyoximes used in copper recovery.

Industrial applications

Solvent extraction is used at large scale to produce copper (accounting for a substantial share of world production via the SX-EW process), uranium, nickel, cobalt, rare-earth elements, and platinum-group metals. In the nuclear industry, the PUREX process uses TBP in kerosene to separate uranium and plutonium from spent fuel. The petrochemical industry uses extraction to separate aromatics, and the food industry to produce edible oils from seeds and decaffeinated coffee. Industrial plants use mixer-settlers, pulsed columns, or centrifugal contactors to achieve many extraction stages in countercurrent flow.

Laboratory use

In the laboratory, extractions are performed in a separatory funnel: the two phases are shaken together, allowed to settle, and drained separately. Practical difficulties include the formation of emulsions, which can be broken by adding brine, filtering, or centrifugation. Acid–base extraction exploits ionisation: an organic acid or base can be driven into an aqueous phase by converting it to its water-soluble salt, then recovered by reversing the pH.
