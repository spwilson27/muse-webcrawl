Myers–Briggs Type Indicator

The Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) is a self-report questionnaire that sorts respondents into 16 psychological types. It was developed by Katharine Cook Briggs and her daughter Isabel Briggs Myers during the Second World War, building on Carl Jung's 1921 theory of psychological types, and was first published commercially in 1962. Despite its popularity in business and career counselling, the test has been widely criticised by psychologists for weak reliability and validity.

The four dichotomies

The indicator classifies people along four pairs of preferences: extraversion (E) vs. introversion (I); sensing (S) vs. intuition (N); thinking (T) vs. feeling (F); and judging (J) vs. perceiving (P). Combining one preference from each pair yields a four-letter type such as INTJ or ESFP, to which type descriptions attribute characteristic strengths and tendencies.

Use and popularity

Millions of assessments are taken annually; the MBTI is used by many companies and government agencies for team-building and leadership development, though the publisher itself cautions against using it for hiring decisions.

Criticism

Scientific reviews have found that scores on most MBTI scales cluster near the midpoint rather than falling into distinct types, test–retest reliability is low, and there is little evidence that MBTI types predict job performance. Many psychologists therefore classify it as a pseudoscientific instrument, while supporters value it as a framework for self-reflection and discussion.
