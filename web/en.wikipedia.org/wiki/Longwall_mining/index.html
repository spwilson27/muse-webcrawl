Longwall mining

A longwall shearer working a coal face under hydraulic roof supports

Longwall mining is a form of underground coal mining where a long wall of coal is mined in a single slice, typically 0.6 to 6.0 metres thick. A panel (the block of coal being mined) is normally 150 to 300 metres wide and 1,000 to 3,500 metres long. A mechanised shearer cuts the coal from the face, which falls onto an armoured face conveyor and is carried to the surface; as the shearer advances, self-advancing hydraulic supports hold up the roof at the face line, and the roof behind the supports is allowed to collapse into the void (the "goaf" or "gob").

Advantages and disadvantages

Longwall mining achieves a much higher recovery of the coal seam than room-and-pillar methods — often over 75% of a panel — and, once set up, is highly productive and comparatively safe, because miners work under powered supports and exposure to the unsupported face is limited. It is used extensively in Australia, the United States, China and Europe.

Its disadvantages include very high capital costs for equipment, inflexibility once a panel layout is chosen, and surface subsidence: as the roof collapses behind the face, the ground above subsides predictably, which can damage buildings, watercourses and wetlands, and requires planning and remediation.

History

The method developed in Britain from the late 18th and 19th centuries as an advance on earlier "longwall" hand methods, and was progressively mechanised in the 20th century with power loaders, armoured conveyors and powered supports, becoming the dominant method in deep coal mining worldwide.
