Transient ischemic attack

A transient ischemic attack (TIA), often called a mini-stroke, is a temporary period of symptoms similar to those of a stroke, caused by a brief blockage of blood flow to part of the brain, spinal cord or retina. It usually lasts only a few minutes and causes no permanent damage; the modern tissue-based definition requires no infarction on imaging.

Symptoms and causes

Symptoms mirror stroke: sudden weakness or numbness on one side, speech difficulty, vision loss in one eye, or loss of balance, resolving within an hour in most cases. Causes include a clot from the heart (as in atrial fibrillation), carotid artery stenosis, and small-vessel disease; hypertension, diabetes, smoking and high cholesterol are the main risk factors.

Diagnosis and treatment

A TIA is a medical emergency because the risk of a subsequent stroke is highest in the following days. Assessment includes brain imaging, carotid ultrasound and heart rhythm monitoring; the ABCD² score estimates early stroke risk. Urgent treatment — antiplatelet therapy, statins, anticoagulation where appropriate, and carotid endarterectomy for severe symptomatic stenosis — substantially lowers that risk.
