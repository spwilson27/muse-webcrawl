Préciosité

Country: France

Period: c. 1630s–1660s

Key figures: Madeleine de Scudéry, Madame de Rambouillet

Préciosité was a literary and social movement in 17th-century France, centred on the salons of cultivated women known as the précieuses. Its adherents cultivated witty, refined conversation, elaborate codes of courtly love, and a highly metaphorical, euphemistic style of language that avoided anything considered vulgar or commonplace.

Origins

The movement grew out of the salon of Catherine de Vivonne, marquise de Rambouillet, whose Chambre bleue gathered aristocrats and writers in reaction against the coarseness of the court of Henri IV. Later salons, such as that of Madeleine de Scudéry, developed the movement's characteristic concerns: the analysis of tender feeling, exemplified by Scudéry's famous Carte de Tendre (Map of Tenderness) in her novel Clélie (1654–1660), and the elevation of women as arbiters of taste and manners.

Language

The précieuses favoured periphrasis and metaphor: a mirror might be called a "counsellor of the graces" and teeth "the furnishings of the mouth." Antoine Baudeau de Somaize compiled a satirical Grand Dictionnaire des Précieuses (1661) cataloguing this vocabulary.

Satire and legacy

Molière mocked the movement in his comedy Les Précieuses ridicules (1659), which targeted provincial imitators aping Parisian refinement. Though satirised for affectation, préciosité contributed to the purification of French prose style, the development of the psychological novel, and an unprecedented public role for women in French literary culture.
