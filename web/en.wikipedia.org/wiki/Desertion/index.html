Desertion is the abandonment of a duty or post without permission and with the intention of not returning. In military law it is among the most serious offences: it differs from absence without leave (AWOL) precisely in the intent to remain away permanently, or to avoid important service such as deployment or combat. Deserters who flee to the enemy may additionally be treated as defectors or traitors.

History

Armies have punished desertion with death for most of recorded history. In the First World War, the British Army executed 306 of its own soldiers, mostly for desertion or cowardice — men posthumously pardoned by the UK in 2006 — while Germany executed far fewer of its soldiers in that war but tens of thousands in the Second. The United States executed one deserter in the Second World War, Private Eddie Slovik (1945), the only American put to death for the offence since the Civil War. In that war roughly 21,000 US soldiers were sentenced for desertion, most receiving prison terms and dishonourable discharges.

Modern practice

Most Western armed forces no longer execute deserters; the offence is tried by court-martial, with punishment typically imprisonment and a punitive discharge. Under Article 85 of the US UCMJ, desertion in time of war remains theoretically punishable by death, though no execution has occurred since 1945. Desertion rates spike in unpopular wars: during the Vietnam and Iraq wars, some deserters sought refuge in Canada, Sweden, and elsewhere, and their cases became politically contentious. Mass desertion has also marked collapsing armies, from 1917 Russia to modern conflicts.
