Criticality accident

A criticality accident is an unintended and uncontrolled nuclear fission chain reaction. It is sometimes referred to as a critical excursion, critical power excursion, divergent chain reaction, or simply a critical accident. Any such event involves the unintended accumulation or arrangement of a critical mass of fissile material, for example enriched uranium or plutonium.

Criticality accidents can release potentially lethal doses of radiation and are therefore among the most feared hazards in facilities that handle fissile material outside nuclear reactors. The energy released is generally far smaller than in a nuclear weapon explosion, but the intense burst of neutrons and gamma rays close to the event can be fatal within days.

History

The first recorded criticality accident occurred on 21 August 1945 at Los Alamos, when physicist Harry Daghlian accidentally dropped a tungsten carbide brick onto a sphere of plutonium — later nicknamed the "demon core" — causing a burst of radiation that killed him 25 days later. On 21 May 1946, Louis Slotin suffered a similar accident with the same core while demonstrating a criticality experiment, dying nine days later. These accidents led to far stricter controls on hands-on criticality experiments.

Causes and prevention

Process criticality accidents typically result from errors in controlling the mass, geometry, moderation, or concentration of fissile solutions — for example pouring solution into an unsafe vessel geometry. Prevention relies on the "double contingency" principle: process designs should require at least two independent, unlikely changes before a criticality accident becomes possible. Facilities also employ criticality alarms, mass limits, safe-geometry vessels, and neutron-absorbing materials.

Blue flash

Many criticality accidents have been reported as accompanied by a flash of blue light, caused by the ionisation of air and Cherenkov-like effects from the intense burst of radiation and excited molecules returning to a neutral state. Witnesses of the Slotin accident reported such a flash accompanied by a wave of heat.
