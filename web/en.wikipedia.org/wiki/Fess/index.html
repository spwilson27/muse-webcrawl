In heraldry, a fess or fesse (from Middle English fesse, from Old French fesse, from Latin fascia, "band") is a charge on a coat of arms that takes the form of a band running horizontally across the centre of the shield. Writers disagree on how much of the shield's surface it should occupy, generally between one-fifth and one-third.

Description

The fess is one of the ordinaries, the simple geometric charges that form the oldest elements of heraldic design. Like the other ordinaries, it is conventionally thought to derive from the structural reinforcements of a knight's shield. A fess may be accompanied by other charges placed above, below, or "on" the fess itself; when charges are borne on it, they are blazoned as being on a fess.

Variations

Diminutives of the fess include the bar, a narrower horizontal band, of which several may appear on one shield, and the barrulet. A fess with a wavy edge is a fess wavy; other variations include the fess dancetty (zigzag), engrailed, embattled and nebuly. A fess placed higher than the centre is said to be enhanced. The heraldic flag of some municipalities uses a fess as a central element.

Examples

Well-known arms bearing a fess include those of several English and Scottish families, and the fess appears in the civic arms of cities such as those using a plain horizontal band to represent a river crossing or a road.
