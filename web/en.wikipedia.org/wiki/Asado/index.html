Asado

Asado
Type: Barbecue technique / social event
Region: Southern Cone (Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, Chile, southern Brazil)
Main ingredients: Beef, chorizo, morcilla, offal

Asado is a technique and social event of having a barbecue in several South American countries, especially Argentina, Chile, Paraguay, Peru, Uruguay, and southern Brazil. An asado typically features beef alongside pork, chicken, chorizo, and morcilla (blood sausage), cooked on a grill called a parrilla or over an open fire, and is as much a social ritual as a meal.

The cooking

The grill master, the asador, manages hardwood embers (brasas), cooking the meat slowly and seasoning it mainly with coarse salt; chimichurri sauce is a common accompaniment. A traditional sequence begins with offal and sausages — chinchulines (intestines), mollejas (sweetbreads), chorizo, and morcilla, often served in a choripán sandwich — followed by cuts such as vacío (flank), tira de asado (short ribs), and entraña (skirt steak).

Origins

The tradition descends from the gauchos of the Pampas, who roasted beef over open fires on the vast cattle plains. With urbanisation in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the asado moved to backyards, rooftop terraces, and quinchos (dedicated barbecue rooms), becoming a centrepiece of family and social life, typically held at weekends.

Regional variations

In Uruguay the parrilla tradition is equally central; in Brazil the related churrasco uses skewers and a wider range of meats; in Chile and Paraguay local cuts, seasonings, and accompaniments such as sopa paraguaya vary. In Argentina per-capita beef consumption historically ranked among the highest in the world.
