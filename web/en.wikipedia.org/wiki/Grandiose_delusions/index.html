Grandiose delusions

[Image placeholder: Conceptual illustration of grandiose belief]

Grandiose delusions (GDs), also called delusions of grandeur or expansive delusions, are a subtype of delusion characterised by the false belief that one possesses exceptional abilities, fame, wealth, power or importance, or a special relationship with a deity or famous person. They occur most often in schizophrenia and in manic episodes of bipolar disorder, but may also appear in other conditions involving psychosis.

Symptoms

A person with grandiose delusions may believe they are a famous figure (sometimes the actual person), possess a great unrecognised talent or discovery, hold a secret relationship with a prominent person, or have special powers or a divine mission. The beliefs are held with unshakeable conviction despite contradictory evidence, and they exceed mere inflated self-esteem because they are frankly impossible or implausible. Subtypes include delusions of special identity, exceptional ability, wealth, fame and religious significance.

Causes and occurrence

Grandiose delusions are reported in a substantial proportion of people with schizophrenia and in the majority of manic episodes. Researchers have linked them to abnormal reasoning about the self, positive emotion, and reward processing, and they may serve psychological functions such as protecting self-esteem. They also occur in schizoaffective disorder, substance-induced psychosis and some neurological conditions.

Treatment

Treatment targets the underlying disorder, usually with antipsychotic or mood-stabilising medication. Psychological approaches such as cognitive behavioural therapy for psychosis work indirectly on the belief — for example by addressing associated distress and behaviour — since directly confronting a delusion is rarely effective.
