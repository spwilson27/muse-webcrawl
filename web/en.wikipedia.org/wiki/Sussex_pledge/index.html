Sussex pledge

The Sussex pledge was a promise made by Germany to the United States in May 1916, during the First World War, restricting German submarine warfare. After the torpedoing of the French ferry Sussex, President Woodrow Wilson threatened to sever diplomatic relations; Germany pledged that its U-boats would not sink merchant or passenger vessels without warning and without ensuring the safety of those aboard, unless the ships resisted or tried to escape.

Background

Germany's campaign of unrestricted submarine warfare had already caused crises with the neutral United States, above all the sinking of the liner Lusitania in May 1915 (128 Americans killed) and the Arabic incident that August, after which Germany gave the "Arabic pledge" not to sink liners without warning. On 24 March 1916 the German submarine UB-29 torpedoed the cross-Channel ferry Sussex in the English Channel, injuring dozens, including several Americans. In an address to Congress on 19 April, Wilson delivered an ultimatum: unless Germany abandoned its methods against passenger and freight vessels, the United States would break off relations.

Terms

On 4 May 1916 the German government replied with the pledge: merchant vessels would not be sunk without warning and without saving human lives, subject to the conditions that the ships did not flee or resist. Germany reserved the right to resume unrestricted warfare if the United States failed to pressure Britain over its blockade — a qualification Washington rejected, but the crisis subsided.

Consequences

The pledge kept America out of the war for most of another year. On 31 January 1917, however, Germany — calculating that Britain could be starved before American forces arrived — announced the resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare, effectively repudiating the pledge. The United States broke relations on 3 February and, following the Zimmermann Telegram revelations and further sinkings, declared war on Germany on 6 April 1917.
