Rye

Scientific nameSecale cereale L.
FamilyPoaceae (grasses)
UsesBread flour, whiskey, animal feed, cover crop

Rye (Secale cereale) is a grass grown extensively as a grain, a cover crop and a forage crop. It is a member of the wheat tribe (Triticeae), closely related to wheat and barley, and is notable for its hardiness: rye tolerates cold winters, drought and poor, sandy or acidic soils far better than wheat.

History

Rye probably began as a weed of wheat and barley fields in Anatolia and the Caucasus and was selected into a crop in its own right — an example of Vavilovian mimicry. It became the staple bread grain of northern and eastern Europe, where dark rye breads such as pumpernickel and rugbrød remain characteristic. World production is concentrated in Germany, Poland, Russia and Belarus.

Cultivation and uses

Most rye is sown in autumn as a winter cereal. The grain is milled into flour for bread, crispbread and crackers; distilled into rye whiskey and gin; and used for beer, animal fodder and straw. Rye is high in dietary fibre and contains gluten, though less than wheat. Its major disease hazard is infection by the ergot fungus (Claviceps purpurea), whose alkaloid-laden sclerotia historically caused outbreaks of ergotism ("St Anthony's fire") and are now a source of pharmaceuticals.

Hybrid

The wheat × rye hybrid triticale combines rye's hardiness with wheat's yield and quality and is grown widely as a feed grain.
