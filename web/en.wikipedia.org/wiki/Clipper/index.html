Clipper

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A tea clipper under full sail racing through heavy seas]
A clipper at full sail.

A clipper was a very fast sailing ship of the 19th century, typically three-masted and full-rigged, with a long, narrow hull, sharp bow and large sail area. American clippers served the California gold-rush trade; British \'tea clippers\' such as Cutty Sark raced home from China with the season\'s first tea, and wool clippers ran from Australia. Steamships and the Suez Canal ended their reign by the 1870s–80s.

Origins

The type emerged in the United States in the 1840s (Rainbow, 1845; Sea Witch), sacrificing cargo space for speed. Gold discoveries in California (1848) and Australia (1851) created premium freight for speed, and ships like Flying Cloud made record passages — New York to San Francisco in 89 days (1854), a record that stood for over a century.

Tea races

British clippers such as Ariel, Taeping and Cutty Sark (1869) raced annually from Foochow to London; the 1866 Great Tea Race ended in a near dead-heat after some 99 days at sea. Cutty Sark, preserved at Greenwich, is the last surviving tea clipper.

Decline

Steamships on schedule, plus the Suez Canal (1869) — useless to most sailing ships — removed clippers\' markets. The name lived on in the 1930s Pan Am \'Clipper\' flying boats and various cars and aircraft.
