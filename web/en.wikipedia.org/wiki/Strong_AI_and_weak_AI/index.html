Strong AI and weak AI

Weak AI solves specific tasks; strong AI would possess a genuine mind.

The terms strong AI and weak AI describe two hypotheses about artificial intelligence, coined by philosopher John Searle in his 1980 paper "Minds, Brains, and Programs," which presented the Chinese room argument. The distinction concerns not processing power but the nature of what a successful AI would be.

Definitions

  • Weak AI (also called narrow AI): the claim that machines can be built to act as if intelligent — to simulate understanding and solve problems in limited domains — without the machine genuinely understanding or having mental states. Chess engines, speech recognizers, and recommender systems are weak AI.
  • Strong AI: the claim that a suitably programmed computer does not merely simulate a mind but literally is a mind: it would understand, have beliefs, intentions, and other cognitive states, and (in most formulations) be conscious.

The Chinese room

Searle's thought experiment imagines a person in a room manipulating Chinese symbols by rulebook to produce sensible replies to Chinese questions, without understanding Chinese. To outsiders the room passes a Turing test in Chinese, yet no understanding exists inside. Searle argued that running a program similarly produces syntax without semantics — hence strong AI, in the sense that a program alone suffices for a mind, is false. Replies include the systems reply (the whole system understands), the robot reply (embodiment would supply semantics), and the brain simulator reply; the debate remains central to philosophy of mind and cognitive science.

Modern usage

In contemporary industry usage, "weak/narrow AI" refers to systems built for specific tasks (the only kind that exists), while "strong AI" is often used interchangeably with artificial general intelligence (AGI) — a hypothetical system with human-level generality across tasks. The philosophical sense differs subtly: AGI usually denotes capability, while Searle's strong AI denotes genuine mentality. A further hypothetical category, artificial superintelligence, exceeds human ability broadly. Researchers disagree widely on whether and when AGI is achievable, and on whether large language models constitute steps toward it.
