Sui dynasty

The Sui dynasty (隋朝) was a short-lived Chinese dynasty that ruled from 581 to 618 and reunified China after nearly four centuries of division. Founded by Yang Jian (Emperor Wen), who usurped the throne of the Northern Zhou and then conquered the Chen dynasty in the south in 589, the Sui reunified the country for the first time since the fall of the Han, resumed work on the Great Wall, dug the Grand Canal linking the Yellow and Yangtze rivers, rebuilt the capitals of Chang'an (Daxing) and Luoyang, standardised coinage, and institutionalised the civil-service examination system — laying the institutional foundations on which the long and glorious Tang dynasty that followed would build. The dynasty collapsed under Yang Jian's son, Emperor Yang, whose grandiose construction projects, extravagant tours and three disastrous invasions of the Korean kingdom of Goguryeo provoked nationwide rebellion; he was strangled in a palace coup in 618, and within a year Li Yuan had founded the Tang.

Emperor Wen and reunification

Yang Jian was a general of mixed Han and Xianbei aristocratic stock who became regent for the child emperor of the Northern Zhou and in 581 took the throne himself, massacring the Zhou imperial clan. His reign as Emperor Wen was frugal, energetic and reforming: he streamlined the bureaucracy into the durable Three Departments and Six Ministries system, promulgated the Kaihuang Code (a simpler and milder legal code that long outlived the dynasty), reformed the land-equalisation system, established state granaries, and married his forceful empress Dugu Qieluo in a famously monogamous imperial partnership. In 589 his fleets and armies crossed the Yangtze and destroyed the corrupt Chen dynasty at Jiankang (Nanjing) almost without resistance, reuniting north and south after 369 years of division.

Emperor Yang and the great projects

Yang Guang succeeded in 604 after his father's death — later tradition accused him of murdering both father and elder brother. Emperor Yang was brilliant, ambitious and ruinous. He built the eastern capital at Luoyang in a single year with monthly levies of two million labourers; threw the Grand Canal into a completed system of some 1,700–2,000 km joining Luoyang to the Yangtze delta and north to the region of modern Beijing — the artery of Chinese unity ever since, but dug at a cost in corvée labour, much of it female when men ran out, that tradition numbers in the millions of deaths; extended the Great Wall against the Turks; received embassies from Central Asia and Japan (the famous letter from the Japanese prince Shōtoku addressing him from "the Son of Heaven in the land of the rising sun" to his displeasure); and toured his empire in floating palaces.

The Goguryeo wars and collapse

The refusal of Goguryeo, in Korea and Manchuria, to submit set off the catastrophe. Three huge invasions (612, 613, 614) — the first of them said to have mobilised over a million soldiers — ended in disaster: the fleet and army of 612 were ambushed at the Salsu River with losses tradition reckons in the hundreds of thousands, and the second invasion dissolved when the general Yang Xuangan rebelled at home. Rebellion spread across the empire from 613; Turks nearly captured the emperor at Yanmen in 615; and as the court withdrew south to Jiangdu, the north fell to rebel leaders — Li Mi, Dou Jiande, Du Fuwei — and to the Taiyuan governor Li Yuan, whose son Li Shimin would become the great Tang emperor Taizong. In April 618 the imperial guards at Jiangdu, homesick and mutinous, strangled Emperor Yang with his own scarf. Li Yuan took Chang'an and proclaimed the Tang dynasty weeks later.

Legacy

The Sui is the classic Chinese example of the short unifying dynasty — the Qin to the Tang's Han. It gave reunified China its canal system, its examination-based bureaucracy in embryo (the first jinshi degrees date to Emperor Yang's reign), standardised institutions and coinage, and a warning, repeated by every later historian, about the price of megaprojects. The Grand Canal alone, still in use, made it possible to govern north and south as one empire for most of the fourteen centuries since.
