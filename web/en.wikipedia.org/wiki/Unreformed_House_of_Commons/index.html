Unreformed House of Commons

Electioneering in the unreformed system, as satirized by Hogarth.

The unreformed House of Commons is the name given to the British House of Commons before the Reform Act 1832. Between the late Middle Ages and 1832, the distribution of seats and the franchise remained largely unchanged, producing a system notorious for its anomalies: depopulated "rotten boroughs" returned members while great new industrial cities such as Manchester, Birmingham, and Leeds had no direct representation at all.

Structure

The Commons consisted of county members, elected by forty-shilling freeholders, and borough members chosen under a patchwork of local franchises — freemen, burgage tenants, corporation members, or householders — that varied from town to town. Many borough seats were effectively the property of patrons: peers or landowners who nominated the member, sometimes selling the seat for thousands of pounds. In Scotland, representation was even narrower, dominated by a small electorate of county freeholders and self-electing burgh councils.

Criticism and reform

Reformers from the 1760s onward attacked the system as corrupt and unrepresentative; the radical movements of the 1810s and 1820s made reform a mass demand. After two failed bills and a constitutional crisis, Earl Grey's ministry passed the Great Reform Act in 1832, disfranchising 56 rotten boroughs, redistributing seats to the industrial towns, and standardizing borough franchises — the first of a series of reforms extending through 1832, 1867, and 1884–85.

Historiography

Historians debate how "unreformed" the old system really was: revisionists note that elections could be fiercely contested and that patronage interlocked with genuine local opinion, while the traditional Whig view sees 1832 as the decisive break toward modern representative politics.
