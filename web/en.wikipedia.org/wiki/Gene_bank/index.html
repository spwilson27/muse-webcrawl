A gene bank (also spelled genebank) is a repository that preserves genetic material — seeds, plant tissues, pollen, animal sperm and ova, or DNA — for future use in breeding, research and the conservation of biodiversity. Gene banks are a principal form of ex situ conservation, complementing protection of species in their natural habitats.

Plant gene banks

Most plant gene banks store seeds, which for "orthodox" species remain viable for decades when dried and frozen at about −18 °C. The world's collections hold millions of accessions of crops and their wild relatives: the international CGIAR centres (such as IRRI for rice and CIMMYT for maize and wheat) maintain large working collections, while backup facilities include Norway's Svalbard Global Seed Vault, opened in 2008 and holding over a million seed samples, and the Millennium Seed Bank at Kew's Wakehurst site in England.

Species with recalcitrant seeds or vegetatively propagated crops (potato, banana, cassava) are conserved instead as living field collections, in vitro tissue cultures, or cryopreserved shoot tips in liquid nitrogen.

Animal gene banks

Animal gene banks store frozen semen, embryos and somatic cells of livestock breeds and endangered wildlife — sometimes called "frozen zoos" — enabling breeds to be reconstituted or genetic diversity restored. Animal material has been used, for example, in efforts to support the gene pool of the black-footed ferret.

Role and challenges

Gene banks underpin food security: breeders draw on their collections for genes conferring disease resistance, drought tolerance and other traits, and collections have restored varieties lost to war or disaster — including seeds returned to Lebanon and Syria via Svalbard during the Syrian civil war. Challenges include funding, viability loss over time, documentation, and legal questions of access and benefit-sharing governed by the International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources.
