Inscape (visual art)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Inscape
Term also used by Gerard Manley Hopkins and by Roberto Matta.

In visual art, inscape is a term associated with the Chilean surrealist painter Roberto Matta, who used it to describe visual representations of the artist's psyche — imagery of the mind's interior rendered as if it were a landscape. Matta developed the idea in the late 1930s while associated with the Surrealist movement in Paris and later in New York, speaking of "psychological morphologies" that mapped inner states onto spatial, architectural forms.

Origins of the term

The word was coined in the nineteenth century by the poet Gerard Manley Hopkins, for whom "inscape" meant the distinctive inner pattern or essence of a thing — the individualized design that makes each natural object itself. The related term "instress" described the energy by which that essence is apprehended. Hopkins applied the concept chiefly to poetry and nature rather than to painting.

Matta's inscapes

Matta adopted and transformed the term for a series of works beginning around 1938, often titled Inscape or Psychological Morphology. These paintings present fluid, translucent, biomorphic spaces — part landscape, part architecture, part organism — intended as pictures of mental processes rather than of the external world. The inscapes influenced the young Abstract Expressionists in wartime New York, including Arshile Gorky, Robert Motherwell, and Jackson Pollock, particularly in their use of automatism and all-over spatial fields.

Usage

Critics also apply "inscape" loosely to any art that renders an inward, dreamed, or psychological terrain, in contrast to the outward depiction implied by "landscape".
