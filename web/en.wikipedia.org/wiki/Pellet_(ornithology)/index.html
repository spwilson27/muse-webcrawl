Pellet (ornithology)

A pellet, in ornithology, is a mass of undigested parts of a bird's food that some bird species regurgitate. Pellets consist of indigestible material such as fur, feathers, bone, insect exoskeletons, and plant matter that cannot pass through the digestive tract and is instead compacted in the gizzard (in birds of prey, in association with the proventriculus) and cast up through the mouth, usually hours after feeding.

Formation and occurrence

Pellet-casting is best known in owls, whose pellets famously contain whole, articulated rodent skeletons because owls swallow small prey whole and their relatively weak stomach acids leave bones largely intact. Hawks, eagles, falcons, herons, gulls, cormorants, kingfishers, corvids, shrikes, and many other birds also produce pellets, though in most diurnal raptors stronger digestion dissolves much of the bone. Pellets are typically expelled at roosts or nests, accumulating beneath favoured perches.

Scientific use

Because they concentrate the hard remains of prey, pellets are a non-invasive tool for studying diet and small-mammal communities. Dissecting owl pellets is a standard school and citizen-science exercise, and biologists use pellet analysis to monitor prey populations, detect rare species, and assess contaminants. Pellet contents must be interpreted with care: soft-bodied prey are under-represented, and different predators digest material to different degrees.

Distinctions

Pellets differ from droppings, which are excreted waste, and from the chewed boluses produced by some other animals; comparable regurgitated masses in extinct animals are studied in the fossil record as bromalites.
