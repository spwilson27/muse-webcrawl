State school

A state school or public school is a primary or secondary school that educates all students without charge, funded in whole or in part by taxation. State schools are generally administered by local, regional, or national governments and are required to follow state-mandated curricula and standards. They contrast with private (independent) schools, which charge tuition fees.

Terminology

Usage varies by country. In the United States, Canada, and Australia, "public school" is the standard term for a government-funded school. In the United Kingdom, "state school" is used because "public school" historically denotes a group of prestigious fee-charging independent schools such as Eton and Harrow. In many countries state education is compulsory and provided by law as a right.

Funding and governance

State schools are financed through general taxation, often via local property taxes in the United States or central government grants in the United Kingdom. Governance may rest with locally elected school boards, municipal authorities, or national ministries. Most systems combine state funding with national testing and inspection regimes to monitor standards. Variations within the sector include selective schools, faith schools funded by the state, charter schools (US), and academies and free schools (England), which receive state funding but enjoy greater operational autonomy.

History

Mass state schooling spread during the 19th century as industrializing nations introduced compulsory education — Prussia and Massachusetts were early pioneers, and England and Wales followed with the Elementary Education Act of 1870. By the mid-20th century, free state secondary education had become the norm across the developed world, and state schools today educate the large majority of children in most countries.
