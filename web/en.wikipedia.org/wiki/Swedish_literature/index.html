Swedish literature

Swedish literature (Swedish: Svensk litteratur) is the literature written in the Swedish language or by writers from Sweden. It begins with the runic inscriptions and medieval ballads and religious texts of the Middle Ages, develops through the Reformation and the age of the Swedish Empire, and reaches international prominence in the 19th and 20th centuries with writers such as August Strindberg, Selma Lagerlöf, Astrid Lindgren and Tomas Tranströmer. Sweden is the home of the Nobel Prize in Literature, awarded by the Swedish Academy.

Works of Swedish literature, from medieval ballads to modern classics.

Middle Ages and Reformation

The earliest Swedish texts are runestones and provincial laws. Medieval literature includes the rhymed Erik's Chronicle (Erikskrönikan, c. 1320s), courtly romances known as the Eufemia songs, religious writings associated with Saint Bridget of Sweden (Birgitta), and the Swedish ballad tradition. The Reformation brought the first complete Swedish Bible translation (the Gustav Vasa Bible, 1541), which strongly influenced the written language.

17th and 18th centuries

Georg Stiernhielm's allegorical poem Hercules (1658) is considered the starting point of Swedish poetry in the classical sense. The 18th century saw the journalism and poetry of Olof von Dalin, the songs of Carl Michael Bellman — whose Fredman's Epistles remain beloved — and the classicism of Johan Henric Kellgren. The Swedish Academy was founded by Gustav III in 1786.

19th century

Romanticism arrived with Esaias Tegnér, whose poem Frithiofs saga (1825) became internationally famous. The breakthrough of modern Swedish prose came with August Strindberg, whose novel The Red Room (1879) inaugurated the "Modern Breakthrough" and whose plays, including Miss Julie and The Father, reshaped world drama. Selma Lagerlöf won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1909, the first woman to do so; her works include The Wonderful Adventures of Nils and Gösta Berling's Saga.

20th century to present

Twentieth-century Swedish literature includes the proletarian writers (such as Ivar Lo-Johansson and Harry Martinson, a 1974 Nobel laureate, shared with Eyvind Johnson), the modernist poetry of Karin Boye and Gunnar Ekelöf, and the children's literature of Astrid Lindgren, creator of Pippi Longstocking. Per Lagerkvist won the Nobel Prize in 1951, and poet Tomas Tranströmer in 2011. Swedish crime fiction — from Maj Sjöwall and Per Wahlöö's Martin Beck series to Stieg Larsson's Millennium trilogy and the "Nordic noir" phenomenon — has achieved global commercial success.
