Beta Israel

Beta Israel (Hebrew: בֵּיתֶא יִשְׂרָאֵל, "House of Israel"; Ge'ez: ቤተ እስራኤል) is the community of Ethiopian Jews, historically concentrated in the northwestern highlands of Ethiopia, in the Gondar and Tigray regions. Most of the community emigrated to Israel in the late 20th century under the Law of Return, notably in Operation Moses (1984) and Operation Solomon (1991).

Origins

The origins of Beta Israel are debated. Community traditions trace descent to the tribe of Dan or to the entourage of Menelik I, son of Solomon and the Queen of Sheba. Scholars variously propose descent from ancient Jewish migrants via Egypt or Arabia, or origins in Ethiopian converts influenced by Old Testament-based Christianity in the 14th–16th centuries. Genetic studies show predominantly local Ethiopian ancestry with some Levantine admixture. In 1973–75 Israel's Sephardi Chief Rabbi Ovadia Yosef, citing the 16th-century ruling of the Radbaz, recognised the Beta Israel as Jews, paving the way for their immigration.

Religion

Traditional Beta Israel practice, called Haymanot, was based on the Bible (Orit) in Ge'ez, without the Talmud or rabbinic literature. It featured priests (qesim), monks, observance of the Sabbath and dietary laws, and distinctive festivals such as Sigd, which commemorates the giving of the Torah and longing for Jerusalem and is now an Israeli national holiday.

Emigration to Israel

Beginning in the late 1970s, thousands walked to refugee camps in Sudan. In Operation Moses (1984–85) about 8,000 were airlifted; Operation Solomon (May 1991) airlifted 14,325 people in 36 hours as the Ethiopian regime collapsed. The related Falash Mura — descendants of converts to Christianity — have continued to immigrate amid controversy. In Israel, the community has faced challenges of absorption, discrimination and religious recognition, while producing a new generation prominent in the military, politics and culture.
