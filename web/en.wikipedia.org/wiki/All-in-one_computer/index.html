An all-in-one computer (also called an AiO or all-in-one PC) is a type of desktop computer that integrates the system's internal components into the same case as the display, eliminating the separate tower or system unit. All-in-ones occupy less desk space and have fewer cables than conventional desktops, though they generally offer less scope for upgrading or repair.

History

Integrated computers date to the 1970s: the HP 9830 of 1972 combined computer and display in one housing, and machines such as the Commodore PET (1977) and the original Apple Macintosh (1984) popularised the single-unit design. Early portables and the IBM PC generation favoured separate units, but Apple revived the category with the colourful iMac G3 in 1998, whose commercial success led other manufacturers to follow.

Design and trade-offs

Modern all-in-ones typically place laptop-style components behind an LCD or OLED panel, often with a touchscreen. Advantages include a compact footprint, quick setup, and tidy cabling; disadvantages include constrained cooling, limited graphics power, difficult servicing — many models glue components in place — and the fact that a failed display or motherboard can disable the whole machine. All-in-ones are common in homes, offices, schools, hospitals, and point-of-sale installations.

Market

Leading manufacturers include Apple (whose iMac line is the best-known example), HP, Dell, Lenovo, and Microsoft (Surface Studio). Sales form a persistent minority of the desktop market, which itself has shrunk relative to laptops.
