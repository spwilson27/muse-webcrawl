Hellhound

A hellhound is a mythological hound embodying the guardian of the underworld, the devil, or death. In folklore, hellhounds are typically described as enormous black dogs with glowing red or yellow eyes, supernatural speed and strength, and sometimes the ability to speak. Seeing one or hearing its howl is often said to be an omen of death.

Mythology and folklore

The most famous hellhound of classical mythology is Cerberus, the multi-headed dog who guarded the gates of Hades and was captured alive by Heracles as the last of his Twelve Labours. In Norse mythology, Garmr guards Hel, while the monstrous wolf Fenrir is bound by the gods until Ragnarök.

British and European traditions

British folklore is rich in spectral black dogs: Black Shuck of East Anglia, said to have terrified the congregation of Blythburgh church in 1577; the Barghest of Yorkshire; the Moddey Dhoo of Peel Castle on the Isle of Man; and the hounds of Annwn in Welsh tradition. In Germanic and Scandinavian folklore, churchyard guardians and "corpse dogs" performed similar roles. Arthur Conan Doyle drew on Dartmoor legends of a spectral hound for The Hound of the Baskervilles (1902).

In popular culture

Hellhounds appear widely in modern fantasy, horror and gaming — as summoned creatures, demonic familiars or underworld guardians — in works ranging from Goethe's Faust (the poodle that becomes Mephistopheles) to contemporary television and role-playing games.
