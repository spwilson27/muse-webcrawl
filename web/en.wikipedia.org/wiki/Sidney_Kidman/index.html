Sidney Kidman

Sir Sidney Kidman (9 May 1857 – 2 September 1935) was an Australian pastoralist and entrepreneur who built the largest private landholding in the world of his time, earning the title "the Cattle King." At its peak his network of stations covered some 3–3.75 percent of the Australian continent — often cited as around 220,000 to 280,000 square kilometres — linked by a strategy of owning properties along stock routes and river systems so cattle could be moved to wherever rain had fallen.

Early life

Kidman was born in Adelaide, the fifth son of English immigrant parents; his father died when he was an infant, leaving the family poor. Aged 13 he ran away from home with five shillings and a one-eyed horse named Cyclops, working as a roustabout, drover, and stockman in the New South Wales outback around Broken Hill and Cobar. He learned the stock routes, water sources, and trade of the inland, saving to buy his first property, and also ran a butcher's shop and coaching business at Cobar.

The cattle empire

With his brothers, Kidman began acquiring stations in the Channel Country of Queensland and along the great stock routes, buying cheaply during droughts and expanding steadily. His insight was geographic: by holding a chain of properties from the Gulf Country and Channel Country down through western New South Wales and South Australia to markets near Adelaide, he could move cattle from flooded or well-watered country to wherever feed was available, "drought-proofing" his enterprise. Famous holdings included Anna Creek Station (later the world's largest cattle station), Innamincka, and properties on the Birdsville and Strzelecki tracks. He also supplied horses to the British Army in India and remounts during the First World War.

Legacy

Kidman was knighted in 1921. He was a noted philanthropist in Adelaide, supporting hospitals, returned soldiers, and the Salvation Army; the city named the suburb of Kidman Park after him. He died in 1935 leaving an estate of over £300,000. His company, S. Kidman & Co, remained family-owned for generations; in 2016, amid political controversy over foreign ownership, it was sold to a consortium of Hancock Prospecting (67%) and China's Shanghai CRED (33%), after a bid by a Chinese-led group had been blocked on national-interest grounds.
