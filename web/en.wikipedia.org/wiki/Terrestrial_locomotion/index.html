Terrestrial locomotion

Terrestrial locomotion is the movement of animals on land. It has evolved in many groups independently as animals adapted from aquatic life, and it takes three broad forms: legged locomotion (walking, running, climbing), limbless locomotion (crawling and slithering, as in snakes and worms), and rolling. It contrasts with aquatic and aerial locomotion and must overcome gravity rather than drag as its main cost.

Legged locomotion

Animals move on two legs (bipedalism, as in humans, birds, and hopping kangaroos), four legs (quadrupedalism — the majority of terrestrial vertebrates), or six or more legs (insects, arachnids, myriapods). Gaits are defined by the sequence of footfalls and the fraction of time each foot is on the ground: walk, trot, pace, gallop, bound. Digitigrade animals walk on their toes, plantigrade animals on the whole foot, and unguligrade animals on hooves. Energy-saving mechanisms include the inverted-pendulum dynamics of walking and elastic recoil of tendons in running.

Limbless locomotion and rolling

Snakes move by lateral undulation, rectilinear crawling, concertina motion, or sidewinding. Earthworms advance by peristalsis. A few animals roll: pangolins and some salamanders curl into balls, the wheel spider of the Namib cartwheels down dunes, and the flic-flac spider actively somersaults.

Evolution

Tetrapods evolved limbs with digits from lobe-finned fishes in the Devonian period, enabling the colonisation of land; arthropods had taken to land earlier. Terrestrial locomotion has since diversified from sprawling gaits to the upright posture of dinosaurs, birds, and mammals.
