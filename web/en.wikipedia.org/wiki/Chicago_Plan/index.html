Overview

The Chicago Plan was a set of banking reforms proposed by economists, including a group at the University of Chicago, during the Great Depression in 1933. Its central feature was the separation of the monetary and credit functions of the banking system: deposit-taking banks would be required to hold 100 percent reserves against deposits ('full-reserve banking'), while lending would be carried out by separate investment institutions funded by equity and time deposits.

Proposals and reception

The plan, circulated in a 1933 memorandum and endorsed by economists such as Frank Knight and (in a variant) Irving Fisher in his book 100% Money (1935), aimed to end bank runs, smooth credit cycles, reduce public and private debt, and return control of the money supply to the state. Elements influenced debate around the Banking Act of 1935, but full-reserve banking was not adopted.

Legacy

Interest in the Chicago Plan revived after the 2008 financial crisis, notably in a 2012 International Monetary Fund working paper, 'The Chicago Plan Revisited', which modelled the plan's claims. Similar ideas appear in later proposals for sovereign money, narrow banking and central bank digital currencies.
