Sauropterygia ("lizard flippers") is an extinct order of marine reptiles that flourished in the oceans of the Mesozoic Era, from the Early Triassic until their extinction at the end of the Cretaceous, about 66 million years ago. The group includes the heavily armoured placodonts, the long-necked nothosaurs, and the plesiosaurs — among them the long-necked elasmosaurs and the giant short-necked pliosaurs — as well as the plesiosaurs' terrestrial-looking early relatives.

Sauropterygians propelled themselves with four large flippers, "flying" through the water like penguins.

Anatomy

Sauropterygians were unified by a distinctive shoulder girdle and adaptations for aquatic life: limbs modified into paddles, a shortened tail in later forms, and dense (pachyostotic) bones in shallow-water groups that served as ballast. They breathed air and had to surface regularly, and evidence from fossils indicates that at least the plesiosaurs gave birth to live young at sea rather than laying eggs on land. Sizes ranged from small Triassic forms under a metre long to pliosaurs exceeding 10 metres.

Evolution

The group appeared in the recovery interval after the end-Permian mass extinction, diversifying first in the shallow seas of the Tethys and around the Pacific margin of Pangaea. Placodonts and nothosaurs dominated Triassic coastlines, while plesiosaurs radiated in the Jurassic and Cretaceous into the apex predators and long-necked fish-eaters glimpsed in the global fossil record. Like the non-avian dinosaurs, they perished in the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event.
