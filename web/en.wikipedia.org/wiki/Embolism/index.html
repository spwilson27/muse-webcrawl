Embolism

An embolism is the lodging of an embolus — a detached intravascular mass (solid, liquid, or gaseous) carried by the bloodstream — within a blood vessel, causing partial or complete blockage. The embolus may be a blood clot (thrombus), a globule of fat, a bubble of air or other gas, amniotic fluid, or foreign material. An embolism can cause ischemia and infarction of the downstream tissue; the term was coined by Rudolf Virchow in the 19th century.

An embolus travels with the blood flow until it lodges in a vessel too narrow to pass.

Types

  • Thromboembolism — the most common form: a detached thrombus. Deep vein thrombi in the legs can travel to the lungs causing pulmonary embolism (PE); clots from the heart (e.g. in atrial fibrillation) can cause ischemic stroke or systemic embolism.
  • Fat embolism — fat globules released after long-bone fractures or orthopedic surgery; fat embolism syndrome presents with respiratory distress, confusion, and a petechial rash.
  • Air embolism — gas bubbles entering the circulation from trauma, surgery, central lines, or decompression sickness ("the bends"), where nitrogen bubbles form in tissues and blood.
  • Amniotic fluid embolism — a rare, catastrophic obstetric emergency in which amniotic fluid and fetal material enter the maternal circulation, triggering cardiovascular collapse and disseminated intravascular coagulation.
  • Cholesterol embolism — cholesterol crystals showering downstream after disruption of atherosclerotic plaques, often after angiography or anticoagulation.
  • Septic embolism — infected material, e.g. from endocarditis, seeding distant sites with abscesses.

Pathophysiology

Emboli follow the direction of blood flow: venous emboli lodge in the pulmonary arterial tree, while arterial emboli lodge at bifurcations in the brain, limbs, gut, kidneys, or spleen. A paradoxical embolism occurs when a venous clot crosses a right-to-left shunt such as a patent foramen ovale into the arterial circulation. Consequences depend on collateral circulation and the tissue's vulnerability to ischemia.

Prevention and treatment

Prevention targets thrombosis: anticoagulants, compression stockings, and early mobilization after surgery. Established embolism is treated with anticoagulation, thrombolytic drugs, catheter-directed therapy, or surgical embolectomy depending on site and severity; air embolism is treated with positioning and high-flow oxygen (hyperbaric therapy in decompression illness).
