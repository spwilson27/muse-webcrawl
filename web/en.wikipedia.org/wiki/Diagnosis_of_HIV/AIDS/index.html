AIDS

Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) is the most advanced stage of infection with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), a retrovirus that attacks the immune system, particularly CD4 T cells. Without treatment, HIV progressively weakens immunity, leaving the body vulnerable to opportunistic infections and certain cancers that define an AIDS diagnosis — generally a CD4 count below 200 cells/mm³ or the presence of an AIDS-defining illness.

Diagnosis of HIV infection

HIV is diagnosed with blood or oral-fluid tests. The standard laboratory algorithm begins with a fourth-generation antigen/antibody immunoassay, which detects both HIV antibodies and the p24 antigen; reactive results are followed by an antibody differentiation immunoassay, and, if needed, a nucleic acid test (NAT) that detects viral RNA. Nucleic acid and antigen tests shorten the "window period" before infection can be detected. Rapid and self-tests allow screening outside laboratories. Viral load (HIV RNA) and CD4 counts are then used to stage infection and monitor treatment.

Course and treatment

Untreated HIV typically progresses over years: acute infection with a flu-like illness, a long clinical latency phase, and finally AIDS. Antiretroviral therapy (ART) suppresses viral replication, restores immune function, prevents progression, and — when viral load is durably undetectable — eliminates sexual transmission ("U=U"). Early diagnosis and treatment are therefore central to both individual prognosis and public health; pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) prevent acquisition.
