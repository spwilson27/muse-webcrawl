Electrical resistivity and conductivity

Resistivity relates resistance to a material's geometry
Electrical resistivity
Symbolρ (rho)
SI unitOhm-metre (Ω·m)
RelationR = ρL/A
InverseElectrical conductivity σ, in siemens per metre

Electrical resistivity (also called volume resistivity or specific electrical resistance) is a fundamental property of a material that measures how strongly it resists electric current. Its SI unit is the ohm-metre (Ω·m). A low resistivity indicates a material that readily allows current flow. For a uniform conductor of length L and cross-sectional area A, resistance is R = ρL/A, which rearranges to ρ = RA/L. The reciprocal property, electrical conductivity (σ = 1/ρ), is measured in siemens per metre.

Values and mechanisms

Resistivity spans an enormous range — more than 20 orders of magnitude. Excellent conductors include silver (1.59 × 10−8 Ω·m at 20 °C), copper (1.68 × 10−8) and gold, which is why copper and aluminium dominate wiring. Semiconductors such as silicon vary widely with doping; insulators such as glass, rubber and fused quartz reach 1010–1016 Ω·m. In metals, conduction electrons scatter off lattice vibrations, impurities and defects: resistivity rises roughly linearly with temperature (Matthiessen's rule), while alloys such as nichrome and constantan are engineered for high, stable resistivity for heaters and precision resistors.

Temperature and superconductivity

In semiconductors and insulators, resistivity falls as temperature rises because more charge carriers are thermally excited. In electrolytes, ions carry the current. Below a critical temperature, superconductors exhibit exactly zero resistivity. Resistivity is measured with four-point probe techniques to eliminate contact resistance, and is used in geophysics (electrical resistivity tomography) to image subsurface structures.
