Phenylpropanoid

Phenylpropanoids
Cinnamic acid, the archetypal phenylpropanoid
Parent structurePhenyl group with a three-carbon (propane) side chain — C6–C3
PrecursorPhenylalanine (via cinnamic acid)
Found inHigher plants
Major productsLignin, flavonoids, coumarins, stilbenes

Phenylpropanoids are a large and diverse family of natural products synthesised by plants from the amino acids phenylalanine and tyrosine. Their name derives from the six-carbon aromatic phenyl group joined to a three-carbon propene tail (a C6–C3 unit) found in the simplest members, such as cinnamic acid. Thousands of phenylpropanoid compounds are known, and they account for a substantial fraction of plant secondary metabolism.

Biosynthesis

The pathway begins with the enzyme phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), which deaminates phenylalanine to trans-cinnamic acid. In grasses, tyrosine can also enter directly. Cinnamic acid is hydroxylated to p-coumaric acid and then activated to p-coumaroyl-CoA, the central branch-point intermediate from which most phenylpropanoid classes arise.

Classes and functions

  • Lignin and lignans — polymers of hydroxycinnamyl alcohols (monolignols) that stiffen cell walls and waterproof xylem vessels.
  • Flavonoids — including anthocyanin pigments, flavones and isoflavonoids involved in colour, UV protection and signalling.
  • Coumarins — fragrant lactones such as those in sweet clover and cinnamon relatives.
  • Stilbenes — such as resveratrol, produced as defensive phytoalexins.
  • Hydroxycinnamic acid esters — including chlorogenic acid and the capsaicinoids responsible for chilli heat.

Phenylpropanoids protect plants against ultraviolet light, herbivores and pathogens, attract pollinators through floral pigments and scents, and provide the structural polymer lignin that makes wood possible. Many are antioxidants of dietary interest to humans, and flavour compounds such as vanillin, eugenol and cinnamaldehyde belong to this family.
