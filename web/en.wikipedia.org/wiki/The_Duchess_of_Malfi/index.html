The Duchess of Malfi (originally published as The Tragedy of the Dutchesse of Malfy) is a Jacobean revenge tragedy written by the English dramatist John Webster in 1612–1613. It was first performed privately at the Blackfriars Theatre, then later to a larger audience at the Globe Theatre, in 1613–1614. Published in 1623, the play is loosely based on events that occurred around 1508–1513 in the Italian city of Amalfi, concerning Giovanna d'Aragona, Duchess of Amalfi, whose brothers opposed her secret marriage to her steward.

The play is widely considered one of the great tragedies of English Renaissance theatre, noted for its poetic power, its dark atmosphere of corruption and decay, and the character of the Duchess herself, one of the few genuinely heroic figures in Jacobean drama.

Characters

  • The Duchess of Malfi — a young widow who remarries in secret against her brothers' wishes.
  • Ferdinand — Duke of Calabria, the Duchess's twin brother, whose obsession with his sister turns to madness.
  • The Cardinal — the Duchess's elder brother, a corrupt churchman.
  • Antonio Bologna — the Duchess's steward, whom she secretly marries.
  • Bosola — a malcontent and intelligencer employed by the brothers to spy on the Duchess; he becomes her executioner and, too late, her avenger.
  • Cariola — the Duchess's waiting-woman and confidante.
  • Julia — the Cardinal's mistress.
  • Delio — Antonio's friend, a framing voice of the play.

Synopsis

The widowed Duchess, urged by her brothers Ferdinand and the Cardinal not to remarry, nevertheless secretly marries her steward Antonio and bears him three children. The brothers employ the malcontent Bosola to spy on her; when he discovers her pregnancy and, eventually, her marriage, the brothers imprison her. Ferdinand visits her in darkness and presents her with a dead man's hand, which she believes to be Antonio's, and later torments her with a show of madmen. Bosola, disguised, strangles the Duchess, Cariola, and two of her children; the Duchess briefly revives and dies hearing that Antonio still lives.

Bosola, denied his expected reward and moved by remorse, resolves to avenge the Duchess. In the bloody final act, nearly all the principal characters die: the Cardinal is killed by Bosola, Ferdinand kills Bosola and is himself killed, and Antonio is accidentally slain by Bosola in the dark. Delio closes the play with the surviving youngest son of the Duchess and Antonio.

Themes and criticism

The play explores corruption at court, the position of women, ambition, and the contrast between the Duchess's integrity and her brothers' degeneracy. Famous lines include the Duchess's declaration "I am Duchess of Malfi still" and Bosola's reflection that we are "merely the stars' tennis-balls, struck and banded which way please them." Critics have praised Webster's poetry and the play's sustained atmosphere of horror, while noting the notorious violence of the final acts, characteristic of the revenge tragedy tradition descending from Seneca and Thomas Kyd's The Spanish Tragedy.

Performance history

The play was performed by the King's Men in Webster's lifetime, with Richard Burbage likely playing Bosola. After a period of neglect, it returned to the stage in the 19th and 20th centuries and is now regularly revived; notable modern productions include those by the Royal Shakespeare Company and a 2014 Sam Wanamaker Playhouse production. A 1951 BBC radio adaptation and later television versions brought it to wider audiences.
