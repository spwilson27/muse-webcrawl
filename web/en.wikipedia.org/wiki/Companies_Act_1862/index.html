The Companies Act 1862 (25 & 26 Vict. c. 89) was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that consolidated and reformed the law governing the incorporation of joint stock companies. It is regarded as a foundational statute of modern British company law.

Background

Following the Joint Stock Companies Act 1844, which introduced incorporation by registration, and the Limited Liability Act 1855 and Joint Stock Companies Act 1856, which established general limited liability, the 1862 Act brought together the existing legislation into a single comprehensive code.

Provisions

The Act allowed any seven or more persons to form an incorporated company, with or without limited liability, by subscribing their names to a memorandum of association. It provided for companies limited by shares, companies limited by guarantee, and unlimited companies. The Act also contained detailed provisions on the registration of companies, share capital, meetings, and winding up, and included model articles of association in its schedule.

Legacy

The framework established by the 1862 Act endured, with amendments, through successive consolidations including the Companies Act 1908, the Companies Act 1948, the Companies Act 1985, and ultimately the Companies Act 2006. The principle of separate legal personality it embodied was famously affirmed by the House of Lords in Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897].
