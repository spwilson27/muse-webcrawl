Beech is a genus (Fagus) of deciduous trees in the family Fagaceae, native to temperate Europe, Asia, and North America. Beeches are recognizable by their smooth, silvery-grey bark, oval leaves with wavy margins, and small triangular nuts — known as beech mast — enclosed in spiky husks. They are dominant canopy trees in many temperate forests and are valued for timber, as ornamentals, and as a food source for wildlife.

Description

Beeches are large trees, commonly reaching 25–35 metres (80–115 ft) in height, with a dense, spreading crown. The bark remains smooth even in old trees, a trait that has historically made beech trunks targets for carved graffiti. Leaves are alternate, simple, and entire or slightly toothed. Beech is monoecious: male and female flowers appear on the same tree in spring. The fruit is a small, sharply three-angled nut, produced in quantity in irregular "mast years".

Species and distribution

The best-known species are the European beech (Fagus sylvatica), widespread across Europe and extensively planted, including in ornamental copper and weeping forms; the American beech (Fagus grandifolia) of eastern North America; and several East Asian species such as Fagus crenata (Japanese beech). Southern beeches, once placed in Fagus, are now classified in the separate genus Nothofagus.

Uses

Beech wood is hard, strong, and pale, used for furniture, flooring, tool handles, and bentwood chairs; it is also an excellent firewood and is used to smoke foods. The nuts are edible in small quantities and were traditionally pressed for oil and fed to livestock. European beech is one of the most common hedging and park trees in temperate regions.

Ecology and threats

Beech mast feeds birds, rodents, deer, and bears. Beech bark disease, a complex involving scale insects and Neonectria fungi, has killed many American beeches since its introduction to North America around 1890. Climate models suggest European beech is vulnerable to increased summer drought at the southern edge of its range.
