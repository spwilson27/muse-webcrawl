Subjective validation

Subjective validation, sometimes called the personal validation effect, is a cognitive bias by which a person will consider a statement or another piece of information to be correct if it has any personal meaning or significance to them. In other words, a person whose opinion is affected by subjective validation will perceive two unrelated events as related simply because their personal belief or hypothesis demands a relationship.

Relation to the Barnum effect

Subjective validation is closely related to the Barnum effect (also called the Forer effect), in which individuals give high accuracy ratings to descriptions of their personality that supposedly are tailored specifically for them but are in fact vague and general enough to apply to a wide range of people. The term "subjective validation" was developed and used by psychologists studying the mechanisms behind belief in fortune telling, astrology, and other forms of divination.

Mechanism

The bias is thought to arise through several reinforcing processes: people search their memories for instances that confirm a vague statement and overlook counterexamples; they interpret ambiguous statements charitably in light of their own experiences; and they are motivated to find meaning in statements about themselves delivered by an apparent authority. Cold readers exploit this tendency by offering high-probability, two-sided, or easily retrofitted statements, allowing the subject to do the work of making the reading seem accurate.

Experimental evidence

In classic experiments, subjects who received identical, generic personality sketches rated them as highly accurate descriptions of themselves. Studies comparing "genuine" horoscope readings with randomly assigned or reversed readings have likewise found that believers rate both as similarly accurate, demonstrating that perceived validity comes from the reader rather than the reading.
