Time-of-flight camera

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A time-of-flight camera (ToF camera) is a range imaging system that measures the distance to objects by timing how long it takes light to travel from the camera to the scene and back. The whole scene is captured at once — unlike a scanning lidar, there are no moving parts — producing a depth map in which every pixel carries a distance value. ToF cameras use near-infrared illumination, so they work in darkness, and typically measure ranges of a few centimetres to tens of metres with millimetre-to-centimetre precision.

Operating principles

In indirect ToF, the light source is modulated, usually at tens of megahertz, and the sensor measures the phase shift between the emitted and reflected signals at each pixel; distance follows from the phase and the speed of light. This is the approach used in most consumer devices. In direct ToF, short pulses of laser light are emitted and a very fast detector, often a single-photon avalanche diode (SPAD), times each pulse's round trip. Direct ToF can reach longer ranges and is used in the lidar of some smartphones and in automotive and industrial sensors.

Applications

ToF cameras became widely known through the second-generation Microsoft Kinect (2013), which used one for full-body motion tracking. They are now used for face unlock and depth effects in smartphones, gesture recognition, robot navigation, warehouse automation, people counting and augmented reality. Limitations include interference from bright sunlight, errors on shiny or transparent surfaces, and "multi-path" reflections in corners.
