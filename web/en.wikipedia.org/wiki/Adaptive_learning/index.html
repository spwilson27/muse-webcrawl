Adaptive learning

Adaptive learning, also known as adaptive teaching, is an educational method which uses computer algorithms and artificial intelligence to orchestrate the interaction with the learner and deliver customised resources and learning activities to address each learner's needs. The system adapts the presentation, sequence and difficulty of material in response to the individual student's performance, rather than providing a single one-size-fits-all experience.

FieldEducational technology
RelatedIntelligent tutoring systems, computer-adaptive testing, learning analytics

How it works

Adaptive systems typically build a model of the learner's knowledge — sometimes using techniques such as Bayesian knowledge tracing or item response theory — and select the next activity judged most likely to advance mastery. Related applications include computer-adaptive testing (in which test questions adapt to the test-taker's ability, as in the GRE) and spaced repetition software, which schedules review items according to predicted forgetting.

History

Antecedents include Burrhus Frederic Skinner's "teaching machines" of the 1950s and programmed instruction, and the intelligent tutoring systems developed from the 1970s onwards, such as cognitive tutors from Carnegie Mellon. From the 2010s, adaptive platforms such as ALEKS, DreamBox, Knewton and Duolingo spread the approach into schools, universities and consumer learning apps, and later systems incorporate large language models as conversational tutors.

Evidence and criticism

Research finds adaptive tutoring systems can produce meaningful gains in specific subjects, sometimes approaching those of human tutoring, though effect sizes vary by implementation. Criticisms concern the reduction of learning to measurable items, data privacy for minors, algorithmic bias, and the risk of narrowing curricula to what platforms can assess.
