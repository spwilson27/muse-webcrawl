The Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) was a joint mission of NASA and the German Aerospace Center (DLR). Twin satellites took detailed measurements of Earth's gravity field anomalies from their launch in March 2002 until the end of the science mission in October 2017. By tracking changes in the distance between the two satellites and mapping variations in Earth's gravitational pull, GRACE revealed how mass is distributed around the planet and how it varies over time.

The GRACE Follow-On (GRACE-FO) mission, a partnership between NASA and the German Research Centre for Geosciences (GFZ), was launched on 22 May 2018 aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket to continue the measurements.

Principle

The two satellites fly in the same polar orbit about 220 km apart. As they pass over a region of stronger gravity, the leading satellite is pulled slightly ahead, changing the separation; microwave ranging measures these changes to within a few micrometres, and GPS and accelerometers separate gravitational effects from atmospheric drag and solar radiation pressure. GRACE-FO additionally carries a Laser Ranging Interferometer as a technology demonstrator, measuring the separation far more precisely.

Findings

Monthly gravity maps produced by GRACE have been used to track the melting of the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets, groundwater depletion in aquifers such as California's Central Valley and northern India, sea-level rise budgets, ocean currents, and changes in the solid Earth after large earthquakes such as the 2004 Sumatra and 2011 Tōhoku events.

End of GRACE

After more than three times its planned five-year lifetime, GRACE's batteries degraded; the mission was declared complete in October 2017, and the two satellites re-entered the atmosphere in December 2017 and March 2018.
