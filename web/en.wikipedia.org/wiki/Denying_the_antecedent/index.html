Denying the antecedent

Denying the antecedent, sometimes also called inverse error or fallacy of the inverse, is a formal fallacy of inferring the inverse from an original statement. It is committed by reasoning in the form:

If P, then Q.
Not P.
Therefore, not Q.

Symbolically: ((P → Q) ∧ ¬P) → ¬Q, which is invalid. The argument fails because P may not be the only sufficient condition for Q: Q can be true even when P is false. Denying the antecedent is the mirror image of the fallacy of affirming the consequent, and contrasts with the valid inference modus tollens, which denies the consequent.

Explanation

A conditional statement "if P then Q" asserts only that P is sufficient for Q, not that it is necessary. From the falsity of the antecedent nothing follows about the consequent. The fallacy becomes deductively respectable only if the premise is strengthened to a biconditional ("if and only if"), in which case P and Q stand or fall together.

Examples

  • "If it is raining, the ground is wet. It is not raining. Therefore, the ground is not wet." (The ground might be wet from a sprinkler.)
  • "If a person is a professional athlete, they are fit. This person is not a professional athlete. Therefore, they are not fit."
  • "If the alarm was triggered, the bell rang. The alarm was not triggered, so the bell did not ring." (The bell may have other triggers.)

Contrast with valid forms

The valid patterns involving conditionals are modus ponens (affirming the antecedent) and modus tollens (denying the consequent). In propositional logic, a truth table shows that when P is false and Q is true, the premises of the fallacy are true while its conclusion is false, which is exactly what invalidity means.
