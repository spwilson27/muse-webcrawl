Croatian language

Native to: Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina

Native speakers: approx. 5.6 million

Family: Indo-European → Balto-Slavic → Slavic → South Slavic

Writing system: Latin (Gaj's alphabet)

Official status: Croatia; Bosnia and Herzegovina; EU

ISO 639-1: hr

Croatian (hrvatski) is the standardised variety of the Serbo-Croatian pluricentric language mainly used by Croats. It is the national official language and literary standard of Croatia, one of the official languages of Bosnia and Herzegovina, and an official language of the European Union since Croatia's accession in 2013.

Dialects

Croatian dialects are traditionally grouped by their word for "what": Shtokavian (što), the basis of the standard language; Chakavian (ča), spoken along the Adriatic coast and islands; and Kajkavian (kaj), spoken in the north-west around Zagreb. The standard language is based on Neo-Shtokavian, specifically the Eastern Herzegovinian dialect, shared with the Serbian, Bosnian and Montenegrin standards.

History

Medieval Croatian was written in three scripts: Glagolitic, Western Cyrillic, and Latin. The Baška tablet (c. 1100), carved in Glagolitic, is one of the oldest surviving Croatian texts. In the 19th century, Ljudevit Gaj led the Illyrian movement, which reformed the Latin alphabet (producing Gaj's alphabet, closely related to the Czech system) and promoted a unified South Slav literary language. The 1850 Vienna Literary Agreement aligned Croatian and Serbian standards, a union that lasted, with tensions, through the Yugoslav period until Croatian linguistic separation was reasserted after 1991.

Writing system

Croatian uses a 30-letter Latin alphabet in which spelling is almost perfectly phonemic — each letter represents one sound. Letters include the digraphs dž, lj and nj and the accented letters č, ć, đ, š and ž.
