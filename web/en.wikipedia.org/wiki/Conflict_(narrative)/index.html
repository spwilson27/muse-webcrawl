Conflict (narrative)

Conflict in narrative
Image: dramatic confrontation illustration
FieldLiterary theory, screenwriting
TypesInternal, external (character vs. character, nature, society, technology, fate)

In works of narrative, conflict is the challenge main characters need to solve to achieve their goals. Traditionally, conflict is a major literary element of narrative or dramatic structure that creates challenges in a story by adding uncertainty as to whether the goal will be achieved. A narrative is not limited to a single conflict. While conflicts may not always resolve in narrative, the existence of conflict as a concept is universally acknowledged as one of the most important elements of narrative.

Types of conflict

  • Character vs. self (internal conflict): the character's own desires, fears, guilt, or conscience
  • Character vs. character: an antagonist, rival, or opponent
  • Character vs. nature: weather, wilderness, animals, disease
  • Character vs. society: institutions, norms, governments, and the collective
  • Character vs. technology / the machine: a more modern category
  • Character vs. fate / the supernatural: destiny, gods, ghosts

Structure

In the three-act model, conflict typically appears with the inciting incident, intensifies through rising action into a crisis or climax, and is resolved (or deliberately left unresolved) in the falling action and dénouement. Screenwriting handbooks describe a central dramatic question whose answer the audience awaits; subplots carry secondary conflicts that complicate or echo the main one.

Conflict is what gives a story its tension, urgency, and forward motion; without an obstacle between the protagonist and a goal, there is, by this definition, no story.
