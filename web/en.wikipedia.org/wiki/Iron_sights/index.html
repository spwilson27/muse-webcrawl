Iron sights

Iron sights are a system of physical alignment markers used to assist the aiming of a device such as a firearm, crossbow or telescope, without the use of optics. Despite the name, modern iron sights may be made of steel, polymer or other materials.

Design

A typical set consists of a rear sight, usually a notch (open sight) or a ring (aperture or "peep" sight), and a front sight post or bead. The shooter aligns the front sight within the rear sight and places the combination on the target, adjusting for elevation and windage. Aperture sights, used on many military rifles, exploit the eye's natural tendency to centre the front post in the ring and are generally faster and more precise than open sights.

Use

Iron sights are simple, rugged and require no power, so they remain standard on handguns and are fitted as back-up sights (BUIS) on rifles equipped with telescopic or red-dot sights. Their effective range is limited by the shooter's eyesight and the sight radius — the distance between front and rear sights. Folding, adjustable and fibre-optic or tritium-illuminated variants are common.

History

Simple sights date to early firearms and crossbows; precision aperture sights were refined in the 19th century for target shooting and adopted widely on 20th-century military rifles such as the Lee–Enfield, M1 Garand and M16.
