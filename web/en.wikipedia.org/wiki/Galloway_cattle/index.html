Galloway cattle

The Galloway is a Scottish breed of beef cattle, originating in the Galloway region of southwestern Scotland. It is one of the oldest and purest of the native British beef breeds, traditionally reared on rough hill ground. The breed is naturally polled (hornless) and is best known for its long, shaggy double coat, which insulates it against harsh winter weather.

Characteristics

Galloways are usually solid black, though dun, red and white-marked animals also occur; the related Belted Galloway, with its distinctive white band around the middle, is recognised as a separate breed. Cows typically weigh 450–600 kg and bulls 800–1,000 kg. The double coat consists of a soft, wavy undercoat and a long, coarse outer coat that sheds rain. The breed is noted for hardiness, longevity, easy calving, strong maternal instincts, and an ability to finish on forage alone, producing well-marbled beef with a low proportion of subcutaneous fat.

History

Cattle from Galloway were being driven to English markets by the 17th and 18th centuries, and "Galloway" became a byword for quality beef; the region's cattle were exported to the Norfolks and other fattening districts. The Galloway Cattle Society was founded in 1877, and herd books were established for both the Galloway and the Belted Galloway. The breed has been exported worldwide — to North and South America, Australasia, and continental Europe, including a notable population in Germany — and is used in crossbreeding to impart hardiness and maternal quality to commercial herds.

Conservation

Numbers declined with the rise of larger continental breeds in the 20th century, but the Galloway has seen renewed interest for low-input and conservation grazing, where its ability to browse coarse vegetation benefits upland habitats.
