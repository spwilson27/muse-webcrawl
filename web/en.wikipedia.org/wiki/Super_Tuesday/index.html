Super Tuesday

Super Tuesday is the United States presidential primary election day in February or March when the greatest number of states hold primaries or caucuses. About a third of all delegates to the presidential nominating conventions can be won on Super Tuesday, more than on any other single day, making it a pivotal test of a candidate's national appeal and fundraising.

History

The modern Super Tuesday emerged in the 1980s as Southern states, hoping to increase their influence and nominate a moderate Democrat, clustered their primaries on a single day — notably in 1988, when some twenty states voted. The strategy has evolved into a general front-loading of the primary calendar. In 2008, the largest Super Tuesday saw 24 states vote on February 5, a day sometimes called "Tsuanmi Tuesday." In 2024 Super Tuesday fell on March 5, with 15 states and one territory voting, including California and Texas, the two largest delegate prizes.

Significance

Because contests span many media markets simultaneously, Super Tuesday demands large advertising budgets and national organizations. A decisive win often effectively settles a nomination race, while fragmented results can prolong it. The delegate haul — roughly 1,400 Democratic and about 850 Republican delegates in recent cycles — means a candidate who sweeps Super Tuesday is difficult to catch.

Criticisms

Reformers have criticized front-loading for compressing voter deliberation and favouring wealthy frontrunners; both parties have at various times tried to ration their calendars, though the single-day prize continues to dominate early-primary strategy.
