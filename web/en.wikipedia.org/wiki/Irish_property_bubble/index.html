Irish property bubble

The Irish property bubble was the sharp rise and subsequent collapse of real estate prices in Ireland from the late 1990s until 2008. Average house prices roughly quadrupled between 1995 and 2007, fuelled by low eurozone interest rates, loose bank lending, tax incentives, population growth and speculative development.

Boom

During the Celtic Tiger years, construction grew to employ around 13% of the workforce, and government revenue became heavily dependent on property transaction taxes. House completions peaked at about 93,000 units in 2006, far beyond underlying demand. Lending standards deteriorated, with 100% mortgages, interest-only loans and heavy bank exposure to a small number of large developers.

Crash

Prices began falling in 2007 and ultimately dropped by more than half from peak to trough nationally, with larger falls for apartments and in Dublin. The collapse left "ghost estates" of unfinished housing, mass negative equity, and losses that precipitated the Irish banking crisis and the state's 2010 EU–IMF bailout. The state agency NAMA was created in 2009 to take over development loans.

Aftermath

The crash reshaped Irish politics and policy: banking regulation was overhauled, mortgage lending rules with loan-to-income limits were introduced in 2015, and inquiries examined the roles of banks, regulators and government. Prices recovered substantially in the following decade amid a housing shortage.
