In classical statistical mechanics, the H-theorem, introduced by Ludwig Boltzmann in 1872, describes the tendency of the quantity H (defined below) to decrease to a minimum value in a nearly ideal gas of molecules. Since this quantity was claimed to be related to entropy, the theorem was a powerful early statement of the second law of thermodynamics from statistical principles, and an important part of the foundation of kinetic theory of gases.

Definition

The H value is determined by the function f(v, t) d³v, the distribution of molecular velocities at time t:

H(t) = ∫ f(v, t) ln f(v, t) d³v

The equation for H is the Boltzmann transport equation. The H-theorem states that, for collisions described by that equation,

dH/dt ≤ 0,

with equality holding at the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution. Boltzmann's H is closely related to entropy: in equilibrium, S = −kB N H + const., where kB is Boltzmann's constant and N the number of particles.

Impact and limitations

The theorem attracted strong criticism. Loschmidt's paradox noted that the underlying mechanics is time-reversible, so a purely mechanical proof of irreversibility cannot be complete; Zermelo raised the Poincaré recurrence objection. The resolution is that the proof relies on the assumption of molecular chaos (Stosszahlansatz) — that colliding particles are uncorrelated before collision — which introduces a statistical, time-asymmetric assumption. The H-theorem is therefore now understood as a statement about typical behaviour given probabilistic assumptions, not an absolute mechanical law.

Applications

Analogues of the H-theorem appear in many kinetic theories: in the theory of rarefied gases, plasma physics (Landau collision operator), semiconductor transport, and radiative transfer. The concept generalises to entropy production arguments throughout non-equilibrium statistical mechanics.
