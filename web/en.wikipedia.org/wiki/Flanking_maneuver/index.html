Flanking maneuver

Flanking maneuver
TypeMilitary tactic
TargetEnemy flank (side or rear)
RelatedEnvelopment, pincer movement

A flanking maneuver is a movement of an armed force around the side, or flank, of an enemy formation to achieve an advantageous position over it. Attacking a flank concentrates combat power against the enemy's weaker side, where defences are thinner and troops may be forced to fight in two directions.

Types

A single envelopment attacks one flank; a double envelopment attacks both flanks simultaneously, as in Hannibal's victory at the Battle of Cannae in 216 BC. A turning movement is a form of flanking manoeuvre that passes around the enemy's flank to threaten lines of communication and force the enemy to abandon their position or risk being cut off.

History and defence

Flanking attacks are among the oldest tactics in warfare, made famous by Epaminondas at Leuctra and by Alexander the Great. Defences against flanking include refusing the flank, maintaining reserves, using natural obstacles, and keeping reconnaissance to detect the manoeuvre early. In modern warfare, mobility and air power have extended flanking to operational and strategic scales.
