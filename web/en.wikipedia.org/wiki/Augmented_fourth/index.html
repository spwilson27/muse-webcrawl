An augmented fourth is a musical interval composed of three whole tones (six semitones). It is the interval formed by raising a perfect fourth by a chromatic semitone: for example, from C to F♯. Its inversion is the diminished fifth, and the two are enharmonically equivalent in twelve-tone equal temperament, dividing the octave exactly in half. The interval is also known as the tritone (from the Greek trítonon, "of three tones").

History and reputation

The tritone was long regarded as an unstable and dissonant interval, avoided in medieval and Renaissance counterpoint; it later acquired the nickname diabolus in musica ("the devil in music"), a label more often applied in the 18th century than in the medieval period itself. In functional harmony the tritone is central to the dominant seventh chord, whose tritone creates the tension that drives resolution to the tonic. Composers from Bach and Beethoven to Wagner, Liszt, and Debussy exploited its ambiguity, and it became a foundation sonority in jazz (as in tritone substitution), blues, and heavy metal music.

Acoustics and tuning

In just intonation, several ratios approximate the augmented fourth, including 45:32 (about 590 cents) and 729:512 (the Pythagorean augmented fourth, about 612 cents). In twelve-tone equal temperament the interval is exactly 600 cents, the square root of 2 ratio (√2:1), which makes it its own inversion.

Occurrences

The opening of Leonard Bernstein's "Maria" from West Side Story famously ascends by a tritone, as does the theme of The Simpsons. The interval also appears prominently in works such as Liszt's Dante Sonata, Wagner's operas, and the heavy metal repertoire of bands such as Black Sabbath, whose title song opens with a tritone riff.
