Relativistic beaming

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Light from a jet moving near light-speed is beamed toward observers in its path

Relativistic beaming (also known as Doppler beaming, Doppler boosting, or the headlight effect) is the process by which relativistic effects modify the apparent luminosity of emitting matter that is moving at speeds close to the speed of light. Radiation from a source moving toward an observer is concentrated, blueshifted and greatly brightened, while radiation from a receding source is dimmed and redshifted. The observed brightness scales steeply with the Doppler factor, roughly as its third or fourth power depending on whether the source is a continuous jet or a moving blob.

Astrophysical significance

Beaming is central to the interpretation of relativistic jets from active galactic nuclei and gamma-ray bursts. In a twin-jet source, the jet pointing toward Earth is boosted while its counter-jet is de-boosted, explaining the one-sided jets seen in many radio galaxies and quasars. Blazars are understood as active galaxies whose jets point almost directly at the observer, so that beaming accounts for their extreme luminosity, rapid variability and apparent superluminal motion. Beaming implies that for every blazar there exist many parent objects whose jets point elsewhere, and it causes gamma-ray burst observations to sample only those outflows aligned with Earth; the true (beaming-corrected) energies of gamma-ray bursts are far smaller than their isotropic-equivalent values.

Physics

The effect combines relativistic aberration, which concentrates photons into a cone of half-angle ~1/γ around the direction of motion (where γ is the Lorentz factor), the relativistic Doppler shift of photon energies, and time compression of the emitted signal. Together these raise the observed specific intensity by a factor δ³ (δ being the Doppler factor) for a moving source, with an additional power of δ for continuous jets. Relativistic beaming is also essential in models of microquasars, pulsar wind nebulae and tidal disruption events.
