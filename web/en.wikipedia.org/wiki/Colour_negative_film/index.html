Colour negative film

Colour negative film (British English; American: color negative film) is a type of photographic film in which the image recorded has its colours inverted — light areas appear dark and colours are replaced by their complements — and which is intended to be used to make positive photographic prints on paper. It is the most common type of film used in analogue photography.

A strip of colour negative film; note the orange mask and inverted tones.

Structure

A typical colour negative film consists of three main emulsion layers, each sensitive to one primary colour of light: blue, green, and red. Within each layer, dye couplers form dyes of the complementary colour (yellow, magenta, and cyan respectively) during development, in proportion to the silver halide exposed. The result is a negative image in complementary colours. Most colour negative films incorporate an orange-coloured mask that compensates for unwanted dye absorptions during printing, which is why unexposed film appears orange-brown.

Processing

The standard process for colour negative film is Kodak's C-41 process. The film is developed in a colour developer that reduces exposed silver halide and activates the dye couplers, then bleached and fixed to remove the metallic silver, leaving only the dye image. Prints are made by projecting the negative onto colour photographic paper, or by scanning and inversion in digital workflows.

History

Multi-layer colour negative film became practical with Kodacolor film, introduced for amateur cameras in 1942, following the earlier success of Kodachrome reversal (transparency) film in 1935. The integral orange mask arrived with Eastmancolor negative film around 1950, which also transformed colour motion-picture production. Colour negative film became the dominant consumer film type for the rest of the twentieth century, until digital photography displaced it in the 2000s.

Characteristics

Colour negative film is prized for its wide exposure latitude (typically tolerating several stops of overexposure), fine colour rendition, and suitability for both prints and high-quality scans. Common speeds ranged from ISO 100 to 800 for amateur films, with professional emulsions such as Kodak Portra and Fujicolor lines designed for portraiture and weddings.
