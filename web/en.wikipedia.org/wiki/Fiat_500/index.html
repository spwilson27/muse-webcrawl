The Fiat 500 (Italian: Cinquecento, pronounced [ˌtʃiŋkweˈtʃɛnto]) is a city car that has been produced in several distinct generations by the Italian manufacturer Fiat. The name was first used for the "Topolino" of 1936–1955, but it is most famously associated with the rear-engined model launched in 1957, which became an icon of Italian design and post-war motorisation, and with the retro-styled revival launched in 2007.

The 1957 Fiat 500

Launched in July 1957 as the Nuova 500, the car was designed by Dante Giacosa as an inexpensive, practical vehicle for a booming post-war Italy. Measuring just 2.97 metres long, it was powered by a rear-mounted 479 cc two-cylinder, air-cooled engine producing 13 horsepower. Over its production run the engine grew to 594 cc in the 500R of 1972. Variants included the Giardiniera estate and performance versions by Abarth. Nearly 3.9 million examples of the 1957 family were built before production ended in 1975, when the Fiat 126 succeeded it.

The 2007 revival

Fifty years after the original, Fiat revived the 500 nameplate at a launch event in Turin on 4 July 2007. The new car, designed under Frank Stephenson with retro styling echoing Giacosa's original, is a front-engined, front-wheel-drive three-door hatchback built in Tychy, Poland, and (for the Americas) Toluca, Mexico. It was joined by the convertible 500C, the sporty Abarth 500, the larger 500L and 500X crossover, and the battery-electric 500e. A completely new electric Fiat 500 debuted in 2020.

Cultural impact

The 500 is regarded as one of the most influential small-car designs of the twentieth century; an example of the 1957 car is held in the collection of the Museum of Modern Art in New York.
