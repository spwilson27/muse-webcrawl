Troubled Asset Relief Program

Enacted3 October 2008
LegislationEmergency Economic Stabilization Act of 2008
AdministratorU.S. Department of the Treasury

The Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) is a program of the United States government to purchase toxic assets and equity from financial institutions to strengthen the financial sector, signed into law by President George W. Bush on 3 October 2008 as part of the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act of 2008. It was a component of the government's measures in the late-2000s financial crisis to address the subprime mortgage crisis.

History

TARP originally authorized expenditures of $700 billion; the Dodd–Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 reduced the authorization to $475 billion. Treasury Secretary Henry Paulson proposed the program after the collapse of Lehman Brothers in September 2008 threatened a systemic meltdown. Although initially designed to buy mortgage-backed securities, the program was quickly redirected toward direct capital injections into banks through the Capital Purchase Program, in which the Treasury bought preferred stock in hundreds of financial institutions. Funds also supported the automotive industry rescue of General Motors and Chrysler, the insurer AIG, and foreclosure-mitigation programs such as HAMP.

Repayment and assessment

By the program's wind-down, most disbursed funds had been repaid with interest, dividends, and warrant proceeds; assessments by the Congressional Budget Office estimated a modest net cost to taxpayers, concentrated in the housing and automotive programs, while the bank programs returned a profit. TARP remains controversial: supporters credit it with averting a financial collapse, while critics argue it rewarded reckless institutions and created moral hazard.
