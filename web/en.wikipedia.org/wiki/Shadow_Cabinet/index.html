Shadow Cabinet

A shadow cabinet is a feature of the Westminster system of government. It consists of a senior group of opposition spokespeople who, led by the Leader of the Opposition, form an alternative cabinet to that of the government. Each member of the shadow cabinet "shadows" or marks the corresponding government minister, scrutinising their actions, holding them to account, and developing alternative policy in that area.

Practice

The shadow cabinet meets weekly while parliament is sitting, usually chaired by the Leader of the Opposition, and attempts to present a united front. Its most prominent members — the shadow chancellor, shadow foreign secretary and shadow home secretary — lead for the opposition in major debates and at question times opposite their government counterparts. At a general election victory, shadow ministers commonly take up the real portfolios they have shadowed.

Selection

Practices vary by party. Conservative leaders appoint their shadow cabinets outright. In the Labour Party, shadow cabinet members were historically elected by Labour MPs when the party was in opposition, with the leader allocating portfolios among those elected; the elections were abolished in 2011, giving the leader full power of appointment. Liberal Democrat and other Commonwealth parties use variants of appointment and election.

Other countries

The institution is used throughout the Commonwealth: Canada, Australia, New Zealand and many Caribbean states all have recognised shadow cabinets fronted by the Official Opposition. In some countries, such as Japan, opposition parties have also used the label for their policy teams. Shadow cabinets receive varying degrees of support: in the UK, the Leader of the Opposition's office and opposition parties receive public "Short money" funding, and the shadow cabinet's members are typically appointed to the Privy Council to receive confidential briefings.

Criticism

Critics argue that shadow cabinets can entrench a "government-in-waiting" mindset, encourage opposition for its own sake, and that shadow ministers lack the civil-service support needed to develop fully costed policies. Supporters reply that the system provides clear accountability and allows voters to judge a ready-made alternative government before an election.
