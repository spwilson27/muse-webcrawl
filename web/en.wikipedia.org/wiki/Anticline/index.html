Anticline

An anticline exposed in a road cut; the oldest rocks lie at the core of the fold.

In structural geology, an anticline is a type of fold that is an arch-like shape and has its oldest beds at its core, whereas a syncline is the inverse, a trough-like fold with the youngest beds at its core. A large anticline whose limbs themselves are folded is an anticlinorium. Anticlines form under compressional stresses, typically where rock layers are squeezed horizontally, as in mountain-building (orogenic) belts.

Characteristics

An anticline is described by its hinge (the line of maximum curvature), its axial plane, and its limbs, which dip away from the hinge on either side. Folds may be symmetrical, asymmetrical, overturned or recumbent depending on the orientation of the axial plane. Because the oldest strata are in the core, erosion of an anticline exposes progressively older rocks toward its centre — the reverse of a syncline. An anticline that plunges has its hinge tilted from horizontal, so the fold pattern closes in the direction of plunge on a geological map.

Formation

Anticlines commonly develop above thrust faults (fault-propagation folds), during the bending of strata over a fault step (fault-bend folds), or by the buckling of layered rocks under layer-parallel shortening. Salt diapirs and igneous intrusions can also dome strata upward into anticlinal forms.

Economic significance

Anticlines are classic traps for petroleum and natural gas: hydrocarbons migrating through porous reservoir rock accumulate at the crest of the fold beneath an impermeable cap rock. The "anticlinal theory" of oil accumulation, developed in the 19th century, guided early oil exploration; many of the world's great oil fields, including early fields in Pennsylvania and the Middle East, occupy anticlinal traps. Anticlines can also trap groundwater and are studied when siting tunnels and dams, since fractured hinge zones may be weak and permeable.
