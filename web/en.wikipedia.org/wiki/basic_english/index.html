Basic English

Basic English is a controlled language based on standard English, but with a greatly simplified vocabulary and grammar. It was created by the linguist and philosopher Charles Kay Ogden as an international auxiliary language, and as an aid for teaching English as a second language. It was given its name in Ogden's book Basic English: A General Introduction with Rules and Grammar, published in 1930.

The word list

Basic English is built on a core vocabulary of 850 words: 600 nouns (divided into general nouns and picture words), 150 adjectives ("qualities"), and 100 "operations" words including verbs, prepositions, and pronouns. Instead of multiplying verbs, Basic English relies on just 18 operator verbs — such as come, get, give, go, keep, let, make, put, seem, take — combined with directives (prepositions) to express actions; for example, "go into" replaces "enter". Ogden claimed that with these 850 words one could say anything needed for ordinary purposes, and that it would take about seven years to learn English, seven months for Esperanto, and seven weeks for Basic English.

History and reception

The system gained official attention during the Second World War: Winston Churchill praised it in a 1943 speech at Harvard and a British cabinet committee investigated promoting it, though Churchill later cooled on the idea. Critics such as I. A. Richards, Ogden's collaborator, defended it, while others objected that the restricted vocabulary forced awkward, ambiguous paraphrase and that idiomatic fluency still required the full language. Ogden also prepared translations in Basic English, including the Bible and works of Shakespeare.

Legacy

Basic English influenced later controlled languages, including Special English (used by Voice of America broadcasters, with about 1,500 words) and the simplified style guides used in technical writing, such as ASD Simplified Technical English for aerospace documentation. The Simple English Wikipedia likewise uses a restricted vocabulary inspired by such systems.
