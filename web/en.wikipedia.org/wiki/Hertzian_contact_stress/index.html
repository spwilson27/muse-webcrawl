Hertzian contact stress

Hertzian contact stress refers to the localized stresses that develop as two curved surfaces come into contact and deform slightly under load. The theory is named after Heinrich Hertz, who published his classic paper "On the contact of elastic solids" in 1882 while still in his twenties.

Theory

Hertz assumed that two contacting elastic bodies could be treated as elastic half-spaces, that their surfaces are frictionless and non-conforming, and that the strains are small and within the linear elastic range. Under these assumptions, pressing two spheres together produces a circular contact patch of radius a that grows with the cube root of the applied load, and the pressure distribution across the patch is semi-elliptical, with a maximum pressure at the center of 1.5 times the mean pressure.

Analogous solutions exist for cylinders in line contact (producing a rectangular contact band with an elliptical pressure profile) and for elliptical contact between general curved bodies. The subsurface stress field is critical in engineering because fatigue cracks in rolling bearings and gear teeth typically initiate just below the surface, near the point of maximum shear stress.

Applications

Hertzian theory underlies the design and rating of ball and roller bearings, gear tooth contact analysis, railroad wheel–rail contact, cam and follower design, and indentation hardness testing. Because the theory neglects friction, adhesion, plasticity, and surface roughness, modern contact mechanics extends it with models such as the JKR and DMT adhesion theories and elastoplastic contact models.

History

Hertz's work arose from optical experiments on Newton's rings, where he realized that the glass lenses in contact were deforming elastically. His solution remained one of the founding results of tribology and is still the first reference point for contact stress calculations.
