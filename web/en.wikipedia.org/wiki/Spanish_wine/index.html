Spanish wine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Placeholder image — vineyards in La Rioja, Spain's best-known wine region.

Spanish wine has a long history: viticulture in the Iberian Peninsula dates back thousands of years, and Spain today has more land planted with wine grapes than any other country, and is among the world's largest wine producers. Spanish wines are governed by a classification system based on Denominación de Origen (DO), similar to the French appellation system.

Major regions

The most famous Spanish wine region is Rioja, known for red wines based on the Tempranillo grape, traditionally aged in American oak. Ribera del Duero produces powerful Tempranillo-based reds, while Priorat in Catalonia is known for intense Garnacha and Cariñena blends. The sherry-producing region around Jerez de la Frontera in Andalusia makes fortified wines from the Palomino grape, and the Penedès region of Catalonia is the home of Cava, Spain's sparkling wine made by the traditional method.

Classification

Spanish wine laws created the Denominación de Origen system in 1932, modelled on the French AOC. Wines are classified by region and by ageing: crianza, reserva and gran reserva denote progressively longer periods of ageing in barrel and bottle for red wines in regions such as Rioja.

Grape varieties

Spain's most widely planted grape is the white Airén, though Tempranillo is the most prestigious. Other important varieties include Garnacha, Monastrell, Albariño, Verdejo and the sherry grapes Palomino and Pedro Ximénez.
