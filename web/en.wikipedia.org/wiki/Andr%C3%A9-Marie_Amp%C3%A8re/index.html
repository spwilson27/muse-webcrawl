André-Marie Ampère

Born: 20 January 1775, Lyon, France
Died: 10 June 1836, Marseille
Known for: Founding electrodynamics; Ampère's law
Honoured in: The SI unit of current, the ampere

André-Marie Ampère (1775–1836) was a French physicist and mathematician who founded the science of electrodynamics — now known as electromagnetism. His name is immortalised in the SI unit of electric current, the ampere, and in Ampère's law relating magnetic fields to the currents that produce them. James Clerk Maxwell called him "the Newton of electricity".

Electrodynamics

In September 1820, within days of hearing of Hans Christian Ørsted's discovery that an electric current deflects a compass needle, Ampère demonstrated before the French Academy of Sciences that two parallel wires attract or repel each other depending on whether their currents flow in the same or opposite directions. Over the following years, largely without laboratory assistance, he built a complete mathematical theory of the forces between currents, published in his 1827 memoir Théorie mathématique des phénomènes électro-dynamiques. He proposed that magnetism itself arises from tiny circulating currents within matter — an early form of the molecular current hypothesis.

Other work and life

Largely self-taught after a childhood shadowed by the Revolution — his father, a Lyon merchant, was guillotined in 1793 — Ampère taught mathematics and philosophy, contributed to probability and partial differential equations, coined terms such as "current" and "voltage" in their electrical senses, and devised a classification of the sciences. He also proposed the name "cybernetics" for the science of government. He held chairs at the École Polytechnique and the Collège de France, and died in Marseille in 1836.
