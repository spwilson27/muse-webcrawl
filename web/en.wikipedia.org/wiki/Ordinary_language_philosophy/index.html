Ordinary language philosophy

Ordinary language philosophy is a philosophical methodology that views traditional philosophical problems as the result of misunderstanding the ordinary usage of words, and seeks to dissolve them by paying close attention to how words are actually used in everyday language. It was the dominant approach in British analytic philosophy in the mid-20th century, particularly at Oxford, and is sometimes called "Oxford philosophy".

Origins

The approach derives chiefly from the later philosophy of Ludwig Wittgenstein, whose Philosophical Investigations (1953) argued that meaning is a matter of use within "language-games" and forms of life, and that philosophical confusion arises when language "goes on holiday". Related influences include G. E. Moore's defence of common sense and the early work at Cambridge. The leading Oxford figures were J. L. Austin and Gilbert Ryle, with important contributions from Peter Strawson, H. L. A. Hart in jurisprudence, and John Wisdom and P. F. Strawson on philosophical method.

Key ideas

Ryle's The Concept of Mind (1949) attacked Cartesian dualism as a "category mistake", analysing mental concepts through dispositions and behaviour. Austin developed the theory of speech acts in How to Do Things with Words (1962), distinguishing performative from constative utterances, and practised minute analysis of ordinary distinctions — for example, between "by accident" and "by mistake" — as a philosophical tool. Strawson's Individuals (1959) practised "descriptive metaphysics": describing the actual structure of our thought about the world rather than revising it.

Criticism and legacy

Critics, notably Ernest Gellner in Words and Things (1959), charged ordinary language philosophy with conservatism, triviality and an unjustified appeal to usage as an authority. The movement waned from the 1960s as analytic philosophy turned toward formal methods and naturalism, but its legacies persist in speech-act theory, philosophy of language, and contextualist approaches in several fields.
