Ruby

A faceted ruby crystal

A ruby is a pinkish-red to blood-red coloured gemstone, a variety of the mineral corundum (aluminium oxide). Ruby is one of the traditional cardinal gems, alongside sapphire, emerald and diamond. Its colour is caused mainly by the element chromium.

Properties

Rubies have a hardness of 9.0 on the Mohs scale, second only to diamond among gemstones. All natural rubies have imperfections, including colour impurities and inclusions of rutile needles known as "silk"; gemmologists use these to distinguish natural stones from synthetics. The most valued colour is a vivid, slightly bluish red called "pigeon's blood".

Sources and history

The Mogok Valley in Upper Myanmar (Burma) was for centuries the world's main source of rubies and produced the finest stones; other sources include Thailand, Sri Lanka, Vietnam, Mozambique, Tanzania, Kenya, Afghanistan and Greenland. The Sanskrit word for ruby, ratnaraj, means "king of precious stones".

Synthetic rubies and uses

Synthetic rubies have been produced since the 19th century by the Verneuil flame-fusion process and other methods. Beyond jewellery, ruby crystals were the gain medium of the first working laser (1960) and are used in watch movements and scientific instruments.
