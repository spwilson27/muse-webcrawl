Ethnic cleansing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Refugee columns on a road in the Balkans during the 1990s wars]
Refugee columns on a road in the Balkans during the 1990s wars

Ethnic cleansing is the systematic forced removal of ethnic, racial or religious groups from a given territory, by intimidation, expulsion or mass killing, with the intent of making the area ethnically homogeneous. The term (a calque of Serbo-Croatian etničko čišćenje) entered wide use during the Bosnian War of 1992–95. It is not a distinct crime under international law, but its constituent acts — deportation, persecution, murder — are punishable as war crimes and crimes against humanity, and it can shade into Genocide.

History of the practice

Forced homogenisation long predates the term: the expulsion of Jews and Muslims from Spain (1492 onwards), the Greek–Turkish population exchange (1923, internationally sanctioned), Stalin's deportations of whole peoples (Chechens, Crimean Tatars, 1944), the flight and expulsion of Germans from Eastern Europe (1945–50, some 12–14 million people), and the Partition of India (1947, up to a million or more dead). Retrospectively applying the term is common but contested.

The Yugoslav wars and law

In Bosnia, Bosnian Serb forces expelled and massacred Bosniak and Croat populations to create ethnically pure territories — culminating in the Srebrenica massacre of July 1995, ruled genocide by the ICTY. The tribunal prosecuted ethnic cleansing's acts (deportation, persecution, extermination) rather than the label itself; the 1948 Genocide Convention requires intent to destroy a group, a threshold ethnic cleansing may or may not meet.

Later cases

The term has been applied to Kosovo (1998–99), Darfur, the expulsion of the Rohingya from Myanmar (2016–17, described by the UN as a 'textbook example of ethnic cleansing'), and others — with debates over precision versus euphemism following each use.
