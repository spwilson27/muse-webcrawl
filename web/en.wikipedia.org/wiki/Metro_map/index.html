Metro map

A metro map (also called a tube map, subway map, or transit map) is a schematic diagram of a public transport network — typically a rapid transit system — that shows lines, stations, and interchanges without geographic accuracy. Unlike conventional maps, metro maps distort distances, straighten lines, and regularise angles to maximise legibility.

History

Early underground railway maps were drawn geographically, becoming cluttered as networks grew. The breakthrough came with Harry Beck's 1933 map of the London Underground: inspired by electrical circuit diagrams, Beck restricted lines to horizontal, vertical, and 45° diagonal directions, expanded the crowded centre, and equalised station spacing, showing the topology of the network rather than its geography. Initially rejected by the publicity department, the map was an immediate success with the public and became the template for transit maps worldwide.

Design principles

Typical conventions include strong colour coding for lines, tick marks or station labels, circles for interchange stations, and simplified representations of rivers or zones. Modern variations grapple with accessibility (colour blindness), fare zones, and very large networks such as those of Tokyo, Seoul, and Shanghai, which stretch the schematic approach. Massimo Vignelli's 1972 New York City Subway map is a famous, controversial application of the same modernist principles.
