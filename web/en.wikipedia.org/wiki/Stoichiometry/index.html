Stoichiometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stoichiometry is the branch of chemistry concerned with the quantitative relationships between reactants and products in chemical reactions — how much of each substance is consumed and produced. It rests on the law of conservation of mass and on the mole.

Using the coefficients of a balanced equation as mole ratios, chemists calculate reactant masses, product yields and the limiting reagent (the reactant that runs out first). Theoretical yield assumes perfect conversion; actual yield is usually lower, and their ratio is the percentage yield.

[Image: A balance weighing chemicals with a balanced equation written beside it]

Worked example

For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, the ratio is 2:1:2. Burning 4 g of hydrogen (2 mol) therefore needs 1 mol of oxygen (32 g) and produces 2 mol of water (36 g) — mass conserved, as Lavoisier showed.

History

The term (Greek: 'element measuring') was introduced by Jeremias Benjamin Richter in the 1790s, who studied the fixed proportions in which acids and bases neutralise each other.
