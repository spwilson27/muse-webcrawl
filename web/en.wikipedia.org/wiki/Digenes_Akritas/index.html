Digenes Akritas (Greek: Διγενῆς Ἀκρίτας) is the most famous of the Byzantine Acritic Songs — epic poems celebrating the heroes (akritai) who guarded the eastern frontier of the Byzantine Empire. The hero's name encodes his mixed parentage: Digenes means "born of two peoples" — his father was an Arab emir who converted to Christianity, his mother the daughter of a Byzantine general — and Akritas means "frontiersman".

The poem

The epic, compiled in versions dating from about the 12th century but drawing on older oral tradition, follows Digenes from his parents' courtship through his own extraordinary childhood, his marriage won by abduction and combat, his battles with wild beasts, brigands (apelatai) and Amazons, to his death on the banks of the Euphrates, where he had built a palace and garden. The poem survives in six manuscripts, of which the Grottaferrata version (in more literary Greek) and the Escorial version (closer to the vernacular) are the best known.

Significance

Digenes Akritas is regarded as the foundational work of modern Greek literature: the earliest substantial text in a recognisably demotic form of Greek, and the starting point of the Greek folk-song tradition. Its frontier world — in which Byzantine and Arab heroes admire each other's prowess across the religious divide — reflects the bicultural society of the medieval Anatolian borderlands. The hero became a lasting symbol in Greek culture, frequently invoked in later literature and in the art and ideology of the modern Greek state.
