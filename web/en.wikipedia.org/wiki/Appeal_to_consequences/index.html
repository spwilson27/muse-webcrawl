Appeal to consequences

An appeal to consequences (Latin: argumentum ad consequentiam) is a logical fallacy in which a claim is argued to be true or false based on whether its consequences are desirable or undesirable, rather than on evidence about the claim itself. For example, rejecting a scientific finding because accepting it would be upsetting, or accepting a belief because it is comforting, appeals to consequences.

Positive and negative forms

In the positive form, a proposition is said to be true because it leads to good outcomes: “If this were false, life would be meaningless, so it must be true.” In the negative form, a proposition is said to be false because it leads to bad outcomes. Both substitute the desirability of a belief for its truth.

Related reasoning

Considering consequences is legitimate when deciding what to do — for instance in policy or ethics — but it does not by itself settle what is factually true. Related fallacies include wishful thinking, appeal to emotion and appeal to force, where a threat is used in place of evidence.
