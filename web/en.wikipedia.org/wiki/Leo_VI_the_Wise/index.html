Leo VI the Wise

Leo VI, called the Wise (Greek: Λέων ὁ Σοφός; 19 September 866 – 11 May 912), was Byzantine emperor from 886 to 912, the second ruler of the Macedonian dynasty. A scholar-emperor, he completed the codification of Byzantine law begun by his father Basil I and was credited with works on military tactics, sermons, and prophecies, which earned him his epithet.

Reign

Leo succeeded Basil I in 886 — although rumours held that he was actually the son of Michael III. His reign saw continued war with the Bulgarians under Simeon I, who defeated Byzantine armies and extracted concessions, and with the Arabs, during which the empire lost Taormina in Sicily (902) and suffered the sack of Thessalonica (904). In the east, Byzantine forces recovered ground in Armenia and Mesopotamia. Leo also organised a joint expedition with the Kievan Rus' context of trade treaties, and Oleg of Kiev's attack on Constantinople in 907 led to a commercial agreement.

Legal and literary work

Leo promulgated the Basilika, a massive Greek-language recompilation of Justinianic law in sixty books, and issued 113 new constitutions (Novels) addressing gaps in the law. The Book of the Eparch, regulating Constantinople's guilds, and the military manual known as the Taktika are associated with his reign. Collections of oracles attributed to him ("Oracles of Leo the Wise") circulated for centuries.

The tetragamy controversy

Leo's desperate need for a male heir led him to marry four times, scandalising the Church, which forbade even third marriages. After his third wife died, he took a mistress, Zoe Karbonopsina, who bore the future Constantine VII; Leo married her in 906, provoking the "tetragamy" (fourth marriage) controversy with Patriarch Nicholas Mystikos, whom he deposed. The dispute was only settled after his death, and the Church formally condemned fourth marriages in 920.
