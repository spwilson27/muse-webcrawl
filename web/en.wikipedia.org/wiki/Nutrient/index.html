A nutrient is a substance used by an organism to survive, grow, and reproduce. The requirement for dietary nutrient intake applies to animals, plants, fungi, and protists. Nutrients can be incorporated into cells for metabolic purposes or excreted by cells to create non-cellular structures such as hair, scales, feathers, or exoskeletons. Some nutrients can be metabolically converted into smaller molecules in the process of releasing energy, as with carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and fermentation products; others, such as water, minerals, and vitamins, serve structural, electrolytic, or catalytic roles.

Types

Macronutrients are consumed in relatively large quantities and supply energy or bulk building material. They comprise carbohydrates (about 4 kcal/g), proteins (4 kcal/g), and fats (9 kcal/g), together with water and, in plant nutrition, the primary elements nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. Micronutrients are needed in trace amounts: the 13 recognised vitamins (A, C, D, E, K, and the B-complex group) and dietary minerals such as calcium, iron, zinc, iodine, and selenium.

Essential and non-essential

An essential nutrient is one the organism cannot synthesise in sufficient quantity and must obtain from its environment. For humans these include nine amino acids, two fatty acids (linoleic and alpha-linolenic acid), all the vitamins, and a range of minerals. Non-essential nutrients can be made internally, though they are also commonly obtained from food; some substances, such as dietary fibre, are not strictly essential but benefit health.

Plant nutrients

Green plants obtain most nutrients from the soil through their roots and carbon from atmospheric carbon dioxide. Seventeen elements are considered essential to plants, divided into macronutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, sulfur) and micronutrients (including iron, boron, manganese, and molybdenum). Deficiencies produce characteristic symptoms such as chlorosis, and fertilisers are applied in agriculture to replace nutrients removed by harvests.

Deficiency and excess

Malnutrition arises from intakes that are insufficient, excessive, or unbalanced. Classic deficiency diseases include scurvy (vitamin C), rickets (vitamin D), beriberi (thiamine), and kwashiorkor (protein). Excess intake can also be harmful — for example hypervitaminosis A — and nutrient runoff from agriculture is a major driver of eutrophication in lakes and coastal waters.
