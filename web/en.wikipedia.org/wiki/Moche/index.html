Moche

The Moche civilization (also known as the Mochica) flourished on the north coast of present-day Peru from approximately 100 to 700 CE, during the Early Intermediate Period. Centered in the Moche and Chicama valleys near modern Trujillo, its influence extended across a series of river valleys along the arid Pacific coast.

Society and art

The Moche are famed for their highly naturalistic portrait-head vessels, stirrup-spout bottles, and fine-line painted ceramics depicting warriors, rituals, animals, and supernatural beings. They built monumental adobe pyramid platforms, notably the Huaca del Sol and Huaca de la Luna, and developed sophisticated irrigation agriculture and metallurgy in gold, silver, and copper alloys.

Decline

Archaeological evidence points to severe El Niño flooding and drought in the late 6th and 7th centuries, accompanied by social upheaval and the apparent practice of ritual combat and human sacrifice, before Moche polities gave way to successors such as the Lambayeque and Chimú cultures. Rich burials including the Lord of Sipán, excavated in 1987, transformed understanding of Moche elites.
