Resin

A resin is a solid or highly viscous substance of plant or synthetic origin that is typically convertible into polymers. Natural resins are secreted by plants, particularly coniferous trees, often as a protective response to injury; they harden on exposure to air and are usually insoluble in water but soluble in alcohol and other organic solvents. Amber is fossilised tree resin.

Natural resins

Plants produce resin in specialised ducts or glands. Well-known natural resins include frankincense and myrrh (from Boswellia and Commiphora species), mastic, copal, dammar, rosin (obtained from pine oleoresin after removing turpentine) and shellac, which is secreted by the lac insect rather than a plant. Resins have long been used as varnishes, adhesives, incense, perfumes and medicines; violin bows are treated with rosin to increase friction on the strings.

Synthetic resins

The term is also applied to synthetic polymers that are used in liquid or soft form before curing, such as epoxy resins, polyester resins, phenol-formaldehyde resins (including Bakelite, the first fully synthetic plastic) and ion-exchange resins. Synthetic resins are used in paints, adhesives, moulding compounds, fibre-reinforced composites and coatings.
