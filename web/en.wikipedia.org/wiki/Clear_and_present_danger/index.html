Clear and present danger

The clear and present danger doctrine is a legal test in United States constitutional law concerning restrictions on free speech under the First Amendment. It was introduced by Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. in the Supreme Court's 1919 decision in Schenck v. United States, in which the Court upheld the conviction of Charles Schenck for distributing leaflets opposing the draft during World War I.

The question in every case is whether the words used are used in such circumstances and are of such a nature as to create a clear and present danger that they will bring about the substantive evils that Congress has a right to prevent.

In the same opinion Holmes offered the now-famous analogy that the First Amendment would not protect a man "falsely shouting fire in a theatre and causing a panic."

Development

The doctrine was applied in the companion 1919 cases Frohwerk v. United States and Debs v. United States, and was refined by Justices Holmes and Louis Brandeis in their dissents and concurrences of the 1920s, notably Brandeis's concurrence in Whitney v. California (1927), which argued that only imminent, serious harm could justify suppressing speech. During the Cold War, the test was reformulated in Dennis v. United States (1951) using Judge Learned Hand's "gravity of the evil, discounted by its improbability" formula.

Replacement

In Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969), the Supreme Court replaced the clear and present danger test with the stricter imminent lawless action standard, holding that speech may be prohibited only where it is directed to inciting imminent lawless action and is likely to produce such action. The phrase "clear and present danger" nevertheless remains widely used in public discourse and is the title of a 1989 Tom Clancy novel and its 1994 film adaptation.
