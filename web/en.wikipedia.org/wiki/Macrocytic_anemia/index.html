Macrocytic anemia

SpecialtyHaematology
Key findingMean corpuscular volume (MCV) > 100 fL
Major causesVitamin B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, alcohol use, hypothyroidism, drugs

Macrocytic anemia is a form of anemia characterised by abnormally large red blood cells, termed macrocytes, reflected in an elevated mean corpuscular volume (MCV), conventionally above 100 femtolitres. Because the bone marrow produces red cells that are larger but often fewer in number, oxygen-carrying capacity falls despite the increased cell size.

Causes

Macrocytic anemias are divided into megaloblastic and non-megaloblastic types. Megaloblastic anemia results from impaired DNA synthesis, most commonly due to deficiency of vitamin B12 or folate. The marrow shows characteristic megaloblasts — large precursor cells with immature nuclei — and peripheral blood may contain hypersegmented neutrophils. The classic B12-deficiency disease is pernicious anemia, an autoimmune gastritis destroying intrinsic factor and preventing B12 absorption.

Non-megaloblastic macrocytosis occurs with chronic alcohol use, liver disease, hypothyroidism, myelodysplastic syndromes, reticulocytosis, and certain medications including methotrexate, antiretrovirals such as zidovudine, and some anticonvulsants.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Symptoms mirror those of anemia generally: fatigue, pallor, breathlessness and tachycardia. B12 deficiency can additionally cause neurological features — peripheral neuropathy, glossitis, and in severe cases subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord — which may be irreversible if treatment is delayed. Diagnosis relies on a full blood count with MCV, blood film examination, and measurement of serum B12 and folate, supplemented by thyroid and liver function tests and medication review.

Treatment

Treatment targets the underlying cause: B12 is replaced parenterally or in high oral doses, folate is supplemented (never alone where B12 deficiency is possible, as folate can mask haematological signs while neurological damage progresses), alcohol use is addressed, and offending drugs may be withdrawn. Haemoglobin typically recovers over weeks once the deficiency is corrected.
