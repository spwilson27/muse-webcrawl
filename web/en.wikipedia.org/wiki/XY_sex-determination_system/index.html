The XY sex-determination system is a system used to classify the sex of many organisms, including humans and most other mammals, in which sex is determined by a pair of sex chromosomes. Females typically have two copies of the same sex chromosome (XX, the homogametic sex), while males have two distinct sex chromosomes (XY, the heterogametic sex).

Mechanism

In mammals, embryonic sex is triggered by the SRY gene (sex-determining region Y), normally located on the Y chromosome. Its presence initiates testis development, and the resulting hormones direct male development; in its absence, ovaries develop. In the fruit fly Drosophila, by contrast, sex is set by the ratio of X chromosomes to sets of autosomes, and the Y is needed only for male fertility. Because the mechanism differs between lineages, the XY system is thought to have evolved independently in several groups.

History

The system was discovered independently in 1905 by Nettie Stevens, working with mealworms, and Edmund Beecher Wilson, overturning earlier ideas that sex was determined by environment or nutrition alone. The human karyotype of 46 chromosomes, including the XX/XY pair, was established in 1956, and the SRY gene was identified in 1990.

Variations and exceptions

Some species with XY systems have lost the Y entirely (the X0 system, found in many insects). Others have multiple sex chromosomes. Chromosomal anomalies in humans include XXY (Klinefelter syndrome), X0 (Turner syndrome) and rare cases of XX males, usually caused by translocation of SRY onto an X chromosome. In the platypus, males have a chain of five X and five Y chromosomes partly homologous to the bird ZW system.

Other systems

Alternative systems include the ZW system of birds and some reptiles (where females are heterogametic), the X0 system, haplodiploidy in bees and ants, and environmental sex determination, in which temperature during incubation sets the sex of many reptiles.
