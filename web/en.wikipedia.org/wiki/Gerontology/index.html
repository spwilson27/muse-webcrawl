Gerontology

Term coined1903, by Élie Metchnikoff
FocusAgeing: biological, psychological, social and societal aspects
Related fieldsGeriatrics, biogerontology, social gerontology, demography

Gerontology is the study of ageing and the problems of older adults, encompassing the biological, psychological, social and cultural aspects of the ageing process. The term was coined in 1903 by the microbiologist Élie Metchnikoff. It is distinguished from geriatrics, the branch of medicine devoted to the health care of elderly people, although the fields overlap.

Branches

Biogerontology studies the biological mechanisms of ageing — cellular senescence, telomere shortening, accumulated DNA damage and metabolic change — with the long-term aim of understanding, and potentially slowing, age-related decline. Social gerontology examines the social relations, institutions and policies surrounding old age, including retirement, caregiving, ageism and the demography of ageing populations. Psychogerontology focuses on cognition, memory and mental health in later life.

Significance

Population ageing is one of the major demographic trends worldwide: the number of people aged 65 and over is projected to more than double by mid-century. Gerontology informs public policy on pensions, health and long-term care, age-friendly environments, and the preservation of function and quality of life into advanced age, including among centenarians and supercentenarians.
