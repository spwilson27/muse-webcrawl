Twin paradox

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of the twins' worldlines in spacetime: one straight, one bent at the turnaround point]
The travelling twin's bent worldline accumulates less proper time.

In physics, the twin paradox is a thought experiment in special relativity involving twins, one of whom makes a journey into space in a high-speed rocket and returns home to find that the twin who remained on Earth has aged more. The result appears puzzling because each twin sees the other's clock as slowed by Time dilation, suggesting a contradiction — hence 'paradox'.

The resolution is that the situation is not symmetric: the travelling twin must accelerate (turn around), changing inertial frames, while the Earth twin remains in one frame. In spacetime terms, the twins traverse different worldlines between the same two events, and proper time — the length of a worldline — is path-dependent: the straight (inertial) path maximises it. The effect is real and has been measured, in miniature, with atomic clocks flown around the world (Hafele–Keating, 1971) and in the lifetimes of accelerated particles. Proposed by Paul Langevin in 1911, the paradox generated a paper industry for decades and remains relativity's most popular classroom puzzle.

Analysis

The travelling twin's turnaround does the work: at the moment of reversal, her surfaces of simultaneity swing, and in her reckoning the Earth twin's age jumps. Every consistent accounting — Doppler signals exchanged, spacetime geometry, accelerated frames — agrees: the traveller returns younger. In general relativity the same path-dependence applies with gravity, and GPS works precisely because engineers respect it.
