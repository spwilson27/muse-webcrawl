Constitution of Ireland

[Hero image placeholder: cover of the official printed text of Bunreacht na hÉireann]

The Constitution of Ireland (Irish: Bunreacht na hÉireann) is the fundamental law of Ireland. It asserts the national sovereignty of the Irish people and sets out the organs of government and fundamental rights. Drafted under the direction of Éamon de Valera, it was adopted by plebiscite on 1 July 1937 and came into operation on 29 December 1937, replacing the 1922 constitution of the Irish Free State.

Main features

The constitution establishes Ireland as a sovereign, independent, democratic state. It provides for a directly elected, largely ceremonial President as head of state; a bicameral parliament, the Oireachtas, consisting of Dáil Éireann and Seanad Éireann; a government headed by the Taoiseach; and an independent judiciary with power of judicial review of legislation. Articles 40–44 enumerate fundamental rights, including equality before the law, personal liberty, freedom of expression and of religion, and protection of property rights.

Amendments

The constitution can be amended only by referendum. Over forty amendment proposals have been put to the people. Major amendments include joining the European Communities (1972), removing the ban on divorce (1995), the Good Friday Agreement changes to Articles 2 and 3 on the territorial claim to Northern Ireland (1998), marriage equality (2015), and removal of the offences of blasphemy (2018) and the Eighth Amendment on abortion (2018).

Earlier versions contained provisions reflecting the social teaching of the Catholic Church, such as the ban on divorce (until 1995) and recognition of the church's "special position" (removed in 1972).

Language

The constitution is written in both Irish and English; in case of conflict, the Irish text prevails. Article 8 declares Irish the national and first official language.
