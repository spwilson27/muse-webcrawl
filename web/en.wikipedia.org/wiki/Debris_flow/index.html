Debris flow

A debris flow is a fast-moving form of mass wasting in which a saturated slurry of soil, rock fragments, organic material and water flows downhill under gravity. Debris flows behave like a wet concrete: they can carry boulders metres across, move at speeds from a few metres per second to more than 50 km/h, and travel long distances along channels before depositing their load as lobate fans of unsorted debris.

Formation and hazards

Debris flows typically start when intense rainfall, rapid snowmelt or a failed natural dam saturates loose slope material, often in steep catchments stripped of vegetation by wildfire. They are among the most destructive types of landslide: the 1999 Vargas disaster in Venezuela, in which debris flows and flash floods killed tens of thousands, and the 2018 Montecito mudflows in California, which followed wildfire and killed 23 people, are prominent examples. Volcanic debris flows of ash and water are called lahars and can be far larger still.

Mitigation

Defences include debris basins and check dams that trap sediment, deflection walls and channels, land-use zoning away from alluvial fans, and early-warning systems based on rainfall thresholds and channel sensors. Distinguishing debris flows from watery flash floods matters for engineering: their density — often twice that of water — gives them far greater impact forces for a given depth.
