Alliterative verse

Alliterative verse is a form of verse in which the metrical structure is organised not by rhyme or syllable count but by alliteration — the repetition of initial stressed sounds in words across each line. It was the traditional poetic form of the early Germanic languages, used in Old English, Old Norse, Old Saxon and Old High German epic and heroic poetry.

Structure

In the classical form, each line is divided by a caesura into two half-lines, each containing two stressed syllables (lifts). One or both stresses in the first half-line alliterate with the first stress of the second half-line; the final stress usually does not alliterate. Vowels alliterate with any other vowel. Beowulf, the greatest Old English poem, is written in this metre, as are The Battle of Maldon and the Norse Eddic poems.

Alliterative Revival

After the Norman Conquest the form declined in England but resurfaced in the 14th-century "Alliterative Revival", producing William Langland's Piers Plowman, the Gawain-poet's Sir Gawain and the Green Knight and Pearl, and the alliterative Morte Arthure. J. R. R. Tolkien wrote modern alliterative verse, including his version of the Sigurd legend, and W. H. Auden experimented with the form.
