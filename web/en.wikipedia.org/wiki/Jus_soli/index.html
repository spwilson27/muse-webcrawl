Jus soli

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jus soli (Latin for “right of the soil”) is the principle that a person's nationality is determined by their place of birth. A child born in a jus soli state is a citizen of that state regardless of the parents' nationality, subject in some systems to exceptions (children of foreign diplomats, and in a few states to additional conditions).

Unconditional jus soli is characteristic of the Americas: the United States (Fourteenth Amendment, 1868; United States v. Wong Kim Ark, 1898), Canada and most Latin American states grant citizenship by birth on the soil, a legacy of settler societies and of the common law's older allegiance-by-birth rule. Most European, African and Asian states historically followed Jus sanguinis (descent) instead, though many have added conditional jus soli (France and Germany among them: birth in the territory plus parental residence), so that the two principles now commonly operate in combination. The political salience of birthright citizenship has risen with debates over immigration, and changes to it have been proposed in the United States — where any change would require constitutional amendment or a new Supreme Court reading.
