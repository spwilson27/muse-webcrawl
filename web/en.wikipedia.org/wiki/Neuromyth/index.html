Neuromyth

Neuromyths are commonly held misconceptions about the brain and learning, often generated by a misunderstanding, misreading, or deliberate misquoting of scientifically established facts. The term was coined in the 1980s by neurosurgeon Alan Crockard and popularised by the OECD's 2002 report Understanding the Brain, which warned that oversimplified translations of neuroscience were circulating widely in education.

Common neuromyths

Frequently documented examples include the claims that humans use only 10 percent of their brains; that people are "left-brained" or "right-brained" learners; that children learn best when teaching matches their visual, auditory, or kinaesthetic "learning style"; that listening to classical music (the "Mozart effect") makes children more intelligent; that brain training games generalise to overall cognition; and that critical periods mean certain skills cannot be learned after a fixed early age. Surveys in several countries find that large proportions of teachers endorse several of these claims.

Origins

Neuromyths typically arise when genuine findings — such as hemispheric specialisation, sensitive periods, or the benefits of enriched environments in rats — are stripped of qualification and packaged into commercial programmes. Bridge disciplines between neuroscience and education ("neuroeducation") are both a remedy and, when loosely practised, a further source of myths.

Consequences and countermeasures

Belief in neuromyths can waste classroom time and school budgets on ineffective products. Researchers recommend improving neuroscience literacy in teacher training, direct collaboration between educators and neuroscientists, and critical appraisal of "brain-based" claims. Studies show that general knowledge about the brain reduces, but does not eliminate, susceptibility to neuromyths.
