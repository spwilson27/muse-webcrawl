Overview

Muammar Muhammad Abu Minyar al-Gaddafi (c. 1942–2011), commonly known as Colonel Gaddafi, was a Libyan revolutionary and politician who ruled Libya from the 1969 coup that deposed King Idris until his death in the 2011 Libyan civil war. His 42-year rule made him one of the longest-serving non-royal leaders of the modern era.

Ideology and rule

Gaddafi's "Third International Theory", set out in The Green Book (1975), proclaimed a direct-democracy "Jamahiriya" (state of the masses) that in practice was a personalist dictatorship ruling through revolutionary committees. Oil wealth funded free education, healthcare and the Great Man-Made River water project, alongside suppression of dissent, sponsorship of foreign armed groups, and confrontations with the West — including the 1986 US bombing and the Lockerbie bombing of 1988, for which Libya accepted responsibility in 2003 and paid compensation, after which Gaddafi dismantled his weapons programmes and was briefly rehabilitated diplomatically.

Downfall

In 2011, amid the Arab Spring, protests in Benghazi escalated into civil war; NATO intervened under a UN mandate. Gaddafi was captured and killed by militia in Sirte on 20 October 2011. Libya fractured into rival governments and militias in the years that followed.
