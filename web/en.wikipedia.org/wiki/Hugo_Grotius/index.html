Hugo Grotius (Dutch: [ˈɦyɣoː ˈɣroːtsijʏs]; 10 April 1583 – 28 August 1645), also known as Hugo de Groot, was a Dutch humanist, diplomat, lawyer, theologian, jurist, statesman, poet and playwright. A teenage intellectual prodigy, he was born in Delft and studied at Leiden University. He performed in a variety of political roles, including as an early champion of the Arminian cause, and was imprisoned for his participation in the intra-Calvinist disputes of the Dutch Republic, before escaping hidden in a chest of books.

Grotius is often regarded as the founder of international law, based on his influential works Mare Liberum (1609) and De jure belli ac pacis (1625).

Early life and career

Born into a patrician Delft family, Grotius entered Leiden University at eleven, published scholarly editions of classical authors in his teens, accompanied the statesman Johan van Oldenbarnevelt on a diplomatic mission to France in 1598, and was appointed historiographer of Holland. As advocate-fiscal and pensionary of Rotterdam (1613), he became a leading figure in the Remonstrant (Arminian) party in the conflict with the orthodox Calvinist Counter-Remonstrants led by Maurice of Nassau.

Imprisonment and escape

After Maurice's coup of 1618, Grotius was arrested alongside Oldenbarnevelt, tried for high treason, and in 1619 sentenced to life imprisonment at Loevestein Castle; Oldenbarnevelt was executed. In 1621, with the help of his wife Maria van Reigersberch and maidservant Elsje van Houwening, Grotius famously escaped concealed in a book chest that was regularly ferried to Gorinchem.

Exile and major works

Living mostly in Paris, Grotius wrote De jure belli ac pacis libri tres ("On the Law of War and Peace", 1625), a systematic treatise grounding the law of nations in natural law and reason — "even if we should concede that there is no God" (etsi Deus non daretur). His earlier Mare Liberum ("The Free Sea", 1609), extracted from De Indis, argued that the sea is international territory open to all nations — a doctrine written to justify Dutch trade in the East Indies and long contested by English "closed sea" claims. He also wrote biblical commentaries, the theological treatise De veritate religionis Christianae, histories, tragedies and Latin poetry. From 1634 he served as Sweden's ambassador to France for Chancellor Axel Oxenstierna.

Death and legacy

Recalled by Sweden in 1645, Grotius died at Rostock after a shipwreck on the return voyage. His natural-law framework influenced Pufendorf, Locke and the Enlightenment, and the Peace Palace library at The Hague holds one of the world's largest Grotius collections.
