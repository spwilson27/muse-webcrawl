Tendinopathy

SpecialtyRheumatology, sports medicine, orthopedics
SymptomsPain, swelling and impaired function of a tendon

Tendinopathy is a clinical syndrome describing pain, swelling and impaired performance in and around a tendon, typically caused by overuse. It is a broad umbrella term encompassing both tendinitis (associated with inflammation) and tendinosis (degenerative change without prominent inflammation). Histological studies of chronic cases usually show a failed healing response: disorganised collagen fibres, increased ground substance, proliferation of tenocytes and ingrowth of small blood vessels and nerves, with few inflammatory cells. The term "tendinopathy" is therefore preferred in clinical practice.

Common sites

  • Achilles tendon (Achilles tendinopathy)
  • Patellar tendon ("jumper's knee")
  • Rotator cuff tendons of the shoulder
  • Common extensor origin at the elbow ("tennis elbow", lateral epicondylalgia)
  • Common flexor origin ("golfer's elbow")
  • Gluteal tendons and hamstring origin

Causes and risk factors

Tendinopathy usually results when mechanical loading exceeds the tendon's capacity to adapt, for example through sudden increases in training volume or intensity. Risk factors include age, repetitive occupational or sporting activity, metabolic conditions such as diabetes and dyslipidaemia, and certain medications, notably fluoroquinolone antibiotics and corticosteroids. The influential continuum model of Cook and Purdam describes tendon pathology progressing through reactive tendinopathy, tendon disrepair, and degenerative tendinopathy stages.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is mainly clinical, based on localised tendon pain related to loading. Ultrasound and MRI may show tendon thickening, disorganised structure, calcification or partial tears, but imaging findings correlate imperfectly with symptoms and are also common in pain-free people.

Treatment

First-line management is load modification and progressive tendon-loading exercise programmes, including eccentric exercise and heavy slow resistance training, usually over at least 12 weeks. Adjuncts with varying evidence include shockwave therapy, topical glyceryl trinitrate, and injections; corticosteroid injections give short-term relief but worse long-term outcomes in several conditions. Surgery is reserved for cases failing prolonged conservative care.
