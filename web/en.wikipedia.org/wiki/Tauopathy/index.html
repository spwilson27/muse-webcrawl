Tauopathy

[Image: Neurofibrillary tangles, the hallmark lesion of tauopathies]

A tauopathy is a class of neurodegenerative diseases involving the aggregation of abnormal tau protein into neurofibrillary tangles in the human brain. Tau is a microtubule-associated protein, encoded by the MAPT gene on chromosome 17, that normally stabilises microtubules in neurons. In tauopathies, tau becomes abnormally hyperphosphorylated, detaches from microtubules, and assembles into insoluble filaments that disrupt cell function.

Classification

Tauopathies are often divided into primary tauopathies, where tau aggregation is the dominant pathology, and secondary tauopathies, where it accompanies other protein deposits. Tau isoforms contain either three (3R) or four (4R) microtubule-binding repeats, and diseases show characteristic 3R, 4R, or mixed profiles.

Examples

The best-known tauopathy is Alzheimer's disease, in which tau tangles spread through the cortex in the stereotyped Braak pattern alongside amyloid-beta plaques. Primary tauopathies include progressive supranuclear palsy, corticobasal degeneration, Pick's disease, globular glial tauopathy, and chronic traumatic encephalopathy, associated with repeated head injury in athletes and military personnel. Mutations in MAPT cause frontotemporal dementia with parkinsonism (FTDP-17), proving that tau dysfunction alone can drive neurodegeneration.

Research

Tau pathology can be visualised in living patients with tau PET tracers, and blood and spinal-fluid phosphorylated-tau biomarkers are increasingly used for diagnosis. Therapeutic strategies under investigation include anti-tau antibodies, antisense oligonucleotides that lower tau production, and inhibitors of tau aggregation and spread.
