James Fenimore Cooper

BornSeptember 15, 1789, Burlington, New Jersey, United States
DiedSeptember 14, 1851 (aged 61), Cooperstown, New York, United States
OccupationNovelist, naval officer, writer

James Fenimore Cooper (September 15, 1789 – September 14, 1851) was an American writer of the first half of the 19th century, whose historical romances depicting colonial and Native American life created a unique form of American literature. He lived much of his boyhood and his last years in Cooperstown, New York, on the shore of Otsego Lake, a settlement founded by his father, William Cooper.

Life

Cooper was born in Burlington, New Jersey, and moved as an infant to the frontier settlement his father established at the head of the Susquehanna River. He attended Yale University but was expelled, then served as a midshipman in the United States Navy. After his father's death he inherited a fortune, married Susan De Lancey, and settled as a gentleman farmer. According to family tradition, he began writing after boasting that he could write a better novel than the English one his wife was reading; his first novel, Precaution (1820), was followed by the successful The Spy (1821).

Works

Cooper is best known for the Leatherstocking Tales, five novels featuring the frontiersman Natty Bumppo: The Pioneers (1823), The Last of the Mohicans (1826), The Prairie (1827), The Pathfinder (1840), and The Deerslayer (1841). The Last of the Mohicans, set during the French and Indian War, remains his most widely read work. He also wrote sea novels such as The Pilot (1824), political and social commentary including The American Democrat (1838), and a history of the United States Navy.

Legacy

Cooper was among the first American writers to earn international fame and to make a living from writing. His portrayal of the frontier and Native Americans shaped the mythology of the American West, though later critics, most famously Mark Twain in "Fenimore Cooper's Literary Offenses" (1895), mocked his style and plotting. His home, Otsego Hall in Cooperstown, burned during his lifetime; he is buried in the Cooper family plot at Christ Episcopal Church in the village.
