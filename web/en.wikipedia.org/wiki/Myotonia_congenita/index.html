Myotonia congenita

The "fainting" goat stiffens and topples over when startled, a veterinary analogue of the condition.

Myotonia congenita is a genetic disorder of skeletal muscle in which relaxation after voluntary contraction is delayed, causing transient stiffness. It is caused by mutations in the CLCN1 gene, which encodes the main chloride channel (ClC-1) of skeletal muscle; loss of chloride conductance makes the muscle membrane hyperexcitable, so fibres fire repeatedly after a contraction. Two forms are recognised: the autosomal dominant Thomsen disease, described by the Danish physician Julius Thomsen (who had the condition himself) in 1876, and the later-onset, often more severe recessive Becker disease.

Symptoms and signs

Stiffness is worst after rest and on initiating movement — rising from a chair, starting to run, gripping a handle and being unable to let go — and characteristically improves with repeated activity, the "warm-up phenomenon". Symptoms are aggravated by cold, stress and pregnancy, and many patients develop striking muscle hypertrophy giving an athletic build. Sudden startling stimuli can cause a fall with generalised stiffness, without loss of consciousness. The disorder is a channelopathy, not a muscular dystrophy: strength is normal or near-normal between episodes, though the Becker form may bring mild weakness.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on the clinical picture, electromyography showing characteristic "dive-bomber" myotonic discharges, and genetic testing of CLCN1. Many people need no treatment beyond avoiding triggers. Where stiffness is disabling, the sodium-channel blocker mexiletine is the usual choice, supported by trial evidence; other options include lamotrigine, carbamazepine and acetazolamide. The condition is also famous in animals: the myotonic ("fainting") goat stiffens and falls over when startled. See also channelopathy.
