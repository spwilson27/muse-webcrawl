Chester Carlson

Chester Floyd Carlson (February 8, 1906 – September 19, 1968) was an American physicist, inventor and patent attorney who invented xerography, the dry photocopying process that led to the modern photocopier and laser printer. Working largely alone and with his own money, Carlson persisted for years in the face of rejection by more than twenty companies before his invention was commercialised.

Early life

Carlson was born in Seattle and grew up in poverty in California, supporting his invalid parents from a young age. He studied physics at the California Institute of Technology, graduating in 1930, and later earned a law degree while working in the patent department of the electronics firm P. R. Mallory & Co. in New York. Frustrated by the laborious copying of patent documents, he began experimenting in his kitchen with photoconductivity.

The invention

On October 22, 1938, in a rented room in Astoria, Queens, Carlson and his assistant Otto Kornei produced the first xerographic image: the words "10.-22.-38 ASTORIA" written on a microscope slide, transferred using a sulphur-coated zinc plate, static charge and lycopodium powder. Carlson called the process "electrophotography"; it was later renamed xerography, from the Greek for "dry writing".

Commercialisation

After years of rejection, the Battelle Memorial Institute funded development in 1944, and in 1947 the small Haloid Company of Rochester, New York, licensed the patents. Haloid — renamed Xerox Corporation — launched the Xerox 914 plain-paper copier in 1959, one of the most successful products in American business history. Carlson became wealthy and gave away much of his fortune, an estimated $150 million, to charity.
