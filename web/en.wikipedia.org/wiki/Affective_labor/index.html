Affective labor

Affective labor
Related conceptsEmotional labor, immaterial labor, care work
Key theoristsMichael Hardt, Antonio Negri, Arlie Russell Hochschild

Affective labor is work carried out to produce or modify the emotional experiences of people. It is a category of immaterial labor developed by theorists of autonomist Marxism, notably Michael Hardt and Antonio Negri, to describe labor whose products are intangible: a feeling of ease, excitement, well-being, satisfaction, or passion, and social states such as a sense of connectedness or community.

Definition and scope

Affective labor includes the work of care — in health care, childcare, eldercare, and domestic service — as well as work in entertainment, advertising, and customer service that produces moods and social bonds. Hardt distinguishes two forms: principally bodily care work, and labor that produces affects in virtual or communicative form, as in the entertainment industries. Because such labor produces social relationships and forms of life themselves, Hardt and Negri describe it as "biopolitical labor".

Relation to emotional labor

The term is related to, but distinct from, Arlie Russell Hochschild's concept of emotional labor, which focuses on the management of feeling to create a publicly observable facial and bodily display as part of a job, such as the smiling service of flight attendants. Feminist scholars have emphasized that affective labor is disproportionately performed by women, is often unpaid or underpaid in households, and is distributed along global "care chains" in which migrant workers care for families in wealthier countries while their own families are cared for by others.

Criticism

Critics argue that the category can blur important differences between paid employment and unpaid domestic work, and between emotional management required by employers and freely given care. Others note that the novelty claimed for affective labor underestimates how central such work has always been to economies.
