Amalfi

Amalfi (Italian: [aˈmalfi]) is a town and comune in the province of Salerno, in the region of Campania, Italy, on the Gulf of Salerno. It lies at the mouth of a deep ravine, at the foot of Monte Cerreto (1,315 metres, 4,314 feet), surrounded by dramatic cliffs and coastal scenery. The town gives its name to the Amalfi Coast, a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of Italy's most famous tourist destinations.

History

Traditionally founded as a Roman trading post, Amalfi grew into a powerful maritime republic. From the 9th to the 12th century it rivalled Pisa, Genoa, and Venice as a trading power in the Mediterranean, and its merchants maintained fondachi (trading colonies) across the Byzantine and Muslim worlds. The Amalfitan Tables (Tabula Amalphitana), a maritime law code compiled in Amalfi, were recognised throughout the Mediterranean for centuries. Amalfitans founded the hospital in Jerusalem from which the Knights Hospitaller (Order of St John) developed.

The republic declined after being sacked by the Pisans in 1135 and devastated by a tsunami following an earthquake in 1343, which destroyed much of the port. Thereafter Amalfi was eclipsed, becoming part of the Kingdom of Naples and later of unified Italy.

Sights

The town's central monument is Amalfi Cathedral (Duomo), dedicated to Saint Andrew, whose relics are kept in its crypt. The mainly Romanesque cathedral, with its striking Arab-Norman cloister (the Chiostro del Paradiso, 1266–1268) and a later Baroque interior and neo-Byzantine façade, is reached by a broad staircase from the Piazza del Duomo. Other attractions include the Museo della Carta (paper museum) in a medieval paper mill — Amalfi was an early centre of papermaking in Europe — and the annual Regatta of the Ancient Maritime Republics, contested with Genoa, Pisa, and Venice. Amalfi is also famous for its lemons and limoncello.
