Efficiency wage

The efficiency wage hypothesis in labour economics holds that paying workers above the market-clearing wage can increase their productivity, so that it may be profitable for firms to keep wages high even in the presence of unemployment. The term was associated with Alfred Marshall's notion of "efficiency earnings" and was formalised by economists including Harvey Leibenstein (who applied it to nutrition in developing countries) and, in modern form, by Joseph Stiglitz, Janet Yellen and George Akerlof.

Mechanisms

  • Shirking model (Shapiro–Stiglitz): where monitoring is imperfect, a higher wage raises the cost of being fired, discouraging shirking; the resulting equilibrium involves involuntary unemployment acting as a "worker discipline device".
  • Labour turnover: higher wages reduce quits, cutting hiring and training costs.
  • Adverse selection: higher wages attract a better-quality pool of applicants.
  • Sociological/gift-exchange: workers repay generous wages with greater effort and loyalty (Akerlof's gift-exchange model); fairness perceptions matter.
  • Nutritional: in poor economies, higher wages improve workers' health and strength, directly raising productivity.

Implications

Efficiency wages explain why wages differ between similar workers in different industries (inter-industry wage differentials), why markets may not clear, and provide a microfoundation for involuntary unemployment in macroeconomic models. Henry Ford's famous "Five-Dollar Day" of 1914 is often cited as a real-world example.
