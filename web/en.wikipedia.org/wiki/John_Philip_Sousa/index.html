John Philip Sousa (6 November 1854 – 6 March 1932) was an American composer and conductor of the late Romantic era, known primarily for American military marches. He is commonly called "The March King" or "The American March King", having composed 136 marches, some of which remain standards of the repertoire.

Career

Sousa, the son of a trombonist in the United States Marine Band, was apprenticed to that band as a boy. He became its leader in 1880, serving until 1892 and raising it to prominence as the nation's premier military ensemble. He then formed his own civilian Sousa Band, which toured widely in the United States and Europe and made some of the earliest successful phonograph recordings.

Works

His best-known marches include "Semper Fidelis" (1888), the official march of the U.S. Marine Corps; "The Washington Post" (1889); "The Liberty Bell" (1893); and "The Stars and Stripes Forever" (1896), designated the national march of the United States. Sousa also composed operettas, suites and songs, and championed the development of the sousaphone, a large bass tuba variant named for him. He wrote the autobiography Marching Along (1928) and campaigned for composers' royalties and music education.
