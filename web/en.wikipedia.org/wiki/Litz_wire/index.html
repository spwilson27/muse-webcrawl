Litz wire

Litz wire is a type of multistrand wire (or cable) used in electronics to carry alternating current (AC) at radio frequencies. The wire is designed to reduce the skin effect and proximity effect losses in conductors used at frequencies up to about 1 MHz. It consists of many thin wire strands, individually insulated and twisted or woven together, following one of several carefully prescribed patterns often involving several levels (twisted groups are twisted together, and so on).

The term litz comes from the German Litzendraht, meaning braided, stranded, or woven wire.

Litz wire with multiple twisted bundles

Principle of operation

At high frequencies, current tends to flow near the surface of a conductor — the skin effect — so the effective resistance of a solid wire rises with frequency because much of its cross-section carries little current. In litz wire each strand is thinner than the skin depth at the operating frequency, so current uses nearly the whole cross-section. The twisting pattern ensures that each strand occupies, on average, every position in the bundle for an equal fraction of its length, so that magnetic flux linkages — and therefore current distribution — are equalised among the strands and the proximity effect is also reduced.

Construction

Strands are insulated with enamel or served (wrapped) with silk, nylon, or other textile. Constructions range from a few strands for small inductors to thousands of fine strands in high-power applications. Bundles may be twisted in multiple levels and sometimes arranged in rectangular profiles for better space filling in transformer windings.

Applications

  • High-Q inductors and radio-frequency coils, including the ferrite-rod and frame aerials of AM radio receivers
  • High-frequency transformers and switching power supplies
  • Induction heating and induction cooking equipment
  • Wireless power transfer systems and electric-vehicle charging coils
  • Ultrasonic transducers and medical equipment

Above roughly 1–2 MHz the skin depth becomes so small that the required strand diameter becomes impractical, and solid or tubular conductors, or silver-plated surfaces, are used instead. Litz wire is more expensive than solid wire of the same cross-section, so it is used only where the reduction in AC resistance justifies the cost.
