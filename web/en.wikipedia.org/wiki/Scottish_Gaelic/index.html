Scottish Gaelic (Gàidhlig) is a Goidelic language native to the Gaels of Scotland. Closely related to Irish and Manx, it developed from Middle Irish and was once spoken across most of Scotland.

History

Gaelic arrived via the kingdom of Dál Riata in Argyll. By the 11th–12th centuries it dominated Scotland, giving the country its name in Gaelic, Alba. From the late Middle Ages it retreated to the Highlands and Islands (the Gàidhealtachd) under pressure from Scots and later English, through the Statutes of Iona (1609), education policies, and the Highland Clearances.

Status today

The 2022 census recorded about 69,701 speakers and 130,161 people with some Gaelic skills. The Gaelic Language (Scotland) Act 2005 gave it official status, and Bòrd na Gàidhlig promotes it. Gaelic-medium education, BBC Alba television, and signage support revitalization, though UNESCO classifies the language as endangered. Its strongest communities are in the Outer Hebrides.

Features

Gaelic uses an 18-letter alphabet, initial consonant mutations (lenition), a verb–subject–object word order, and broad/slender consonant contrasts. Dialects include those of Lewis, Skye, and Islay.
