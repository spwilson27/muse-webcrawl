Atlantic slave trade

The Atlantic slave trade, or transatlantic slave trade, involved the transportation by slave traders of enslaved African people, mainly to the Americas, between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries. An estimated twelve million or more Africans were shipped across the Atlantic, with many dying in transit during the brutal Middle Passage; millions more died in the wars and marches that fed the trade.

Course of the trade

Portuguese traders began purchasing captives on the West African coast in the fifteenth century, and the trade grew massively with the plantation economies of Brazil, the Caribbean and North America. On the coast of the Bight of Benin — the "Slave Coast" — kingdoms such as Dahomey sold war captives at ports like Whydah in exchange for firearms, textiles and cowrie shells. The trade was abolished by Britain and the United States in 1807–08, and Brazil, the last major destination, ended it in the 1850s.

Legacy

The trade profoundly shaped the demographics, economies and cultures of both Africa and the Americas, and its memory is central to the history of the African diaspora.
