Military–industrial complex

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

President Eisenhower, who popularised the term in his 1961 farewell address.

The military–industrial complex (MIC) describes the relationship between a country's military and the defence industry that supplies it, seen together as a vested interest which influences public policy. The term is used to describe the close working relationship between politicians, the armed forces, and the suppliers of military hardware and services, and the concern that this network may drive arms races, inflate defence budgets, or push governments toward military conflict.

Origin of the term

The phrase was popularised by U.S. President Dwight D. Eisenhower in his farewell address of 17 January 1961. Eisenhower, himself a retired five-star general, warned that "we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex", cautioning that the "potential for the disastrous rise of misplaced power exists and will persist." An earlier draft of the speech reportedly used the phrase "military-industrial-congressional complex", emphasising the role of elected legislators.

Concept and criticism

Critics of the concept argue that defence contractors benefit financially from large weapons programmes and armed conflict, that a "revolving door" moves officials between the Pentagon, Congress, and industry, and that contractors deliberately spread subcontracts across many congressional districts to make programmes politically difficult to cancel. Similar dynamics have been identified in other countries with large arms industries, including Russia, France, and China.

Defenders of close military–industrial cooperation counter that a strong domestic defence industry is necessary for national security, technological innovation, and employment, and that complex modern weapons can only be produced through sustained partnership between the state and private firms. Many technologies with civilian applications — including jet engines, satellite navigation, and the early Internet — emerged from military-industrial research.

Related concepts

Related terms include the "iron triangle" of congressional committees, executive agencies, and interest groups; the military–industrial–media complex; and Eisenhower's own countervailing ideal of a balance between military strength and civilian prosperity. The phrase has entered general political vocabulary worldwide, often used more loosely to criticise any entrenched network of defence interests.
