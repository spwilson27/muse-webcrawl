Cosmic censorship hypothesis

[Image placeholder: event horizon diagram]

The cosmic censorship hypothesis is a conjecture in general relativity, proposed by Roger Penrose in 1969, which asserts that singularities arising from gravitational collapse are always hidden inside event horizons, so that they cannot be observed from the rest of spacetime. A singularity visible to distant observers — a "naked singularity" — would expose regions where the classical theory breaks down and would threaten the predictability of physics; cosmic censorship holds that nature forbids this.

Weak and strong forms

The hypothesis is usually stated in two versions. The weak cosmic censorship conjecture says that singularities formed in realistic collapse are invisible to observers at infinity, being concealed within black holes. The strong cosmic censorship conjecture says that spacetime is globally predictable (globally hyperbolic) for generic initial data, so that no observer, even one who falls in, can receive signals from a singularity in a way that destroys determinism.

Neither version has been proved in full generality. Simplified models and numerical simulations have produced apparent counterexamples that on closer inspection involve non-generic or unstable configurations; in 2017–2018 mathematicians showed that near the inner (Cauchy) horizon of a charged black hole in an expanding universe, the strong form can fail in its classical formulation, though a reformulated version may survive. The question remains one of the major open problems in mathematical relativity, and is related to the Millennium Prize problem concerning the global regularity of solutions of the Einstein equations.
