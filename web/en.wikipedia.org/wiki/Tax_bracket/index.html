Tax bracket

A tax bracket is a range of incomes subject to a particular income tax rate in a progressive tax system. In such systems, income is divided into slices, and each slice is taxed at its own rate; only the income falling within a bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate. As a result, a person's marginal tax rate — the rate on their last dollar of income — is generally higher than their effective tax rate, the average rate across all their income.

How brackets work

Suppose a tax system has brackets of 10% on income up to $10,000, 20% on income from $10,001 to $40,000, and 30% above $40,000. A taxpayer earning $50,000 pays 10% on the first $10,000 ($1,000), 20% on the next $30,000 ($6,000), and 30% on the remaining $10,000 ($3,000), for a total of $10,000 — an effective rate of 20%, even though the taxpayer is "in" the 30% bracket. A common misconception is that moving into a higher bracket taxes all income at the higher rate; in standard bracket systems this is not the case.

United States federal brackets

The United States federal income tax has seven statutory rates — 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, and 37% — following the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017. Bracket thresholds vary by filing status (single, married filing jointly, head of household, etc.) and are adjusted annually for inflation by the Internal Revenue Service. Separate, lower rates generally apply to long-term capital gains and qualified dividends.

Marginal versus effective rates

The marginal rate is relevant for decisions about earning additional income, while the effective rate measures the overall share of income paid in tax. Deductions and credits can lower taxable income and thus the effective rate independently of the bracket structure.
