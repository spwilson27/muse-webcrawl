Art Deco

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Chrysler Building in New York, its stainless-steel terraced crown and sunburst spire glowing at dusk]

Art Deco is a style of visual arts, architecture and product design that first appeared in France in the 1910s and flourished internationally in the 1920s and 1930s. It took its name from the 1925 Paris Exposition internationale des arts décoratifs et industriels modernes. Art Deco combined modern styles with fine craftsmanship and rich materials, favouring bold geometric shapes, symmetry, streamlined forms and lavish ornament in stylised sunbursts, zigzags and chevrons.

Its architecture includes New York's Chrysler Building (1930) and Empire State Building (1931), while its design language covered jewellery, furniture, ocean-liner interiors, posters, fashion and automobiles. Leading designers included Émile-Jacques Ruhlmann, Tamara de Lempicka in painting, and René Lalique in glass.

Decline and revival

Art Deco fell from favour after the Second World War, displaced by the austerity of International Style modernism, but was revived by exhibitions and scholarship from the 1960s onward and remains one of the most recognisable decorative styles of the twentieth century.
