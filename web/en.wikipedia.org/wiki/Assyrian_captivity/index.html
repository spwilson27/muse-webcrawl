The Assyrian captivity (or Assyrian exile) is the period in the history of ancient Israel following the deportation of the population of the northern kingdom of Israel by the Neo-Assyrian Empire in the 8th century BC. It is distinct from the later Babylonian captivity of the southern kingdom of Judah.

Background and events

Assyrian campaigns against Israel began under Tiglath-Pileser III, who annexed Galilee and Gilead in 734–732 BC and deported many inhabitants (2 Kings 15:29). After the Israelite king Hoshea withheld tribute, Shalmaneser V besieged Samaria; the city fell in 722/721 BC, and Sargon II claimed credit for deporting 27,290 inhabitants, resettling them in Mesopotamia and Media and importing foreign settlers into Samaria. The Bible (2 Kings 17) interprets the fall as divine punishment for idolatry.

Consequences and legacy

The deported Israelites largely disappear from the historical record, giving rise to the tradition of the "Ten Lost Tribes of Israel," which has inspired speculation and claimed identifications across the world for over two millennia. The settlers brought into Samaria intermingled with the remaining population, a background to the later hostility between Jews and Samaritans. Unlike the Babylonian exile, the Assyrian captivity produced no recorded return.
