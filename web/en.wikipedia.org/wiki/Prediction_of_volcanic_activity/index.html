Prediction of volcanic activity

The prediction of volcanic activity, or volcanic eruption forecasting, is the interdisciplinary study of monitoring a volcano to predict the timing and severity of its future eruptions. Unlike earthquakes, volcanic eruptions are usually preceded by detectable signs that magma is moving beneath the volcano, so forecasts with useful lead times of hours to months are often possible.

Monitoring methods

Volcanologists combine several techniques: seismometers detect earthquakes and harmonic tremor caused by moving magma and gas; GPS receivers, tiltmeters, InSAR satellite radar and other geodesy methods measure ground deformation as the volcano inflates; gas sensors measure changes in emissions of sulfur dioxide, carbon dioxide and other gases, whose ratios can signal fresh magma; and thermal cameras, webcams and satellite sensors track heat output, dome growth and ash plumes. Patterns in these signals — accelerating earthquake rates, for example — are fitted to models such as the material failure forecast method to estimate eruption times.

Successes and limitations

Notable forecasting successes include the 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines, where escalating monitoring led to evacuations credited with saving thousands of lives and salvaging hundreds of millions of dollars of equipment, and the well-monitored 1980 reawakening of Mount St. Helens. Failures and false alarms — such as the 1982 evacuation of Guadeloupe's La Soufrière in 1976 and unexpected explosions at Mount Ontake in 2014 — show the difficulty of phreatic eruptions and the social cost of false positives. Forecast products include alert-level systems (such as the USGS colour codes) and probabilistic event trees. See also volcano and Mount Pinatubo.
