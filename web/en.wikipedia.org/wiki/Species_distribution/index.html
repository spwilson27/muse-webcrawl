Species distribution

Species distribution, or species dispersion, is the manner in which a biological taxon is spatially arranged. The geographic limits of a species' distribution constitute its range, often represented on maps as shaded areas, occurrence dots, or outline boundaries. Patterns of distribution change through time as species disperse, adapt, are extirpated, or respond to environmental change.

Patterns

Biogeographers describe several characteristic distribution types:

  • Cosmopolitan — found nearly worldwide wherever suitable habitat occurs (e.g., the peregrine falcon).
  • Endemic — restricted to a particular geographic area, such as an island or mountain range.
  • Disjunct — occurring in two or more widely separated areas with a gap between them.
  • Continuous versus patchy (mosaic) distributions across a region.

Determinants

A species' distribution reflects the interaction of its ecological niche — climate tolerance, resource requirements, and interactions with competitors, predators, and mutualists — with historical factors such as continental drift, glaciation, dispersal barriers, and human introduction. At small scales, individuals may be arranged uniformly, randomly, or in clumps, a statistical pattern also called dispersion.

Modelling

Species distribution models (SDMs, also called ecological niche models or habitat suitability models) relate occurrence records to environmental variables, increasingly through machine-learning methods implemented in GIS, to predict where a species can occur. They are widely used in conservation planning, invasive-species management, and forecasting the effects of climate change on biodiversity.
