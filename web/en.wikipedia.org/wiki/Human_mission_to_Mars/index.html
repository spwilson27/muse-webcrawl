Human mission to Mars

The human mission to Mars is a long-standing goal of spaceflight, proposed since the dawn of the space age as the next major step after the Moon landings. No crewed mission has yet been attempted. Robotic exploration has proceeded since the 1960s, and agencies and private companies continue to develop the technologies needed for a crewed voyage.

Challenges

A round trip takes roughly two to three years: about seven months in transit each way under typical conjunction-class mission plans, plus a long surface stay while the planets realign. Crews would face galactic cosmic radiation and solar particle events beyond the protection of Earth's magnetosphere, prolonged weightlessness and its effects on bone, muscle, vision and the immune system, psychological strain from isolation and communication delays of up to about 22 minutes one way, and the need to land heavy payloads through Mars's thin atmosphere. Life support must be largely closed-loop, and most plans rely on in-situ resource utilisation — for example producing methane propellant or oxygen from the Martian atmosphere and water ice.

Proposals

Wernher von Braun outlined a Mars expedition in The Mars Project (1952); NASA studied designs repeatedly, including the post-Apollo plans and the 1989 Space Exploration Initiative. In the twenty-first century, NASA's Moon-to-Mars strategy treats its Artemis lunar programme as a proving ground, with crewed Mars missions notionally in the late 2030s or 2040s. SpaceX is developing the fully reusable Starship system with the stated goal of enabling Mars settlement, and China has announced long-term ambitions for crewed Mars missions. Analogue research stations such as Mars-500 and the Mars Society's desert and Arctic stations have simulated aspects of surface operations and isolation.
