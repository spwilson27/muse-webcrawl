Common-ion effect

Adding a common ion shifts the dissolution equilibrium, decreasing solubility.

The common-ion effect refers to the decrease in the solubility of an ionic precipitate or weak electrolyte when a soluble compound containing one of the ions already present in the equilibrium is added to the solution. It is a direct consequence of Le Chatelier's principle: increasing the concentration of a product ion drives the equilibrium back toward the reactants.

Solubility effects

Consider the dissolution equilibrium of silver chloride:

AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag+(aq) + Cl−(aq)

If sodium chloride is added to a saturated solution of silver chloride, the extra chloride ions constitute a common ion. The equilibrium shifts left, more AgCl precipitates, and the concentration of dissolved silver ions falls. Quantitatively, the effect is described by the solubility product constant, Ksp: the solubility of the salt in the presence of a common ion at concentration c is approximately Ksp/c when c is much larger than the solubility itself.

Weak electrolytes and buffers

The same principle explains the behaviour of buffer solutions. Adding sodium acetate to a solution of acetic acid suppresses the acid's dissociation because acetate is a common ion, reducing the hydrogen-ion concentration and raising the pH relative to the pure acid. Conversely, adding a strong acid to a salt solution can increase the solubility of slightly soluble salts whose anion is a weak-acid conjugate base, the reverse phenomenon.

Applications

The common-ion effect is exploited in qualitative inorganic analysis, where group reagents precipitate selected metal ions; in gravimetric analysis, where a slight excess of precipitant ensures complete precipitation; and in industrial processes such as soap manufacture, where sodium chloride is used to salt out soap from solution.
