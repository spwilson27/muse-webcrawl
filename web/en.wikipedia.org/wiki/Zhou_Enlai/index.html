Zhou Enlai

Zhou Enlai (Chinese: 周恩来; pinyin: Zhōu Ēnlái; 5 March 1898 – 8 January 1976) was a Chinese statesman and diplomat who served as the first Premier of the People's Republic of China from its founding in 1949 until his death in 1976. Serving under Chairman Mao Zedong, Zhou was China's head of government for more than a quarter of a century and played a central role in the Chinese Communist Party's rise to power and in shaping the foreign policy of the new state.

Early life and revolutionary career

Zhou was born in Huai'an, Jiangsu, and educated in Tianjin, where he became involved in the May Fourth Movement. He studied and organised communist groups in France in the early 1920s under the work-study programme, becoming a leading figure among Chinese communists in Europe. After returning to China he served as political commissar of the Whampoa Military Academy and took part in the Northern Expedition. He survived the 1927 Shanghai massacre, helped organise the Nanchang Uprising, and became a key organiser of the Red Army and a senior leader during the Long March, supporting Mao's ascendancy at the Zunyi Conference.

War years and diplomacy

During the Second United Front, Zhou served as the Communist Party's chief liaison with the Nationalist government in Chongqing and negotiated with foreign representatives. He played a major role in the Xi'an Incident settlement and in the negotiations that followed the Second World War. As foreign minister (1949–1958) as well as premier, he represented China at the 1954 Geneva Conference and the 1955 Bandung Conference, where he promoted the Five Principles of Peaceful Coexistence. In the early 1970s he worked with Mao to orchestrate the rapprochement with the United States, meeting Henry Kissinger and President Richard Nixon during the latter's 1972 visit.

Later years and legacy

During the Cultural Revolution Zhou worked to moderate excesses and protect officials and cultural figures where he could, though his position was politically precarious. Diagnosed with cancer, he died in January 1976, months before Mao. Public mourning for Zhou contributed to the Tiananmen Incident of April 1976. He is widely remembered in China as a skilled, pragmatic administrator and diplomat, and his role in moderating radical policies remains a subject of historical study.
