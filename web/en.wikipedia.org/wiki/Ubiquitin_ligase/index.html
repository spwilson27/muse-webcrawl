Ubiquitin ligase

Ubiquitin ligase (E3)
FunctionTransfers ubiquitin from an E2 conjugating enzyme to a substrate protein
Main familiesRING, HECT, RBR
Number in humans~600–700 E3 enzymes

A ubiquitin ligase (also called an E3 ubiquitin ligase) is a protein that recruits an E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme that has been loaded with ubiquitin, recognizes a target substrate, and transfers the ubiquitin — typically to a lysine residue on the substrate — in a process called ubiquitination (or ubiquitylation). The ubiquitin may be attached singly (monoubiquitination) or as chains of different linkages, which serve as signals for different outcomes, most famously targeting the substrate for degradation by the proteasome.

Mechanism and families

E3 ligases fall into three main families defined by their mechanism. RING (Really Interesting New Gene) ligases, the largest family, act as scaffolds that bring the E2~ubiquitin conjugate and the substrate together so ubiquitin passes directly from E2 to substrate; many function as multi-subunit complexes such as the Cullin-RING ligases. HECT ligases first accept ubiquitin onto their own catalytic cysteine, forming a thioester intermediate, before transferring it to the substrate. RBR (RING-between-RING) ligases combine features of both mechanisms.

Biological importance

Ubiquitin ligases confer substrate specificity on the ubiquitin–proteasome system, which regulates the cell cycle, DNA repair, immune signaling, and protein quality control. Well-studied examples include MDM2, which targets the tumor suppressor p53; the anaphase-promoting complex (APC/C); SCF complexes; and parkin, mutations in which cause early-onset Parkinson's disease. Defects in E3 ligases are implicated in cancers, neurodegenerative disorders, and immune diseases.

Drug discovery

E3 ligases have become central to targeted protein degradation: PROTACs and molecular glues work by recruiting an E3 such as cereblon or von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) to a disease protein, forcing its ubiquitination and destruction.
