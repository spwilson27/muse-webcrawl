Aboriginal title in Canada

Aboriginal title in Canada is a legal doctrine recognizing that Indigenous peoples hold inherent, collective rights to their traditional lands by virtue of their prior occupation of those lands before the assertion of European sovereignty. The rights are recognized and affirmed by section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982, and have been developed through a series of landmark decisions of the Supreme Court of Canada.

History

The doctrine has roots in the Royal Proclamation of 1763, which reserved unceded lands for Indigenous nations and required that land be surrendered only to the Crown by treaty. For much of the 19th and 20th centuries Canadian courts gave the doctrine little effect, but the 1973 Calder case — brought by the Nisga'a — confirmed that Aboriginal title existed in Canadian law independent of the Royal Proclamation, splitting the Supreme Court on whether it had been extinguished in British Columbia.

Key jurisprudence

In Delgamuukw v British Columbia (1997), the Supreme Court held that Aboriginal title is a right to the land itself, encompassing exclusive use and occupation, and that oral histories of Indigenous peoples must be given independent weight as evidence. In Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia (2014), the Court issued the first declaration of Aboriginal title in Canadian history, granting the Tsilhqot'in Nation title to roughly 1,700 square kilometres in the British Columbia interior, and confirmed that title can be established through regular use of definite tracts of land by semi-nomadic peoples.

Test for proof

To prove Aboriginal title, a claimant group must show occupation of the land prior to the assertion of Crown sovereignty that was sufficient, continuous (where present occupation is relied on), and exclusive. Title confers the right to decide how the land is used and to enjoy its economic benefits, subject to an inherent limit: the land cannot be used in ways irreconcilable with the group's continuing attachment to it. The Crown may infringe title only with consent or, failing consent, where the infringement is justified by a compelling and substantial objective and is consistent with the Crown's fiduciary duty.
