Arrow poison

Arrow poisons are substances applied to the tips of arrows, darts and blowgun projectiles to increase their lethality in hunting and warfare. They have been used by indigenous peoples on every inhabited continent, drawing on toxic plants, frogs and other animals, and several arrow poisons — notably curare — later became important in medicine.


Poisoned projectiles used for hunting

Major types

In South America, hunters use curare, prepared from bark of Chondrodendron and Strychnos species, which paralyses skeletal muscle. In Africa, poisons were made from Strophanthus and Acokanthera (sources of cardiac glycosides such as ouabain), from the latex of Euphorbia, and from crushed beetle larvae. In Asia, aconite (monkshood) preparations were widely used, while poison dart frogs of the family Dendrobatidae provided batrachotoxin for blow darts in Colombia.

Mechanism and safety

Most arrow poisons act only when introduced into the bloodstream, not when eaten, so game killed with poisoned projectiles can usually be consumed safely once the flesh around the wound is removed. Curare's active principle, tubocurarine, blocks nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction, causing flaccid paralysis and death by respiratory failure.

Medical legacy

Curare derivatives were adopted in anaesthesia from the 1940s as muscle relaxants, allowing lighter anaesthesia during surgery. Cardiac glycosides from African arrow-poison plants are related to digoxin, long used in treating heart failure.
