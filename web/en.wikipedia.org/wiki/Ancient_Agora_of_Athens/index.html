Ancient Agora of Athens

LocationAthens, Greece — northwest of the Acropolis
TypeAgora (civic centre and marketplace)
Period6th century BCE – 6th century CE
Key buildingsHephaisteion, Stoa of Attalos, Odeon of Agrippa, Bouleuterion
ExcavationsAmerican School of Classical Studies, from 1931

The Ancient Agora of Athens (Greek: Ἀρχαία Ἀγορά) is the best-known example of an ancient Greek agora, located to the northwest of the Acropolis in Athens, Greece. For some 800 years it was the centre of the city's political, commercial, judicial, religious, and social life — the place where Athenian democracy was administered and where Socrates conversed and was later tried.

The Agora with the Temple of Hephaestus on the hill of Kolonos Agoraios.

History

The area was used as a burial ground in the Neolithic and Bronze Ages. In the 6th century BCE, under the Peisistratids and the reforms of Solon and Cleisthenes, it was laid out as the civic centre of the city. It was sacked by the Persians in 480 BCE, rebuilt in the classical period, enriched with temples and stoas under the Hellenistic kings, and again adorned by Roman benefactions such as the Odeon of Agrippa (c. 15 BCE). After the Herulian sack of 267 CE the centre of administration shifted; the site was finally abandoned after the Slavic invasion of the 6th century CE and covered by later housing.

Principal buildings

The Temple of Hephaestus (the Hephaisteion), on the western hill, is the best-preserved Doric temple in Greece. The Stoa of Attalos, a gift of King Attalos II of Pergamon, was reconstructed in the 1950s and now houses the Agora museum. Other remains include the Bouleuterion (council house), the Tholos (headquarters of the prytaneis), the Metroon, the lawcourts, the Mint, the Altar of the Twelve Gods — the zero point from which distances from Athens were measured — and the Panathenaic Way, which crossed the square diagonally to the Acropolis.

Excavations

Systematic excavation by the American School of Classical Studies at Athens began in 1931 and required the demolition of some 400 modern houses. Finds include ostraka used in ostracism votes — some bearing the name of Themistocles — bronze ballots, and official measures.
