Bengali cuisine

Bengali cuisine is the culinary style of Bengal, the region of eastern South Asia now divided between the Indian state of West Bengal and the nation of Bangladesh. Centred on rice and fish, it is known for its subtle use of spices, its distinctive use of mustard oil and mustard seed, the five-spice mixture panch phoron, and its celebrated sweets based on chhena (curdled milk).

Characteristics

A traditional Bengali meal is served in courses progressing through flavours — bitter, then leafy greens, vegetables, lentils, fish and meat, ending with chutney and sweets. The delta of the Ganges and Brahmaputra makes freshwater fish central: hilsa (ilish), rohu, and catla are prized, and the saying machhe bhate Bangali ("fish and rice make the Bengali") sums up the region's food identity. Mustard oil, poppy seeds (posto), green chillies, turmeric, and nigella seeds are characteristic seasonings.

Notable dishes

  • Shorshe ilish — hilsa steamed in a pungent mustard sauce.
  • Chingri malaikari — prawns in a coconut-milk curry.
  • Kosha mangsho — slow-cooked spicy mutton.
  • Luchi — deep-fried puffed white-flour bread, often served with alur dom (spiced potatoes).
  • Mishti doi — sweetened baked yogurt.
  • Sweets — rasgulla (rosogolla), sandesh, and rasmalai made from chhena.

Influences

Bengali cooking reflects centuries of outside influence: Mughal rule brought biryanis and kebabs to Dhaka and Murshidabad; Portuguese traders introduced chhena-based sweets and chillies; British colonial Calcutta developed a famous street-food and bakery culture; and Chinese immigration gave Kolkata its distinctive Indo-Chinese cuisine. The partition of Bengal in 1947 also created a lasting culinary dialogue — and rivalry — between the Ghoti traditions of West Bengal and the Bangal traditions of the east.
