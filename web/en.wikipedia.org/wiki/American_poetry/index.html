American poetry refers to the poetry written in the United States, from the colonial period to the present day. It developed out of British literary traditions but gradually acquired distinctive voices, forms, and themes shaped by the American landscape, democratic ideals, and the country's diverse immigrant and indigenous cultures. Today American poetry encompasses everything from formal verse to experimental and performance-based work.

Colonial and early national period

The first notable American poet was Anne Bradstreet (1612–1672), whose collection The Tenth Muse Lately Sprung Up in America (1650) was the first book of poetry published by an American colonist. Edward Taylor, a Puritan minister, wrote meditative devotional verse that was not published until the twentieth century. Phillis Wheatley, an enslaved African-born poet in Boston, published Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral in 1773, becoming one of the earliest published African-American authors. The Revolutionary era produced political verse by writers such as Philip Freneau, sometimes called the "Poet of the American Revolution."

The nineteenth century

The early republic saw the rise of the Fireside Poets — Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, William Cullen Bryant, John Greenleaf Whittier, and Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. — whose accessible, rhymed verse was memorized in schoolrooms. Longfellow's narrative poems such as The Song of Hiawatha (1855) and Paul Revere's Ride (1860) made him the most popular American poet of his century.

A decisive break came with Walt Whitman, whose Leaves of Grass (1855) introduced sweeping free verse and a democratic catalogue of American life, and Emily Dickinson, who wrote nearly 1,800 short, slant-rhymed lyrics in Amherst, Massachusetts, almost none published in her lifetime. Together they are often regarded as the twin founders of a distinctly American poetic idiom. Edgar Allan Poe, better known for his tales, contributed influential poems such as "The Raven" (1845) and essays on poetic composition.

Modernism

In the early twentieth century, American poets were central to international modernism. Ezra Pound promoted Imagism and edited younger writers; T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land (1922) became the era's landmark long poem. William Carlos Williams championed an American vernacular idiom in works such as Spring and All (1923) and the epic Paterson. Marianne Moore, Wallace Stevens, H.D., and Hart Crane each developed highly individual modernist styles. Robert Frost combined traditional forms with New England speech, winning four Pulitzer Prizes.

The Harlem Renaissance of the 1920s brought African-American poetry to national prominence through Langston Hughes, Claude McKay, Countee Cullen, and Jean Toomer, blending jazz rhythms and vernacular speech with classical forms.

Postwar and contemporary poetry

After the Second World War, American poetry diversified into competing schools: the Beat Generation (Allen Ginsberg's Howl, 1956; Jack Kerouac; Gary Snyder), the confessional poets (Robert Lowell, Sylvia Plath, Anne Sexton, John Berryman), the New York School (John Ashbery, Frank O'Hara), the Black Mountain poets around Charles Olson, and the Deep Image and surrealist-influenced writers. Gwendolyn Brooks became the first African American to win the Pulitzer Prize for Poetry in 1950.

Later decades saw the rise of Language poetry, spoken word and slam performance, and an increasingly multicultural canon including Joy Harjo (the first Native American U.S. Poet Laureate), Rita Dove, Billy Collins, and Mary Oliver. The position of Poet Laureate of the United States, administered by the Library of Congress, has helped bring poetry to a broad public audience.
