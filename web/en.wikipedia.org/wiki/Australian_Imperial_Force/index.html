Australian Imperial Force

Australian Imperial Force
Country: Australia
First AIF: 1914–1921
Second AIF: 1939–1947
Type: Expeditionary force (volunteer)

The Australian Imperial Force (AIF) was the name given to two volunteer expeditionary forces raised by Australia for overseas service in the First and Second World Wars. The First Australian Imperial Force (1st AIF) served from 1914 to 1921, and the Second Australian Imperial Force (2nd AIF) from 1939 to 1947. Because Australian law restricted the militia to home defence, separate all-volunteer forces were created for service abroad.

First AIF

Formed in August 1914 following Britain's declaration of war, the 1st AIF eventually grew to five infantry divisions, the Australian Light Horse and the Australian Flying Corps. More than 416,000 Australians enlisted, of whom about 331,000 served overseas; over 60,000 were killed and 156,000 wounded, giving Australia one of the highest casualty rates of any belligerent. The force fought at Gallipoli in 1915 — the landing on 25 April becoming central to the Anzac legend — and then in Egypt and Palestine and on the Western Front, where the Australian Corps under General Sir John Monash played a leading role in the victories of 1918, including Villers-Bretonneux, Hamel and the Hindenburg Line.

Second AIF

The 2nd AIF was raised in 1939 and formed four infantry divisions (6th, 7th, 8th and 9th) and an armoured division. It fought in North Africa — including the Siege of Tobruk, whose defenders became known as the "Rats of Tobruk", and the battles of El Alamein — as well as in Greece, Syria and Lebanon. After Japan's entry into the war, most of the force returned to the Pacific, fighting in New Guinea, Bougainville and Borneo, while the 8th Division was largely captured at the fall of Singapore in 1942.

Legacy

The AIF's service, and particularly the Gallipoli campaign, profoundly shaped Australian national identity. The term "AIF" remains strongly associated with the Anzac tradition commemorated on Anzac Day.
