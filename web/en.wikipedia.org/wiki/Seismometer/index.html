Seismometer

A seismometer is an instrument that measures ground motion caused by earthquakes, volcanic activity, explosions, and other seismic sources. Its output — a record of ground displacement, velocity, or acceleration over time — is called a seismogram. A seismometer combined with a recording system is often termed a seismograph.

Principle

The classic design suspends an inertial mass on a spring or pendulum inside a frame fixed to the ground. When the ground moves, inertia keeps the mass nearly still, and the relative motion between mass and frame is measured — historically by a pen tracing on a rotating drum, and in modern instruments by electromagnetic transducers that convert motion to voltage, with electronic feedback (force-balance sensors) keeping the mass centred and greatly extending sensitivity and bandwidth. Most stations deploy three components, recording vertical and two horizontal directions of motion.

Types and history

The first seismoscope — Zhang Heng's dragon-and-toad device of 132 CE in China — indicated an earthquake's direction without recording it. Scientific seismometers emerged in the late 19th century (Milne, Wiechert and others), enabling the discovery of Earth's internal structure. Modern classes include short-period instruments for local quakes, broadband seismometers capturing periods from fractions of a second to hundreds of seconds, strong-motion accelerometers that stay on scale during major earthquakes for engineering use, and cheap MEMS sensors. Networks of seismometers locate earthquakes, compute magnitudes, monitor nuclear tests, and feed tsunami and early-warning systems. Seismometers have also been landed on the Moon (Apollo) and Mars (InSight).
