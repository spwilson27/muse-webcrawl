Glioma

A glioma is a type of primary tumor that starts in the glial cells of the brain or spinal cord. Gliomas comprise about 30 percent of all brain tumors and 80 percent of all malignant ones. They are named for the type of glial cell from which they arise: astrocytomas arise from astrocytes, oligodendrogliomas from oligodendrocytes, and ependymomas from ependymal cells.

Classification

Gliomas are classified by cell type, by grade, and by location. The World Health Organization grades gliomas from I (least advanced and potentially curable by surgery) to IV (most advanced and malignant). Low-grade gliomas are well differentiated and grow slowly, whereas high-grade gliomas are undifferentiated, malignant, and carry a worse prognosis. Modern classification also incorporates molecular markers, particularly mutations in the IDH1 and IDH2 genes and 1p/19q codeletion status. Glioblastoma is the highest-grade (grade IV) glioma.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms depend on which part of the central nervous system is affected and may include headaches, seizures, personality changes, weakness, numbness, and speech or vision difficulties.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the location and grade of the tumor and typically combines surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy. Prognosis varies widely by type and grade.
