Optical illusion

Optical illusion
FieldPerceptual psychology, neuroscience, optics
Main typesPhysiological, cognitive, literal, paradox
Related conceptsVisual perception, Gestalt psychology, pareidolia

An optical illusion (also called a visual illusion) is an illusion caused by the visual system and characterized by a visual percept that arguably appears to differ from reality. Illusions come in a wide variety; their categorization is difficult because the underlying cause is often not clear, but a classification proposed by Richard Gregory is useful as an orientation. According to that scheme there are three main classes: physical, physiological, and cognitive illusions, and in each class there are four kinds: ambiguities, distortions, paradoxes, and fictions.

A typical fiction is when a figure is perceived even though it is not present in the stimulus, as in the Kanizsa triangle. Ambiguities are pictures or objects that elicit a perceptual "switch" between alternative interpretations, such as the Necker cube and the Rubin vase. Distortions involve errors of size, length, position or curvature, as in the Müller-Lyer illusion. Paradoxes present figures that are paradoxical or impossible, such as the Penrose triangle.

Physiological illusions

Physiological illusions are the effects of excessive stimulation of a specific type — brightness, colour, position, size, motion, and so on — on the eyes and brain. The theory is that a stimulus follows a dedicated neural path early in visual processing, and that repetitive or excessive stimulation of that path causes an imbalance that alters perception. Afterimages, in which an image continues to appear briefly after exposure has ended, are the best known example. Motion aftereffects, such as the waterfall illusion, similarly result from adaptation of motion-sensitive neurons.

Cognitive illusions

Cognitive illusions are commonly divided into ambiguous illusions, distorting illusions, paradox illusions and fictional illusions. They arise from the interaction of perceptual assumptions about the world with sensory evidence — the "unconscious inferences" first described by Hermann von Helmholtz in the 19th century. In the Müller-Lyer illusion, two line segments of equal length appear different because the visual system interprets the arrow-like fins as depth cues and misapplies size constancy scaling.

Gestalt psychologists showed that the mind groups visual elements according to principles such as proximity, similarity, closure and continuity, and illusions exploit these organizing rules.

Explanation

The visual system does not record the retinal image passively; it constructs a model of the world from incomplete data. Because the projection of the three-dimensional world onto the two-dimensional retina is ambiguous, perception relies on assumptions about lighting, depth, and object regularity. Illusions expose the assumptions when they are applied to artificial stimuli that violate the statistics of natural scenes. Research in neuroscience has linked particular illusions to processing in the primary visual cortex (V1), the middle temporal area (MT) for motion, and higher object-recognition areas.

In art and culture

Artists have exploited illusions since antiquity. Greek architects adjusted the columns of the Parthenon to counteract optical distortions (entasis); Renaissance painters mastered linear perspective and anamorphosis; and in the 20th century the Op art movement of Bridget Riley and Victor Vasarely, together with the impossible constructions of M. C. Escher, made illusion itself the subject of art. Trompe-l'œil painting aims to deceive the viewer into taking a painted surface for reality.
