Chemical structure

A chemical structure of a molecule is a spatial arrangement of its atoms and their chemical bonds. Its determination includes a chemist's specifying the molecular geometry and, when feasible and necessary, the electronic structure of the target molecule. Structure theory, developed in the 19th century by chemists such as August Kekulé, Archibald Scott Couper and Alexander Butlerov, established that molecules with the same chemical formula can have different structures — the phenomenon of isomerism.

Representation

Structures are commonly drawn as skeletal formulae, in which carbon atoms are implied at line ends and vertices and hydrogen atoms on carbon are omitted. Other representations include Lewis structures, condensed formulae, ball-and-stick models, and space-filling models. Machine-readable encodings such as SMILES and InChI allow structures to be stored and searched in databases.

Determination

Experimental methods for determining structure include X-ray crystallography, NMR spectroscopy, electron diffraction, and cryo-electron microscopy. Computational chemistry predicts structures by minimising the molecular energy.
