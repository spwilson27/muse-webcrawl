Shock (circulatory)

Shock is a life-threatening medical condition in which the circulatory system fails to deliver enough oxygenated blood to the tissues, resulting in cellular hypoxia and, if prolonged, organ failure and death. It is a final common pathway of many critical illnesses and injuries, distinct from the emotional sense of the word.

Classification

Shock is conventionally divided into four categories. Hypovolemic shock results from loss of blood or fluid volume. Cardiogenic shock follows failure of the heart to pump, as after myocardial infarction. Obstructive shock arises from mechanical obstruction to blood flow, such as cardiac tamponade or massive pulmonary embolism. Distributive shock involves loss of vascular tone, encompassing septic shock, anaphylactic shock, and neurogenic shock.

Signs and stages

Early (compensated) shock may show only tachycardia and anxiety as the body diverts blood to vital organs. Progressive shock brings hypotension, rapid weak pulse, cold clammy skin (warm in early sepsis), reduced urine output, confusion, and lactic acidosis. Refractory shock is irreversible despite treatment.

Management

Treatment follows the ABCDE approach: secure the airway, support breathing, and restore circulation with intravenous fluids, blood products, vasopressors such as norepinephrine, and definitive treatment of the underlying cause — haemorrhage control for bleeding, antibiotics and source control for sepsis, epinephrine for anaphylaxis, and reperfusion for cardiogenic shock after heart attack. Early recognition dramatically improves survival.
