Hellenistic Greece

Hellenistic Greece is the historical period of Greece following the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BC and ending with the Roman annexation of the Achaean League in 146 BC, after the Battle of Corinth. During this era the Greek mainland was dominated first by the Macedonian kingdom of the Antigonid dynasty and by powerful federal leagues, while Greek culture spread throughout the Mediterranean world and the Near East.

Background

After Alexander's death, his generals — the Diadochi — fought over his empire. Greece proper became a contested prize between Cassander, Antigonus Monophthalmus and their successors. Demetrius Poliorcetes briefly seized Macedon before his son Antigonus II Gonatas established the durable Antigonid dynasty in 276 BC.

The leagues

Two great federal states shaped the period: the Aetolian League in central Greece and the Achaean League in the Peloponnese. Under leaders such as Aratus of Sicyon, Aristaenus and Philopoemen, the Achaean League expanded to embrace much of the Peloponnese, including a reluctant Sparta. The leagues fielded professional armies, minted their own coinage and conducted independent foreign policies, though they repeatedly sought Macedonian or Roman help against each other.

Macedonian and Roman wars

Philip V of Macedon's ambitions brought Greece into conflict with Rome. Defeat at the Battle of Cynoscephalae in 197 BC ended Macedonian dominance, and Titus Quinctius Flamininus proclaimed the "freedom of the Greeks" at the Isthmian Games of 196 BC. Further wars followed: Perseus was crushed at Pydna in 168 BC, and after the Achaean War of 146 BC Rome destroyed Corinth and dissolved the league, making Greece a Roman dependency.

Culture

The Hellenistic age saw Athens remain a centre of philosophy — home to the schools of Epicurus and Zeno of Citium — while new centres such as Alexandria and Pergamon rose to rival it. Koine Greek, the common dialect descended from Attic, became the lingua franca of the eastern Mediterranean.
