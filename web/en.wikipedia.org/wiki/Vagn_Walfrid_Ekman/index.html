Vagn Walfrid Ekman

Vagn Walfrid Ekman (3 May 1874 – 9 March 1954) was a Swedish oceanographer whose work laid much of the theoretical foundation of modern physical oceanography. Born in Stockholm, the son of oceanographer Fredrik Laurentz Ekman, he studied mathematics and physics at Uppsala University and took his doctorate in 1902.

The Ekman spiral

Ekman's most famous result grew out of Fridtjof Nansen's Fram expedition observations: Arctic ice drifted 20–40° to the right of the wind direction, an effect Nansen attributed to the Earth's rotation. Working at Vilhelm Bjerknes's suggestion, Ekman solved the equations of a wind-driven ocean with friction and Coriolis force and showed that the surface current flows at 45° to the wind (to the right in the Northern Hemisphere), with the current vector rotating and weakening with depth — the Ekman spiral. The depth-integrated result, Ekman transport at right angles to the wind, explains coastal upwelling and is fundamental to wind-driven ocean circulation.

Later career

Ekman worked with Bjerknes and Nansen at the International Laboratory for Oceanographical Research in Oslo (Christiania), where he also developed instruments, including the Ekman current meter and a reversing water bottle. In 1910 he became professor of mathematical physics at Lund University, a post he held until 1939. His comprehensive 1905 paper "On the influence of the Earth's rotation on ocean-currents" remains a classic of geophysical fluid dynamics, and his name is commemorated in the Ekman layer, Ekman pumping, and the dimensionless Ekman number.
