National Park Service rustic

Periodc. 1916–1942

National Park Service rustic — often nicknamed "parkitecture" — is a style of architecture that developed in the early twentieth century in the national parks of the United States. Its buildings are deliberately built from native materials — rough, unhewn stone and massive logs — in a handcrafted manner intended to harmonise with the natural landscape rather than impose upon it.

Characteristics

Rustic buildings feature battered (sloping) stone foundations and walls, oversized log beams with protruding ends, low-pitched shingled roofs, deep porches, and stones left with their weathered faces exposed. Structures were sited to blend into landforms and vegetation; even bridges, signs, entrance stations and trailside shelters followed the idiom. The style drew on Adirondack Great Camp architecture, pioneer log construction and the Arts and Crafts movement, and was codified by the Park Service's landscape architects and engineers, whose pattern books — notably Albert Good's Park and Recreation Structures (1938) — standardised the look across the system.

Examples and legacy

Classic examples include the Old Faithful Inn in Yellowstone (1904, a precursor), Glacier National Park's Many Glacier Hotel, the lodges of Zion, Bryce Canyon and the Grand Canyon's North Rim, and hundreds of Depression-era structures built by the Civilian Conservation Corps in national and state parks between 1933 and 1942. Construction largely ended with the Second World War, and the more functional Mission 66 era that followed reacted against it. Today the surviving rustic buildings are prized and heavily protected, and many are listed on the National Register of Historic Places as the defining architectural heritage of the American park movement.
