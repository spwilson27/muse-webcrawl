Translation (geometry)

In Euclidean geometry, a translation is a geometric transformation that moves every point of a figure or a space by the same distance in a given direction. A translation is an isometry: it preserves distances, angles and orientation, so a figure and its image under a translation are congruent. Unlike rotation and reflection, a translation has no fixed points (unless it is the trivial translation by the zero vector).

Description

A translation can be specified by a displacement vector v: every point P is mapped to the point P + v. In Cartesian coordinates, translation by (a, b) maps (x, y) to (x + a, y + b). In three dimensions it maps (x, y, z) to (x + a, y + b, z + c). Translations can be represented by matrices using homogeneous coordinates.

Properties and applications

The set of all translations forms a group under composition, the translation group, which is a subgroup of the Euclidean group. Translations commute with one another, and the composition of two translations is again a translation. Together with rotations, translations generate all rigid motions of the plane and of space. In physics, translational symmetry — invariance of a system under translation — is linked by Noether's theorem to the conservation of linear momentum, and in crystallography translations define the unit cells of lattices.
