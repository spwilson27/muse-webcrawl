Exotica

Exotica is a musical genre that flourished in the United States in the 1950s and early 1960s, evoking an idealised, imaginary version of tropical places — especially the South Pacific, Hawaii, and an imagined "jungle" — for suburban American listeners. The name comes from the 1957 album Exotica by Martin Denny, the genre's most famous practitioner. Exotica is closely related to lounge music and space-age pop, and formed the soundtrack of the tiki culture craze.

Characteristics

Exotica recordings typically combine lush orchestral arrangements or small jazz combos with vibraphone, exotic percussion (bongos, congas, gongs, bamboo sticks), wordless vocals, and imitations of bird calls and jungle sounds — on Denny's records these were often produced by the band members themselves. Compositions such as "Quiet Village" by Les Baxter, the genre's chief architect, conjured a fantasy tropics far removed from any real folk music, though they freely borrowed melodies and instruments from Latin American, Polynesian, and Asian sources.

History

Les Baxter's 1951 album Ritual of the Savage laid the groundwork. Martin Denny's group, playing in Waikiki, took "Quiet Village" to the top of the charts in 1959 after their debut album became a surprise hit; Denny's pianist was Julius Wechter and his vibraphonist Arthur Lyman, who went on to his own successful exotica career. Other notable artists include Yma Sumac, with her extraordinary vocal range, Esquivel, Korla Pandit, and Robert Drasnin.

Interest faded with the arrival of the British Invasion, but exotica was rediscovered in the 1990s as part of the lounge revival, with reissue labels restoring the classic albums and new listeners appreciating the genre's craft and escapism.
