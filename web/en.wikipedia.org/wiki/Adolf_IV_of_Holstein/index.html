Adolf IV of Holstein

Image placeholder: Adolf IV, Count of Schauenburg and Holstein

Adolf IV (before 1205 – 8 July 1261) was Count of Schauenburg (Schaumburg) and Holstein from the 1220s until 1238. He is best known for defeating the Danes at the Battle of Bornhöved in 1227, which ended Danish supremacy over northern Germany, and for founding the city of Kiel.

Born: before 1205
Died: 8 July 1261, Neukloster
House: Schauenburg
Reign: c. 1225 – 1238

Life

Adolf inherited a county largely occupied by King Valdemar II of Denmark, who had captured his father, Adolf III. After Valdemar was himself captured by Henry of Schwerin in 1223 and forced to relinquish his German conquests, Adolf returned and recovered his lands. Together with allies from Lübeck, Hamburg, Saxony and the Slavic princes, he crushed the Danish army at Bornhöved on 22 July 1227, a victory traditionally attributed to a vow he made to the Virgin Mary during the battle.

In fulfilment of religious vows — legend holds that he also promised at Bornhöved to enter a monastery if granted victory — Adolf founded churches and the town of Kiel around 1233–1242, then abdicated in 1238, took Franciscan orders and was ordained a priest in Rome in 1244. He spent his later years at the priory of Neukloster, dying in 1261. His sons John I and Gerhard I divided his inheritance, founding the lines of Holstein-Kiel and Holstein-Itzehoe.

Legacy

Adolf's victory at Bornhöved secured the independence of Lübeck as a free imperial city and fixed the southern boundary of Danish influence for centuries. He is venerated in regional tradition as a founder-figure of both Kiel and Hamburg's Neustadt parish churches.
