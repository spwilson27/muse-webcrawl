Margin of appreciation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Margin of appreciation
Type: Doctrine of international human rights law
Origin: European Court of Human Rights
Key case: Handyside v. United Kingdom (1976)
Codified reference: Protocol No. 15 to the ECHR (preamble)
The European Court of Human Rights in Strasbourg, whose case law developed the margin of appreciation doctrine.

The margin of appreciation is a doctrine in international human rights law, developed in the jurisprudence of the European Court of Human Rights, under which states are granted a degree of discretion — a latitude of judgment — in how they fulfil their obligations under the European Convention on Human Rights, particularly when restricting qualified rights. The doctrine reflects the subsidiary role of the international court: national authorities, by reason of their direct and continuous contact with the vital forces of their countries, are in principle better placed than the international judge to assess local needs and conditions.

Operation

The width of the margin varies with context. It tends to be narrow where a particularly important facet of individual identity is at stake, or where a European consensus supports the applicant's position; it is wider in matters of morals, national security, and the regulation of property and social policy, where reasonable democracies may differ. In the leading case, Handyside v. United Kingdom (1976), the Court held that the machinery of the Convention is subsidiary to national systems, and that it is not the Court's task to take the place of the competent national courts but to review their decisions under the Convention.

Development and criticism

The concept grew out of the Court's early case law on emergency derogations and was generalised through cases such as Handyside and the Sunday Times case. Protocol No. 15, which entered into force in 2021, inserted a reference to subsidiarity and the margin of appreciation into the Convention's preamble. Critics argue the doctrine can dilute universal human rights protection and invite deference where scrutiny is needed; defenders regard it as essential to the legitimacy and workability of the system. Similar deference doctrines appear, in different forms, in the practice of other human rights bodies.
