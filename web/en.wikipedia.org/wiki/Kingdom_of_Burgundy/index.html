Kingdom of Burgundy was the name borne by several successive polities of the Burgundians in early medieval western Europe. The first kingdom was founded by the Germanic Burgundians on the middle Rhine and later in Sapaudia (Savoy) and the Rhône valley, before being conquered by the Franks in 534. Name and territory revived under the Carolingians' successors in the ninth and tenth centuries, and the later Kingdom of Arles (Burgundy) united Provence and Cisjurane Burgundy from 933 until its lands were progressively absorbed into the Holy Roman Empire and France.

The first Burgundian kingdom (411–534)

The Burgundians, an East Germanic people, crossed the Rhine in 406/407 and in 411 established a kingdom around Worms under King Gundahar (Gunther), the historical kernel of the legendary Burgundian kings of the Nibelungenlied. In 437 the kingdom was destroyed by Hunnic mercenaries in Roman service; Gundahar and much of his people were killed. The survivors were resettled by the Roman general Aetius in Sapaudia (the Geneva–Savoy region) around 443, whence the kingdom expanded under Gundioc and Gundobad to cover the Rhône and Saône valleys, with centres at Lyon and Geneva.

Gundobad (r. 473–516) codified Burgundian law in the Lex Burgundionum and sought reconciliation between his Arian Burgundians and their Catholic Gallo-Roman subjects. His son Sigismund converted to Catholicism and founded the abbey of Saint-Maurice d'Agaune, but was defeated and killed by the Franks in 524. After further campaigns, the Frankish kings annexed the kingdom in 534, though Burgundy long retained a distinct identity within the Merovingian and Carolingian worlds.

Later Burgundies

After the Treaty of Verdun (843), Burgundian lands were divided between West and Middle Francia. In 879 Boso of Provence founded the kingdom of Lower Burgundy (Cisjurane Burgundy/Provence), and in 888 Rudolph I established Upper Burgundy (Transjurane Burgundy) around Lake Geneva and the Valais. In 933 Rudolph II of Upper Burgundy acquired Lower Burgundy, uniting them as the Kingdom of Arles. In 1032–1034, on the death of the childless Rudolph III, the kingdom passed by inheritance to the Holy Roman Emperor Conrad II and became one of the three kingdoms composing the Empire, alongside Germany and Italy. Real power devolved to local counts and bishops; over the following centuries the constituent territories — Provence, Dauphiné, Savoy, the Franche-Comté — were gradually absorbed by France or remained imperial fiefs, and the title of King of Burgundy/Arles lapsed with the effective disappearance of the kingdom in the later fourteenth century.
