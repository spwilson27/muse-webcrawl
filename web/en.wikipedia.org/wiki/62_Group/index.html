62 Group

Anti-fascist activists confront a fascist march in the East End, a hallmark of the 62 Group's tactics.

The 62 Group was a militant anti-fascist organisation in the United Kingdom, active from 1962 to about 1975. Modelled on the earlier 43 Group of Jewish ex-servicemen, it was founded to confront physically the revived British fascist movement, in particular Oswald Mosley's Union Movement and Colin Jordan's National Socialist Movement, at a time when many felt the police and mainstream Jewish communal bodies were too passive.

Activities

The Group's membership drew heavily on Jewish ex-servicemen, East End tradespeople, and later younger activists. Its stewards broke up fascist meetings, disrupted marches, infiltrated far-right organisations, and gathered intelligence on their members. A key figure was solicitor Harry Bidney, and its intelligence work fed material to journalists and researchers; Gerry Gable, a 62 Group activist, went on to found the anti-fascist magazine Searchlight. Clashes at rallies in Trafalgar Square in 1962 and elsewhere attracted national press attention.

Dissolution and legacy

As the Union Movement declined and the National Front rose as a more electoral threat in the 1970s, the 62 Group wound down, formally dissolving around 1975. Its approach — "no platform" militancy combined with research and exposure — influenced later anti-fascist organisations including Anti-Fascist Action and modern research collectives.
