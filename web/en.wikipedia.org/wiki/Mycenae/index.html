Mycenae

Mycenae (Ancient Greek: Μυκῆναι, Mykēnai) is an archaeological site and ancient city in the Peloponnese, in modern-day Greece, located about 90 kilometres southwest of Athens. In the second millennium BC it was one of the major centres of Greek civilization — a military stronghold that dominated much of southern Greece, Crete, the Cyclades, and parts of southwest Anatolia. The period of Greek history from about 1600 BC to 1100 BC is called Mycenaean in reference to this site.

History

Bronze Age citadel

Mycenae's acropolis was fortified with massive "Cyclopean" walls — so named because later Greeks believed only the Cyclopes could have moved the stones. Within and around the citadel lay a palace complex, workshops, storerooms, and the famous shaft graves of Grave Circles A and B, rich in gold, which testified to a wealthy warrior elite. At its height around 1350 BC, the citadel and its lower town housed perhaps 30,000 people.

Administrators at Mycenae kept records in the Linear B script, the earliest attested form of Greek, deciphered by Michael Ventris in 1952. Along with other palatial centres such as Pylos, Thebes, and Tiryns, Mycenae was destroyed around 1200 BC during the Late Bronze Age collapse, and Greek palatial civilization ended.

Legendary tradition

In Greek myth, Mycenae was founded by Perseus and was the seat of the Atreid dynasty: Atreus, Agamemnon, and Menelaus. In Homer's Iliad, Agamemnon, "lord of men", led the Greek expedition against Troy from "well-built Mycenae, rich in gold".

Archaeology

The site was excavated from 1876 by Heinrich Schliemann, who uncovered Grave Circle A with its shaft graves and the gold funerary object he dubbed the "Mask of Agamemnon". Landmarks include the Lion Gate (c. 1250 BC), the chief entrance to the citadel; the massive tholos tombs conventionally called the Treasury of Atreus and the Tomb of Clytemnestra; and the palace with its great court and megaron. Together with nearby Tiryns, Mycenae was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1999.
