Jacksonian democracy, also known as Jacksonianism, was a 19th-century political philosophy in the United States that expanded suffrage to most white men over the age of 21 and restructured a number of federal institutions. Originating with the seventh U.S. president, Andrew Jackson and his supporters, it became the nation's dominant political worldview for a generation.

The philosophy is characterized by a belief in the common man, opposition to concentrated economic and political privilege, the spoils system of patronage, and an expansionist foreign policy summed up in the idea of Manifest Destiny.

Origins

Jacksonian democracy grew out of the older Jeffersonian democracy of Thomas Jefferson, but its tone was set by the contested election of 1824, in which Jackson won pluralities of both the popular and electoral votes but lost in the House of Representatives to John Quincy Adams under what Jacksonians denounced as a "corrupt bargain." Jackson's victory in 1828 marked the arrival of mass party politics and a new democratic era.

Principles

Key principles included expanded white male suffrage, the belief that political office should rotate among ordinary citizens rather than a permanent elite, hostility to the Second Bank of the United States (destroyed in the "Bank War" of 1832–1836), and support for westward expansion and Indian removal, policies that had devastating consequences for Native American peoples, including the Trail of Tears.

Legacy

Historians such as Arthur M. Schlesinger Jr. treated Jacksonianism as a populist movement against corporate privilege, while others emphasize its racial limits: democracy expanded for white men even as slavery persisted and Native peoples were dispossessed. The Democratic Party forged in this era remains one of the two major American parties.
