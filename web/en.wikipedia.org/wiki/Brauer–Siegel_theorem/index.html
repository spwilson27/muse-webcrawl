Brauer–Siegel theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In algebraic number theory, the Brauer–Siegel theorem, named after Richard Brauer and Carl Ludwig Siegel, is an asymptotic result about the behaviour of algebraic number fields in infinite families. Roughly speaking, it states that for a sequence of number fields whose degrees grow in a controlled way relative to their discriminants, the product of the class number and the regulator behaves asymptotically like the square root of the absolute value of the discriminant.

Statement

Let K range over a family of number fields with degree nK and discriminant DK, and let hK denote the class number and RK the regulator of K. If nK / log|DK| tends to 0 as K ranges through the family, then the Brauer–Siegel theorem asserts:

log(hK RK) / log(√|DK|) → 1.

The theorem generalises the classical result for imaginary quadratic fields, where the regulator is trivial and the class number therefore grows like the square root of the discriminant, a fact intimately connected with Dirichlet's class number formula and with estimates for L-functions near s = 1. For real quadratic fields it implies that log(hK RK) is asymptotic to log √|DK|, but does not by itself control hK and RK separately.

Ineffectivity

The theorem is notoriously ineffective: the proof, which passes through a lower bound for L(1, χ) of Siegel type, cannot produce a computable rate of convergence, because of the possible existence of a Siegel zero. This ineffectivity propagates to several consequences of the theorem — for instance, proofs that there are only finitely many number fields of given class number in certain families — which likewise cannot be made explicit.
