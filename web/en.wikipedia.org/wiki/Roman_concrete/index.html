Roman concrete

Roman concrete, also called opus caementicium, was a building material used in construction in ancient Rome. It consisted of a mortar made from slaked lime and volcanic ash (pozzolana) mixed with an aggregate of broken stone, brick or ceramic fragments. Roman concrete began to be used from about 150 BC, although earlier forms existed around Naples, and it revolutionised Roman architecture by allowing complex, large-scale structures that could set even under water.

Composition

The core innovation was the addition of pozzolana, a volcanic ash found near Pozzuoli on the Bay of Naples. When mixed with lime and water, the ash undergoes a pozzolanic reaction that produces a hydraulic cement capable of setting underwater. The mortar was combined with aggregates (caementa) such as tuff, travertine, pumice and recycled rubble. In the Pantheon's dome, the aggregate becomes progressively lighter toward the top, reducing the load on the walls.

Properties and durability

Roman marine concrete has proved exceptionally durable. Modern studies of harbour structures such as those at Baiae and Portus Cosanus show that seawater interacting with the concrete promotes the growth of rare minerals, including Al-tobermorite and phillipsite, which strengthen the material over time instead of degrading it. In 2023, researchers at MIT proposed that the distinctive white lime clasts found throughout Roman concrete gave it a "self-healing" capacity: when cracks form, water dissolves the clasts and the resulting calcium-rich solution recrystallises, sealing the crack.

Use in architecture

Roman concrete enabled the construction of vast vaulted spaces, including the Pantheon, the Baths of Caracalla, the markets of Trajan and the great harbours of the Mediterranean. Because it could be poured into wooden formwork, it freed Roman builders from the limitations of cut stone and allowed curved forms such as domes, barrel vaults and groin vaults on an unprecedented scale.

Decline and rediscovery

Knowledge of the recipe faded after the fall of the Western Roman Empire. Vitruvius's De architectura preserved a description of pozzolanic mortar, and hydraulic concretes were sporadically reinvented in the Middle Ages, but Roman concrete was not systematically matched until the development of Portland cement in the 19th century. Scientific study of surviving Roman samples continues to inform research into longer-lasting, lower-carbon modern concretes.
