In situ hybridization

In situ hybridization (ISH) is a laboratory technique that uses a labelled complementary DNA, RNA, or modified nucleic acid probe to locate a specific nucleic acid sequence within a tissue section, whole organism, or cells — that is, in situ ("in place"). By revealing exactly which cells contain a gene or its messenger RNA, ISH links molecular biology to anatomy and is widely used in developmental biology, neuroscience, and medical diagnosis.

Dark staining marks the cells expressing the targeted RNA in this embryo section.

Method

A probe — a strand of nucleic acid complementary to the target — is labelled with a radioactive isotope, a coloured enzyme substrate system, or a fluorescent dye. The tissue is fixed and permeabilised so the probe can reach the nucleic acids while preserving morphology. The probe is applied, allowed to hybridise with its target, and unbound probe is washed away; the label is then detected under a microscope.

Variants and applications

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) uses fluorescent probes and can detect several sequences at once in different colours. In clinical cytogenetics, FISH identifies chromosomal abnormalities, such as the HER2 gene amplification that guides breast-cancer treatment, and translocations characteristic of particular leukaemias and lymphomas. In developmental biology, ISH maps where and when genes are switched on in embryos, revealing the patterns that build body plans; large-scale atlases of gene expression in the mouse and fly brain were built with automated ISH. Chromogenic ISH (CISH) uses ordinary light microscopy and permanent stains, making it convenient for routine pathology.
