Rotten and pocket boroughs

Rotten borough
CountryEngland / United Kingdom
Era17th–19th centuries
Notorious examplesOld Sarum, Dunwich, Gatton
AbolishedReform Act 1832 (largely)

A rotten borough was a parliamentary borough in England and later the United Kingdom that had decayed to a tiny population yet still returned members of Parliament (usually two), giving it grossly disproportionate representation. A pocket borough was one whose voters were so few, or so dependent on a single patron, that the patron effectively controlled the seat — it was "in his pocket".

Background

Borough boundaries and franchises had scarcely changed since the Middle Ages, so places that had declined — Dunwich, largely lost to the sea, or Old Sarum, an uninhabited hill with seven voters — kept their MPs, while growing industrial cities such as Manchester and Birmingham had none. Patrons such as the Duke of Newcastle treated pocket boroughs as property that could be bought, sold or bestowed; William Pitt the Elder entered Parliament for Old Sarum.

Reform

Agitation against the system grew after the American and French revolutions. The Reform Act 1832 disfranchised 56 rotten boroughs entirely, reduced others, and redistributed seats to the new cities; subsequent acts in 1867 and 1884–85 further equalised representation. The secret ballot (1872) and anti-corruption legislation ended the practical control of pocket boroughs. The terms survive as bywords for electoral corruption and malapportionment.
