The Low Pay Commission (LPC) is an independent statutory body in the United Kingdom which advises the Government on the National Minimum Wage (NMW) and the National Living Wage (NLW). Established in 1997 ahead of the introduction of the minimum wage under the National Minimum Wage Act 1998, it is widely credited with ensuring that the UK minimum wage was set using an evidence-based, social partnership approach and has been a model studied by policymakers in many other countries.

Role and membership

The Commission has nine members appointed by the Secretary of State: a chair, three commissioners with an employer background, three with an employee (trade union) background, and two independent academics or experts. It operates by consensus, consulting widely each year with businesses, unions, workers and economists, and visiting workplaces across the UK before recommending the rates that take effect the following April.

Each year the Government issues the Commission a remit. Since 2016 this has included the National Living Wage for older workers, introduced with a target of reaching 60 per cent of median earnings by 2020; in 2019 the remit adopted a target of two-thirds of median earnings by 2024, which the Commission judged had been met when it recommended the April 2024 rate. The Commission also recommends the youth rates, the apprentice rate and the accommodation offset, and advises on compliance and enforcement issues and the future trajectory of the wage floor, including the Government's post-2024 commitment to remove age thresholds so that a single adult rate applies.

Reports and influence

The Commission's first report in 1998 recommended the initial adult rate of £3.60 per hour, which the Government accepted; its recommendations have generally been accepted in full ever since. Extensive academic evaluation, much of it commissioned or reviewed by the LPC, has found that the minimum wage raised pay at the bottom of the distribution with little measurable adverse effect on employment, and the Commission's annual reports are a principal source of data on low pay in Britain. Its consensus-based structure has been praised by organisations including the OECD and the International Labour Organization.
