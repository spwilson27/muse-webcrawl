Michael Brecker

Michael Leonard Brecker (March 29, 1949 – January 13, 2007) was an American jazz saxophonist and composer. Acknowledged as "a quiet, gentle musician widely regarded as the most influential tenor saxophonist since John Coltrane", he won 15 Grammy Awards as a performer and composer and was inducted into the DownBeat Jazz Hall of Fame in 2007. With his older brother, trumpeter Randy Brecker, he co-led the Brecker Brothers, pioneers of jazz fusion, and recorded hundreds of sessions across jazz, funk and rock.

Biography

Born in Philadelphia into a musical family — his father played jazz piano, his brother Randy trumpet and his sister Emily classical piano — Brecker took up clarinet, then alto and finally tenor saxophone. After moving to New York in 1969 he joined his brother in the band Dreams, played with Horace Silver, and built a reputation as the most sought-after studio tenor in the city, appearing on albums by James Taylor, Steely Dan, Paul Simon, Frank Zappa, Bruce Springsteen and hundreds of others. The Brecker Brothers' albums of the 1970s (including the hit single "East River") defined jazz-funk, and he co-founded the acoustic fusion group Steps Ahead with vibraphonist Mike Mainieri.

Solo career

Brecker's self-titled solo debut appeared in 1987 on Impulse! and began a series of acclaimed albums including Now You See It... (Now You Don't) (1990), Tales from the Hudson (1996), Time Is of the Essence (1999) and Nearness of You: The Ballad Book (2001), featuring Pat Metheny, Herbie Hancock and James Taylor. He was also a pioneer of the EWI (electronic wind instrument).

Illness and death

In 2005 Brecker was diagnosed with the blood disorder myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), which progressed to leukaemia. A worldwide appeal for a stem-cell donor — he needed a rare genetic match — raised awareness of donor registries, and an experimental partial-match transplant followed, but he died in New York on 13 January 2007, aged 57. His final album, Pilgrimage (2007), recorded with Hancock, Metheny, Brad Mehldau, John Patitucci and Jack DeJohnette as his health failed, won two posthumous Grammys.
