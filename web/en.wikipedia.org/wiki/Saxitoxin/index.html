Saxitoxin

FormulaC10H17N7O4
Molar mass299.29 g/mol
Source organismsDinoflagellates (e.g. Alexandrium), cyanobacteria, pufferfish
IllnessParalytic shellfish poisoning

Saxitoxin (STX) is a potent neurotoxin and the best-known member of the paralytic shellfish toxins. It is produced naturally by certain species of marine dinoflagellates — including Alexandrium, Gymnodinium and Pyrodinium — and by some freshwater cyanobacteria. Filter-feeding shellfish such as clams, mussels and oysters accumulate the toxin without apparent harm, and humans who eat contaminated shellfish develop paralytic shellfish poisoning.

Mechanism

Saxitoxin blocks voltage-gated sodium channels in nerve and muscle membranes with very high affinity, binding at the extracellular mouth of the channel pore (site 1, the same site bound by tetrodotoxin). By preventing sodium influx, it stops the propagation of action potentials. Symptoms begin with tingling of the lips and face and can progress to muscular paralysis and, in severe cases, death from respiratory failure. There is no specific antidote; treatment is supportive, including mechanical ventilation.

History and significance

The toxin takes its name from the butter clam Saxidomus gigantea, from which it was first isolated. Because of its potency — the estimated human lethal dose is under a milligram by ingestion — saxitoxin is listed under the Chemical Weapons Convention as a Schedule 1 substance and has been studied by military programs. It is also an invaluable research tool in neuroscience for dissecting the function of sodium channels, and monitoring programs worldwide test shellfish beds for saxitoxin during harmful algal blooms ("red tides").
