Background

The Jacobite rising of 1745, known as the Forty-Five, was the final and largest attempt to restore the exiled House of Stuart to the British throne. Charles Edward Stuart, the "Young Pretender," landed on the Scottish island of Eriskay on 23 July 1745 with only seven companions, despite French support never fully materialising. He raised his standard at Glenfinnan on 19 August, gathering the first of the Highland clans to his father's cause.

The campaign

The Jacobite army, fighting in the traditional Highland manner with broadsword and targe, routed the government forces under Sir John Cope at Prestonpans (21 September 1745) and took Edinburgh without a fight. For six weeks the Prince held court at Holyrood Palace while the Hanoverian government, its troops committed to the War of the Austrian Succession, scrambled to respond.

The march to Derby

In November the Jacobites invaded England, capturing Carlisle and Manchester and reaching Derby on 4 December 1745 — some 125 miles from London, which fell into panic. But no English rising materialised, French support failed to arrive, and at a council of war on 5 December the clan chiefs voted to retreat. The withdrawal to Scotland destroyed the rising's momentum.

Culloden

Pursued by the Duke of Cumberland, the starving, demoralised Jacobite army made a final stand on Culloden Moor on 16 April 1746. Cumberland's disciplined infantry and artillery shattered the Highland charge in under an hour; as many as 2,000 Jacobites were killed, many in the infamous rout and subsequent "pacification."

Aftermath

Charles fled with a price on his head — famously sheltered by Flora MacDonald — and escaped to France in September 1746, never to return. The government responded with repression: the wearing of Highland dress was banned by the Dress Act 1746, the clan chiefs' heritable jurisdictions were abolished, and the Highlands were disarmed and garrisoned. The rising became the central event of Scottish romantic memory, immortalised in song ("Will Ye No Come Back Again?", "The Skye Boat Song") and later literature.
