Neoclassical architecture

Neoclassical architecture is an architectural style produced by the Neoclassical movement that began in the mid-18th century in Italy and France and flourished across Europe and the Americas into the 19th century. It drew on the architecture of classical antiquity — Greek and Roman temples, the principles of Vitruvius, and the work of Renaissance architect Andrea Palladio — as a reaction against the ornament of Baroque and Rococo and in the light of new archaeological discoveries at Herculaneum and Pompeii.

Origins

Enlightenment rationalism, the Grand Tour, and excavations in Italy convinced architects and patrons that classical forms embodied reason and civic virtue. Theorists such as Marc-Antoine Laugier, with his "primitive hut", argued for structural clarity and the honest use of the orders. In France the style produced Soufflot's Panthéon in Paris; in Britain it informed the work of Robert Adam (whose "Adam style" was a lighter, more decorative Neoclassicism) and John Soane; in Prussia, Karl Friedrich Schinkel designed Berlin's Altes Museum and Schauspielhaus; in Russia, the style shaped imperial St Petersburg.

Characteristics

  • Symmetrical, geometric plans and elevations with clean lines and minimal ornament
  • Temple fronts: pedimented porticoes with columns in the Greek or Roman orders
  • Domes and rotundas modelled on the Roman Pantheon
  • Smooth ashlar surfaces, grand monumental scale for public buildings
  • Archaeologically correct classical detail, including Greek Doric in the "Greek Revival" phase

Spread and variants

In the young United States, Thomas Jefferson championed Neoclassicism — the Virginia State Capitol and the University of Virginia — as the architecture of republican virtue; the related Federal and later Greek Revival styles dominated American building for decades. Napoleon's Empire style applied the idiom to imperial propaganda. In the early 20th century a stripped, monumental classicism was revived for government buildings in many countries, and echoes persist in "New Classical" architecture today.
