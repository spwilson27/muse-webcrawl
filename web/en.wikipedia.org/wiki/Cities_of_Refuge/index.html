Cities of Refuge

The cities of refuge (Hebrew: ערי המקלט, arei ha-miklat) were six Levitical towns named in the Hebrew Bible to which a person who had killed another unintentionally could flee for asylum. Inside the city, the manslayer was protected from the "avenger of blood" (the go'el ha-dam, a relative charged with exacting retribution) until he could stand trial before the congregation, and, if acquitted of intentional murder, until the death of the high priest, after which he could return home in safety.

The institution is described principally in Numbers 35, Deuteronomy 19, and Joshua 20. It reflects an ancient Near Eastern legal tradition that distinguished between deliberate murder and accidental killing, moderating the practice of blood vengeance by inserting a judicial process and a place of sanctuary.

Biblical account

According to Numbers 35, Moses was commanded to set apart six cities of refuge out of the forty-eight cities given to the Levites: three west of the Jordan River and three east of it. Joshua 20 names them as Kedesh in Galilee, Shechem in the hill country of Ephraim, and Hebron (Kiriath Arba) in Judah on the western side; and Bezer in the territory of Reuben, Ramoth in Gilead, and Golan in Bashan on the eastern side.

The roads to the cities were to be kept in good repair, and tradition held that signposts were erected at crossroads marked "Refuge! Refuge!" so that a fugitive could reach safety quickly. Deuteronomy 19 further required that the cities be located so that no part of the land was too far from one, and contemplated adding three more cities if the territory of Israel expanded.

The trial and the high priest

Upon arrival, the fugitive stated his case to the elders at the city gate. He was admitted provisionally, but was then returned to the place where the killing occurred to stand trial before the congregation. If the killing was judged intentional, he was handed over to the avenger of blood for execution; if unintentional, he was restored to the city of refuge, where he had to remain until the death of the high priest. Rabbinic tradition adds that the mother of the high priest would supply the exiles with food and clothing so that they would not pray for her son's death.

Related provisions

The altar itself offered a more limited asylum: Exodus 21:14 provides that a deliberate murderer could be taken even from the altar to be executed, while 1 Kings records that Adonijah and Joab clung to the horns of the altar with differing outcomes. Scholars have compared the cities of refuge with sanctuaries of asylum in other ancient cultures, including Greek temple asylum and later medieval church sanctuary.
