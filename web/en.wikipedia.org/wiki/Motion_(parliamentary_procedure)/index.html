Motion (parliamentary procedure)

A member moves a motion during a meeting

In parliamentary procedure, a motion is a formal proposal by a member of a deliberative assembly that the assembly take certain action. In non-legislative deliberative bodies such as a board, motions are the principal means by which members introduce business and reach decisions.

Process

A motion is typically introduced by a member saying "I move that…", may require a second from another member, and is then stated by the chair before debate. Most motions are amendable and debatable, and are decided by vote — usually a majority vote, although certain motions require a two-thirds majority. A motion properly before the assembly must be disposed of in some way before other business of lower precedence can be taken up.

Classification

Authorities such as Robert's Rules of Order classify motions as main motions, which bring business before the assembly, and secondary motions, including subsidiary motions (such as motions to amend, postpone, or refer to a committee), privileged motions (such as motions to adjourn or recess), and incidental motions (such as points of order and appeals). Each class has a defined order of precedence.

In legislatures

In legislatures such as the United Kingdom Parliament and the United States Congress, motions take specialised forms, including early day motions, motions of no confidence, and motions to proceed, governed by the standing orders and precedents of each body.
