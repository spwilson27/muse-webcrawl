Cold chain

Cold chain
Type: Temperature-controlled supply chain
Typical range: 2–8 °C (refrigerated); −20 °C or below (frozen)
Key users: Food, pharmaceutical industries

A cold chain is a temperature-controlled supply chain used to maintain perishable goods—such as food, pharmaceuticals, vaccines, and chemicals—within specified low-temperature ranges from production through storage, transport, and distribution to the end user. An unbroken cold chain is a series of uninterrupted refrigerated storage and distribution activities that preserve product quality and safety.

Components

Cold chains rely on refrigerated warehouses and vehicles, insulated packaging, phase-change materials and dry ice, temperature monitoring devices and data loggers, and trained personnel following validated handling procedures. Temperature excursions outside the permitted range can spoil food or reduce the potency of medicines.

Pharmaceutical cold chain

Many vaccines and biologics must be kept between 2 and 8 °C, while some products, including certain mRNA COVID-19 vaccines, initially required ultra-low temperatures around −70 °C. International guidelines from the World Health Organization govern vaccine cold chains, which use refrigerators, cold boxes, and vaccine carriers to reach remote clinics.

Food cold chain

In the food sector, cold chains extend the shelf life of meat, dairy, seafood, and fresh produce and reduce food waste and foodborne illness. Breaks in the chain are a major cause of spoilage, particularly in regions with unreliable electricity.

Environmental impact

Refrigeration consumes significant energy and historically used refrigerants with high global warming potential, prompting efforts to improve efficiency and adopt natural refrigerants.
