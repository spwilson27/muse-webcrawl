Knoevenagel condensation

Named afterEmil Knoevenagel
Reaction typeCoupling reaction
Productα,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound

The Knoevenagel condensation is an organic reaction in which a carbonyl compound (an aldehyde or ketone) condenses with a compound containing an active methylene group, such as malonic acid or its esters, in the presence of a weak base, typically an amine. The product is an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound, and water (or, after decarboxylation, carbon dioxide) is lost. The reaction is named after the German chemist Emil Knoevenagel, who described it in the 1890s.

Mechanism

The base deprotonates the active methylene compound to form a stabilised carbanion (enolate), which attacks the carbonyl carbon of the aldehyde or ketone. Depending on conditions, the mechanism may instead proceed through an iminium ion formed from the carbonyl compound and the amine catalyst. Subsequent dehydration yields the carbon–carbon double bond of the final product.

Applications

The condensation is a standard method for carbon–carbon bond formation and is used in the synthesis of fine chemicals, pharmaceuticals, dyes and polymers. A well-known application is the preparation of cinnamic acid derivatives. When malonic acid is used in pyridine with an amine catalyst, concomitant decarboxylation often occurs, a variant known as the Doebner modification. The reaction is also a step in the Hantzsch pyridine synthesis.
