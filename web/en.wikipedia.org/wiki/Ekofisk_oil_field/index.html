Ekofisk is an oil field in block 2/4 of the Norwegian sector of the North Sea, about 320 km southwest of Stavanger. Discovered in 1969 by Phillips Petroleum, it was the first major oil discovery in Norwegian waters and the find that launched Norway's petroleum era. Production began in 1971, initially through the converted tanker facilities and later permanent platforms.

The Ekofisk complex, among the largest offshore installations in the world.

Geology

The reservoir lies in fractured chalk of the Ekofisk and Tor formations, at roughly 3,000 metres depth beneath about 70 metres of water. The chalk's initially high porosity, maintained by overpressure, gave unusually high recovery for a chalk field; as pressure declined, the reservoir compacted dramatically.

Subsidence

Compaction of the chalk caused the seabed over Ekofisk to subside by several metres during the 1980s, reducing the air gap beneath the platforms. In a famous engineering operation in 1987, the entire Ekofisk platform complex was jacked up by about six metres. Water injection, begun to maintain pressure, later arrested and partly reversed the subsidence.

Later development

The field has been repeatedly redeveloped, with new platforms (including Ekofisk 2/4 L) extending its expected life toward 2050. Oil is exported by pipeline to Teesside, England, and gas to Emden, Germany. The nearby Bravo platform blowout of April 1977 — the first major North Sea blowout — led to significant tightening of offshore safety regulation.
