A price signal is information conveyed to consumers and producers through the price charged for a product or service, which helps coordinate economic activity in a market. Rising prices signal scarcity and encourage producers to supply more and consumers to economise; falling prices signal relative abundance and do the opposite.

Role in market economies

The informational role of prices was emphasised by Friedrich Hayek in his 1945 essay "The Use of Knowledge in Society", which argued that the price system aggregates dispersed knowledge that no central planner could collect: a rise in the price of tin, for example, tells users everywhere to conserve tin without their needing to know why tin became scarcer. Prices thus perform a signalling function alongside their rationing and incentive functions.

Distortions

Price signals can be distorted by taxes, subsidies, price controls, monopoly power and externalities — costs or benefits not borne by the transacting parties. Where externalities are significant, prices fail to signal true social cost, motivating corrective measures such as Pigouvian taxes or carbon pricing, which attempt to restore the informational content of prices. Speculation and asset bubbles are sometimes interpreted as breakdowns of price signalling, though defenders argue that speculative prices still aggregate information about expected future scarcity.

In planned economies

The economic calculation debate of the 1920s–30s centred on whether rational allocation is possible without market price signals; critics of central planning, following Ludwig von Mises and Hayek, held that abolishing market prices destroys the signals planners would need.
