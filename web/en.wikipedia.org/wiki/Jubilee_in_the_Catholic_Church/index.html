Jubilee in the Catholic Church

A jubilee is a special year of remission of sins and universal pardon in the Roman Catholic Church. During a jubilee, or Holy Year, the faithful can obtain plenary indulgences under prescribed conditions, traditionally including a pilgrimage to Rome and passing through the Holy Doors of the major basilicas.

Origins

The Catholic jubilee takes its name and inspiration from the biblical jubilee year of Leviticus 25, in which slaves were freed and debts forgiven every fiftieth year. The first Christian jubilee was proclaimed by Pope Boniface VIII in 1300, drawing enormous crowds of pilgrims to Rome; Dante, who set the Divine Comedy in that year, alluded to the crowds on the bridges of the city. The interval between jubilees was successively fixed at 100, then 50, then 33 years, before Paul II settled it at 25 years in 1470, the practice that has generally continued.

Ordinary and extraordinary jubilees

Ordinary jubilees occur at the regular 25-year interval: notable recent ones include 1975, the Great Jubilee of 2000 under John Paul II, and the 2025 jubilee proclaimed by Pope Francis with the theme "Pilgrims of Hope." Popes may also proclaim extraordinary jubilees for special occasions, such as the 1933 Holy Year of Redemption, the 1983 Holy Year, and the 2015-16 Extraordinary Jubilee of Mercy, during which Holy Doors were opened in cathedrals around the world, not only in Rome.

Rites and practices

A Holy Year traditionally begins at Christmas with the opening of the Holy Door (Porta Santa) of St Peter's Basilica, followed by the doors of the other papal basilicas - St John Lateran, St Mary Major and St Paul Outside the Walls. The doors are sealed again at the end of the year. Pilgrims who pass through them in a state of grace, with confession, communion and prayer for the Pope's intentions, may gain a plenary indulgence. Jubilees have repeatedly been occasions for major works of charity, reconciliation initiatives, and vast pilgrimages to Rome.
