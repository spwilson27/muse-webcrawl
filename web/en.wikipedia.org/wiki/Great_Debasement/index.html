Great Debasement

The Great Debasement (1544–1551) was a currency debasement policy introduced in England by the government of King Henry VIII and continued under his son Edward VI. The standard Tudor coinage, which had maintained high silver fineness for generations, was progressively reduced in precious-metal content so that the Crown could profit from striking more face value from the same quantity of bullion.

Course of the debasement

Beginning in 1544, the fineness of silver coinage was reduced from the sterling standard of 92.5% first to 75%, then in successive issues to 50%, 33%, and finally 25% silver. Gold coinage was also debased. The policy generated roughly £1.3 million in profit for the Crown, which helped finance wars against France and Scotland, but at the cost of monetary stability.

Effects

Debasement contributed substantially to the mid-Tudor price inflation. In accordance with what later became known as Gresham's law, older full-weight coins were hoarded or exported while base coins circulated, so some issues — notably the testoons of Henry VIII, whose thin silver wash wore off the king's nose — became notorious as "coppernoses." Foreign merchants discounted English coin, and the exchange rate deteriorated.

Recoinage

The debased currency was finally remedied under Elizabeth I, whose recoinage of 1560–61 called in the base coins and restored the sterling standard, laying a foundation for the relative monetary stability of her reign.
