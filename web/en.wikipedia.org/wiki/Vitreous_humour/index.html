Vitreous humour

The vitreous humour (also vitreous body or simply vitreous) is the clear gel that fills the space between the lens and the retina of the eyeball in humans and other vertebrates. It is often referred to as the vitreous body or, when removed from the eye, as the vitreous gel. The vitreous is the largest structure within the eye, occupying about four millilitres and roughly 80% of the globe's volume.

Structure and composition

The vitreous is composed of 98–99.7% water, with a network of collagen fibrils (mainly type II collagen) and the glycosaminoglycan hyaluronic acid giving it a gelatinous, viscoelastic consistency. It contains very few cells, chiefly hyalocytes in the cortex, and lacks blood vessels and nerves. The gel is enclosed by the vitreous membrane and is attached most firmly at the vitreous base near the ora serrata, at the optic disc, around retinal vessels and at the fovea; the patellar fossa cradles the lens.

Function

The vitreous maintains the shape of the eyeball, holds the retina against the retinal pigment epithelium, and provides a clear optical pathway for light. It also acts as a shock absorber and a reservoir and diffusion medium for nutrients and metabolites of the lens and retina.

Development and ageing

The primary vitreous forms during embryogenesis around the hyaloid vessels, which normally regress, leaving the secondary (adult) vitreous secreted by the retina. With age the gel gradually liquefies (syneresis) and its collagen network aggregates, producing floaters — shadows of the fibres cast on the retina. The shrinking gel may separate from the retina in a posterior vitreous detachment, which is common after middle age and usually harmless, though it can occasionally tear the retina and lead to retinal detachment.

Clinical significance

Blood in the vitreous (vitreous haemorrhage) can result from diabetic retinopathy or trauma. Surgeons may remove and replace the vitreous in a vitrectomy to treat retinal detachment, macular holes and other posterior segment disease. Unlike aqueous humour, the vitreous is essentially not regenerated after removal; it is replaced by fluid resembling aqueous.
