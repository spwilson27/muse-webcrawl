Corpus callosotomy

Corpus callosotomy
SpecialtyNeurosurgery
PurposePalliative treatment of intractable epilepsy
First performed1940, by William P. van Wagenen

A corpus callosotomy is a palliative neurosurgical procedure that cuts the corpus callosum, the broad band of nerve fibres connecting the two cerebral hemispheres. By severing the main pathway along which seizure activity spreads from one hemisphere to the other, the operation reduces the severity and frequency of certain types of epileptic seizures, particularly atonic ("drop") seizures in patients who are not candidates for curative focal resection.

History

The procedure was first performed in 1940 by the American neurosurgeon William P. van Wagenen, who had noticed that a patient whose tumour had destroyed part of the corpus callosum experienced less severe seizures. In the 1960s, studies of callosotomy patients — the famous "split-brain" research of Roger Sperry, Michael Gazzaniga and colleagues, initially in animals and later in humans — transformed understanding of hemispheric specialisation and consciousness, work recognised by Sperry's Nobel Prize in 1981.

Procedure

Through a craniotomy, the surgeon sections part or all of the corpus callosum. Many surgeons perform a partial (anterior two-thirds) callosotomy first, completing the section in a second operation if seizures persist, since sparing the posterior portion reduces disconnection effects. Less invasive variants using radiosurgery or laser ablation have also been developed.

Effects and risks

Callosotomy does not usually abolish seizures but can markedly reduce drop attacks and injuries. The divided brain may produce "disconnection syndrome": the hemispheres cannot share information, so a patient may be unable to name an object felt with the left hand (whose sensory signals reach the right, usually non-speaking hemisphere), and rare complications such as alien hand syndrome can occur. Most everyday function is surprisingly well preserved because subcortical pathways and environmental cues remain.
