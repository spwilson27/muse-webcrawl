Corporal punishment

Corporal punishment or physical punishment is the infliction of physical pain upon a person as a penalty for an offence or to discipline them. Common methods have included spanking, caning, flogging, birching, paddling, and whipping. Historically it was imposed by courts (judicial corporal punishment), schools, the military and navy, prisons, and parents in the home (domestic corporal punishment). Its use has declined markedly since the twentieth century under pressure from human-rights law and child-development research, though it remains legal in various forms in many countries.

History

Corporal punishment is documented in the earliest legal codes and was for centuries a standard sentence for theft, vagrancy, and offences by servants, slaves, soldiers, and sailors. Naval flogging with the cat o' nine tails, army flogging, and the stocks and pillory were characteristic punishments of early modern Europe. Britain abolished judicial corporal punishment for most offences in 1948 (it survived for prison offences until 1967), and flogging in the Royal Navy was suspended in 1879 and never resumed. Many jurisdictions retained whipping into the mid-twentieth century; today judicial caning or flogging is still prescribed in countries including Singapore, Malaysia, Brunei, and parts of the Middle East and Africa, usually for male offenders and specific offences such as drug trafficking and sexual crimes in Singapore.

Schools

School corporal punishment — caning in Britain and the Commonwealth, paddling in the United States — was near-universal historically. Sweden banned all corporal punishment of children in 1979, the first country to do so; Britain banned it in state schools in 1986 and in private schools by 2003. As of the 2020s, more than sixty countries have prohibited all corporal punishment of children, including in the home, following the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child; however, school corporal punishment remains lawful in a number of U.S. states (practised mainly in the rural South) and in many countries in Africa and Asia.

Domestic punishment and research

Parental corporal punishment, typically spanking, is the most widespread form. Large meta-analyses, notably by Elizabeth Gershoff and colleagues, associate it with increased childhood aggression and antisocial behaviour and find little evidence of benefit beyond immediate compliance, leading bodies such as the American Academy of Pediatrics (2018 policy statement) to advise against it. Defenders argue that mild, non-abusive spanking by loving parents is harmless or effective; most child-development organizations dispute this on the evidence.

International law

The UN Committee on the Rights of the Child interprets the Convention as requiring the prohibition of all corporal punishment of children, however light. Judicial corporal punishment is regarded by the UN Human Rights Committee as capable of breaching the prohibition on cruel, inhuman or degrading punishment in the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights.
