The 2000 United States census, conducted by the Census Bureau, determined the resident population of the United States on 1 April 2000 to be 281,421,906, an increase of 13.2 percent over the 248,709,873 people counted in 1990. It was the twenty-second federal census and, at the time, the largest and most expensive in American history.

Overview

The census used a short-form questionnaire for most households and a long form, sent to roughly one in six households, collecting detailed social, housing and economic data. For the first time, respondents could identify themselves as belonging to more than one race, following revised federal standards adopted in 1997; about 2.4 percent of respondents selected two or more races.

Results and apportionment

California remained the most populous state with nearly 34 million residents, while Wyoming was the least populous. Population growth was fastest in the South and West — Nevada grew by 66 percent — driving a shift of congressional seats: states such as Georgia, Arizona and Texas gained seats in the House of Representatives, while New York and Pennsylvania each lost two. The results were used to redraw congressional and state legislative districts for the following decade.

Controversies

The 2000 census was preceded by a political and legal dispute over whether statistical sampling could be used to adjust for the undercount of minority and urban populations. In 1999 the Supreme Court ruled that sampling could not be used for congressional apportionment, though adjusted figures could be released for other purposes.
