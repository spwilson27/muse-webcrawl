Terza rima

Terza rima (Italian: [ˈtɛrtsa ˈriːma], also US: /ˈtɛərtsə -/) is a rhyming verse stanza form that consists of an interlocking three-line rhyme scheme. It was first used by the Italian poet Dante Alighieri in his Divine Comedy. The form uses tercets (three-line stanzas) in which the second line of each stanza rhymes with the first and third lines of the next, giving the pattern ABA BCB CDC DED and so on.

Structure

Each tercet advances the rhyme of the middle line, which becomes the outer rhyme of the following stanza, creating a chain that pulls the poem forward. The poem ends with a single line or a couplet that rhymes with the middle line of the final tercet. Dante wrote the Divine Comedy in hendecasyllabic (eleven-syllable) lines; English adaptations usually use iambic pentameter.

Use in English

Because English is relatively rhyme-poor compared with Italian, terza rima is difficult to sustain in English. Nevertheless it has been used by Geoffrey Chaucer (in his Complaint to His Lady), Percy Bysshe Shelley ("Ode to the West Wind" uses a terza rima sonnet variant), Lord Byron, W. H. Auden, T. S. Eliot (in part of "Little Gidding"), and Robert Frost ("Acquainted with the Night"). Many translators of Dante adopt it, while others use blank verse or prose.
