Total war

Total war is a type of warfare that includes any and all civilian-associated resources and infrastructure as legitimate military targets, mobilizes all of the resources of society to fight the war, and gives priority to warfare over non-combatant needs.

The term has been defined as "a war that is unrestricted in terms of the weapons used, the territory or combatants involved, or the objectives pursued, especially one in which the laws of war are disregarded". The nineteenth-century Prussian general and theorist Carl von Clausewitz described war's tendency toward the extreme — "absolute war" — in his work On War. The German general Erich Ludendorff popularized the term Der totale Krieg in his 1935 memoir of the First World War.

History

Historians differ over when total war began. Candidates include the campaigns of the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars, with mass conscription (the levée en masse of 1793) putting whole societies on a war footing; the American Civil War, with Sherman's March to the Sea deliberately targeting Confederate infrastructure and morale; and the two world wars of the twentieth century.

In the Second World War, total war was practised on an unprecedented scale: conscription of labour including women, rationing, the strategic bombing of cities (the Blitz, Hamburg, Dresden, Tokyo), unconditional surrender demands, genocide, and the complete reordering of national economies for war production. On the Eastern Front and in Asia, warfare erased most distinctions between soldiers and civilians, and civilian deaths outnumbered military ones.

Nuclear age

The advent of nuclear weapons made "total war" between great powers potentially suicidal; the Cold War was instead characterized by limited and proxy wars. Historians and strategists debate whether total war in its classic form can recur under conditions of nuclear deterrence.
