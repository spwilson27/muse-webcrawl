Nikola Tesla

Nikola Tesla (1856–1943) was a Serbian-American inventor, electrical engineer and futurist whose work laid the foundations of modern alternating-current power systems. The SI unit of magnetic flux density, the tesla, is named in his honour.

Early life and Edison

Tesla was born in Smiljan, in the Austrian Empire (now Croatia), to a Serbian Orthodox priest. He studied engineering at Graz and Prague, worked on telephony and electrical equipment in Budapest and Paris, and emigrated to New York in 1884 to work for Thomas Edison. The two soon fell out — over money, by Tesla's account — and Tesla struck out on his own, developing his AC induction motor and polyphase system, patented in 1888 and licensed to George Westinghouse.

War of the currents and Niagara

Tesla's polyphase AC triumphed in the "war of the currents" against Edison's direct current: Westinghouse lit the 1893 Chicago World's Columbian Exposition with AC, and the Adams Power Plant at Niagara Falls (1896) transmitted AC power to Buffalo, inaugurating large-scale hydroelectric distribution. Tesla's other work included the Tesla coil, fluorescent lighting experiments, remote control (demonstrated with a radio-guided boat in 1898), and pioneering observations in radio and X-rays.

Later years

His grand projects — the Wardenclyffe Tower on Long Island, meant to provide wireless power and communications worldwide — collapsed when financing was withdrawn, and the tower was demolished in 1917. Tesla spent his later decades in New York hotels, increasingly impoverished and reclusive, feeding pigeons and announcing unbuilt inventions. He died alone in the Hotel New Yorker in January 1943. His reputation has grown enormously since, and his name now fronts an electric-car company founded in his honour in 2003.
