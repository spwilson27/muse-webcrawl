Throwing stick

A throwing stick, or throw stick, is a wooden tool used to hurl projectiles farther and harder than by hand alone. The term covers two related implements: the spear-thrower (such as the atlatl or woomera), a hooked stick that acts as an extension of the arm when launching a dart or spear, and throwing sticks proper, heavy curved sticks thrown directly at game, of which the Australian boomerang is a specialised aerodynamic form.

Spear-throwers

The spear-thrower works on the principle of leverage: the dart rests along the stick with its butt against a hook or spur, and the thrower's arm motion propels both stick and dart, adding the stick's length to the effective arm and imparting greater velocity and range. Spear-throwers are among the oldest known hunting technologies, attested in Upper Palaeolithic Europe (including decorated examples from Magdalenian sites) and used worldwide: the atlatl of Mesoamerica (depicted in Aztec art and feared by Spanish conquistadors), the woomera of Aboriginal Australia, and throwing boards of Arctic peoples.

Throwing sticks proper

Non-returning throwing sticks — sometimes called rabbit sticks — were used for hunting small game and birds in many cultures, including ancient Egypt (depicted in tomb paintings of fowling in the marshes), the Hopi of the American Southwest, and Aboriginal Australians, whose heavy hunting boomerangs double as clubs and digging tools. Returning boomerangs, by contrast, were used mainly for sport and recreation.
