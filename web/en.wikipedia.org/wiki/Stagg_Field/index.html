Stagg Field

Stagg Field is an athletic field on the campus of the University of Chicago in Chicago, Illinois. It is named after Amos Alonzo Stagg, the university's legendary football coach. The site is famous in the history of science: beneath the west stands of the original Stagg Field, on 2 December 1942, scientists of the Manhattan Project led by Enrico Fermi achieved the first controlled, self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction in the reactor known as Chicago Pile-1.

History

The original field, first known as Marshall Field, was the home of the Chicago Maroons football team during the era when Chicago was a power in the Big Ten Conference under Stagg, who coached from 1892 to 1932. The stadium was renamed for Stagg and at its peak held more than 50,000 spectators.

In 1942 the stands became the unlikely setting for one of the most consequential experiments of the twentieth century. In a squash court under the west stands, Fermi's team stacked graphite blocks and uranium into a "pile" and, on the afternoon of 2 December 1942, withdrew the control rods to achieve criticality. The reaction ran for about 28 minutes before being shut down. The coded message relayed to Washington — "The Italian navigator has landed in the new world" — announced that the atomic age had begun.

The modern field

The original stadium was demolished in 1957. The Joseph Regenstein Library was built on part of the site, and a new Stagg Field — a smaller athletics facility with a running track — was constructed nearby and dedicated in the 1990s. A plaque and Henry Moore's bronze sculpture Nuclear Energy, unveiled on the 25th anniversary of the experiment in 1967, commemorate the chain reaction. The site is a designated National Historic Landmark.
