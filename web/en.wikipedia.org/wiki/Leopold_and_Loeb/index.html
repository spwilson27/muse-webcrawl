Nathan Freudenthal Leopold Jr. (November 19, 1904 – August 29, 1971) and Richard Albert Loeb (June 11, 1905 – January 28, 1936), usually referred to collectively as Leopold and Loeb, were two wealthy University of Chicago students who kidnapped and murdered 14-year-old Robert "Bobby" Franks in Chicago on May 21, 1924. They committed the murder—characterized at the time as "the crime of the century"—as a demonstration of their supposed intellectual superiority, which, they believed, rendered them exempt from the laws and moral norms binding ordinary people.

The crime

Leopold, a child prodigy and ornithologist, and Loeb, who had graduated from the University of Michigan at 17, had previously committed a series of petty crimes together. Influenced by a crude reading of Nietzsche's concept of the Übermensch, they planned a "perfect crime": the kidnapping and murder of a young boy, followed by a ransom demand to test their ingenuity. Franks, a neighbor and Loeb's second cousin, was lured into their rented car, struck with a chisel, and smothered; his body was concealed in a culvert near Wolf Lake in Hammond, Indiana, and a ransom note typed on a stolen typewriter was sent to his parents. The plot unraveled within days when a pair of distinctive eyeglasses Leopold had dropped near the body was traced to him; both men confessed.

Trial

The families hired Clarence Darrow, the era's most famous defense attorney, who unexpectedly entered guilty pleas to avoid a jury trial and instead argued at a sentencing hearing against the death penalty. In a celebrated twelve-hour summation, Darrow appealed to science, youth, and mercy, denouncing capital punishment. Judge John R. Caverly sentenced both to life imprisonment for murder plus 99 years for kidnapping.

Aftermath

Loeb was killed by a fellow inmate at Stateville Penitentiary in 1936. Leopold, a model prisoner who helped run the prison school, was paroled in 1958 and moved to Puerto Rico, where he married, worked in health services, and wrote an autobiography. The case has inspired numerous plays and films, including Alfred Hitchcock's Rope (1948) and Compulsion (1959), as well as the musical Thrill Me.
