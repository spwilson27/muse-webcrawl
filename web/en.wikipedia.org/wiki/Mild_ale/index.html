Mild ale

Mild ale is a traditional style of British beer characterized by low hop bitterness, a malty, often sweet and lightly roasted flavor, and — in its modern form — modest alcohol content, typically 3% to 3.6% ABV. Historically the word "mild" simply meant fresh or unaged ale, as opposed to aged "stale" beer; only over time did it come to denote a distinct, lightly hopped style.

History

In the 18th and 19th centuries, mild ales could be strong — often 6% or more — and "mild" described youth rather than strength. The style as now known emerged as gravity and alcohol fell under the pressure of temperance sentiment, malt taxes, and the two World Wars' restrictions. By the mid-20th century mild was the drink of industrial workers across the Midlands and North of England and Wales, sold in huge volumes as a cheap, sessionable draught beer.

From the 1960s, mild declined steeply as lager and keg bitter rose, and by the 1990s it was considered endangered. CAMRA championed its revival with campaigns such as "Make May a Mild Month", and British craft brewers, followed by American ones, have renewed interest in both dark and pale milds.

Characteristics

Milds are usually dark brown from roasted and crystal malts, with flavors of caramel, chocolate, toast, and dried fruit, though pale ("light") milds also exist. Bitterness is restrained, carbonation low when cask-conditioned, and the emphasis is on drinkability. Modern style guidelines list alcohol roughly from 3% to 4.5% ABV, with historical and "strong mild" examples exceeding that.
