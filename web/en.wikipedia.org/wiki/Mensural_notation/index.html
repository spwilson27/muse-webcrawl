Mensural notation

Mensural notation is the musical notation system used for European vocal polyphonic music from the later part of the 13th century until about 1600. The term "mensural" refers to the ability of the system to describe precisely measured rhythmic durations in terms of the numerical relationships between note values.

Note values

Unlike the earlier modal notation of the Notre Dame school, in which rhythm was inferred from patterns of note groupings (ligatures), mensural notation assigned each note shape a definite durational value. The main note values, from longest to shortest, were the maxima, longa, breve, semibreve, minim, semiminim, fusa, and semifusa. Each value was normally worth either two or three of the next smaller value, depending on the mensuration.

Mensuration

The rhythmic organization of a piece was governed by four hierarchical levels: modus (relation of maxima to longa), tempus (relation of breve to semibreve), and prolatio (relation of semibreve to minim). A division into three was called "perfect" and into two "imperfect". Mensuration signs placed at the beginning of a staff — circles and semicircles, sometimes with dots or strokes — indicated the combination in force, and are the ancestors of modern time signatures. Authors such as Franco of Cologne (c. 1260) and Philippe de Vitry (Ars nova, c. 1320) codified the system.

Other features

Mensural notation employed ligatures with special rules for their rhythmic interpretation, rests with exact durations, and techniques such as coloration — notes written in red (later, filled-in black) to indicate temporary rhythmic alteration, often hemiola. "White mensural notation", in which the longer note values were written as unfilled outlines, became standard in the 15th century as paper replaced parchment. The system gradually evolved into the modern bar-line notation during the 17th century.
