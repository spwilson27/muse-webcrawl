Miller–Urey experiment

The Miller–Urey experiment (or Miller experiment) was a chemical experiment carried out in 1952 that simulated the conditions thought to exist on the early, prebiotic Earth, in order to test the chemical origin of life under those conditions. The experiment used water (H2O), methane (CH4), ammonia (NH3), and hydrogen (H2) in a sealed apparatus, and applied a continuous electrical spark discharge to simulate lightning. Stanley Miller, working under Harold Urey at the University of Chicago, found that the experiment produced amino acids and other organic compounds, demonstrating that organic compounds could be formed spontaneously from inorganic precursors under simulated early-Earth conditions.

History

The experiment grew out of the Oparin–Haldane hypothesis, proposed independently by Alexander Oparin and J. B. S. Haldane in the 1920s, which suggested that the early Earth's atmosphere was chemically reducing and that organic compounds could form gradually from simple inorganic molecules. Miller, a graduate student, proposed testing the idea experimentally, and Urey agreed to supervise. The results were published in the journal Science in 1953 in a paper titled "A Production of Amino Acids Under Possible Primitive Earth Conditions".

The experiment

The apparatus consisted of a closed system of glass flasks and tubes. Water in a small flask was heated to produce vapor, which mixed with the gases of the "atmosphere" — methane, ammonia, and hydrogen. Sparks were fired continuously between electrodes in the atmosphere flask to simulate lightning. The mixture then passed through a condenser, and the condensed products collected in a trap representing the ocean. After about a week of continuous operation, the water in the trap had turned pink, and analysis revealed the presence of amino acids including glycine, alanine, and aspartic acid, along with other organic compounds.

Results and significance

About 10–15% of the carbon in the system was converted into organic compounds, and around 2% formed amino acids. Later re-analyses of Miller's original samples, using modern techniques, found more than 20 different amino acids, a richer yield than Miller himself had reported. The experiment is widely considered a classic in the study of abiogenesis and prebiotic chemistry, showing that key building blocks of life can arise through natural processes. Later work suggested the early atmosphere may have been less reducing than Miller assumed, though volcanic gases and other conditions could still have produced similar chemistry.
