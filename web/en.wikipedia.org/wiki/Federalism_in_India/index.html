Federalism in India

Federalism in India refers to the division of powers between the Union government and the governments of the states under the Constitution of India, adopted in 1950. India is described in Article 1 as a "Union of States"; scholars such as K. C. Wheare called the system "quasi-federal" because of its strong centralising features, while others describe it as federalism with a unitary bias.

Constitutional scheme

The Seventh Schedule divides legislative subjects into three lists: the Union List (defence, foreign affairs, railways, banking and other matters of national importance), the State List (police, public health, agriculture, local government), and the Concurrent List (education, forests, marriage, bankruptcy), on which both may legislate, with Union law prevailing in case of conflict. Residuary powers lie with the Union. India has a single citizenship, an integrated judiciary headed by the Supreme Court, and All-India Services whose officers staff both levels.

Centralising features

The Union can legislate on state subjects in the national interest (with Rajya Sabha approval), during a proclaimed emergency, or when a state's constitutional machinery is said to have failed (President's Rule under Article 356). The President appoints state governors, and Parliament may alter state boundaries. Fiscal dependence of the states on central transfers, moderated by the Finance Commission's tax-sharing recommendations, is another centralising force.

Evolution

The States Reorganisation Act of 1956 redrew state boundaries largely along linguistic lines. The rise of regional parties and coalition governments from 1989 strengthened practical federalism, and the Supreme Court's judgment in S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) limited the misuse of Article 356. Bodies such as the Inter-State Council and, since 2015, NITI Aayog promote "cooperative federalism"; the Goods and Services Tax Council (2016) is a major experiment in pooled fiscal sovereignty.
