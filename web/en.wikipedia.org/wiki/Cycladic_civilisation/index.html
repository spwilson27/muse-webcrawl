Cycladic civilisation

Cycladic civilisation (also known as Cycladic culture) is a Bronze Age culture (c. 3100 – c. 1000 BC) found throughout the islands of the Cyclades in the Aegean Sea. In chronological terms, it is a relative dating system for artefacts which serves as a mnemonic for the main sequence of the Cycladic period; it roughly corresponds to the Helladic and Minoan systems. The culture is best known for its schematic flat female idols carved out of the islands' pure white marble.

Chronology

The Early Cycladic period is divided into three phases: Early Cycladic I (Grotta–Pelos culture, c. 3100–2600 BC), Early Cycladic II (Keros–Syros culture, c. 2600–2300 BC), and Early Cycladic III (Kastri group, c. 2300–2000 BC). During the Middle Cycladic period towns such as Phylakopi on Melos grew, and from around 2000 BC the islands came increasingly under the influence of Minoan Crete. In the Late Cycladic period, sites such as Akrotiri on Thera flourished until the Thera eruption (c. 1600 BC); later, Mycenaean influence predominated.

Art and society

The most distinctive products of the culture are marble vessels and figurines. Most figurines depict nude females with arms folded across the abdomen; the canonical type is thought to be associated with fertility or with funerary ritual, since most excavated examples come from graves. Other figures include musicians playing the harp and flute, and warriors. The islanders were accomplished seafarers: longboats depicted on ceramic "frying pans" and lead models suggest extensive maritime trade in obsidian from Melos, metals, and marble across the Aegean. Settlements were small, often fortified, and cemeteries with rectangular or circular tombs were placed outside them.

Excavation and looting

Because Cycladic figurines are highly prized on the art market, extensive looting of graves has destroyed much contextual information; a large proportion of known figurines lack secure provenance. Major museum collections are held by the Museum of Cycladic Art and the National Archaeological Museum in Athens.
