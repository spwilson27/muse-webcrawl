Handedness

DistributionRoughly 90% right-handed, 10% left-handed worldwide
Related termsAmbidexterity, cross-dominance, chirality

Handedness is a human attribute reflecting an individual's preferential use of one hand — the dominant hand — for tasks such as writing, throwing, and using tools, owing to the hand's superior fine motor skill and strength. Most people are right-handed; a minority, around ten percent in most populations studied, are left-handed, and a smaller number are ambidextrous or mixed-handed, preferring different hands for different tasks.

Types

Researchers distinguish right-handedness, left-handedness, mixed-handedness (preference varies by task), and ambidexterity (genuinely equal skill with both hands, which is rare). Hand preference usually emerges in early childhood and is well established by the time children begin school, though it can be identified earlier from reaching and grasping patterns.

Causes

Handedness arises from a complex interaction of genetic, developmental, and environmental factors. It is associated with asymmetry of the brain: in most right-handers, and a majority of left-handers, language is lateralised to the left hemisphere. Twin and family studies show a substantial hereditary component, though no single "handedness gene" determines it. Prenatal hormone exposure, asymmetrical development of the spinal cord and motor cortex, and cultural pressure have all been investigated; in many societies children were historically forced to write with the right hand, suppressing measured rates of left-handedness.

Distribution and correlations

The proportion of left-handers varies somewhat between cultures, from under 3 percent where social pressure against it is strong to around 12–13 percent in many Western countries. Left-handedness is slightly more common in men than women. Studies have reported statistical associations between handedness and various cognitive, health, and sporting outcomes, most of them small and contested; left-handers are over-represented in some interactive sports such as boxing, fencing, and table tennis, plausibly because opponents encounter them less often.

In other animals

Individual limb preferences occur in many species — parrots favour one foot for holding food, and primates show hand preferences for particular tasks — but the strong population-level bias toward one side found in humans appears to be unusual, and is often linked to the evolution of language and tool use.
