The Panic of 1819 was the first widespread financial crisis in the United States, marking the end of the economic expansion that had followed the War of 1812 and ushering in a depression that lasted into 1821. It featured collapsing land and commodity prices, bank failures, foreclosures and high unemployment, especially in the western states.

Causes

After the war, state banks multiplied and issued paper money freely, fuelling a speculative boom in western public lands and cotton. In 1818 the Second Bank of the United States, under Langdon Cheves, sharply reversed course, demanding specie payment from state banks and calling in loans. At the same time, falling European demand and renewed competition drove cotton prices down by roughly half, and land values collapsed.

Effects

Banks failed across the South and West; thousands of borrowers lost farms to foreclosure, and unemployment rose sharply in cities such as Philadelphia and Pittsburgh. The crisis produced lasting political resentment against the Bank of the United States and banks generally, fuelled demands for debtor relief laws in states such as Kentucky, and reached the Supreme Court in cases concerning state relief legislation. It also shaped the monetary politics of the Jacksonian era.
