Crab Pulsar

The Crab Pulsar (PSR B0531+21) is a relatively young neutron star at the centre of the Crab Nebula, the remnant of the supernova SN 1054 recorded by Chinese and Arab astronomers in 1054. It was discovered in 1968 and was the first pulsar to be connected with a supernova remnant.

Characteristics

The pulsar rotates about 30 times per second, emitting beams of radiation from gamma rays to radio waves that sweep past Earth like a lighthouse. Its rotation is gradually slowing as it loses energy; the energy radiated away — about 1031 watts of spin-down power — is what lights up the surrounding nebula, making the Crab visible across the electromagnetic spectrum. The slowdown is occasionally interrupted by sudden spin-ups called glitches, attributed to rearrangements inside the neutron star.

Significance

Because its age is known exactly from historical records, the Crab Pulsar is a key object for testing theories of neutron-star formation, pulsar emission and spin-down. Its pulses were detected at optical wavelengths shortly after discovery, making it one of the first optical pulsars known.
