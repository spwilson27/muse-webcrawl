Graph isomorphism

Two graphs that are isomorphic, although their drawings look different.

In graph theory, an isomorphism of graphs G and H is a bijection between the vertex sets of G and H, f : V(G) → V(H), such that any two vertices u and v of G are adjacent in G if and only if f(u) and f(v) are adjacent in H. Two graphs that contain such an isomorphism are called isomorphic graphs.

The formal notion of "isomorphism" captures the informal idea that two graphs have "the same structure": they differ only in the names or drawing positions of their vertices, not in their connectivity.

Definition

Formally, given graphs G = (VG, EG) and H = (VH, EH), an isomorphism is a bijective map f : VG → VH such that for all u, v ∈ VG, {u, v} ∈ EG if and only if {f(u), f(v)} ∈ EH. When such a map exists, G and H are isomorphic, written G ≅ H. A mapping of a graph onto itself is called an automorphism.

Computational complexity

The graph isomorphism problem is the computational problem of determining whether two finite graphs are isomorphic. It is one of the few problems in NP that is known to be neither solvable in polynomial time nor NP-complete (unless the polynomial hierarchy collapses). It lies in NP ∩ co-AM, and its exact complexity status is a famous open question in theoretical computer science.

In December 2015, László Babai announced a quasipolynomial-time algorithm for the problem, running in time exp((log n)O(1)); a claimed gap in the analysis was identified by Harald Helfgott and repaired by Babai in 2017. Practical algorithms, based largely on vertex refinement and individualisation as pioneered by the nauty program of Brendan McKay, solve almost all instances quickly.

Related problems include subgraph isomorphism, which is NP-complete, and graph canonization, which computes a canonical labelling of a graph.

Recognition of graph families

For many special classes of graphs, isomorphism can be tested in polynomial time: for example, trees, planar graphs, graphs of bounded degree, graphs of bounded treewidth and interval graphs. Testing isomorphism is used in practice in chemistry (comparing molecular structures), network analysis, pattern recognition and computer-aided design.

Whitney's theorem

Whitney's isomorphism theorem, due to Hassler Whitney (1932), states that two connected graphs are isomorphic if and only if their line graphs are isomorphic, with a single exception: the triangle K3 and the claw K1,3 are not isomorphic, but both have K3 as their line graph.
