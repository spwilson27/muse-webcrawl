Paul Celan

Paul Celan (23 November 1920 – c. 20 April 1970), born Paul Antschel (later Ancel) in Czernowitz, Romania (now Chernivtsi, Ukraine), was a Romanian-born German-language poet and translator. He is regarded as one of the major German-language poets of the post-World War II era and the pre-eminent poet of the Holocaust.

Life

Celan was born into a German-speaking Jewish family. His parents were deported and died in Nazi camps in 1942; Celan himself endured a Romanian labour camp. After the war he worked in Bucharest as a translator and editor, published his first poems, and moved via Vienna to Paris in 1948, where he lived for the rest of his life, teaching German literature at the École Normale Supérieure.

Work

His best-known poem, "Todesfuge" ("Death Fugue", written c. 1945, published 1948), confronts the Holocaust in fugue-like, incantatory German. Collections include Mohn und Gedächtnis (1952), Sprachgitter (1959), Die Niemandsrose (1963) and Atemwende (1967). His later poetry grew increasingly compressed, fractured and neologistic, wrestling with the capacity of the German language — the language of the murderers — to bear witness. Celan received the Georg Büchner Prize in 1960, with a famous acceptance speech, "The Meridian".

Suffering from depression, Celan drowned himself in the Seine in Paris in April 1970.
