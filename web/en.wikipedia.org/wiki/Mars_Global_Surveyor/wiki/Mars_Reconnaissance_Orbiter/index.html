The Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (MRO) is a spacecraft launched by NASA on August 12, 2005, to study the geology and climate of Mars, provide reconnaissance of future landing sites, and relay data from surface missions back to Earth. It entered Mars orbit on March 10, 2006, and after a period of aerobraking began its primary science phase in November 2006. It remains one of the most productive Mars missions ever flown, having returned more data than all other interplanetary missions combined at the time.

Instruments

MRO carries the HiRISE camera, the most powerful telescopic camera ever sent to another planet, capable of resolving features about one metre across from orbit. Other instruments include the CRISM imaging spectrometer, which maps surface mineralogy and identified hydrated minerals associated with past water; the CTX context camera; the MARCI weather camera; the Shallow Radar (SHARAD), which probes subsurface ice deposits; and an optical navigation camera used for precise orbit determination.

Discoveries

Data from MRO revealed vast buried glaciers and ground ice at mid-latitudes, evidence of recent gully-forming flows, recurring slope lineae that may involve briny water, and polar layered deposits recording climate change. HiRISE imaged the Phoenix lander descending by parachute in 2008 and the Curiosity rover's descent in 2012, and continues to monitor surface changes such as new impact craters and avalanches.

Relay role

MRO serves as a key communications relay for rovers and landers on the Martian surface, including Curiosity and Perseverance, transmitting their data to Earth at rates far higher than the landers could achieve directly.
