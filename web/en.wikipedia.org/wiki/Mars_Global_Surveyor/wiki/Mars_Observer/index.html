Mars Observer was a robotic space probe launched by NASA on September 25, 1992, to study the Martian surface, atmosphere, climate, and magnetic field. It was the first Mars mission launched by the United States since the Viking program in the 1970s. Contact with the spacecraft was lost on August 21, 1993, three days before it was scheduled to enter orbit around Mars, and the mission was declared a failure.

Background

The Mars Observer mission was conceived as the first of a series of "Observer" class planetary missions designed to use modified Earth-orbiting satellite technology to reduce cost. The spacecraft bus was derived from the RCA Satcom and DMSP/TIROS satellite designs. It carried a suite of instruments including the Mars Observer Camera (MOC), the Mars Orbiter Laser Altimeter (MOLA), the Thermal Emission Spectrometer (TES), the Pressure Modulator Infrared Radiometer (PMIRR), a magnetometer, and the Gamma Ray Spectrometer (GRS).

Loss of the spacecraft

On August 21, 1993, during preparations for the orbit insertion maneuver, ground controllers lost telemetry from the spacecraft. Despite repeated attempts, communication was never re-established. The most probable cause identified by the investigation board was a rupture of the fuel pressurization system: leaking monomethylhydrazine fuel likely mixed with nitrogen tetroxide oxidizer in a titanium pressurant line, causing an explosion that damaged the spacecraft's transmitters and electrical systems as the tanks were pressurized in preparation for the main engine burn.

Legacy

Although Mars Observer failed, its instruments were reflown on later missions. Descendants of its payload flew aboard Mars Global Surveyor (1996), Mars Odyssey (2001), and Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (2005), which together returned much of the science Mars Observer had been intended to collect. The failure also prompted NASA to adopt the "faster, better, cheaper" philosophy of smaller, more frequent missions.
