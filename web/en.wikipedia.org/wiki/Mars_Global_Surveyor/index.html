Mars Global Surveyor

Mars Global Surveyor (MGS) was an American robotic space probe developed by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory and launched in November 1996. It began the United States' return to Mars after the loss of the Mars Observer spacecraft in 1993. MGS was a global mapping mission designed to examine the entire planet, from the ionosphere down through the atmosphere to the surface and deep interior. Originally planned as a two-year mission, it operated for nearly a decade—until contact was lost in November 2006—returning more data about Mars than all previous missions combined. Its observations transformed understanding of Martian topography, magnetic history, and the evidence for past and possibly present liquid water on Mars.

Mission profile

MGS launched on 7 November 1996 aboard a Delta II rocket from Cape Canaveral and arrived at Mars on 12 September 1997. The plan called for aerobraking—using the friction of the Martian upper atmosphere over many orbits to shrink the spacecraft's elongated orbit into a near-circular mapping orbit. During early aerobraking, one of the two solar panels, which had been damaged shortly after launch, flexed more than expected, forcing mission managers to adopt a gentler, much longer aerobraking campaign. Mapping finally began in March 1999, more than a year behind schedule. The primary mission ran through January 2001, after which NASA approved a series of extended missions; the orbiter also served as a communications relay for the Mars Exploration Rovers in 2004 and attempted to photograph other spacecraft, including the failed Mars Polar Lander.

Instruments

The spacecraft carried a suite of instruments largely derived from the lost Mars Observer: the Mars Orbiter Camera (MOC), which returned more than 240,000 images, including wide-angle weather monitoring and narrow-angle views resolving features a few meters across; the Mars Orbiter Laser Altimeter (MOLA), which produced the first precise global topographic map of Mars; the Thermal Emission Spectrometer (TES), which mapped surface mineralogy; a magnetometer/electron reflectometer, which discovered crustal magnetic anomalies; an ultrastable oscillator for radio-science gravity measurements; and the Mars Relay antenna.

Key findings

MOLA's topography revealed the dramatic dichotomy between Mars's smooth northern lowlands and cratered southern highlands, measured the heights of the great Tharsis volcanoes, and showed the dimensions of the Hellas impact basin, among the largest in the Solar System. The magnetometer found striped patterns of magnetized crust in the southern hemisphere, evidence that Mars once had an internally generated magnetic field and possibly plate-tectonic-like processes early in its history, but no global field today. TES identified coarse-grained hematite in Meridiani Planum, a mineral that on Earth commonly forms in water—data used to select the landing site of the rover Opportunity, which confirmed the interpretation in 2004.

The Mars Orbiter Camera found gullies on crater and canyon walls that scientists interpreted as possibly carved by recent flows of liquid water, one of the mission's most debated results; later images appeared to show changes in some gullies over time. MOC also observed the seasonal advance and retreat of the polar caps, dust storms including planet-encircling events, and layered sedimentary rock formations suggesting that early Mars had lakes and a thicker atmosphere.

End of mission

On 2 November 2006, after nearly ten years in service, MGS sent a signal indicating it had entered safe mode following a solar-panel positioning problem. Repeated attempts to re-establish contact failed, and NASA formally ended the mission on 21 January 2007. An investigation concluded that a flawed software update months earlier had written commands to the wrong memory addresses, eventually causing a battery to overheat and fail when the spacecraft oriented itself so that the battery was exposed to sunlight. Despite its end, MGS remains one of the most productive planetary missions ever flown, and its datasets continue to be used in research and in planning for later orbiters such as Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter.
