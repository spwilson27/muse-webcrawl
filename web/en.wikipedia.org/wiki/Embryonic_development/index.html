Embryonic development

In developmental biology, animal embryonic development, also known as animal embryogenesis, is the developmental stage of an embryo after fertilization and before hatching or birth. It proceeds through a series of well-defined stages: cleavage, blastula formation, gastrulation, and organogenesis.

Stages

Fertilization produces a diploid zygote. Cleavage is a series of rapid mitotic divisions that partition the zygote into smaller cells called blastomeres, forming a morula and then a hollow blastula containing the blastocoel. During gastrulation, cells rearrange and invaginate to form the three germ layers — ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm — in triploblastic animals. Organogenesis follows, in which the germ layers differentiate into tissues and organs; in vertebrates, neurulation forms the neural tube, the precursor of the central nervous system.

Model organisms

Embryonic development has been studied intensively in model organisms including the sea urchin, the frog Xenopus, the zebrafish, the chick, the mouse, and the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster, revealing both conserved mechanisms and species-specific adaptations.
