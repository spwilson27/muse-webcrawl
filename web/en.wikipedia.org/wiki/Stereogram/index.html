Stereogram

A stereogram is a pair of two-dimensional images (or a single image so designed) that, when viewed with proper technique, produces the impression of a three-dimensional scene through stereopsis — the brain's fusion of the slightly different images received by the two eyes.

History

Charles Wheatstone described the principles of stereopsis in 1838 and constructed the first stereoscope using mirrors; David Brewster's lenticular stereoscope and Oliver Wendell Holmes's inexpensive handheld viewer made stereo photographs a mass entertainment of the Victorian era. Stereo cards were widely produced by firms such as Keystone View Company and Underwood & Underwood.

Types

Side-by-side stereograms present separate left- and right-eye views, viewed free-style by diverging or crossing the eyes, or with a stereoscope. Anaglyphs encode the two views in complementary colours (usually red and cyan) for viewing through coloured filters. An autostereogram is a single two-dimensional image, typically of a repeating pattern, from which a hidden three-dimensional shape emerges when the eyes are converged or diverged appropriately; random-dot autostereograms were popularized by the Magic Eye books of the 1990s, building on the random-dot stereograms devised by Béla Julesz in 1959 to demonstrate that stereopsis does not require recognizable forms.
