Cape (garment)

TypeOuter garment
MaterialWool, velvet, silk, cotton
ClosureClasp, button, or cord at the neck

A cape is a clothing accessory or a sleeveless outer garment which drapes the wearer's back, arms, and chest, and connects at the neck. Unlike a cloak, which is generally longer and may fasten at the front, a cape is typically shorter, often reaching no further than the waist or mid-thigh, though long ceremonial capes exist. Capes have been worn throughout history in many cultures, serving as protection against weather, as a marker of rank or office, and as an element of fashionable and ceremonial dress.

History

Capes have been in use since ancient times. In the medieval period, hooded capes were common travelling garments, protecting the wearer from rain and cold. During the 19th century, capes became a staple of women's outerwear in Europe and North America, often made of velvet or wool and trimmed with lace or fur. Military uniforms in several countries incorporated short capes or cape-like attachments, and capes formed part of the traditional dress of nurses, whose uniforms in the early 20th century often included a distinctive short cape lined in a contrasting colour.

Styles

Capes vary widely in length, cut, and material. Common styles include the short shoulder cape, the hooded travelling cape, the opera cape worn with evening dress, and the full-length ceremonial cape used in academic and ecclesiastical dress. In heraldry, the cape or mantle appears as part of the external ornament of a coat of arms, draped behind the shield to denote rank.

In popular culture

The cape is strongly associated with fictional superheroes, most famously Superman and Batman, whose capes serve both dramatic and, within the stories, functional purposes. Magicians, vampires such as Count Dracula, and figures such as the matador — whose short, stiff capote is a working tool of the bullring — are also popularly depicted wearing capes.
