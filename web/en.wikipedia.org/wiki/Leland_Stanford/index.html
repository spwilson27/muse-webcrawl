Leland Stanford

Amasa Leland Stanford (March 9, 1824 – June 21, 1893) was an American industrialist, politician and philanthropist. He was one of the "Big Four" railroad tycoons who built the Central Pacific Railroad, which formed the western portion of the first transcontinental railroad in North America, and he drove the ceremonial golden spike at Promontory Summit in 1869. Stanford served as the eighth governor of California (1862–1863) and later as a United States senator from California (1885–1893). With his wife Jane, he founded Stanford University in memory of their only child.

Early life and business

Born in Watervliet, New York, Stanford studied law and was admitted to the bar in 1848, practising in Port Washington, Wisconsin. He followed the California Gold Rush in 1852, prospering as a merchant in Sacramento by selling supplies to miners. In 1861 he became president of the Central Pacific Railroad, joining Collis P. Huntington, Mark Hopkins and Charles Crocker — the "Big Four" — in building the line eastward through the Sierra Nevada with the labour of thousands of Chinese immigrant workers.

Politics

Stanford helped organise the California Republican Party and was elected governor in 1861. After one two-year term he returned to railroads, later heading the Southern Pacific. He entered the U.S. Senate in 1885 and served until his death, though the Senate absorbed less of his energy than his business and philanthropic interests.

Stanford University

The Stanfords' son, Leland Stanford Jr., died of typhoid fever in Florence in 1884, just before his sixteenth birthday. His parents resolved that "the children of California shall be our children" and endowed a university on their Palo Alto stock farm. Stanford University opened in 1891; after Leland's death Jane Stanford preserved and expanded it through the financial crises of the 1890s.

Other interests

Stanford bred racehorses at Palo Alto, where he also sponsored Eadweard Muybridge's pioneering motion studies of animal locomotion, settling the question of whether a galloping horse lifts all four feet from the ground — work that anticipated the motion picture.
