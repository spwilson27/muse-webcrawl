Scriptio continua

Scriptio continua (Latin for "continuous script"), also known as scriptura continua, is a style of writing in which the words run together without spaces or other word dividers. It was the normal convention for Greek and Latin manuscripts throughout antiquity and into the early Middle Ages.

Reading practice

Because word boundaries had to be inferred from context, ancient texts were typically read aloud, even in private, so that the ear could parse the stream of letters. Scholars have argued that this made reading slower and more communal, and that silent reading was comparatively rare in the ancient world.

Decline

Word separation was pioneered by Insular (Irish and Anglo-Saxon) scribes in the 7th–9th centuries, for whom Latin was a foreign language learned from books. The practice spread through Carolingian scriptoria and, together with punctuation and minuscule scripts, transformed medieval literacy, making rapid silent reading — and with it new forms of scholarship — practical.
