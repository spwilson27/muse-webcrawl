Sumerian religion was the religion practised by the people of Sumer, the first literate civilisation of ancient Mesopotamia. The Sumerians regarded their divinities as responsible for all matters pertaining to the natural and social orders, and their theology profoundly influenced the later religions of Mesopotamia, including Akkadian, Babylonian and Assyrian belief.

Beliefs

Before the beginning of kingship in Sumer, the city-states were effectively ruled by priesthoods in a theocratic system, with the gods owning the land and humans created to serve them. Each city-state worshipped its own patron deity in a great temple — typically upon a stepped ziggurat — at its centre. The foremost gods of the pantheon were An, the god of heaven; Enlil, lord of the air and chief executive of the gods; Enki, god of fresh water, wisdom and creation; and Ninhursag, the mother goddess. Also prominent were Inanna, goddess of love and war; Utu, the sun god and god of justice; and Nanna, the moon god.

The Sumerians believed the universe consisted of a flat earth covered by a dome of heaven, beneath which lay the Kur, a bleak underworld where the dead ate clay and dust. Humans were formed from clay to serve the gods, and death offered no reward or punishment based on earthly conduct.

Mythology

Sumerian myths preserved on cuneiform tablets include the creation of the world from the primeval sea, the organisation of the universe by Enlil, the descent of Inanna into the underworld, and the flood story of Ziusudra, a precursor to the later Babylonian and biblical flood narratives. The exploits of the hero-king Gilgamesh of Uruk, recorded in the earliest versions of the Epic of Gilgamesh, straddle the boundary between history and myth.

Worship and priesthood

Temples were staffed by hierarchies of priests and priestesses who performed daily offerings of food, drink and clothing to the god's statue, conducted festivals such as the New Year (Akitu) rites, and administered vast temple estates. Kings presented themselves as chosen stewards of the gods, building and restoring temples to secure divine favour.

Legacy

As Sumerian died out as a spoken language in the early second millennium BC, its religion was absorbed into Akkadian-speaking cultures: most Sumerian gods continued to be worshipped under Akkadian names — Inanna as Ishtar, Utu as Shamash, Enki as Ea, Nanna as Sin — and Sumerian remained the liturgical language of Mesopotamian temples for two thousand years.
