Illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing

Illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing (IUU fishing) is a term used in international fisheries law and policy for fishing activities that contravene conservation and management measures, or that take place outside the reach of such measures. Popularized by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO), the concept underpins global efforts to combat overfishing and protect marine ecosystems.

IUU fishing is estimated to account for a substantial share of the world catch.

Definitions

Under the FAO's International Plan of Action on IUU fishing (2001), the three components are:

  • Illegal fishing — fishing conducted in violation of the laws of a coastal state, or in waters managed by a regional fisheries management organization (RFMO) in contravention of its binding measures.
  • Unreported fishing — fishing that is not reported, or is misreported, to the relevant national authority or RFMO.
  • Unregulated fishing — fishing in areas or for stocks for which no applicable conservation measures exist, or by vessels without nationality or flagged to states that are not parties to the relevant RFMO, in a manner inconsistent with state responsibilities under international law.

Scale and impacts

Estimates commonly cited suggest that IUU fishing accounts for somewhere between 11 and 26 million tonnes of fish annually, worth roughly US$10–23 billion. The practice undermines stock assessments, disadvantages law-abiding fishers, threatens food security in coastal developing states — West Africa is considered particularly affected — and is frequently linked to other abuses such as forced labor aboard fishing vessels.

Countermeasures

Key international instruments include the FAO Port State Measures Agreement (in force since 2016), the first binding treaty designed to prevent IUU-caught fish from entering ports and markets; catch documentation schemes for species such as Patagonian toothfish; vessel monitoring systems and satellite surveillance; RFMO blacklists of IUU vessels; and measures by major markets — the European Union's IUU Regulation with its "carding" system for non-cooperating countries, and the United States' Seafood Import Monitoring Program.
