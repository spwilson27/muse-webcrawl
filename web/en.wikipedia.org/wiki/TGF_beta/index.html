Transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) is a multifunctional cytokine belonging to the transforming growth factor superfamily, which also includes the activins, inhibins and bone morphogenetic proteins. In mammals there are three isoforms — TGF-β1, TGF-β2 and TGF-β3 — encoded by the genes TGFB1, TGFB2 and TGFB3. TGF-β is secreted by many cell types, including macrophages, in a latent complex, and once activated it regulates cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, migration and immune function.

Structure and activation

Mature TGF-β is a homodimer of two 112-amino-acid subunits joined by a disulfide bond, characterised by a "cysteine knot" motif. It is synthesised as a large precursor and secreted bound to its propeptide (the latency-associated peptide) and latent TGF-β binding proteins, which sequester it in the extracellular matrix. Activation — by integrins, proteases, thrombospondin-1 or acidic environments — releases the mature cytokine to bind its receptors. TGF-β1 knockout mice die of overwhelming inflammation, demonstrating its essential role in immune regulation.

Functions

TGF-β is a potent inhibitor of proliferation for most epithelial, endothelial and haematopoietic cells, largely by inducing cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors such as p15 and p21, yet it can stimulate mesenchymal cells. It is central to wound healing and extracellular matrix production; excessive signalling causes fibrosis in the lung, liver and kidney. In the immune system TGF-β maintains tolerance, suppresses effector T cells, promotes regulatory T cell differentiation and is required for IgA class switching.

Role in disease

In cancer, TGF-β has a dual role: it acts as a tumour suppressor early in carcinogenesis but promotes invasion, metastasis and immune evasion in established tumours, and mutations in its receptors and Smad effectors are frequent in colorectal and pancreatic cancers. Inherited defects in TGF-β signalling cause connective tissue disorders including Loeys–Dietz syndrome. Because of its broad effects, the TGF-β signalling pathway is an active target for antifibrotic and anticancer drugs.
