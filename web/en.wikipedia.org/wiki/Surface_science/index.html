Surface science

A scanning tunnelling microscope image of a surface

Surface science is the study of physical and chemical phenomena that occur at the interface of two phases, including solid–liquid, solid–gas, solid–vacuum, and liquid–gas interfaces. It overlaps with surface chemistry and surface physics, and underpins technologies such as heterogeneous catalysis, semiconductor devices, fuel cells, self-assembled monolayers, and corrosion science.

History

The field traces its roots to Paul Sabatier's work on hydrogenation over metals and Irving Langmuir's pioneering studies of adsorption, for which Langmuir received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1932. The development of ultra-high vacuum (UHV) techniques in the 1960s allowed scientists to prepare and keep atomically clean surfaces long enough to study them, transforming surface science into a precision discipline.

Techniques

Key experimental methods include low-energy electron diffraction (LEED) for surface structure, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Auger electron spectroscopy for composition, and the scanning tunnelling microscope (STM) and atomic force microscope (AFM), which image surfaces at atomic resolution. Temperature-programmed desorption measures how molecules bind to surfaces, while molecular beam scattering probes reaction dynamics.

Concepts

Central ideas include adsorption and desorption, surface reconstruction and relaxation, surface diffusion, steps and kinks as active sites, and the dangling bonds that make surface atoms more reactive than bulk atoms. In catalysis, the Sabatier principle holds that the interaction between a catalyst surface and a reactant should be neither too strong nor too weak — an idea quantified in modern theory by the d-band model of Hammer and Nørskov.

Applications

Surface science explains how catalytic converters clean exhaust gases, how the Haber–Bosch catalyst synthesises ammonia, why metals corrode, and how thin films grow in microelectronics fabrication.
