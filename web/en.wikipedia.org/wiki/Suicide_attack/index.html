Suicide attack

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A suicide attack is a deliberate attack in which the perpetrator knowingly sacrifices their own life as part of carrying out the assault. Because the attacker does not expect to survive, suicide attacks allow weapons to be guided to their target with a precision and timing that other delivery methods cannot match, and they require no escape plan. Suicide attacks have been used by both state and non-state actors, in wartime and as acts of terrorism.

History

Self-sacrificial attacks have ancient precedents, but the modern suicide attack is usually traced to the late Second World War, when Japanese kamikaze pilots deliberately crashed aircraft into Allied ships. In the late twentieth century the tactic was adopted by militant groups in Lebanon during the civil war there — most notoriously the 1983 Beirut barracks bombings — and by the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE) in Sri Lanka, whose "Black Tigers" carried out numerous attacks, including the assassination of former Indian prime minister Rajiv Gandhi in 1991. Palestinian groups used suicide bombings extensively during the Second Intifada, and al-Qaeda's attacks of 11 September 2001 were the deadliest suicide attacks in history. During the Iraq War and the conflict in Afghanistan, suicide bombings became a routine tactic of insurgent groups.

Motivations and strategy

Scholars such as Robert Pape have argued that suicide attacks follow a strategic logic: they are typically used by weaker actors against stronger opponents, often with the goal of compelling democratic states to withdraw forces from territory the attackers regard as their homeland. Other researchers emphasise religious motives, ideology, personal grievance, revenge, and the social dynamics of the organisations that recruit, train and dispatch attackers. Organisations provide training, indoctrination and practical support, and frequently record "martyrdom" videos intended for propaganda and recruitment.

Methods

Common methods include explosive vests or belts worn by a person on foot, vehicle-borne improvised explosive devices driven into targets, boats laden with explosives, and aircraft. Because the attacker can adjust course up to the moment of detonation, suicide attacks are difficult to defend against and have a psychological impact disproportionate to their physical damage.
