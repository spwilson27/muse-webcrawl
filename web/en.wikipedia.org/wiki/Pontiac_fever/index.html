Pontiac fever

Pontiac fever
SpecialtyInfectious disease
SymptomsFever, headache, muscle aches
CausesLegionella species

Pontiac fever is an acute, self-limiting, flu-like illness caused by inhalation of water aerosols contaminated with bacteria of the genus Legionella, most commonly Legionella pneumophila. Unlike Legionnaires' disease, the more severe form of legionellosis, Pontiac fever does not involve pneumonia and resolves without treatment.

History

The condition was first recognised in 1968, when workers at the Oakland County health department in Pontiac, Michigan, developed fever and mild flu symptoms. The outbreak was retrospectively attributed to Legionella pneumophila after the bacterium was identified following the 1976 outbreak of Legionnaires' disease in Philadelphia.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms appear within 5 to 72 hours of exposure, most often within 24–48 hours, and include fever, headache, myalgia, chills, dizziness and nausea. The attack rate in outbreaks is very high — up to 95% of exposed persons may become ill — but the illness is mild and resolves within two to five days without complications.

Cause and transmission

Pontiac fever is acquired by inhaling fine droplets of water from contaminated sources such as whirlpool spas, cooling towers, decorative fountains and shower heads. It is not transmitted from person to person. The milder course is thought to reflect an immunological reaction to the bacteria rather than invasive infection.

Treatment

No specific treatment is required; symptoms are managed supportively and antibiotics are generally unnecessary. Prevention focuses on proper maintenance and disinfection of water systems.
