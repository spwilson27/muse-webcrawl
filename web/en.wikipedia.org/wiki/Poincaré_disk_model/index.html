Poincaré disk model

The Poincaré disk model, also called the conformal disk model, is a model of two-dimensional hyperbolic geometry in which points lie inside a unit disk and straight lines consist of arcs of circles orthogonal to the boundary of the disk, together with diameters of the disk. It is named after Henri Poincaré, who described it in 1882, though it was discovered independently in related forms by Eugenio Beltrami.

Properties

The model is conformal: angles between curves in the model equal the corresponding hyperbolic angles, so local shapes are faithfully represented. However, distances are distorted: points near the boundary of the disk are hyperbolically far from the centre, and the boundary itself (the "circle at infinity") is at infinite hyperbolic distance. The metric is given by ds = 2|dz| / (1 − |z|²), from which the distance between two points can be computed.

Lines and angles

Hyperbolic lines appear either as diameters of the boundary circle or as circular arcs meeting the boundary at right angles. Through a point not on a given line there pass infinitely many lines parallel to it, illustrating the failure of Euclid's parallel postulate in hyperbolic geometry. The sum of the angles of a hyperbolic triangle is less than 180°, and the angle defect is proportional to the triangle's area.

Visualisations

The model is famous through M. C. Escher's Circle Limit woodcuts, which depict regular tessellations of the hyperbolic plane: figures identical in hyperbolic size appear progressively smaller as they approach the boundary. The dual conformal model on the upper half-plane, the Poincaré half-plane model, is related to the disk model by a Möbius transformation.
