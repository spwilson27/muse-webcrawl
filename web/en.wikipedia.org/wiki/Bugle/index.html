Bugle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: A brass military bugle with a coiled tube]
A standard military bugle

The bugle is a simple brass instrument in the natural horn family, without valves or other pitch-changing devices. It is one of the simplest of all brass instruments, consisting of a mouthpiece, a mainly conical tube, and a flared bell. Because it has no valves, the bugle can play only the notes of the harmonic series of its fundamental pitch, which makes it well suited to the short, distinctive calls used for military and ceremonial signalling.

History

The bugle developed from early signalling horns and animal horns used in hunting and warfare. Its name derives from the Latin buculus, a young bull or ox. Coil-shaped brass bugles were used by the military from the early 19th century; the keyed bugle, invented around 1810, added keys to fill in the missing notes and briefly became a popular solo instrument before being displaced by the valved cornet. The simple valveless bugle endured because of its robustness and its role in signalling.

Construction and playing

Most bugles are pitched in B-flat (or, in some traditions, G) and are played by varying the tension of the lips (embouchure) and air speed to select different harmonics. The usable calls employ only about five or six notes, but skilled players can produce melodies of surprising range within the harmonic series. Cavalry and some other traditions use a longer instrument; the fanfare trumpet is a related elongated form used for ceremonial announcements.

Military use and bugle calls

Before radio, bugle calls regulated the daily routine of soldiers and communicated orders on the battlefield. Standard calls include Reveille (waking), Assembly, Mess Call, Retreat, and Tattoo; the most solemn is Last Post in Commonwealth tradition and Taps in the United States, played at military funerals and memorial ceremonies. Bugles remain in ceremonial use even though their practical signalling role has long been superseded by electronic communications.

Bugle corps

In the United States, drum and bugle corps developed from military and veterans' organisations into competitive marching ensembles. Modern drum corps use valved bugles in several voices (soprano, mellophone, baritone, and contrabass), while traditional corps and groups such as scout associations preserve the valveless instrument. In Britain, bugle bands are associated with cadet forces and organisations like the Boys' Brigade.
