Optic ataxia

Optic ataxia
FieldNeuropsychology / neurology
Site of lesionPosterior parietal cortex (superior parietal lobule, intraparietal sulcus)
Key featureImpaired visually guided reaching despite intact vision and strength

Optic ataxia is a neurological condition characterised by an inability to accurately reach for and point to objects using visual guidance, despite normal vision, normal limb strength and intact understanding of the task. It results from damage to the posterior parietal cortex, particularly the superior parietal lobule and the region around the intraparietal sulcus — part of the dorsal "vision-for-action" stream. The term was coined by the Austrian neurologist Rudolf Bálint in 1909.

Clinical features

Patients misreach for objects, with errors typically worse for targets in the peripheral visual field and when using the hand opposite the lesion; reaching is often more accurate to objects they are looking directly at, and improves if the movement is delayed or performed without vision (for example, reaching to parts of their own body). Grasp formation may also be clumsy, with hand orientation and grip aperture poorly matched to the object. Optic ataxia can occur in relative isolation or as one element of Bálint's syndrome, together with simultanagnosia and ocular apraxia (psychic paralysis of gaze).

Causes and significance

Causes include stroke, trauma and neurodegenerative conditions such as posterior cortical atrophy. The condition has been central to the "two streams" model of visual processing developed by Melvyn Goodale and David Milner, in which the dorsal stream computes the spatial information needed to guide action in real time, distinct from the ventral stream supporting conscious perception — patients with optic ataxia can often describe an object they cannot accurately reach for.
