Terrestrial Time

Terrestrial Time (TT) is a modern astronomical time standard defined by the International Astronomical Union, primarily for time measurements of astronomical observations made from the surface of Earth. For example, the Astronomical Almanac uses TT for its tables of positions (ephemerides) of the Sun, Moon and planets as seen from Earth. In this role, TT continues Terrestrial Dynamical Time (TDT or TD), a former standard.

Definition

TT is a theoretical ideal scale that, in practice, is realized by International Atomic Time (TAI) with an exact offset: TT = TAI + 32.184 seconds. The offset preserves continuity with the earlier ephemeris time scale. Unlike TAI, TT is defined in terms of proper time on the rotating geoid, so it does not advance at exactly the same rate as a clock at every location on Earth's surface, though the difference is negligible in practice.

Relation to other time scales

Terrestrial Time differs from Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) by the accumulated leap seconds plus the constant 32.184 s offset. TT is also closely related to Barycentric Dynamical Time (TDB), which is used for ephemerides referred to the barycenter of the Solar System; the two differ only by small periodic relativistic terms of at most about 2 milliseconds.
