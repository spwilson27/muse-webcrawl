Cloud forcing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Clouds both reflect sunlight (cooling) and trap outgoing heat (warming)

Cloud forcing (more precisely, the cloud radiative effect) is the difference in Earth's energy balance caused by the presence of clouds compared with hypothetical clear skies. Clouds have two competing effects: they reflect incoming sunlight to space (a cooling "albedo" effect), and they absorb and re-emit outgoing infrared radiation (a warming "greenhouse" effect).

Net effect

Satellite measurements (notably NASA's CERES instruments) show that the shortwave cooling effect dominates: clouds currently reduce the net energy absorbed by Earth by roughly 20 W/m². Low, thick clouds (such as marine stratocumulus) cool strongly, while high, thin cirrus clouds warm, since they are transparent to sunlight but opaque to infrared.

Forcing vs. feedback

"Cloud forcing" describes the static effect of today's clouds. The separate question of how clouds change as climate warms is the cloud feedback — the largest single uncertainty in estimates of climate sensitivity.
