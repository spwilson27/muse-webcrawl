Hung parliament

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A hung parliament is a parliament in which no single political party or pre-existing coalition has an absolute majority of legislators, so that a coalition government, a minority government or a new election is required. The term is used mainly in Westminster-style systems with single-member districts, where hung parliaments are relatively uncommon but politically dramatic (the UK in February 1974, 2010 and 2017; Australia in 2010 and 2022's near-miss pattern; Canada recurrently).

Formation period

The days after a hung result are governed by constitutional conventions: the incumbent prime minister usually has the first right to attempt to form a government and meet the new parliament; the head of state acts on advice or clear signals of confidence, avoiding partisan discretion (the Lascelles principles in UK practice; the 1975 Australian dismissal shows the explosive exception when supply is blocked by a hostile Senate). Negotiations produce coalition contracts, confidence-and-supply agreements, or informal deals — scrutinised against parties' campaign statements, sometimes denounced as "coalitions of losers" when the largest party is excluded, a legitimacy debate hung parliaments reliably regenerate.
