Cordillera Oriental (Colombia)

Countries: Colombia, Venezuela

Highest point: Ritacuba Blanco

Elevation: 5,410 m (17,750 ft)

Length: about 1,200 km

Parent range: Andes

The Cordillera Oriental ("Eastern Cordillera") is the easternmost and widest of the three branches of the Colombian Andes, the others being the Cordillera Central and the Cordillera Occidental. Extending about 1,200 kilometres from south to north, it separates the Magdalena River valley from the Llanos (eastern plains) draining to the Orinoco and Amazon basins, and near the Venezuelan border it bifurcates: one branch continues as the Serranía del Perijá and the other crosses into Venezuela as the Cordillera de Mérida.

Geography

The range traverses the departments of Caquetá, Huila, Tolima, Cundinamarca, Boyacá, Santander, Norte de Santander, Cesar, and La Guajira. Its highest summit is Ritacuba Blanco (5,410 m) in the Sierra Nevada del Cocuy. Broad high plateaus characterize the range: the Altiplano Cundiboyacense, on which Bogotá (2,640 m) sits in the Bogotá savanna, was in pre-Columbian times the heartland of the Muisca Confederation, and the area around modern Tunja and Duitama remains one of Colombia's most densely populated highland regions. Farther north the range is cut by the deep Chicamocha Canyon.

Geology

Unlike the largely volcanic Central Cordillera, the Cordillera Oriental is built mainly of sedimentary and metasedimentary rocks — marine Cretaceous sandstones, shales, and limestones deposited on the old Guiana Shield basement and folded and uplifted principally during the Miocene–Pliocene as the range was squeezed between the Magdalena valley and the Llanos foreland. Its sedimentary basins make it important for coal (notably in Cesar and La Guajira's Cerrejón mine, partly in the Perijá foothills) and for petroleum in foothill structures along the Llanos margin.

Ecology

Above roughly 3,000 metres the range carries extensive páramo — high-altitude grassland and shrubland dominated by frailejones (Espeletia) — which acts as a water reservoir for Bogotá and other cities. Chingaza National Natural Park and Sumapaz National Park, the largest páramo in the world, protect these ecosystems, which grade downward into Andean cloud forest and then into piedmont forests.
