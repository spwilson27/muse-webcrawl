Protest song

A protest song is a song that is associated with a movement for social or political change and hence part of the broader category of topical songs (or songs connected to current events). Such songs are frequently written to be part of and sustain a movement, to encourage solidarity, and to articulate grievances or hopes.

History

Protest music has roots in labour movements, abolitionism, and suffrage campaigns of the 19th century. In the United States, the Almanac Singers and performers such as Woody Guthrie ("This Land Is Your Land") and Pete Seeger helped define the modern protest song in the 1930s–40s. The civil rights movement adopted and adapted spirituals and folk songs — "We Shall Overcome" became its anthem — and artists such as Bob Dylan ("Blowin' in the Wind", "The Times They Are a-Changin'"), Nina Simone ("Mississippi Goddam"), and Sam Cooke ("A Change Is Gonna Come") brought protest themes to popular music in the 1960s.

Opposition to the Vietnam War produced a large repertoire, from Country Joe McDonald's "I-Feel-Like-I'm-Fixin'-to-Die Rag" to John Lennon's "Give Peace a Chance". Protest traditions also flourished elsewhere: Víctor Jara in Chile, Fela Kuti in Nigeria, the nueva canción movement in Latin America, anti-apartheid songs in South Africa, and punk, hip hop, and rock songs addressing war, inequality, and state violence in later decades.

Characteristics

Protest songs typically use simple, singable melodies and direct language so they can be learned and sung collectively. Scholars debate their effectiveness: they rarely convert opponents directly, but they build cohesion and morale within movements and preserve a memory of dissent.
