Northern Ndebele (isiNdebele) is a Bantu language of the Nguni group spoken by the amaNdebele (Matabele) people of southwestern Zimbabwe, centred on Bulawayo and the provinces of Matabeleland North and South, with speakers also in northeastern Botswana. It is closely related to Zulu and Xhosa and, to a lesser degree, to the distinct Southern Ndebele language of South Africa's Mpumalanga and Gauteng.

History

The language arrived in present-day Zimbabwe in the 1820s–1830s, when the Zulu general Mzilikazi broke from Shaka and led his followers north in the upheavals of the Mfecane, founding the Ndebele kingdom (Matabeleland) with its capital at Bulawayo. The kingdom fell to the British South Africa Company in the First Matabele War of 1893–94. Because its speakers came from the Zulu nation, Northern Ndebele is sometimes described as a dialect of Zulu heavily influenced by Shona, but it is standardized and taught as a separate language.

Features

Northern Ndebele is a tonal, agglutinative language with a system of noun classes and (unusually for the region) click consonants inherited from contact with Khoisan languages, shared with Zulu and Xhosa. It is written in the Latin alphabet. Sample phrases include Sawubona ("I see you" — hello) and Ngiyabonga ("thank you").

Status and use

Under Zimbabwe's 2013 constitution isiNdebele is one of sixteen officially recognised languages. It is a medium of instruction in early primary schooling in Matabeleland, an examination subject, and is broadcast by national radio stations and used in newspapers such as Umthunywa. Language politics remain sensitive in Zimbabwe, where Ndebele speakers often complain of marginalisation relative to the Shona-speaking majority.
