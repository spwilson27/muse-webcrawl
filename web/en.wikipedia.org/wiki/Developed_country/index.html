Developed country

[Image: a modern developed city skyline with public transport and offices]

A developed country is a nation with a highly developed economy, advanced technological infrastructure and a high standard of living, typically measured by high GDP per capita, a high Human Development Index and a service-dominated economy. The category — roughly the International Monetary Fund's 'advanced economies', some forty countries — includes the United States, Japan, Germany, France, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia and South Korea, in contrast to developing countries.

Features

Developed countries share low absolute poverty, near-universal literacy and secondary education, extensive welfare states and infrastructure, low fertility and ageing populations, and deep financial markets and rule-of-law institutions. Their economies are dominated by services (70%+ of output), with industry largely automated and agriculture a few percent of employment. The boundary is historical, not natural: today's developed countries industrialised in the 19th and 20th centuries, and recent graduates (Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan) show the category can change — the analytical focus of development and growth economics. Critics note the terms are value-laden and prefer income classifications.
