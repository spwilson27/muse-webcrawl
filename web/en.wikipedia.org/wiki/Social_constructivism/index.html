Social constructivism

Social constructivism emphasises learning through social interaction
Key figureLev Vygotsky
FieldPsychology of learning, education, sociology of knowledge
Key conceptsZone of proximal development, more knowledgeable other, scaffolding, cultural mediation
Seminal workThought and Language (1934), Mind in Society (1978)

Social constructivism is a theory of knowledge and learning holding that individuals construct understanding and knowledge through their interactions with others and with their culture. Whereas the related theory of social constructionism focuses on how social categories and institutions are collectively created, social constructivism in its psychological and educational form focuses on how individual learning itself is socially mediated. Its foundational figure is the Soviet psychologist Lev Vygotsky (1896–1934).

Vygotsky's theory

Vygotsky argued that cognitive development proceeds from the social plane to the individual plane: every higher mental function appears twice, first between people (interpsychological) and then inside the child (intrapsychological). Language is the principal tool of this process; children internalise the speech of caregivers and peers, turning social dialogue into private speech and then into inner verbal thought.

Key concepts

The zone of proximal development (ZPD) is the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with guidance from a more knowledgeable other — a teacher, parent or peer. Teaching should target this zone. The later notion of scaffolding (developed by Wood, Bruner and Ross in 1976) describes the temporary support that lets a learner accomplish a task within the ZPD and is gradually withdrawn as competence grows.

Applications in education

Social constructivism underlies collaborative learning, peer tutoring, reciprocal teaching, problem-based learning and communities of practice. In the classroom it favours discussion, group projects and authentic tasks over passive reception of facts, and it treats assessment of assisted performance as a better guide to potential than tests of solo performance alone.

Relation to other theories

Social constructivism is one branch of constructivism, contrasting with Jean Piaget's cognitive constructivism, which stresses the individual child's active construction of knowledge through stages of development. In international relations and sociology, constructivist approaches influenced by Berger and Luckmann instead examine how shared ideas constitute identities, interests and institutions.
