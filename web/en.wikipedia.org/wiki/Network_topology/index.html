Network topology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Network topology is the arrangement of the elements (links, nodes, etc.) of a communication network. Network topology can be used to define or describe the arrangement of various types of telecommunication networks, including command and control radio networks, industrial fieldbuses and computer networks.

Physical and logical topology

Physical topology describes the actual layout of cables and devices, while logical topology describes how data flows through the network regardless of its physical design. A network may, for example, be wired as a physical star (through a switch) while behaving logically as a shared bus, as in early Ethernet over hubs.

Basic topologies

  • Point-to-point — a dedicated link between two endpoints.
  • Bus — all nodes share a common backbone; used by early Ethernet (10BASE2/10BASE5).
  • Ring — each node connects to two neighbors forming a loop, as in Token Ring and FDDI.
  • Star — all nodes connect to a central switch or hub; the dominant topology in modern LANs.
  • Tree — a hierarchy of stars, common in enterprise and cable networks.
  • Mesh — nodes interconnect with multiple paths (full or partial), providing redundancy; used in wireless mesh networks and internet backbones.
  • Hybrid — a combination of two or more basic topologies.

Considerations

Choice of topology affects cost, scalability, fault tolerance, and performance: a star has a single point of failure at its centre but simplifies troubleshooting, while mesh topologies are resilient but expensive to wire. Topology is also studied mathematically in graph theory, where networks are modelled as graphs of vertices and edges.
