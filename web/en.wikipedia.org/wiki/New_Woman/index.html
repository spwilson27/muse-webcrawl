New Woman

The New Woman was a feminist ideal that emerged in the late 19th century and had a significant influence on feminism well into the 20th century. The term was popularised by writer Sarah Grand in her 1894 article "The New Aspect of the Woman Question" and was developed in responses by writers including Ouida (Maria Louise Ramé).

Characteristics

The New Woman pushed against the limits placed on women in a male-dominated society. She was typically educated, independent and career-minded, sought marriage based on equality or remained single, rode a bicycle, wore rational dress, and claimed the right to vote and to participate in public life. Playwrights and novelists including Henrik Ibsen, George Bernard Shaw, Thomas Hardy and Henry James explored the figure, while magazines and cartoons alternately celebrated and satirised her.

Literature

"New Woman fiction" of the 1880s and 1890s — by writers such as Sarah Grand (The Heavenly Twins), Mona Caird, Olive Schreiner and George Gissing (The Odd Women) — debated marriage, women's work, sexuality and the double standard. The ideal influenced the suffrage movement and anticipated the more visible changes of the 1920s, associated with the flapper.
