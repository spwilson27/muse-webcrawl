Petrol

Also known as: Gasoline (North America)

Appearance: Colourless to pale yellow liquid

Boiling range: approx. 30–210 °C

Energy density: approx. 34 MJ/L

Petrol, known as gasoline in North America, is a transparent, petroleum-derived liquid used primarily as a fuel in spark-ignition internal combustion engines. It consists mostly of organic compounds obtained by the fractional distillation of petroleum, enhanced with a variety of additives. A typical barrel of crude oil yields about 72 litres (19 US gallons) of petrol.

Production and composition

Petrol is blended from distillation fractions — straight-run gasoline, reformate, alkylate and cracked naphtha — composed of hydrocarbons from C4 to C12, including paraffins, naphthenes and aromatics. Refineries adjust the blend seasonally for volatility. Its resistance to engine knocking is measured by its octane rating; for decades the anti-knock additive tetraethyllead was used ("leaded petrol"), but it was phased out worldwide — completed globally in 2021 — because of lead's toxicity. Modern unleaded grades are commonly sold in Europe in 95 and 98 RON grades and often contain ethanol (E5 or E10 blends).

History and use

Early motorists bought petrol in cans from pharmacies and hardware shops; dedicated filling stations appeared from about 1905. Petrol became the dominant transport fuel of the 20th century, powering cars, motorcycles, light aircraft (as avgas) and countless small engines. Its use is now a major source of carbon dioxide emissions, driving policy shifts toward electrification; many countries have announced future bans on new petrol-only cars.
