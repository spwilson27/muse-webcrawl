Pont Saint-Bénézet

The Pont Saint-Bénézet (French pronunciation: [pɔ̃ sɛ̃ benezɛ]), also known as the Pont d'Avignon, is a famous medieval bridge over the Rhône River in the town of Avignon, in southern France. Only four of its original twenty-two arches survive, and the bridge ends mid-river on the Île de la Barthelasse. It is celebrated worldwide through the folk song "Sur le pont d'Avignon."

History

According to legend, the bridge was inspired by a shepherd boy named Bénézet (Benedict), who in 1177 heard a divine voice instructing him to build a bridge across the Rhône at Avignon. Mocked at first, he is said to have lifted a gigantic stone to prove his calling; construction began in 1177 and was completed in 1185. Bénézet was later canonised and buried in a small chapel on the bridge itself.

The original bridge, some 900 metres long with 22 arches, was built of wood and then rebuilt in stone. It stood on a key route between Spain and Italy and became strategically crucial when the popes resided in Avignon in the fourteenth century — hence the chapel of Saint Nicholas built on one of its piers. The Rhône repeatedly damaged the bridge; arches collapsed in floods from the 13th century onward, and after a catastrophic flood in 1669 most of the structure was abandoned. Only four arches, the gatehouse, and two chapels (the lower and upper chapels of Saint Nicholas) remain.

The song

The bridge is famous for the children's song "Sur le pont d'Avignon" ("On the bridge of Avignon"), which describes dancing on the bridge — actually beneath it ("sous le pont") on the island, where fairs were held. The melody in its current form dates from the 19th century and was popularised through Adolphe Adam's opéra comique Si j'étais roi (1852) and subsequent tradition.

Today

The bridge, together with the Palais des Papes and Avignon Cathedral, was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1995 as part of the Historic Centre of Avignon. It is one of the best-known landmarks of France and a major tourist attraction, with the surviving span open to visitors.
