Xuanzang

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Xuanzang carrying scrolls on a back-frame, from a Japanese depiction of the pilgrim monk]

Xuanzang (602 – 5 February 664) was a Chinese Buddhist monk, scholar and traveller of the early Tang dynasty, famed for his seventeen-year pilgrimage (629–645) from China to India in search of authoritative Buddhist scriptures. Defying an imperial travel ban, he crossed the Gobi, the Pamirs and the Hindu Kush, studied at the great monastery-university of Nalanda under Śīlabhadra, toured India's holy places and courts (including Harsha's assembly at Kannauj), and returned to Chang'an with 657 Sanskrit texts, relics and images. His record, the Great Tang Records on the Western Regions (Da Tang Xiyu Ji, 646), is a primary source for 7th-century Central and South Asia; his translations reshaped Chinese Buddhism (the Yogācāra/Faxiang school). In legend he became Tang Sanzang, the monk hero of the Ming novel Journey to the West, escorted by Sun Wukong the Monkey King.

The journey

Xuanzang's route is recoverable in detail: smuggled out past the Jade Gate watchtowers, guided and nearly abandoned in the Gobi, honoured in Turfan (whose king tried to keep him), received by the Western Turk khagan, and through Samarkand and Balkh into India by 633. In India he spent years at Nalanda mastering Yogācāra philosophy, visited the sites of the Buddha's life from Lumbini to Kushinagar, debated (by tradition, undefeated) at Harsha's great assembly of 643, and collected manuscripts across kingdoms — his itinerary names over a hundred polities, many known from no other source.

Return and translation

Emperor Taizong, who had refused him permission, received him in triumph in 645 and pressed him (in vain) into government service; Xuanzang instead directed a translation bureau of hundreds, producing 75 works in 1,335 volumes, and dictated the Xiyu Ji to his disciple Bianji. His insistence on translating anew from Sanskrit — including the vast Mahāprajñāpāramitā corpus — set the scholarly standard of East Asian Buddhism; the Wild Goose Pagoda in Xi'an was built to house his library. The pilgrim-monk became, in the centuries after, the archetypal hero of the Chinese imagination.
