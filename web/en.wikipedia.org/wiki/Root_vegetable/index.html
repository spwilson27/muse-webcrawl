Root vegetable

Root vegetables are underground plant parts eaten by humans as vegetables. In culinary use the term covers true roots (such as carrots, parsnips, beets, radishes and turnips), tubers (potatoes — botanically stem tubers), bulbs (onions, garlic), corms and rhizomes (ginger, turmeric), all of which serve the plant as storage organs for energy, typically starch.

Types and examples

True taproots include carrot, parsnip, beetroot, radish, turnip, swede (rutabaga) and daikon; tuberous roots include sweet potato and cassava (yuca), major staple crops in the tropics. Storage organs overwinter in the ground, making root vegetables reliable, energy-dense foods that store well — historically vital in northern climates before refrigeration.

Nutrition and use

Root vegetables are generally rich in carbohydrates, fibre and micronutrients — carrots in beta-carotene (vitamin A), beets in folate and betalain pigments. They are roasted, boiled, mashed, made into soups and stews, or eaten raw, and many are also important animal feeds and sources of sugar (sugar beet) and industrial starch.
