Priesthood (ancient Israel)

The priesthood of ancient Israel was the hereditary religious office held by the kohanim (singular kohen, "priest"), male descendants of Aaron, brother of Moses, within the tribe of Levi. Priests performed the sacrificial cult at the Tabernacle and later at the Temple in Jerusalem, blessed the people, taught and judged matters of purity, and were sustained by tithes and portions of the offerings. At their head stood the high priest (kohen gadol).

Origins and duties

According to the Torah, Aaron and his sons were consecrated as priests during the Exodus, and the office was granted to Aaron's line "for an everlasting priesthood". Priestly duties included offering the daily tamid sacrifices and festival offerings, burning incense, lighting the menorah, presenting the showbread, pronouncing the priestly blessing over the people, diagnosing skin disease (tzaraat) and other purity questions, and sounding the trumpets. Only priests could enter the sanctuary proper, and only the high priest could enter the Holy of Holies, once a year on Yom Kippur. Priests served in rotating divisions (mishmarot) — twenty-four courses by the late Second Temple period, each serving a week at a time in Jerusalem.

Vestments and support

Ordinary priests wore white linen garments; the high priest wore additional vestments of blue, purple and scarlet with gold: the ephod, the breastplate (hoshen) set with twelve stones for the tribes and containing the Urim and Thummim, and a golden frontlet inscribed "Holiness to the LORD". Having no tribal land inheritance, the priests were supported by the terumah (heave offering), tithes of the Levites' tithe, firstfruits, and portions of sacrifices; Levitical cities were assigned to them throughout Israel.

History

Before the centralisation of worship, priests served at local shrines such as Shiloh (the house of Eli). Solomon's Temple in Jerusalem made the Zadokite line pre-eminent. After the Babylonian exile (from 538 BCE) the priesthood became the governing institution of Yehud: high priests such as Joshua and later the Hasmonean priest-kings ruled Judea. Under Herod and Rome the high priesthood became an appointed, politicised office dominated by families such as that of Annas and Caiaphas. Sacrificial priestly service ended with the Roman destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, but priestly descent retained liturgical honours in Judaism — the first call to the Torah (aliyah), the priestly blessing, and the redemption of the firstborn — and kohanim still observe distinctive rules today.
