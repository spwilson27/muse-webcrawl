Juvenile delinquency

Juvenile delinquency, also known as juvenile offending, is the act of participating in unlawful behaviour as a minor (usually a person below the statutory age of majority). Most legal systems prescribe specific procedures for dealing with juveniles, such as juvenile detention centres and courts.

Definitions and scope

Delinquent behaviour includes crimes committed by adults as well as "status offences" that are unlawful only because of the offender's age, such as truancy, running away and violating curfews. In the United States, a juvenile delinquent is a minor, usually defined as a person under 18, who is found by a court to have committed a criminal act.

Causes

Research in criminology and developmental psychology points to a combination of individual factors (impulsivity, early aggression, substance abuse), family factors (poverty, harsh or neglectful parenting, parental criminality), peer influence, school failure and neighbourhood disorganisation. Terrie Moffitt's taxonomy distinguishes a small group of "life-course-persistent" offenders from a much larger group of "adolescence-limited" offenders who desist in adulthood.

Justice systems and prevention

Modern juvenile justice, beginning with the Illinois juvenile court of 1899, was founded on rehabilitation rather than punishment, guided by the doctrine of parens patriae. Landmark US Supreme Court cases such as In re Gault (1967) extended due-process rights to juveniles, while later decisions (Roper v. Simmons, 2005; Miller v. Alabama, 2012) limited capital punishment and mandatory life without parole for minors. Prevention strategies include early-childhood interventions, mentoring, diversion programmes and restorative justice.
