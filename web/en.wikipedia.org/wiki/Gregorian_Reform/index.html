The Gregorian Reform was a series of reforms initiated under Pope Gregory VII and the circle he formed in the papal curia, c. 1050–80, which sought to free the Catholic Church from secular control, end abuses among the clergy, and assert the primacy of the papacy. Named for Gregory (pope 1073–1085), the movement had roots in earlier tenth- and eleventh-century monastic reform, notably that of Cluny.

Programme

The reformers attacked three principal abuses: simony (the purchase of church offices), clerical marriage and concubinage (nicolaitism), and lay investiture — the appointment of bishops and abbots by kings and nobles. They asserted the doctrine of libertas ecclesiae, the freedom of the Church, and in 1059 the Lateran synod under Nicholas II transferred the election of the pope to the cardinal-bishops, creating the basis of the College of Cardinals. Gregory's Dictatus papae (1075) listed sweeping claims for papal authority, including that the pope could depose emperors.

Investiture Controversy

Gregory's prohibition of lay investiture brought him into open conflict with Emperor Henry IV, who had Gregory declared deposed; Gregory excommunicated Henry, releasing his subjects from obedience. Henry's penitential journey to Canossa in 1077, where he stood barefoot in the snow for three days to beg absolution, became an emblematic moment of the struggle, though the conflict resumed and Gregory died in exile at Salerno in 1085.

Legacy

The struggle was settled only in 1122 by the Concordat of Worms, which distinguished spiritual from temporal investiture. The reform profoundly reshaped the medieval Church: it strengthened canon law, enforced clerical celibacy, centralised authority in Rome, and — in the view of many historians — amounted to a "papal revolution" that laid foundations for the Western legal tradition.
