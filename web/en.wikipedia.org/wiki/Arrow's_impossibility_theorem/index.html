Arrow's impossibility theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Arrow's impossibility theorem, proved by the economist Kenneth Arrow in his 1951 book Social Choice and Individual Values, states that when voters have three or more distinct alternatives, no ranked-order electoral system can convert the ranked preferences of individuals into a community-wide ranking while meeting a specified set of criteria — unrestricted domain, non-dictatorship, Pareto efficiency and independence of irrelevant alternatives. Any system meeting the others is a “dictatorship” in the theorem's technical sense: one voter's preferences always prevail regardless of the rest.

The theorem founded modern social-choice theory and the rigorous study of voting: it generalises the Condorcet paradox (majority cycles) and constrains the design of constitutions, committees and multi-criteria decisions. Responses include relaxing the criteria (allowing cardinal utility and interpersonal comparison; single-peaked preferences, which restore majority coherence — Black's theorem), and the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem's companion result that every non-dictatorial voting rule with three or more outcomes is manipulable, so strategic voting is unavoidable in principle. Arrow shared the 1972 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics partly for this work.
