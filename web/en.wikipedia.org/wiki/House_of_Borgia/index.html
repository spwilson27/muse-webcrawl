The House of Borgia (Italian: Borgia; Spanish: Borja) was an Italian noble family of Spanish origin that rose to prominence during the Italian Renaissance. They produced two popes — Callixtus III and Alexander VI — and became notorious as symbols of papal corruption, nepotism and political ruthlessness, though modern historians have reassessed some of the black legend surrounding them.

Origins and rise

The Borja family came from Valencia in the Crown of Aragon. Alfonso de Borja became Pope Callixtus III in 1455 and advanced his nephews, among them Rodrigo Borgia, who was created cardinal at a young age and accumulated offices and wealth over decades as vice-chancellor of the Church. Elected Pope Alexander VI in 1492 amid charges of simony, Rodrigo fathered several acknowledged children, including Cesare and Lucrezia Borgia.

Cesare and Lucrezia

Alexander used his children as instruments of policy: Lucrezia was married successively into the Sforza, Aragonese and Este families, ending as Duchess of Ferrara. Cesare, released from the cardinalate, became a soldier-statesman who, with French support, carved out a duchy in the Romagna; Machiavelli, who observed him as a Florentine envoy, made him a central example of the new prince in The Prince. Cesare's power collapsed when Alexander died in 1503; Cesare died in battle in Navarre in 1507.

Later history and legacy

The family continued in Spanish and Italian branches; Francis Borgia, Duke of Gandía, became a Jesuit and was canonised as a saint. The Borgias' reputation for poisoning (with the legendary cantarella) and depravity was amplified by their enemies, especially Pope Julius II's circle, and has fed novels, plays and television series ever since.
