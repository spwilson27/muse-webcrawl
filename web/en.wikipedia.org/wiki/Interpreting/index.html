Language interpretation, or interpreting, is the oral or signed translation of speech from one language into another, in real time, enabling communication between speakers of different languages. It is distinguished from translation, which deals with written text. Interpreters must render meaning, register and intent quickly and accurately, often under strict time pressure and with specialised terminology.

Modes

Simultaneous interpretation

The interpreter speaks while the speaker continues, with a lag of only a few seconds (décalage). It requires soundproof booths, consoles and headsets and is the standard mode for multilingual conferences at the United Nations and the European Union. Because of the extreme cognitive load, interpreters work in pairs and rotate roughly every 20–30 minutes. Simultaneous interpretation was first used on a large scale at the Nuremberg trials (1945–46), which pioneered its equipment and techniques.

Consecutive interpretation

The speaker pauses after a segment — from a sentence to several minutes — while the interpreter reproduces it, typically from memory aided by a specialised note-taking system of symbols and abbreviations. It is common in diplomacy, business, courts and medical encounters where booths are impractical.

Other modes

Whispered interpreting (chuchotage) is simultaneous interpretation whispered to one or two listeners. Signed language interpreting serves Deaf and hearing participants at conferences, in courts and on television. Remote simultaneous interpreting (RSI) expanded rapidly with videoconferencing platforms, bringing new concerns about sound quality and working conditions.

Settings and training

Conference interpreters work for international organisations, governments and the private market; community interpreters work in hospitals, schools and asylum procedures; legal interpreters are often certified or sworn. Postgraduate training schools (e.g. ESIT Paris, the University of Geneva's FTI, Monterey) teach the techniques, and AIIC sets professional standards. Interpreters work into their strongest "A" language from "B" and "C" languages, and must combine linguistic mastery with rapid analysis, public-speaking skill and deep general knowledge.
