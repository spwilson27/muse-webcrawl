Populus tremuloides

Quaking aspen

Populus tremuloides is a deciduous tree native to cooler areas of North America, one of several species referred to by the common name aspen. It is commonly called trembling aspen, American aspen, mountain or golden aspen, quaking aspen, white poplar, and popple, as well as others. The trees have tall trunks, up to 25 metres (82 feet) tall, with smooth pale bark, scarred with black. The glossy green leaves, dull beneath, become golden to yellow in autumn. The species often propagates through its roots to form large clonal colonies originating from a shared root system. These roots are not rhizomes, as new growth develops from adventitious buds on the parent root system (the ortet).

Distribution

Quaking aspen has the widest distribution of any tree in North America, ranging from Newfoundland and Labrador west across Canada and Alaska to British Columbia, and south through the western United States to the mountains of Mexico, with scattered populations in the upper Midwest and New England. It occupies a wide variety of sites, from moist lowlands to rocky alpine slopes, and is a classic early-successional species that colonises ground disturbed by fire or logging.

Description

The leaves are nearly round, 3–7 cm across, with fine teeth and a flattened petiole (leaf stalk) that causes them to tremble or "quake" in the slightest breeze — the source of both the common name and the specific epithet tremuloides, meaning "resembling the trembling poplar" (Populus tremula of Europe). The bark is smooth and white to greenish-grey on young trees, darkening and furrowing with age. The species is dioecious, with male and female catkins on separate trees, flowering in spring before the leaves emerge.

Clonal colonies

Although individual stems rarely live more than 100–150 years, the root system of a colony can persist for thousands of years, sending up successive generations of trunks. The most famous example is "Pando," a colony in Utah's Fishlake National Forest covering about 43 hectares with roughly 47,000 stems, all genetically identical males, with a total mass estimated at some 6,000 tonnes — among the largest and oldest living organisms known. Because entire colonies leaf out and change colour in unison, clonal boundaries are often visible on autumn hillsides.

Ecology and uses

Aspen stands support rich understories and are keystone habitat for wildlife: beavers, moose, deer, elk, grouse, and many songbirds depend on them, and their bark and buds are important winter forage. The soft, light wood is used for pulp and paper, oriented strand board, matches, and sauna benches; the trees are also planted as ornamentals, though they are short-lived in warm, humid climates. Like other poplars, the species contains salicin-related compounds historically used in folk remedies.
