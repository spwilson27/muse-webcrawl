No first use

In nuclear strategy, no first use (NFU) is a pledge or policy by a nuclear-armed state not to use nuclear weapons as a means of warfare unless it is first attacked by an adversary using nuclear weapons. The concept is distinct from but related to negative security assurances given to non-nuclear-weapon states.

Countries with NFU policies

China declared a no-first-use policy in 1964, immediately after its first nuclear test, and has consistently reaffirmed it. India adopted an NFU policy as part of its nuclear doctrine announced in 1999 and formalized in 2003, though Indian officials have occasionally suggested the policy could be revisited depending on circumstances. North Korea has also stated that it would not use nuclear weapons first unless its sovereignty was threatened by a hostile nuclear power.

Countries without NFU policies

The United States, Russia, the United Kingdom, France, and Pakistan have declined to adopt NFU pledges, preserving the option of first use in extreme circumstances — for example, in response to overwhelming conventional attack. NATO's strategic concept similarly retains calculated ambiguity. Debates over NFU occurred within the United States during the Obama and Biden administrations, with proponents arguing it would reduce the risk of accidental escalation and opponents arguing it would weaken deterrence and reassure adversaries.

Criticism

Critics argue NFU pledges are unverifiable and may not be credible in a crisis, since a state facing defeat might renege. Supporters contend that a declared policy shapes force posture — discouraging tactical nuclear weapons and launch-on-warning postures — and lowers the salience of nuclear weapons in military planning.
