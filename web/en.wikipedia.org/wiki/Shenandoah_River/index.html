Shenandoah River

The Shenandoah River is a tributary of the Potomac River, about 55 miles (89 km) long in its main stem, in the US states of Virginia and West Virginia. It is formed near Front Royal, Virginia, by the confluence of its two long forks — the South Fork (about 104 km longer, roughly 160 miles from its headwaters near Staunton) and the North Fork (about 105 miles) — and flows northeast through the Shenandoah Valley to join the Potomac at Harpers Ferry, West Virginia.

Geography and geology

Unusually, the Shenandoah flows from south to north. Both forks meander dramatically through limestone valley floor between the Blue Ridge Mountains to the east and the Allegheny Front to the west; the South Fork is famous for its eight great bends visible from Skyline Drive in Shenandoah National Park and for the "Seven Bends" near Woodstock. The river drains a karst landscape of caves and sinkholes, and its watershed covers about 2,900 square miles. The river is a popular recreational waterway for canoeing, kayaking, tubing, and smallmouth bass fishing.

History

The name derives from a Native American expression rendered in various forms, often glossed as "daughter of the stars". The river valley was a contested corridor during the American Civil War, featuring in Stonewall Jackson's Valley Campaign of 1862 and the campaigns of 1864. In the folk song "Oh Shenandoah", and in John Denver's "Take Me Home, Country Roads" (in which the river is associated with West Virginia, though only its lower few miles lie in that state), the river became an enduring American cultural symbol.

Environmental issues

Agricultural runoff and industrial pollution have caused large fish kills (notably in 2005–2007) and algal blooms; the river has been listed on conservation groups' endangered-rivers lists, and its forks are the subject of long-term water-quality monitoring.
