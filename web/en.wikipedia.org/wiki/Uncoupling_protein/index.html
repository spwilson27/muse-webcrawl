Uncoupling protein

An uncoupling protein (UCP) is a mitochondrial inner membrane protein that can dissipate the proton gradient generated by oxidative phosphorylation, releasing the stored energy as heat rather than using it to produce ATP. This process, called mitochondrial uncoupling or proton leak, is the basis of non-shivering thermogenesis.

Types

Several uncoupling proteins are known in mammals. UCP1, also called thermogenin, is found almost exclusively in brown adipose tissue, where it generates heat in infants and hibernating animals. UCP2 is expressed widely in tissues including immune cells and pancreatic beta cells, while UCP3 is found mainly in skeletal muscle. In plants, uncoupling proteins such as those in the potato and skunk cabbage help generate heat during flowering and protect against oxidative stress.

Mechanism

UCP1 provides an alternative channel for protons to flow back into the mitochondrial matrix, bypassing ATP synthase. Its activity is inhibited by purine nucleotides such as GDP and activated by free fatty acids released during lipolysis, which is stimulated by norepinephrine in response to cold. Chemical uncouplers such as 2,4-dinitrophenol mimic this action; DNP was briefly marketed as a weight-loss drug in the 1930s but abandoned because of its narrow therapeutic index and fatal hyperthermia cases.
