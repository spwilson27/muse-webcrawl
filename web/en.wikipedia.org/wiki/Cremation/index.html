Cremation

Modern cremation takes place in a specially designed furnace called a cremator

Cremation is a method of final disposition of a dead body through burning. It is an alternative to burial and is practised in many cultures and religions around the world. After cremation, the remaining ashes (cremains) may be kept in an urn, scattered, buried, or incorporated into memorial objects.

History

Cremation is an ancient practice, attested archaeologically from at least the early Neolithic and widespread in Bronze Age Europe, where cremated remains were placed in urns in urnfields. It was common in ancient Greece and Rome and has been practised continuously for millennia in Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism, in which open-air funeral pyres remain traditional in India and Nepal. In Europe, cremation declined with Christianisation but was revived in the late nineteenth century, driven by public-health concerns and new furnace technology; the first modern crematoria opened in Italy and England in the 1870s–1880s.

Process

In modern practice, the body is placed in a combustible container and cremated in a chamber heated to roughly 760–980 °C (1,400–1,800 °F) for one to three hours. The remaining bone fragments are processed into a coarse ash. Regulations in most jurisdictions require identification procedures, permits, and removal of items such as pacemakers before cremation.

Religious views

Hindu tradition regards cremation as releasing the soul from the body. The Roman Catholic Church long prohibited cremation but has permitted it since 1963, while preferring burial and requiring that ashes be kept in a sacred place. Eastern Orthodoxy and Orthodox Judaism generally prohibit it, as does Islam. Protestant denominations vary, though most now permit it.

Prevalence

Cremation rates have risen steadily in many Western countries: it is now chosen in the majority of deaths in the United Kingdom, Canada, and (since the mid-2010s) the United States, and rates are highest of all in Japan, where it is near-universal. Cost, land scarcity, and changing religious observance are commonly cited drivers.

Environmental aspects

Cremation consumes fuel and emits carbon dioxide and trace pollutants such as mercury from dental amalgam, which modern crematoria filter. Newer alternatives such as alkaline hydrolysis (resomation) and natural organic reduction have been promoted as lower-impact options.
