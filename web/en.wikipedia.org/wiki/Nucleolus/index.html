Nucleolus

The nucleolus is the largest structure in the nucleus of eukaryotic cells. It is best known as the site of ribosome biogenesis, where ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is transcribed and assembled with ribosomal proteins into the small and large ribosomal subunits. The nucleolus is not bounded by a membrane; it is a biomolecular condensate formed around the chromosomal regions containing rRNA genes, the nucleolar organiser regions.

Structure

Electron microscopy reveals three components: the fibrillar centre, where rRNA genes reside and transcription begins; the dense fibrillar component, containing nascent rRNA transcripts being processed; and the granular component, where maturing pre-ribosomal particles accumulate. Their arrangement reflects the stepwise production of ribosomes.

Function

RNA polymerase I transcribes a 47S precursor that is processed into the 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNAs; the 5S rRNA is made elsewhere and imported. Small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs) guide chemical modification and cleavage of the precursor. Beyond ribosome production, the nucleolus participates in stress sensing and cell-cycle control: under stress, nucleolar proteins such as ARF stabilise the tumour suppressor p53, and disruption of nucleolar function ("nucleolar stress") is implicated in ribosomopathies and cancer.

Cell cycle

The nucleolus disassembles during mitosis, when rRNA transcription stops, and reforms in telophase around the nucleolar organiser regions. AgNOR staining of nucleolar proteins is used in pathology as a marker of cell proliferation.
