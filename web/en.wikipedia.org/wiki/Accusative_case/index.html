Accusative case

The accusative case (abbreviated ACC) of a noun is the grammatical case used to receive the direct object of a transitive verb. In the syntax of a language that uses the nominative–accusative alignment, the accusative case marks the object, in contrast to the nominative case, which marks the subject of both transitive and intransitive verbs.

Characteristics

Languages with an overt accusative include Latin, Greek, German, Russian and most other Slavic languages, Japanese (marked by the particle を wo) and Korean. In Latin, for example, the accusative of puella ("girl") is puellam: in Puella puerum videt ("The girl sees the boy"), puerum is accusative as the object of videt. The accusative may also be used after certain prepositions and to express extent of time or space.

English

English retains the accusative (often called the objective case) only in pronouns: me, him, her, us, them and whom are the object forms corresponding to the subject forms I, he, she, we, they and who.

Alignment

Not all languages group grammatical roles the same way: in ergative–absolutive languages the patient of a transitive verb is grouped with the subject of an intransitive verb in the absolutive case, and there is no accusative. Some languages, such as Basque, distinguish an accusative only for definite or animate objects (differential object marking).
