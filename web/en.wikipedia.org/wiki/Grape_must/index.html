Must

Must (from the Latin vinum mustum, "young wine") is freshly crushed fruit juice (usually grape juice) that contains the skins, seeds, and stems of the fruit. The solid portion of the must is called pomace and typically makes up 7–23% of the total weight of the must. Making must is the first step in winemaking.

Winemaking

Because of its high amount of glucose, typically between 10 and 15% by weight, must is also used as a sweetener in a variety of cuisines. Unlike commercially marketed grape juice, which includes added sugars, grape must is thick with a much higher ratio of solids. In winemaking, must is fermented by yeast into wine as the sugars are converted to ethanol and carbon dioxide.

The sugar content of must is commonly measured in degrees Brix or by potential alcohol, and determines the ripeness of the grapes and the strength of the finished wine. Chaptalization — the addition of sugar to must — is practised in some cooler wine regions.

Uses

Must is used in various culinary traditions: in Greece it is boiled down into a syrup called petimezi; in Turkey a similar product, pekmez, is made; in Georgia it is used to make the sweet churchkhela. In Roman Catholic tradition, mustum may exceptionally be used for the Eucharist in place of wine. See also wort, the analogous unfermented liquid in brewing.
