Presbycusis (from the Greek presbys "old" + akousis "hearing"), also called age-related hearing loss, is the cumulative effect of ageing on hearing. It is a progressive, bilateral, sensorineural hearing loss that primarily affects the higher frequencies, and it is the most common cause of hearing loss in older adults. About one third of people over 65 and roughly half of those over 75 have hearing loss to some degree.

Signs and symptoms

Presbycusis characteristically makes it hard to distinguish high-pitched consonant sounds such as /s/, /f/ and /th/, so speech seems muffled even when it is loud enough to hear, and understanding speech in background noise is particularly difficult. Sufferers may ask others to repeat themselves, turn up television volume, or withdraw from conversation; tinnitus commonly accompanies it. Because of the social isolation it can produce, presbycusis is associated with depression and is correlated with accelerated cognitive decline and dementia risk.

Causes and pathophysiology

The condition arises from age-related degeneration throughout the auditory system: loss of hair cells in the organ of Corti (especially at the basal turn serving high frequencies), degeneration of the stria vascularis that maintains the endocochlear potential, loss of spiral ganglion neurons, and changes in central auditory processing. The audiologist Harold Schuknecht classified presbycusis into sensory, neural, metabolic (strial) and cochlear-conductive types. Lifetime noise exposure, cardiovascular risk factors, ototoxic medications, smoking and genetic susceptibility all modify its onset and severity.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is by pure-tone audiometry, which typically shows a sloping bilateral sensorineural loss worst at high frequencies, together with speech audiometry demonstrating reduced speech discrimination. Other causes of hearing loss — such as cerumen impaction, otosclerosis, Ménière's disease or acoustic neuroma — must be excluded, especially if the loss is asymmetric.

Treatment

There is no cure, but hearing aids substantially improve communication and quality of life; severe cases may benefit from cochlear implants. Assistive listening devices, hearing loops and communication strategies (facing the listener, reducing background noise) also help. Untreated hearing loss is a modifiable risk factor receiving increasing public-health attention.
