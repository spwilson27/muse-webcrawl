Christian fundamentalism

Christian fundamentalism is a movement within Protestant Christianity that arose in the late 19th and early 20th centuries in reaction against theological liberalism and modernism. It is characterised by a strict belief in the literal interpretation and inerrancy of the Bible, and by adherence to a set of core doctrines regarded as the "fundamentals" of the faith.

History

The movement took its name from The Fundamentals: A Testimony to the Truth, a series of twelve volumes of essays published between 1910 and 1915 and funded by oil millionaires Lyman and Milton Stewart. The term "fundamentalist" was coined in 1920 by Baptist editor Curtis Lee Laws for those ready "to do battle royal for the Fundamentals".

The movement gained national attention in the 1925 Scopes Trial in Tennessee, in which teacher John Scopes was prosecuted for teaching evolution. Although the fundamentalist side won the verdict, hostile press coverage — including H. L. Mencken's reporting — led fundamentalists to withdraw from the cultural mainstream and build separate institutions: churches, colleges, publishing houses and mission agencies.

Beliefs and later development

In the 1940s and 1950s a more engagement-minded wing broke away, calling itself neo-evangelicalism, associated with Billy Graham, Fuller Theological Seminary, and the National Association of Evangelicals. Self-identified fundamentalists, by contrast, emphasised separation from theological compromise. The movement became politically influential in the United States from the late 1970s through organisations such as the Moral Majority.
