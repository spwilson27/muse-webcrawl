The Whore of Babylon is a figure of evil mentioned in the Book of Revelation in the New Testament. Described in Revelation 17 as "Babylon the Great, the Mother of Prostitutes and Abominations of the Earth" (Greek: Βαβυλὼν ἡ μεγάλη, ἡ μήτηρ τῶν πορνῶν καὶ τῶν βδελυγμάτων τῆς γῆς), she rides a scarlet beast with seven heads and ten horns and holds a golden cup "full of abominations and the impurities of her fornication". Her fall and destruction are described in Revelation 18.

Biblical description

In Revelation 17, an angel shows the narrator the woman "with whom the kings of the earth have committed fornication, and with the wine of whose fornication the dwellers on earth have become drunk". She is clothed in purple and scarlet, adorned with gold, jewels and pearls, and has a name of mystery written on her forehead. She is "drunk with the blood of the saints and the blood of the martyrs of Jesus". The angel explains that the seven heads are seven mountains on which the woman sits — widely read as a reference to the seven hills of Rome — and that the beast and the ten horns will turn on the woman, despoil her and burn her with fire. Revelation 18 then laments Babylon's fall: her merchants weep because no one buys their cargo any more, and a mighty angel casts a millstone into the sea as a sign of the city's violent downfall.

Interpretations

  • Preterist: the figure represents first-century Rome or Jerusalem, centres of pagan power and persecution of early Christians; "Babylon" was a known code-name for Rome in early Jewish and Christian literature.
  • Historicist: during the Reformation, Protestant writers — including Martin Luther, who called the papacy "the kingdom of Babylon and the true Antichrist" — identified the Whore with the Roman Catholic Church, an identification retained in some confessional documents and later polemic.
  • Futurist: an end-times religious or political system that will dominate the world before the return of Christ.
  • Idealist/symbolic: a timeless symbol of corrupt worldly power, seductive luxury and organised opposition to God.

Catholic and many modern scholarly readings reject the anti-papal identification, instead treating Babylon as Rome of the apostolic age or as an archetype of godless civilisation. The imagery draws heavily on Old Testament prophecies against Tyre, Nineveh and Babylon itself in Isaiah, Jeremiah and Ezekiel.

In culture

The figure has inspired centuries of art, from medieval Apocalypse manuscripts and Albrecht Dürer's woodcuts to later painting and print, and the phrase "Whore of Babylon" has passed into general usage as a byword for corrupt, decadent power.
