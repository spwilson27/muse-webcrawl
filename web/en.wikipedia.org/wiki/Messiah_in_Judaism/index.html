Messiah in Judaism

The Messiah in Judaism (Hebrew: מָשִׁיחַ, mašíaḥ) is the saviour and liberator figure in Jewish eschatology, believed to be the future redeemer of the Jewish people. The term originally referred to an "anointed one" — a king or priest consecrated with holy anointing oil. In later Jewish tradition, the Messiah is a leader arising from the Davidic line who will rule the Jewish people during the Messianic Age and the world to come.

Beliefs

Classical rabbinic belief holds that the Messiah will be a human descendant of King David through Solomon, a righteous king steeped in Torah, who will gather the exiles of Israel back to the Land of Israel, rebuild the Temple in Jerusalem, and usher in an era of world peace in which all nations acknowledge the God of Israel. Maimonides codified these expectations in his Mishneh Torah: the Messiah will restore the Davidic kingdom, fight the wars of the Lord, rebuild the Temple, and bring about universal observance of the Torah and knowledge of God; miraculous powers are not required of him.

Belief in the coming of the Messiah is the twelfth of Maimonides' Thirteen Principles of Faith, traditionally affirmed in the daily liturgy ("I believe with perfect faith in the coming of the Messiah, and though he may tarry, I will wait daily for his coming"). Jewish tradition also speaks of a forerunner, Messiah ben Joseph, who in some sources dies in battle before the triumph of Messiah ben David.

Views among movements

Orthodox Judaism maintains the traditional belief in a personal Messiah. Reform Judaism historically replaced the idea of a personal redeemer with the hope for a Messianic Age of human progress, though later platforms restored some traditional language. Movements within Judaism have at times rallied around claimed messiahs, most famously Bar Kokhba in the 2nd century CE and Sabbatai Zevi in the 17th century, whose failure or apostasy reshaped Jewish thought.
