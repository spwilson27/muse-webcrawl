Key signature

In Western musical notation, a key signature is a set of sharp (♯), flat (♭), or rarely natural (♮) symbols placed on the staff at the beginning of a section of music. The initial key signature in a piece is placed immediately after the clef at the beginning of the first line. The key signature may also appear after a double bar line, announcing a change of key, a convention called modulation when it governs a substantial passage.

The purpose of the key signature is to minimise the number of accidentals needed within a piece: rather than writing a sharp or flat before every affected note, the signature declares that those lines and spaces are routinely sharp or flat throughout, unless cancelled by an explicit accidental.

Order of sharps and flats

Sharps and flats are always written in a fixed order. The order of sharps is F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯, A♯, E♯, B♯ — remembered by mnemonics such as "Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle". The order of flats is the reverse: B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, G♭, C♭, F♭ ("Battle Ends And Down Goes Charles's Father"). Successive key signatures add one sharp or flat at a time, each new key centre lying a perfect fifth above (for sharps) or below (for flats) the previous one.

Major and minor keys

Each key signature corresponds to one major key and its relative minor, a minor third below. The key with no sharps or flats is C major (or A minor); one sharp indicates G major (E minor); one flat indicates F major (D minor), and so on through the circle of fifths. Three key signatures are enharmonic equivalents: B major (5 sharps) sounds the same as C♭ major (7 flats), F♯ major (6 sharps) as G♭ major (6 flats), and C♯ major (7 sharps) as D♭ major (5 flats).

Conventions

In the minor key, melodic and harmonic minor variants use accidentals in the body of the music rather than in the signature itself. When a piece changes key signature mid-staff, the old signature is customarily cancelled with naturals before the new one is written (a practice not always followed in older editions). Key signatures are read together with the clef: the same written sharp occupies a different staff position in treble, bass, alto and tenor clefs, though it always falls on the line or space of the note it alters.
