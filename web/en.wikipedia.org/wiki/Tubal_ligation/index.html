Tubal ligation

Tubal ligation (commonly called "getting one's tubes tied") is a surgical procedure for female sterilisation in which the fallopian tubes are permanently blocked, clipped, cut or removed, preventing eggs from reaching the uterus and sperm from reaching the egg. It is one of the most widely used contraceptive methods in the world and is considered a permanent form of birth control, with a failure rate of well under one percent over ten years.

TypeSterilisation (female)
TargetFallopian tubes
Effectiveness>99%
ReversibilityIntended to be permanent

Procedures

Techniques include laparoscopic occlusion with clips (such as the Filshie clip), rings or bipolar coagulation; the Pomeroy method of tying and excising a loop of tube, often performed after caesarean delivery; and bilateral salpingectomy, complete removal of the tubes, which is increasingly favoured because it also reduces the risk of ovarian cancer, many cases of which are now thought to originate in the tube.

Risks

Risks are mainly those of surgery and anaesthesia. When pregnancy does occur after ligation, it is disproportionately likely to be ectopic. The procedure does not affect hormone production or the menstrual cycle.
