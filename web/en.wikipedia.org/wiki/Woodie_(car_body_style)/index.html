Woodie (car body style)

A woodie (or woodie wagon) is a car body style—originally a type of station wagon—in which the rear bodywork is constructed of wood or, in later examples, of materials styled to imitate wood. Early woodies were among the first utility and depot vehicles, while post-war examples became prestige family cars, and in California the style became closely associated with surf culture.

History

The earliest woodies were converted "depot hacks" used to carry passengers and luggage from railway stations. By the 1930s major manufacturers offered factory-built wooden station wagons, with coachbuilt ash framing and mahogany or birch panelling that demanded laborious varnishing and maintenance. Wood construction phased out after the Second World War as all-steel wagon bodies took over; the last true American woodie wagons were built in the early 1950s, though simulated woodgrain trim (such as on the Ford Country Squire) persisted for decades.

Surfer culture

In 1960s California, cheap used woodies became the transport of choice for surfers hauling longboards to the beach, cementing the style in popular culture through songs and films. Preserved and restored woodies are now valuable collector cars, celebrated at dedicated shows; the National Woodie Club was founded in 1974.
