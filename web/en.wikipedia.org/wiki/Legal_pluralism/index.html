Legal pluralism

Legal pluralism is the presence of multiple legal systems within one society or state. In its classic form it described colonial situations in which a European colonial power superimposed its law on pre-existing indigenous customary law, so that different populations within the same territory were governed by different bodies of law in matters such as marriage, inheritance and land. In its modern, expanded form it describes the coexistence of state law with religious law, customary law, transnational law and the internal normative orders of organisations and communities.

Development of the concept

Legal anthropologists such as Leopold Pospisil and Sally Falk Moore developed the study of plural legal orders empirically; Moore's concept of the "semi-autonomous social field" described social arenas that generate their own rules while remaining vulnerable to outside law. John Griffiths's influential 1986 article "What is Legal Pluralism?" distinguished "weak" pluralism, recognised and administered by the state, from "strong" or deep pluralism, in which multiple legal orders exist as a matter of social fact regardless of state recognition.

Examples

Many states formally recognise customary or religious law: personal law systems in India apply Hindu, Muslim and other religious law to family matters; several African constitutions recognise customary courts; and religious tribunals such as Jewish beth din courts and Islamic sharia councils operate by consent in Western countries. International and supranational law, commercial arbitration and lex mercatoria are also cited as instances of pluralism above and beyond the state.

Criticism and debate

Critics ask whether non-state normative orders should be called "law" at all, and warn that recognition of group law can entrench inequality, particularly for women. Supporters respond that pluralism describes reality more accurately than legal centralism and can protect minority communities' rights, including indigenous rights recognised in instruments such as the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples.
