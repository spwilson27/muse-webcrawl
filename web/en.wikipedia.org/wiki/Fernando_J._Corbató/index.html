Fernando José "Corby" Corbató (July 1, 1926 – July 12, 2019) was an American computer scientist, notable as a pioneer in the development of time-sharing operating systems. He received the Turing Award in 1990 "for his pioneering work in organizing the concepts and leading the development of the general-purpose, large-scale, time-sharing and resource-sharing computer systems."

Career

Corbató, the son of Spanish immigrants from Villarreal, Spain, served as a U.S. Navy electronics technician during the Second World War, then studied at Caltech and earned his PhD in physics from MIT in 1956. He joined the MIT Computation Center and in 1961 demonstrated the Compatible Time-Sharing System (CTSS), one of the first time-sharing operating systems, which let multiple users interact with a single mainframe simultaneously and introduced the computer password for user accounts.

He then led the development of Multics (Multiplexed Information and Computing Service), begun in 1964 with MIT, General Electric, and Bell Labs. Multics introduced influential concepts including a hierarchical file system, dynamic linking, access control lists, and rings of protection; it strongly influenced Ken Thompson's design of Unix. Corbató also formulated "Corbató's Law": the number of lines of code a programmer can write in a fixed period is the same regardless of the language used.
