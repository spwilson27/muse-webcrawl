Full Faith and Credit Clause

The Full Faith and Credit Clause is Article IV, Section 1 of the United States Constitution, which provides: "Full Faith and Credit shall be given in each State to the public Acts, Records, and judicial Proceedings of every other State. And the Congress may by general Laws prescribe the Manner in which such Acts, Records and Proceedings shall be proved, and the Effect thereof."

Purpose

The clause ensures that states within the federal union respect one another's laws, official records and court judgments, so that a judgment obtained in one state is enforceable in another and legal transactions are portable across state lines. An earlier version appeared in the Articles of Confederation. Congress implemented the clause in the Act of 26 May 1790, whose modern descendant, 28 U.S.C. § 1738, extends the obligation to the courts and requires states to give sister-state judgments the same preclusive effect they have at home.

Scope

The clause applies most forcefully to judicial proceedings: a final judgment of a court with jurisdiction must generally be enforced even if it rests on a claim repugnant to the forum state's policy (Fauntleroy v. Lum, 1908). It applies more weakly to public acts (statutes): under choice-of-law rules a state may apply its own law when it has a sufficient interest, so long as it does not discriminate against sister-state law (Hughes v. Fetter, 1951). Records such as birth certificates, marriage licences and adoption decrees are routinely recognised; in V.L. v. E.L. (2016) the Supreme Court summarily required Alabama to recognise a Georgia adoption judgment.

Marriage and the Defense of Marriage Act

The clause figured in the debate over same-sex marriage: the Defense of Marriage Act (1996) purported to allow states to refuse recognition of same-sex marriages from other states. After Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) required all states to license and recognise such marriages, and the Respect for Marriage Act (2022) mandated interstate recognition of valid marriages, the clause remains the constitutional backdrop to recognition of marriages, divorces and custody decrees across state lines.
