The Respect for Marriage Act (RFMA) is a landmark United States federal law enacted in 2022 that repeals the Defense of Marriage Act (DOMA) and requires the federal government and all U.S. states and territories to recognise the validity of same-sex and interracial marriages performed in another state. It also provides protections for religious liberty. President Joe Biden signed it into law on 13 December 2022.

Background

The Supreme Court's decisions in United States v. Windsor (2013) and Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) invalidated DOMA's core provisions and guaranteed same-sex couples the right to marry nationwide, while Loving v. Virginia (1967) had earlier struck down bans on interracial marriage. After Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022) overruled Roe v. Wade, and Justice Clarence Thomas's concurrence suggested reconsidering substantive-due-process precedents including Obergefell, members of Congress moved to codify recognition protections in statute.

Provisions

  • Repeals DOMA in full.
  • Requires states to give full faith and credit to marriages performed in other states, regardless of the sex, race, ethnicity or national origin of the spouses.
  • Requires the federal government to recognise any marriage valid in the state where it was performed.
  • Protects nonprofit religious organisations from being required to provide services, facilities or goods for the celebration of a marriage, and confirms that religious beliefs about marriage are protected; these provisions were key to securing bipartisan support.
  • Notably, the Act does not require states to issue marriage licences to same-sex couples; it mandates recognition of marriages validly performed elsewhere.

Legislative history

Bills under the same name had been introduced in Congress since 2009. The 2022 version passed the House in July 2022 by 267–157; after the Senate added a religious-liberty amendment, it passed the Senate 61–36 on 29 November 2022, and the House approved the amended bill 258–169 on 8 December. Biden signed it at a White House ceremony attended by lawmakers and advocates.

Reactions

LGBT and civil-rights organisations praised the Act as essential insurance against judicial reversal, while noting it stops short of a full statutory guarantee of marriage equality. Some conservative groups opposed it, arguing religious protections were insufficient; others, including the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, supported the amended bill's balance of marriage recognition and religious freedom.
