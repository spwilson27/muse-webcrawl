Vampire

A vampire is a legendary creature, usually a reanimated corpse, that subsists by feeding on the blood or vital essence of the living. Belief in blood-drinking revenants is ancient and widespread, but the vampire of modern folklore emerged in southeastern Europe in the early 18th century, when reports of exhumations and staking of corpses in Serbia and neighbouring regions drew the attention of Austrian officials and Western European writers.

Folklore

In Slavic and Balkan tradition, a person might become a vampire through an improper burial, suicide, excommunication, a violent death, or because a cat or bird had passed over the corpse. Suspected vampires were recognised by a ruddy, bloated appearance and blood at the mouth; corpses that failed to decompose were prime suspects. Communities countered them by staking the body through the heart, decapitation, burning, or placing poppy seeds, millet or a sickle in the grave. Vampire scares in 18th-century Serbia — notably the cases of Petar Blagojević (1725) and Arnold Paole (1731–32) — produced official reports that spread the word vampir across Europe.

In literature and popular culture

The vampire entered English literature with John Polidori's The Vampyre (1819), which recast the folkloric peasant corpse as an aristocratic seducer modelled partly on Lord Byron. The penny dreadful Varney the Vampire (1845–47), Sheridan Le Fanu's Carmilla (1872) and above all Bram Stoker's Dracula (1897) fixed the modern image: a nocturnal aristocrat with fangs, hypnotic powers and weaknesses to garlic, sunlight, crosses and stakes. Twentieth-century cinema, from Nosferatu (1922) onward, and later authors such as Anne Rice and Stephenie Meyer repeatedly reinvented the figure, variously as monster, romantic hero or social metaphor.

Explanations

Scholars have linked vampire beliefs to premature burial, the visible stages of decomposition, epidemics such as tuberculosis and rabies, and the blood disorder porphyria (a connection popularised in the 1980s but widely disputed). Similar blood-drinking beings occur in many cultures, from the Mesopotamian lilitu traditions to the Chinese jiangshi and the Mexican chupacabra legends of the 1990s.
