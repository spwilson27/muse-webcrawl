Nuclear weapon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A nuclear weapon is an explosive device whose destructive force comes from nuclear reactions — fission, fusion, or both. A single weapon can destroy a city; the largest ever tested, the Soviet Tsar Bomba (1961), yielded about 50 megatons, thousands of times the power of the bombs used in 1945.

Types

Pure fission weapons (the atomic bombs of 1945) split heavy nuclei such as uranium-235 or plutonium-239. Thermonuclear weapons ("hydrogen bombs") use a fission primary to ignite a fusion secondary, and can be scaled to far greater yields. Delivery systems include gravity bombs, ballistic and cruise missiles (land-, sea- and air-launched), and artillery in early Cold War designs. Weapons are classed as strategic or tactical (non-strategic) by range and intended use.

History and arsenals

Nine states are known or believed to possess nuclear weapons: the United States, Russia, the United Kingdom, France, China, India, Pakistan, North Korea and Israel. Global stockpiles peaked at roughly 70,000 warheads in the 1980s and have since fallen to roughly a fifth of that, though modernisation programmes continue in all nuclear-armed states.

Control efforts

The Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1970) commits non-nuclear states not to acquire weapons and nuclear states to pursue disarmament. Bilateral US–Soviet/Russian treaties limited and reduced strategic arsenals, and a Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty (1996) has been observed as a moratorium though not ratified by all. The 2017 Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons, supported by most non-nuclear states, is rejected by all nuclear-armed states.
