William of Norwich

William of Norwich (2 February 1132 – c. 22 March 1144) was an English boy apprentice whose unexplained death was attributed to the Jewish community of Norwich. It is the earliest known medieval accusation of ritual murder against Jews — a blood libel — and William became the focus of a saint's cult.

William of Norwich (2 February 1132 – c. 22 March 1144) was an English apprentice tanner whose unexplained death was falsely blamed on the Jews of Norwich. The case generated the first recorded medieval "blood libel" — the charge that Jews ritually murder Christian children — a belief that spread across Europe with deadly consequences for Jewish communities.

Death

William, aged about twelve, worked as an apprentice to a skinner in Norwich. He disappeared during Holy Week in March 1144, and his body was found in Thorpe Wood outside the city on Holy Saturday (25 March 1144). No cause of death was established at the time, and no contemporary secular inquiry accused anyone.

The accusation and cult

Suspicion against the Jews grew over the following years, promoted locally by William's family and by clergy. In 1150 the monk Thomas of Monmouth arrived in Norwich and began writing The Life and Miracles of St William of Norwich, the only source for the story, in which he claimed that Norwich's Jews had crucified the boy in mockery of Christ. The accusation was never tested in court; Sheriff John de Chesney protected the city's Jews, who took refuge in Norwich Castle.

William's remains were translated into Norwich Cathedral, and a local cult developed around his tomb, though he was never formally canonised. Historians regard the case as the origin of the ritual-murder libel in medieval Europe, preceding the better-known case of Little Saint Hugh of Lincoln (1255).

Modern assessment

Modern scholarship rejects Thomas of Monmouth's account entirely: there is no evidence of Jewish involvement in William's death, and historians such as Gavin Langmuir and E. M. Rose have examined how the myth was constructed and propagated.
