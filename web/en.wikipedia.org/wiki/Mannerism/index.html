Mannerism is a style in European art that emerged in the later years of the Italian High Renaissance around 1520, spreading by 1530 and lasting until about the end of the 16th century, when the Baroque style began to replace it. The name derives from the Italian maniera — "style" or "manner" — as used by Giorgio Vasari to praise the graceful, effortless technique of artists he admired. Northern Mannerism continued into the early 17th century.

Characteristics

Mannerist art deliberately departs from the balance, harmony, and naturalism of the High Renaissance. Its hallmarks include elongated proportions, exaggerated and twisting poses (the serpentine figura serpentinata), crowded or irrationally compressed space, ambiguous scale, acidic or clashing colours, and displays of virtuoso difficulty for their own sake. Parmigianino's Madonna with the Long Neck and Pontormo's Deposition are classic examples; late Michelangelo — the Laurentian Library vestibule and the Last Judgment — is often treated as the movement's fountainhead.

Development and spread

Early Mannerism (c. 1520–1535) grew in Florence and Rome amid political crisis — the Sack of Rome in 1527 dispersed artists across Italy and Europe. Rosso Fiorentino and Primaticcio carried the style to the court of Fontainebleau in France; other centres flourished at the courts of Mantua (Giulio Romano's Palazzo Te), Parma, Venice (Tintoretto, Veronese), Prague under Rudolf II, and in Spain, where El Greco developed its most spiritual expression.

Reputation

Long dismissed as decadent or bizarre — the term was pejorative for centuries — Mannerism was rehabilitated by 20th-century scholars such as Max Dvořák and Walter Friedländer, who read its distortions as expressive responses to an age of religious and political upheaval. It also has recognised phases in literature and music (the madrigal, Gesualdo's chromaticism).
