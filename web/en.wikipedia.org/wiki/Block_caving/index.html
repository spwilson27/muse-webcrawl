Block caving

Block caving is an underground hard-rock mining method in which a large block of ore is undercut — cut off from the rock mass beneath it — so that it collapses under its own weight and fractures into smaller pieces. The broken ore is drawn off through a grid of drawpoints on a production level below. Because the caving process does much of the work of breaking the rock, block caving is among the lowest-cost underground mining methods per tonne, suited to large, low-grade, vertically extensive ore bodies.

Method

Development involves excavating an undercut level and, beneath it, an extraction level with drawbells connecting to drawpoints. Once undercutting is complete, the ore column begins to cave; the cave propagates upward as broken ore is drawn, and fractured ore flows by gravity to the drawpoints, where it is loaded by LHD vehicles or passes through ore passes to a haulage level, crushers and conveyors or hoisting.

Suitability and examples

The method requires an ore body that will cave and fragment predictably, competent enough surrounding rock to support the infrastructure, and acceptance of surface subsidence above the cave. Major block cave mines include the El Teniente and Chuquicamata underground operations in Chile, the Grasberg block cave in Indonesia, Oyu Tolgoi in Mongolia, the Resolution Copper project in Arizona, and Australia's Cadia East and Northparkes mines. Many of these are conversions of large open pits to underground mining as pits reach their economic limits.

Risks

Block caving involves long lead times and large upfront capital. Principal technical risks include poor fragmentation, cave stalls, air blasts from sudden cave collapses, mud rushes at drawpoints and uncontrolled subsidence.
