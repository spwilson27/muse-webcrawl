Vine training

Vine training in viticulture is the practice of shaping and supporting a grapevine's permanent wood and annual growth through pruning, trellising and canopy management, in order to optimise fruit quality, yield, sunlight exposure, air circulation and the ease of vineyard work such as spraying and harvesting.

Principles

A trained vine consists of the trunk, permanent arms (cordons) or a head, and fruiting canes or spurs renewed by pruning each dormant season. Training arranges the canopy so that leaves receive ample light for photosynthesis while clusters are shaded enough to avoid sunburn yet exposed enough to ripen and resist fungal disease. The choice of system depends on climate, grape variety, vigour, soil fertility, mechanisation and tradition.

Common systems

  • Head training (gobelet/bush vine): a short trunk with a crown of spurs, self-supporting without trellis; common in dry Mediterranean regions.
  • Cordon training: one or two permanent horizontal arms along a wire, pruned to short spurs.
  • Cane (Guyot) training: one or two fruiting canes tied horizontally each year, widely used in cool climates such as Burgundy and Bordeaux.
  • Vertical shoot positioning (VSP): shoots are tucked upright between pairs of foliage wires.
  • Divided canopy systems (e.g. Geneva Double Curtain, Lyre, Scott Henry): split the canopy to increase light interception on vigorous sites.
  • Pergola: an overhead trellis traditional in parts of Italy and elsewhere, shading fruit beneath a leafy canopy.
