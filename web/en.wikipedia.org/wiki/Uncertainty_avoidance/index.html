Uncertainty avoidance

In cross-cultural psychology, uncertainty avoidance is how cultures differ in the extent to which their members are comfortable with, or stressed by, ambiguous and uncertain situations. It is one of the dimensions of culture identified by Dutch social psychologist Geert Hofstede in his cultural dimensions theory, based originally on survey research conducted among IBM employees in more than 50 countries.

The dimension

Societies with high uncertainty avoidance maintain rigid codes of belief and behaviour and are relatively intolerant of unorthodox ideas and deviance; they tend to have many rules and laws, value security and formal structure, and show higher levels of anxiety and stress. Societies with low uncertainty avoidance take a more relaxed attitude in which practice counts more than principles, rules are fewer, and ambiguity and risk are more readily accepted.

Hofstede's Uncertainty Avoidance Index (UAI) scores countries on a scale roughly from 0 to 100. Greece, Portugal, Guatemala, Uruguay, and Belgium scored among the highest in the original research, while Singapore, Jamaica, Denmark, Sweden, and Ireland scored among the lowest.

Related research

Uncertainty avoidance should not be confused with risk avoidance: a high-UAI society may accept familiar risks while avoiding ambiguity. The dimension has been applied in international business, marketing, and management research, though Hofstede's framework has also drawn criticism for its static view of national culture and its reliance on a single company's workforce. The later GLOBE project of Robert J. House and colleagues measured a related dimension with somewhat different results, distinguishing cultural practices ("as is") from cultural values ("should be").
