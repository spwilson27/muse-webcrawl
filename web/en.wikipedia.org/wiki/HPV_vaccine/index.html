Human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccines are vaccines that prevent infection by certain types of human papillomavirus (HPV). Available HPV vaccines protect against either two, four, or nine types of HPV. All HPV vaccines protect against at least HPV types 16 and 18, which cause the greatest risk of cervical cancer. It is estimated that HPV vaccines may prevent 70% of cervical cancer, 80% of anal cancer, 60% of vaginal cancer, 40% of vulvar cancer, and show more than 90% efficacy in preventing HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancers. They additionally prevent some genital warts, with the quadrivalent and nonavalent vaccines providing the greatest protection.

Recommendations

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends HPV vaccines as part of routine vaccinations in all countries, along with other prevention measures. Most national programmes target girls aged 9 to 14, before sexual debut, and many countries also recommend vaccination of boys to reduce transmission and prevent HPV-related cancers in men. Two doses are generally recommended for those vaccinated before age 15, and three doses for older recipients and immunocompromised people.

Effectiveness and impact

Population studies in countries with high coverage, including Australia, Sweden, and England, have documented steep declines in HPV infections, genital warts, high-grade cervical lesions, and — in Swedish registry data — invasive cervical cancer among vaccinated cohorts. Australia has been projected to approach elimination of cervical cancer as a public-health problem within decades.

Safety

HPV vaccines are generally well tolerated; the most common reactions are pain and redness at the injection site and transient fainting in adolescents. Large reviews by the WHO, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and the European Medicines Agency have repeatedly found no causal link to autoimmune disorders or other serious adverse events.

History

The vaccines use virus-like particles assembled from the HPV L1 capsid protein, a technology developed in part by Ian Frazer and Jian Zhou, and by researchers at the U.S. National Cancer Institute. Gardasil was first licensed in 2006; the nine-valent Gardasil 9 followed in 2014.
