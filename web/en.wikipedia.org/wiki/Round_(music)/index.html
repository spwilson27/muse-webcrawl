Round (music)

A round in music is a composition in which two or more voices sing exactly the same melody in unison (which may be a canon at the unison), with each voice beginning at a different time so that the entries overlap. The melody must be composed so that it harmonises with itself at the offset points. Rounds are the simplest form of canon and have been sung socially for centuries, by both children and adults.

Characteristics

A round is typically short, repetitive, and circular: when the last voice finishes, the others are partway through, so the piece can repeat indefinitely until the singers choose to stop. The lines of the melody are arranged so that chords implied at each offset are consonant. Unlike a fugue, a round uses identical material throughout at the same pitch; unlike the related catch, a round's words do not traditionally hide a second, comic meaning — though in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century England the two genres overlapped.

History and examples

The earliest known round in English is "Sumer is icumen in" (c. 1250), a rota for up to six voices celebrating spring. Famous examples from the oral and school traditions include "Frère Jacques," "Row, Row, Row Your Boat" (first published in 1852), "Three Blind Mice," and "London's Burning." Composers from Thomas Ravenscroft, whose collections of 1609 printed many rounds and catches, to Henry Purcell wrote rounds for convivial singing, and the form survives in children's music education, campfire singing, and barbershop and choral warm-ups.
