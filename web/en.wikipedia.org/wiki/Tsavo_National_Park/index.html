Tsavo National Park is a national park in Kenya, divided by the Nairobi–Mombasa road and railway into Tsavo East and Tsavo West. Together they form one of the largest protected areas in the world, covering roughly 22,000 square kilometres of semi-arid savanna, volcanic hills and riverine forest. Established in 1948, Tsavo is famous for its large herds of elephants coated in the region's red volcanic dust, and for the legend of the man-eating Tsavo lions.

Tsavo East

Tsavo East, the larger section (about 13,747 km²), is generally flat, with dry plains through which the Galana River flows, formed by the confluence of the Tsavo and Athi rivers. Landmarks include the Lugard Falls, the Yatta Plateau — one of the world's longest lava flows — and the Aruba Dam, a popular wildlife-viewing waterhole. Elephants, lions, leopards, buffalo, giraffes and large antelope herds are common.

Tsavo West

Tsavo West (about 9,065 km²) is hillier and wetter, with volcanic cones, the Chaimu lava flows, and the crystal-clear Mzima Springs, where filtered water bubbles up through lava rock and hippos and crocodiles can be watched from an underwater viewing chamber. The Ngulia Rhino Sanctuary protects a population of black rhinoceroses.

The man-eaters of Tsavo

In 1898, during construction of the Uganda Railway bridge over the Tsavo River, two maneless male lions terrorised the workforce for months, dragging workers from their tents at night; Lt. Col. John Henry Patterson eventually shot both. Estimates of the number killed vary wildly — from 28 documented railway workers to Patterson's claim of 135. The skins and skulls are displayed at the Field Museum in Chicago, and the episode inspired the 1996 film The Ghost and the Darkness.
