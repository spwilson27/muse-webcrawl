British honours system

The British honours system comprises the orders of chivalry, decorations and medals by which the British monarch rewards personal bravery, achievement and service in the United Kingdom and the Commonwealth realms. The monarch, as fount of honour, remains the sole authority for granting many honours, usually acting on the advice of the government. Honours lists are published twice a year, at New Year and on the monarch's official birthday in June.

Grades and titles

The senior grades of some orders — Knight or Dame Grand Cross and Knight or Dame Commander — confer the title Sir or Dame on British citizens. Lower grades (CBE, OBE, MBE) carry post-nominal letters but no title. The Order of the Companions of Honour (1917) is limited to 65 members, and the Order of Merit (1902) to 24, both in the monarch's personal gift.

Gallantry and campaign awards

The Victoria Cross (1856) is the highest award for gallantry in the presence of the enemy, followed by the George Cross (1940) for civilian and non-combat gallantry. Serving personnel also receive campaign and long-service medals.

Criticism and reform

The system has repeatedly been criticised: the sale of honours under Lloyd George led to the Honours (Prevention of Abuses) Act 1925, and modern controversies have concerned honours for political donors and the word "Empire" in the OBE, which some recipients have declined. Nominations from the public are considered by independent honours committees before lists are approved by the Prime Minister and the monarch.
