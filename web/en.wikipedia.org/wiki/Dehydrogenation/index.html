Dehydrogenation

Dehydrogenation is a chemical reaction that involves the removal of hydrogen, generally from an organic molecule. It is the reverse of hydrogenation. Dehydrogenation converts saturated compounds into unsaturated ones — for example, alkanes into alkenes or alkenes into alkynes — and is typically endothermic, requiring high temperatures and a catalyst. It is widely used in the petrochemical industry and also occurs in biological systems, where enzymes called dehydrogenases carry out analogous oxidations.

Dehydrogenation of ethylbenzene gives styrene, a major industrial monomer

Industrial processes

The most important large-scale dehydrogenation is the conversion of ethylbenzene to styrene, carried out over iron oxide catalysts with steam at around 600 °C. Dehydrogenation of light alkanes is used to produce olefins: propane is dehydrogenated to propylene in processes such as Oleflex and Catofin, and butanes are converted to butenes and butadiene. Dehydrogenation of cyclohexane to benzene and of methylcyclohexane to toluene has also been studied for chemical hydrogen storage, since the hydrogen released can be used in fuel cells and the aromatic product shipped and rehydrogenated. In petroleum refining, catalytic reforming involves dehydrogenation and aromatisation reactions that upgrade naphtha into high-octane gasoline components.

Chemistry

Because dehydrogenation increases unsaturation, it is formally an oxidation. Laboratory and commercial catalysts include platinum-group metals, chromia–alumina and mixed metal oxides; promoters and steam are used to limit coking. Equilibrium limitations at high conversion are mitigated by low pressure, steam dilution or membranes that remove hydrogen. In biochemistry, dehydrogenases such as alcohol dehydrogenase and succinate dehydrogenase transfer hydrogen to cofactors such as NAD+ and FAD, linking dehydrogenation to cellular respiration.
