Nursery rhyme

A nursery rhyme is a traditional poem or song for children, especially young children, in Britain and many other countries. The term dates from the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, when collections such as Tommy Thumb's Song Book (1744) and the tales and rhymes of Mother Goose began to be published specifically for the nursery. Famous examples include "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star", "Humpty Dumpty", "Jack and Jill", "Mary Had a Little Lamb", and "Baa, Baa, Black Sheep".

Origins

Many rhymes are far older than their first publication, surviving as oral tradition; some began as adult ballads, drinking songs, riddles, proverbs, or political satire later relegated to children. Theories linking rhymes to specific historical events — such as reading "Ring a Ring o' Roses" as a plague rhyme — are popular but often unsupported by the documentary record, and folklorists such as Iona and Peter Opie, whose Oxford Dictionary of Nursery Rhymes (1951) is the standard reference, treated such claims cautiously.

Educational role

Nursery rhymes are valued in early education because their rhythm, repetition, and rhyme support phonological awareness and early literacy, as well as memorization, counting, and social play (many are attached to games, clapping patterns, or actions, as in "Pat-a-cake"). Lullabies form a closely related category of song used to soothe infants to sleep.

In other cultures

Most cultures have equivalent bodies of children's verse; the English canon spread widely through the British Empire and American publishing, where figures such as Mother Goose became generic names for nursery-rhyme collections.
