Jürgen Habermas (born 18 June 1929) is a German philosopher and social theorist in the tradition of critical theory and pragmatism. His work addresses communicative rationality and the public sphere. Associated with the Frankfurt School, Habermas's work focuses on the foundations of epistemology and social theory, the analysis of advanced capitalism and democracy, the rule of law in a critical social-evolutionary context, and contemporary politics, particularly German politics. Habermas's theoretical system is devoted to revealing the possibility of reason, emancipation, and rational-critical communication latent in modern institutions and in the human capacity to deliberate and pursue rational interests.

Early life and education

Habermas was born in Düsseldorf in 1929, the son of Ernst Habermas, executive director of the Chamber of Industry and Commerce in Cologne, whom Habermas described as a Nazi sympathizer. He grew up during the Second World War and was a member of the Hitler Youth as a teenager, an experience he later described as formative in his commitment to democracy. He studied philosophy, history, psychology, German literature, and economics at the universities of Göttingen, Zürich, and Bonn, completing his doctorate at Bonn in 1954 with a dissertation on the concept of the Absolute in the philosophy of Schelling.

Academic career

From 1956 to 1959 Habermas was an assistant to Theodor W. Adorno at the Institute for Social Research in Frankfurt am Main. His habilitation thesis, The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere (1962), examined the emergence of the bourgeois public sphere in the eighteenth century and its subsequent decline. He held professorships at Heidelberg and Frankfurt, and from 1971 to 1983 directed the Max Planck Institute for the Study of the Conditions of Life in the Scientific-Technical World in Starnberg, before returning to Frankfurt, where he remained until his retirement in 1993.

Major works

Habermas's two-volume The Theory of Communicative Action (1981) is widely regarded as his magnum opus, developing the concept of communicative rationality: the idea that human action oriented toward mutual understanding, rather than strategic success, forms the basis of social coordination and rational discourse. In Between Facts and Norms (1992) he applied his theory of discourse to law and democratic politics, articulating a proceduralist model of deliberative democracy. His other major works include Knowledge and Human Interests (1968) and The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity (1985).

Discourse ethics and deliberative democracy

Together with Karl-Otto Apel, Habermas developed discourse ethics, the view that moral norms can claim validity only if all those affected could accept them as participants in rational discourse. This principle underpins his defence of deliberative democracy and of the European project, as well as his concept of "constitutional patriotism" — loyalty to democratic procedures and universalist principles rather than to ethnic or national identity.

Later work and recognition

In his later years Habermas turned increasingly to the role of religion in the public sphere, arguing for a "postsecular" society in which religious and secular citizens engage in mutual translation of their convictions. He received numerous honours, including the Kyoto Prize (2004) and the Holberg Prize (2005), and is frequently cited as one of the most influential philosophers of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries.
