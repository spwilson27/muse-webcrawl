Country calling code

Defined by ITU-T recommendation E.164.

Country calling codes, also known as international dialling codes, are the telephone number prefixes used to reach subscribers in foreign countries or areas in the international public switched telephone network. The codes are defined by the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) in standard E.164; the general practice is to prefix the code with a plus sign (+), representing the international access code of the caller's country (for example 00 or 011).

Structure

E.164 numbers have a maximum of 15 digits: a country code of one to three digits followed by the national significant number. The first digit of the country code identifies a world numbering zone:

ZoneRegion
1North American Numbering Plan (e.g. +1 United States, Canada, Caribbean)
2Mostly Africa (e.g. +20 Egypt, +27 South Africa)
3, 4Europe (e.g. +33 France, +44 United Kingdom, +49 Germany)
5Central and South America (e.g. +52 Mexico, +55 Brazil)
6South-east Asia and Oceania (e.g. +61 Australia, +64 New Zealand)
7Russia and Kazakhstan
8East Asia and special services (e.g. +81 Japan, +86 China)
9West, Central, and South Asia, Middle East (e.g. +91 India)

Usage

To call abroad, a subscriber dials the international call prefix of their own country (or "+" on mobile phones), then the country code, then the national number, usually omitting any leading trunk digit such as 0. Some codes serve special purposes—for example +800 for international freephone services and +881 for satellite networks.

History

International direct dialling spread in the 1960s and 1970s as electromechanical and then digital switching replaced operator-assisted calls. The ITU's E.164 standard, first issued in 1988 and revised since, harmonised numbering internationally. Country codes are occasionally reassigned—for instance following the dissolution of the Soviet Union, Yugoslavia, and Czechoslovakia, whose successor states received new codes.
