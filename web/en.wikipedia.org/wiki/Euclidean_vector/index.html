Euclidean vector

In mathematics, physics, and engineering, a Euclidean vector (sometimes called a geometric or spatial vector) is a geometric object that has magnitude (or length) and direction. Vectors can be added to other vectors according to vector algebra and multiplied by scalars (real numbers). A Euclidean vector is frequently represented by a directed line segment, or graphically as an arrow connecting an initial point A with a terminal point B.

Overview

A vector is what is needed to "carry" the point A to the point B; the Latin word vector means "carrier". Physical examples include displacement, velocity, acceleration, and force. Two vectors are considered equal if they have the same magnitude and direction, regardless of where they are placed (such vectors are called "free vectors"). In coordinates, a vector in three-dimensional space is written as an ordered triple (x, y, z), its components along three perpendicular axes.

Operations

The fundamental operations are addition (by the parallelogram or triangle rule), scalar multiplication (stretching or reversing the vector), the dot product, which yields a scalar related to the angle between two vectors, and the cross product, which yields a vector perpendicular to both inputs in three dimensions. The length (norm) of a vector v is written |v|, and a vector of length one is a unit vector.

The modern concept emerged in the 19th century through the work of Bellavitis, Grassmann, Hamilton (quaternions), and Gibbs and Heaviside, who separated vector analysis from quaternion theory in the form now standard in physics. Euclidean vectors are the concrete model for the abstract vectors of linear algebra in finite-dimensional Euclidean space; see also a different, unrelated sense of "Euclidean" in land-use law.
