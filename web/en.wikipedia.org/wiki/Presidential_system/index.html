Presidential system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A presidential system, or single executive system, is a form of government in which a head of government (president) heads an executive branch that derives its authority and legitimacy from a separate mandate from the legislature and is not subject to a vote of confidence in ordinary circumstances. The president is both head of state and head of government, is elected for a fixed term, and can be removed before its end only by extraordinary procedures such as impeachment.

Features

Key features include a strict separation of personnel between executive and legislature (cabinet members cannot sit as legislators in the classic US model), separate elections producing a "dual legitimacy" problem when president and legislature are controlled by rivals, presidential powers of veto (subject to override), pardon, appointment and command of the armed forces, and decree or executive-order powers that vary by constitution.

Spread and debate

The model originates in the United States Constitution (1787) and predominates in Latin America and parts of Africa and Asia. Juan Linz's famous critique argued presidentialism's rigidity and winner-takes-all character made democracies more fragile than parliamentarism; later scholarship (Cheibub and others) found the statistical link weaker and conditioned by party systems and military legacies. Many states compromise with semi-presidential systems, combining an elected president with a prime minister responsible to parliament.
