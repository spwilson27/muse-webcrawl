The St. Lawrence River (French: Fleuve Saint-Laurent) is a large river in eastern North America flowing northeast from Lake Ontario to the Gulf of St. Lawrence and the Atlantic Ocean. Together with the Great Lakes it forms one of the world's largest freshwater systems and the principal commercial waterway of Canada and the northern United States. Jacques Cartier named the gulf and river for Saint Lawrence, whose feast day fell on his arrival in 1535.

Course

The river runs between Ontario and New York State through the Thousand Islands, then entirely through Quebec past Montreal, Trois-Rivières and Quebec City, widening into one of the world's largest estuaries before reaching the gulf. Tributaries include the Ottawa, Richelieu, Saint-François, Chaudière and Saguenay rivers.

Navigation and the Seaway

Rapids above Montreal long barred oceangoing ships; a series of canals, culminating in the St. Lawrence Seaway (opened 1959, jointly with the United States), allows vessels to pass locks from the Atlantic to Lake Superior. The Moses–Saunders Power Dam also harnesses the river for hydroelectricity. The river freezes in winter, closing navigation seasonally.

Ecology

The estuary supports beluga whales, blue whales, seals and rich birdlife; thirteen whale species visit the gulf. Industrial pollution and invasive species such as zebra mussels remain major concerns. The St. Lawrence beluga population is protected as threatened.

History

The valley was the heartland of the St. Lawrence Iroquoians encountered by Cartier, then of New France. Battles for the river decided the fate of Canada in the Seven Years' War. It remains the demographic and economic spine of French-speaking Quebec.
