Monument

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The floodlit Arc de Triomphe at night, traffic circling beneath it
The floodlit Arc de Triomphe at night, traffic circling beneath it

A monument is a type of structure created to commemorate a person or event, or which has become relevant to a social group as part of their remembrance of historic times or cultural heritage, due to its artistic, historical, political, technical or architectural importance. Monuments range from triumphal arches, obelisks and columns to statues, mausolea and entire complexes (Angkor Wat, the Taj Mahal); the word derives from Latin monere — to remind, to warn.

2. Contested monuments

Functions

Monuments are memory made durable: they legitimate regimes (imperial forums), mourn the collective dead (war memorials, the Cenotaph, Maya Lin's Vietnam Wall), mark faith (cathedrals, stupas) and advertise cities (the Eiffel Tower — built as a temporary exhibition piece). Their placement in public space makes them claims on the future's attention, and every generation renegotiates the claim.

Contested monuments

Because monuments teach, they fight: the toppling and removal of Confederate statues in the US, Rhodes Must Fall (2015), the post-Soviet and post-2022 decommunisation of Ukraine, and debates over empty plinths, museums-of-exile and contextual plaques mark a global reckoning with whom public memory serves. Heritage law (scheduled monuments, UNESCO listing) protects the category even as politics contests its contents.
