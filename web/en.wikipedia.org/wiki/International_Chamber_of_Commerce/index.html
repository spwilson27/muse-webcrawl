The International Chamber of Commerce (ICC; French: Chambre de commerce internationale) is the largest, most representative business organisation in the world. Its members include many of the world's largest companies as well as small and medium enterprises, business associations and local chambers of commerce, drawn from more than 170 countries. The ICC describes itself as the world business organisation and acts as a leading advocate for international trade and investment.

History

The ICC was founded in 1919, in the aftermath of the First World War, at a conference of business leaders in Atlantic City, New Jersey; its permanent headquarters were established in Paris. Its founders, a group of industrialists and financiers sometimes called the "merchants of peace", sought to promote open trade and investment across borders as a foundation for prosperity and peace. The ICC was granted consultative status with the United Nations Economic and Social Council in 1946, and in 2016 it became the first business organisation to receive Observer Status at the UN General Assembly.

Activities

The ICC is best known for three kinds of activity:

  • Rule-setting: it produces widely used voluntary rules and standards for international trade, including the Incoterms rules (first published 1936), the Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits (UCP 600), and model contracts.
  • Dispute resolution: the ICC International Court of Arbitration, founded in 1923, is one of the world's leading institutions for international commercial arbitration.
  • Policy advocacy: it represents business in forums such as the G20, the World Trade Organization and the UN climate negotiations.

Organisation

The ICC operates through national committees in around 90 countries, a World Council, an Executive Board, and specialist commissions on topics from banking and taxation to digital economy and the environment. Its World Chambers Federation links chambers of commerce worldwide.
