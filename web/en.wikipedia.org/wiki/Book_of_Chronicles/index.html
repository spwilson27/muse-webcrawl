The Book of Chronicles (Hebrew: דִּבְרֵי הַיָּמִים, Divrei ha-Yamim, "The Words of the Days") is a book of the Hebrew Bible, found as two books (1 and 2 Chronicles) in most Christian Bibles. It largely retells the history of Israel already narrated in Samuel and Kings, but from a distinct theological perspective centred on the Jerusalem Temple, its worship, and the Davidic monarchy.

Content

The book opens with nine chapters of genealogies running from Adam through the tribes of Israel to the returned exiles. The narrative proper begins with the death of Saul and the reign of David (1 Chronicles 10–29), emphasising David's preparations for the Temple: gathering materials, organising the priests and Levites, and commissioning Solomon. Second Chronicles covers Solomon's building and dedication of the Temple, then the history of the kingdom of Judah alone — the northern kingdom of Israel is largely ignored — down to the destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC and Cyrus the Great's decree permitting the exiles to return (538 BC), with which the book ends.

Composition and themes

Scholars generally date Chronicles to the late Persian or early Hellenistic period (roughly 400–250 BC) and attribute it to an author or circle sometimes called "the Chronicler", possibly connected with the Levites. Its distinctive emphases include "all Israel" as a single worshipping community, the immediate retribution of God on faithless kings, the central role of Temple music and Levitical ministry, and an idealised portrait of David and Solomon. Where Samuel–Kings explains the exile as punishment for centuries of sin, Chronicles stresses that each generation is judged on its own deeds, offering hope to the restored community.

Sources

The Chronicler used Samuel and Kings in something close to their canonical form, together with genealogical lists, psalms, and records no longer extant, and freely adapted them — omitting, for instance, David's adultery with Bathsheba and Absalom's revolt.
