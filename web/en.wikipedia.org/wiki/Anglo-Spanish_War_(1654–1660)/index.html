Anglo-Spanish War (1654–1660)

The Anglo-Spanish War was a military conflict between the Commonwealth of England and the Spanish Empire fought between 1654 and 1660. It arose from Oliver Cromwell's "Western Design" against Spanish colonies in the Caribbean and from commercial rivalry, and it merged into the wider Franco-Spanish War after England allied with France in 1657.

Western Design

In 1655 an English expedition under Admiral William Penn and General Robert Venables attacked Santo Domingo on Hispaniola but was repulsed; it instead seized the weakly defended island of Jamaica, which remained an English (later British) colony for over three centuries. Spain never formally recognised the loss until the Treaty of Madrid in 1670.

War at sea

English fleets under Robert Blake blockaded Cádiz and, in 1656, Captain Richard Stayner captured part of the Spanish treasure fleet off Cádiz. In April 1657 Blake destroyed a Spanish fleet at the Battle of Santa Cruz de Tenerife, one of the most celebrated naval actions of the age. In 1658 another treasure fleet was intercepted off Cádiz.

Alliance with France and Flanders

By the Treaty of Paris (1657) England joined France against Spain in Flanders. English soldiers fought alongside the French at the Battle of the Dunes in June 1658, and Dunkirk was taken and handed to England. The war ended in the wider settlement following the Treaty of the Pyrenees (1659) between France and Spain; hostilities between England and Spain formally ceased after the Restoration of Charles II in 1660.
