Utopia

A utopia is an imagined community or society possessing highly desirable or near-perfect qualities. The term was coined by Sir Thomas More for his 1516 book Utopia, describing a fictional island in the Atlantic with communal property, religious tolerance, and a six-hour working day; it puns on Greek ou-topos 'no place' and eu-topos 'good place'.

Utopia
Coined by: Thomas More, 1516
Opposite: Dystopia
Related: Eutopia, utopian socialism, intentional communities

History of the concept

Precursors include Plato's Republic and biblical Eden. More's work launched a genre including Campanella's City of the Sun and Bacon's New Atlantis. In the 19th century, utopian socialists such as Robert Owen and Charles Fourier founded experimental communities; Bellamy's Looking Backward (1888) was a bestseller.

Types and criticism

Utopias may be economic, ecological, feminist, technological, or religious. Critics argue perfect societies risk stagnation or coercion — a theme inverted in 20th-century dystopias such as Brave New World and Nineteen Eighty-Four. Karl Popper attacked 'utopian engineering' in favour of piecemeal reform.
