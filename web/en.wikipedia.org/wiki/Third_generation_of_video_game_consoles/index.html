In the history of video games, the third generation of game consoles, commonly referred to as the 8-bit era, began on July 15, 1983, with the Japanese release of two systems: Nintendo's Family Computer (commonly abbreviated to Famicom) and Sega's SG-1000. This generation marked the end of the video game crash of 1983 and the shift in dominance of home video game manufacturers from the United States to Japan.

Overview

The best-selling console of the generation was Nintendo's Famicom, released in North America as the Nintendo Entertainment System (NES). Nintendo's strict licensing programme — including the "Seal of Quality" and limits on the number of titles licensees could release per year — was credited with restoring retailer and consumer confidence after the crash. Sega's competing system, the SG-1000's successor the Master System, achieved greater success in Europe and Brazil than in North America or Japan.

Technology

Third-generation consoles used 8-bit processors, hardware scrolling backgrounds, and larger colour palettes than their predecessors. Cartridges increasingly included battery-backed save memory and memory-mapper chips that expanded the capabilities of the base hardware.

Legacy

The generation established many enduring franchises, including Super Mario Bros., The Legend of Zelda, Metroid, Final Fantasy and Dragon Quest, and its iconography remains central to retrogaming culture.
