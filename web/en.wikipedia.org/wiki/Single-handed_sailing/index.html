Single-handed sailing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A solo sailor aboard an offshore racing yacht.

Single-handed sailing is sailing with only one crewmember aboard a vessel. The term typically refers to ocean and long-distance sailing, in racing or cruising, and is distinguished by the extreme demands it places on the sailor's endurance, self-reliance, and sleep management.

History

Early solo voyages include Joshua Slocum's first single-handed circumnavigation aboard Spray (1895–1898), recounted in Sailing Alone Around the World. The modern era of solo racing began with the first Observer Single-handed Trans-Atlantic Race (OSTAR) in 1960, won by Francis Chichester, who later made a celebrated solo circumnavigation in 1966–67. The Sunday Times Golden Globe Race of 1968–69 brought solo non-stop circumnavigation to wide attention; Robin Knox-Johnston was the sole finisher.

Solo races

Major single-handed races include the Vendée Globe (non-stop, unassisted, around the world, held every four years from Les Sables-d'Olonne), the Route du Rhum, the Transat Jacques Vabre classes, the Mini Transat, the Solitaire du Figaro, and The Transat. Boats such as the IMOCA 60 and Class40 are designed specifically for shorthanded offshore sailing.

Techniques

Solo sailors rely on self-steering systems (wind vanes and autopilots), structured polyphasic "cat-nap" sleep routines of 15–30 minutes at a time, radar and AIS alarms, and extensive redundancy of equipment. International collision regulations require a proper lookout at all times, a rule in practical tension with sleeping while single-handed.

Notable single-handers

  • Joshua Slocum; Francis Chichester; Robin Knox-Johnston
  • Ellen MacArthur; Michel Desjoyeaux; Bernard Moitessier
  • Jessica Watson; Laura Dekker (youngest solo circumnavigators, unassisted records disputed)
