Sixth Crusade

The Sixth Crusade (1228–1229), also known as the Crusade of Frederick II, was a military expedition to recover Jerusalem and other Holy Land territories lost to the Muslims after the Fifth Crusade. Uniquely among the major crusades, it achieved its main objective through diplomacy rather than battle, and it was led by a Holy Roman Emperor, Frederick II, who was under excommunication at the time.

Background

Frederick had vowed to go on crusade repeatedly since 1215 but repeatedly delayed, infuriating Pope Gregory IX, who excommunicated him in 1227 when illness again forced his fleet to turn back. Frederick finally sailed from Brindisi in June 1228, still excommunicate. Meanwhile the Ayyubid sultan of Egypt, al-Kamil, faced a threat from his brother's rivals and the approaching Khwarezmians, making him receptive to negotiation — he had previously offered Jerusalem to the Fifth Crusade in exchange for peace.

The Treaty of Jaffa

After months of manoeuvring in Cyprus and at Acre, Frederick and al-Kamil concluded the Treaty of Jaffa on 18 February 1229. The treaty returned Jerusalem, Bethlehem, Nazareth, and a corridor to the coast to Christian control for ten years, while the Temple Mount (Haram al-Sharif), with the Dome of the Rock and al-Aqsa Mosque, remained in Muslim hands with freedom of worship for Muslims. On 18 March 1229 Frederick entered Jerusalem and crowned himself in the Church of the Holy Sepulchre — no priest would crown an excommunicate — and he departed for Italy within days to confront papal forces invading his Sicilian kingdom.

Aftermath

The settlement was condemned by many on both sides — by the papacy and the Latin patriarch as a deal made with an excommunicate, and by Muslim chroniclers as a shameful surrender of Jerusalem — yet it restored a Christian Kingdom of Jerusalem that endured until the Khwarezmians sacked the city in 1244. The crusade remains a famous case of successful crusading diplomacy.
