Capsizing

A sailing dinghy capsized in a gust; its crew will right it by standing on the centreboard. (Image placeholder)

Capsizing or keeling over occurs when a boat or ship is rolled onto its side or further by the action of waves, wind or turning, or is overturned entirely. The act of recovering a vessel from a capsize is called righting. A capsized vessel that remains afloat upside down is said to have turned turtle; if it later sinks, it may be said to have foundered.

Causes

Capsize is fundamentally a loss of transverse stability: when the heeling forces acting on a vessel exceed its righting moment, the vessel rolls beyond its angle of vanishing stability and cannot return upright. Common contributing factors include:

  • Excessive wind pressure on sails, especially in sudden gusts
  • Large or breaking waves, particularly beam seas and broaching in following seas
  • Shifting or excessive cargo, and the free surface effect of liquids in partly filled tanks
  • Overloading or improper weight distribution raising the centre of gravity
  • Grounding, collision or flooding that destroys reserve buoyancy
  • High-speed turns in small craft

Small craft and large vessels

Small sailing dinghies are designed to capsize and be righted as a normal part of sailing; crews practise capsize recovery, using their weight on the daggerboard or centreboard to bring the boat upright. Self-righting lifeboats and some yachts are designed to return to upright even from a full 360-degree roll.

For large ships, capsize is usually catastrophic. Notable examples include the car ferry Herald of Free Enterprise (1987), which capsized shortly after leaving Zeebrugge with her bow doors open; the ferry Estonia (1994); the cruise ship Costa Concordia (2012), which grounded and partially capsized off Giglio Island; and the Swedish warship Vasa (1628), which capsized and sank in Stockholm harbour on her maiden voyage owing to insufficient stability.

Terminology

On small sailing boats, a capsize to windward is sometimes called a "death roll" when it occurs downwind. In kayaking, an intentional capsize and recovery is the Eskimo roll. A vessel that rolls over but rights itself without outside assistance is said to be self-righting.
