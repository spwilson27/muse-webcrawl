Tied island

A tombolo ties the island to the shore behind it.

A tied island is an island that is connected to the mainland (or to another island) by a tombolo: a narrow natural bar of sand, gravel or shingle deposited by wave action in the sheltered water behind the island. At low tide some tied islands can be reached on foot across the exposed bar; others are permanently joined and only their shape betrays their insular origin. The tombolo forms because waves refract and diffract around the island, losing energy in its lee, where currents deposit the sediment they carry until a spit grows out to the island.

Notable examples

  • St Michael's Mount, Cornwall, England — a tidal tied island joined to Marazion by a causeway exposed at low tide.
  • Isle of Portland, Dorset, England — tied to the mainland by Chesil Beach, an 18-mile shingle tombolo (Portland is thus sometimes described as a tied island rather than a true island).
  • St Ninian's Isle, Shetland — joined by the largest active shell-sand tombolo in Britain, which shelters a famous Viking hoard site.
  • Cheung Chau, Hong Kong — a "double tied island", two rocky islets joined by tombolos at both ends, forming the island's dumbbell shape.
  • Monte Argentario, Italy — a former island tied to Tuscany by two tombolos enclosing the Orbetello lagoon.
  • Adam's Bridge (Rama Setu) — a chain of shoals between India and Sri Lanka, sometimes discussed as a tombolo-like feature of exceptional scale.

Formation

As waves bend around an offshore island, the opposing wave trains behind it converge, slowing the water and causing sand and shingle to settle in the calm "shadow" zone. Longshore drift feeds the growing spit from both sides until the bar emerges above water and matures into a tombolo. Storms can breach or shift the bar, and sea-level rise threatens many tied islands with permanent severance or submergence of the connecting strip. Artificial causeways, by contrast, produce "tidal islands" joined by human construction and are not classed as tied islands.
