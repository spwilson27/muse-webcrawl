Île-de-France

Île-de-France (literally "Isle of France") is the most populous of the eighteen regions of France, centred on the capital, Paris, whose metropolitan area the region almost exactly coincides with. It is France's political and economic heartland, home to about 12 million people — roughly 19% of the national population — on just 2% of its territory.

Geography

The region occupies the sedimentary Paris Basin, drained by the Seine and its tributaries the Marne and Oise. Paris's inner ring of departments (the petite couronne) is densely urbanised; the outer grande couronne mixes suburbs, farmland and royal forests such as Fontainebleau and Rambouillet. The name derives from the early medieval royal domain — an "island" of Capetian land between the Seine, Marne and Oise.

History

Île-de-France was the cradle of the French monarchy: Hugh Capet's royal domain from 987, embellished with Gothic cathedrals (Saint-Denis, Notre-Dame, Chartres nearby) and royal residences at the Louvre, Versailles, Saint-Germain and Fontainebleau. The modern region was created in the 20th century (as the "région parisienne", renamed Île-de-France in 1977).

Economy and transport

The region is one of Europe's richest, hosting corporate headquarters at La Défense, global finance, fashion, research (the Saclay plateau) and tourism. It is served by two international airports (Charles de Gaulle and Orly), the Metro, RER and Transilien networks, and the expanding Grand Paris Express lines. Its regional council, based in Saint-Ouen, administers transport (via Île-de-France Mobilités), lycées and development.
