A cello concerto (or violoncello concerto) is a concerto for solo cello and instrumental ensemble, conventionally an orchestra. The genre developed as the cello emerged from its continuo role in the late Baroque, and many of the works at its centre — by Haydn, Dvořák, Elgar and Shostakovich among others — are among the most frequently performed in the concert repertoire.

History

Antonio Vivaldi wrote some 27 cello concertos, the largest Baroque corpus, and C. P. E. Bach and Luigi Boccherini (himself a virtuoso cellist) composed influential early examples. Joseph Haydn's concertos in C major (c. 1761–65) and D major (1783) remain standards; the C major was lost until 1961. In the 19th century Robert Schumann's concerto (1850), Camille Saint-Saëns's First Concerto (1872) and Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky's Variations on a Rococo Theme (1876) expanded the genre. Antonín Dvořák's Concerto in B minor, Op. 104 (1895), composed in New York, is often regarded as the greatest cello concerto and is the most recorded.

Twentieth century and beyond

Edward Elgar's Concerto in E minor, Op. 85 (1919), elegiac in character, became globally famous through Jacqueline du Pré's recordings. Dmitri Shostakovich wrote two concertos (1959, 1966) for Mstislav Rostropovich, who also inspired works by Prokofiev, Britten, Lutosławski and Dutilleux. More recent concertos include those by Helen Grime, Esa-Pekka Salonen and Unsuk Chin. Composers have sometimes had to balance the cello's middle register against the full orchestra, favouring lighter scoring in solo passages.
