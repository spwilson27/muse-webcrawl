Vedanta is one of the six orthodox (āstika) schools of Indian philosophy, based on the philosophical teachings of the Upanishads — the final portion of the Vedas, hence Vedānta, "the end (or culmination) of the Veda". Vedantic schools interpret the threefold canon (prasthānatrayī) — the Upaniṣads, the Bhagavad Gita and the Brahma Sūtra — and differ chiefly over the relation between the self (ātman), the world, and ultimate reality (Brahman).

Schools

Advaita (non-dualism), systematised by Shankara (8th century), holds that ātman and Brahman are identical and plurality is appearance. Viśiṣṭādvaita (qualified non-dualism), taught by Rāmānuja (11th–12th century), holds that selves and world are real modes or "body" of God, identified with Vishnu. Dvaita (dualism), taught by Madhva (13th century), insists on an eternal distinction between God and souls. Other schools include Nimbarka's Dvaitādvaita, Vallabha's Śuddhādvaita, and Caitanya's Acintya Bheda Abheda.

Themes and influence

Common concerns include the nature of Brahman (with or without attributes), the status of the world and illusion (māyā), karma and rebirth, and liberation (Moksha) through knowledge, devotion (bhakti) or both. In the modern period, figures such as Swami Vivekananda presented Vedanta internationally, and "Neo-Vedanta" shaped modern Hindu identity; Vedantic ideas also entered Theosophy and comparative philosophy of religion.
