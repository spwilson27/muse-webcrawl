Defence Regulation 18B

Defence Regulation 18B was a regulation issued by the British Government during the Second World War under the Emergency Powers (Defence) Act 1939. It allowed the Home Secretary to detain without trial persons believed to be of hostile origin or associations, or who had recently been concerned in acts prejudicial to the public safety or the defence of the realm. Around 1,800 people were detained under the regulation during the war.

18B was among the most controversial of the wartime emergency measures

Background

The Defence Regulations were made at the outbreak of war in September 1939. Regulation 18B was initially narrow in scope, but in May 1940, amid fears of a German invasion and a supposed "fifth column", it was amended so that members of organisations deemed subject to foreign influence — in practice the British Union of Fascists — could be detained on the basis of their membership and the Home Secretary's reasonable suspicion.

Detentions

The most prominent detainee was Sir Oswald Mosley, leader of the British Union of Fascists, who was arrested on 23 May 1940 and held first in Brixton Prison and later in Holloway, until his release on health grounds in November 1943. Other detainees included the MP Captain Archibald Maule Ramsay, founder of the Right Club, the fascist activist Diana Mosley, and a number of British citizens of German, Austrian and Italian origin. Many were held on the Isle of Man in requisitioned boarding houses.

Detainees could make representations to an advisory committee chaired by Norman Birkett KC, but the committee's recommendations were not binding and the evidence of MI5 was not disclosed to the detainee. The leading legal challenge, Liversidge v Anderson (1942), upheld the Home Secretary's wide discretion, with Lord Atkin's celebrated dissenting speech protesting against the majority's interpretation.

Release and legacy

As the threat of invasion receded, detainees were progressively released, and the last were freed in the spring of 1945. The regulation lapsed with the expiry of the emergency legislation. 18B remains a frequently cited example in debates about internment, executive power and civil liberties in wartime, and Liversidge v Anderson is still studied as a leading case on statutory interpretation and judicial deference.
