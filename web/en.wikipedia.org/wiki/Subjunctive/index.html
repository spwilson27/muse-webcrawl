Subjunctive

The subjunctive is a grammatical mood — a feature of an utterance that indicates the speaker's attitude toward it. Subjunctive forms of verbs are typically used to express various states of unreality such as wishes, emotions, possibilities, judgments, opinions, obligations, or actions that have not yet occurred; the exact situations in which they are used vary from language to language. The subjunctive is one of the irrealis moods, as opposed to the realis indicative mood, which is used for statements of fact.

In English

Modern English has only a vestigial subjunctive. Its principal uses include:

  • The mandative subjunctive in subordinate clauses after verbs and expressions of demand, recommendation, or necessity: "I insist that he be present."
  • The irrealis "were" in counterfactual conditionals: "If I were you, I would go."
  • Fossilized expressions such as "God save the Queen," "so be it," and "suffice it to say."

The mandative subjunctive remains common in American English, while British English often substitutes a should-construction ("I insist that he should be present").

In other languages

The subjunctive is a living and highly developed part of grammar in Romance languages such as Spanish, French, Italian, and Portuguese, where it is required in many subordinate clauses involving doubt, desire, emotion, or non-assertion, and is distinguished by rich sets of inflected forms. Latin had a fully productive subjunctive used in purpose clauses, result clauses, indirect commands, and subordinate clauses in indirect speech. German retains two subjunctive forms (Konjunktiv I and II), used for indirect speech and hypothetical statements respectively.
