Brigandine

A brigandine is a form of body armour from the Middle Ages. It consists of many small steel plates riveted to the inside of a fabric or leather garment, typically canvas or leather covered with a decorative outer layer such as velvet or silk. The rivet heads, arranged in groups, were usually visible on the outside, giving brigandines a distinctive studded appearance.

History

Brigandines appeared in Europe in the 13th century and were widely used from the 14th to the 16th century by soldiers of many ranks, from common infantry to men-at-arms who wore them either as their main torso defence or over mail. They offered a good compromise between protection, flexibility, cost and ease of manufacture compared with solid plate armour, and they could be produced by textile workers rather than specialist armourers.

The name is often linked to brigands (irregular foot soldiers), though the term "brigand" itself may derive from this type of armour.

Construction

The plates overlapped, usually downward, and were riveted to the foundation garment; the gaps between plates allowed the torso to flex. Finer examples used tinned or gilded rivets and rich fabrics, sometimes bearing heraldic decoration. Similar garments existed in Asia, such as Chinese dingjia ("nail armour") and the Korean dujeonggap.

Related garments

The brigandine is related to the coat of plates, an earlier garment using larger plates, and to the jack of plates, in which plates were recycled into a fabric garment worn by poorer soldiers. In modern usage, the term is sometimes applied loosely to any armour of small plates attached to fabric.
