Dvaita Vedanta

Dvaita Vedanta (Sanskrit: द्वैत वेदान्त) is a dualistic school of Hindu philosophy founded by the 13th-century saint-philosopher Madhvacharya (1238–1317) of Udupi, Karnataka. In contrast to the monism of Advaita Vedanta, Dvaita teaches that God (Vishnu) and individual souls (jivas) are eternally and really distinct, and that the material world is also real.

Doctrine

The system rests on the doctrine of five real and eternal differences (pancha-bheda): between God and soul, God and matter, soul and soul, soul and matter, and one material thing and another. Vishnu alone is wholly independent (svatantra); all else is dependent (paratantra). Souls are infinitely graded (taratamya) and by nature fall into classes, some destined for liberation, others for perpetual existence in samsara or darkness. Liberation (moksha) is attained through devotion (bhakti) and divine grace, and the liberated soul enjoys bliss in Vaikuntha while remaining distinct from God.

Tradition and influence

Madhva's commentaries on the Brahma Sutras, the Upanishads and the Bhagavad Gita, and disciples such as Jayatirtha and Vyasatirtha, developed Dvaita into a rigorous theological system. The eight monasteries (Ashta Mathas) of Udupi maintain the worship of Krishna there to this day. Dvaita's devotional emphasis also influenced the Haridasa movement and, through it, Carnatic music.
