Visual agnosia

Visual agnosia is an impairment in the recognition of visually presented objects despite intact elementary visual function — adequate acuity, fields, colour and brightness perception — and in the absence of a general intellectual deficit. Patients can often describe an object's shape, size and colour, and may copy a drawing of it accurately, yet cannot say what it is until they touch it, hear it, or smell it. The condition results from damage to the ventral ("what") stream of visual processing in the occipitotemporal cortex.

Types

Following Lissauer's classical 1890 distinction, apperceptive visual agnosia involves a failure to form a coherent percept: patients cannot match or copy shapes well. Associative visual agnosia, by contrast, is a failure to link an intact percept with stored knowledge — a "perception stripped of meaning". Specific category deficits include:

  • Prosopagnosia — inability to recognise faces
  • Integrative agnosia — seeing parts but failing to assemble them into wholes
  • Pure alexia (agnosic alexia) — loss of reading ability with preserved writing
  • Cerebral achromatopsia — loss of colour perception from cortical damage
  • Akinetopsia — inability to perceive motion

Causes and famous cases

Causes include stroke (especially posterior cerebral artery infarction), carbon monoxide poisoning, head trauma, and neurodegenerative disease such as posterior cortical atrophy. The best-known case is Oliver Sacks's patient "Dr P.", a music teacher who mistook his wife's head for a hat — the title case of The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat (1985). Another well-studied patient, D.F., developed visual form agnosia after carbon monoxide poisoning yet retained accurate visually guided reaching and grasping, a dissociation central to the two-streams hypothesis of vision.
