Gertrude Belle Elion (23 January 1918 – 21 February 1999) was an American biochemist and pharmacologist who shared the 1988 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with George H. Hitchings and Sir James Black "for their discoveries of important principles for drug treatment". Working with Hitchings at Burroughs Wellcome, she helped develop a landmark series of drugs using a rational, biochemistry-based approach to drug design.

Early life and education

Elion graduated from Hunter College in 1937 and earned a master's degree in chemistry from New York University in 1941. Unable at first to obtain a research post because of her sex, she worked in teaching and industry before joining Hitchings's laboratory in 1944. She never completed a PhD, though she later received numerous honorary doctorates.

Drug discoveries

By studying differences in nucleic acid metabolism between normal and diseased cells, Elion and Hitchings developed drugs that selectively blocked the growth of cancer cells, bacteria and viruses. Her credits include mercaptopurine (the first effective treatment for childhood leukaemia), the immunosuppressant azathioprine (which made organ transplantation practical), allopurinol for gout, pyrimethamine for malaria, trimethoprim for bacterial infections, and acyclovir, the first effective antiviral drug, used against herpes infections.

Later life and honours

Elion became head of the Department of Experimental Therapy at Burroughs Wellcome in 1967 and later a research professor at Duke University. She was elected to the National Academy of Sciences and received the US National Medal of Science in 1991. She mentored many young scientists and remained active in research until her death.
