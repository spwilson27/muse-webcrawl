Welthauptstadt Germania

Welthauptstadt Germania (German for "World Capital Germania") was the projected renewal of the German capital, Berlin, during the Nazi period, part of Adolf Hitler's vision for the future of Germany after the planned victory in the Second World War. Albert Speer, "the first architect of the Third Reich," produced many of the plans and designs in his capacity as General Building Inspector for the Reich Capital.

Plans

The scheme centred on a grand north–south axis about five kilometres long, crossed by an east–west axis, with triumphal arches and monumental buildings. Its centrepiece was to be the Volkshalle ("People's Hall"), an enormous domed assembly hall over 200 metres high, inspired by the Pantheon, intended to hold 150,000–180,000 people. Nearby were planned a gigantic triumphal arch, roughly 117 metres high, inscribed with the names of Germany's war dead, and a new Führerpalast adjoining an enlarged Adolf-Hitler-Platz.

Construction and demolition

Preparatory demolitions began in the late 1930s, clearing districts and displacing tens of thousands of residents, many of them Jewish tenants whose homes were seized. Completed elements include the widened east–west axis (today's Straße des 17. Juni) with its Charlottenburg Gate lanterns and the Schwerbelastungskörper, a massive concrete test block built to study whether Berlin's marshy soil could bear the planned structures. Work halted with the war; the project was never realized.

Legacy

Germania exemplified Nazi monumental classicism and megalomania. Speer's models survived the war and are exhibited in Berlin; the plans feature in alternate-history works such as Robert Harris's novel Fatherland and the TV series The Man in the High Castle.
