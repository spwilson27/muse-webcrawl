Neutron moderator

Neutron moderator
Thermalization of a neutron by elastic scattering
Common materialsLight water, heavy water (D₂O), graphite, beryllium
FunctionSlows fast neutrons to thermal energies
Used inThermal nuclear reactors

A neutron moderator is a medium that reduces the speed of fast neutrons, ideally without capturing them, thereby transforming them into thermal neutrons capable of sustaining a nuclear chain reaction involving fissile nuclei such as uranium-235. Neutrons born in fission have energies around 2 MeV, while the fission cross-section of ²³⁵U is vastly larger at thermal energies (~0.025 eV); moderation therefore enormously increases the probability that each neutron induces another fission.

Principles

Slowing down occurs through elastic collisions. The maximum energy transfer in a single collision is greatest when the struck nucleus has a mass close to the neutron's, so light elements are the most efficient moderators per collision: a neutron loses on average half its energy striking hydrogen (a proton), requiring about 18 collisions to thermalize, versus roughly 114 in carbon. A good moderator also has a low neutron-absorption cross-section; this trade-off is captured by the moderating ratio. Ordinary (light) water is an excellent slowing-down medium but absorbs enough neutrons to require enriched uranium fuel; heavy water absorbs very few neutrons and permits natural-uranium fuel, as in CANDU reactors; graphite, used in early piles, Magnox, RBMK and some gas-cooled reactors, is a compromise.

Temperature and safety

Because the moderator in most power reactors also serves as coolant or is intimately linked to it, loss of moderator usually reduces reactivity — a negative moderator temperature coefficient that is an important safety property. In the RBMK design that failed at Chernobyl, graphite moderation combined with boiling-water channels produced a dangerous positive void coefficient.
