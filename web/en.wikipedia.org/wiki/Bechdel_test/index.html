Bechdel test

The Bechdel test (also known as the Bechdel–Wallace test) is a measure of the representation of women in fiction. A work passes the test if it features at least two women who talk to each other about something other than a man. Many formulations add that the women must be named characters. The test draws attention to the presence — or absence — of active female characters in film, television, literature, and other media.

Origin

The test originates in the comic strip Dykes to Watch Out For by Alison Bechdel. In the 1985 strip "The Rule", a character says she refuses to watch a film unless it satisfies the three requirements: two women, who talk to each other, about something besides a man. Bechdel credited the idea to her friend Liz Wallace, and the underlying notion is often compared to Virginia Woolf's observations in A Room of One's Own (1929) about how rarely women in fiction are shown in relation to each other rather than to men.

Application and criticism

Studies applying the test to large film samples have found that roughly half of films pass, and that passing films tend not to be disadvantaged at the box office. Some Swedish cinemas and television channels have used the test in ratings and programming decisions since 2013. Commentators stress that the test is a crude indicator rather than a measure of quality or feminism: a work can pass with a brief, trivial exchange, while some works centred on women fail on technicalities. Numerous related tests have since been proposed addressing race, LGBTQ representation, and other dimensions of diversity, as well as variants examining the depth of female characters' roles.
