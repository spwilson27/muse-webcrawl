Brutalist architecture is an architectural style that emerged in the 1950s and flourished internationally through the 1960s and 1970s, descended from the modernist architecture of the early 20th century. Brutalist buildings are characterized by minimalist constructions that showcase the bare building materials and structural elements over decorative design, typically massive, monolithic, and "blocky" forms of exposed concrete, often with small, repetitive windows and a rough, unfinished surface texture.

Terminology and origins

The term derives from the French béton brut ("raw concrete"), used by Le Corbusier for the material of his Unité d'Habitation in Marseille (1952) and the buildings of Chandigarh. The British critic Reyner Banham adapted it as "Brutalism" (playing on "New Brutalism," the label of Alison and Peter Smithson's unbuilt work), and the name's accidental echo of "brutal" has coloured public reception ever since.

Characteristics

Brutalism emphasized honesty of materials and structure: formwork-imprinted concrete left exposed, services and circulation expressed externally, and plans organized as legible diagrams. The style was favoured for civic, educational, and social-housing commissions — universities, libraries, council offices, theatres, and housing estates — because it promised monumentality at modest cost and embodied postwar ideals of social progress. Notable examples include Boston City Hall, the Barbican Estate and Trellick Tower in London, the Western City Gate (Genex Tower) in Belgrade, Habitat 67 in Montreal, and the Geisel Library in San Diego.

Criticism and decline

From the late 1970s brutalism fell from favour. Critics associated the style with urban decay, totalitarian aesthetics, and the failures of some modernist housing projects; concrete weathered poorly in wet climates; and the rise of postmodernism and high-tech architecture made its austerity seem dated. Many brutalist buildings were demolished, including London's Robin Hood Gardens.

Reappraisal

Since the 2000s, preservation campaigns and popular interest — including social media communities devoted to concrete architecture — have driven a reappraisal. Several landmarks have been listed or renovated (Park Hill in Sheffield, the Barbican), and historians now emphasize the movement's social ambitions and sculptural variety, though demolition of major works continues worldwide.
