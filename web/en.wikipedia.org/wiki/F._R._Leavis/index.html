Frank Raymond Leavis (14 July 1895 – 14 April 1978) was an English literary critic and academic. One of the most influential figures in twentieth-century English literary studies, he was the driving force behind the journal Scrutiny (1932–1953) and a long-serving teacher at Downing College, Cambridge. Leavis insisted that literary criticism was a serious moral discipline concerned with "life", and his severe judgements reshaped the canon of English literature in the universities.

Life and career

Leavis was born in Cambridge, where his father ran a shop selling pianos and musical instruments. He was educated at the Perse School and Emmanuel College, Cambridge, where he read history and then English. His studies were interrupted by the First World War, in which he served as a stretcher-bearer with the Friends' Ambulance Unit on the Western Front; the experience left him with lasting ill health and insomnia. He completed his doctorate in 1924 and became a lecturer at Emmanuel before moving to Downing College, where he taught until his retirement in 1962.

With his wife Queenie Dorothy Leavis (née Roth), author of Fiction and the Reading Public (1932), he founded Scrutiny, which for two decades set the terms of serious literary debate in Britain. Leavis was famously combative: his exchanges with C. P. Snow over the "two cultures" (the Richmond Lecture controversy of 1962) and with the BBC and the literary establishment became legends of academic polemic.

Critical work

Leavis's major works include New Bearings in English Poetry (1932), which championed Gerard Manley Hopkins, T. S. Eliot, and Ezra Pound against the Georgians; Revaluation (1936); and The Great Tradition (1948), whose famous opening line declared that "the great English novelists are Jane Austen, George Eliot, Henry James and Joseph Conrad". D. H. Lawrence: Novelist (1955) performed a major act of critical rescue on Lawrence's reputation. Late works such as Nor Shall My Sword (1972) turned to William Blake and to a critique of industrial "technologico-Benthamite" civilisation.

Influence and legacy

"Leavisism" dominated English studies in British and Commonwealth universities for a generation, training teachers in the method of close, evaluative reading. Although later movements — structuralism, Marxist criticism, and theory — defined themselves partly against him, Leavis's central claims about the moral seriousness of literature and the critic's duty of judgement remain part of the discipline's inheritance.
