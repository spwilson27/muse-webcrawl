Social cost of carbon

The social cost of carbon (SCC) is an estimate, expressed in monetary terms, of the economic damage caused by emitting one additional tonne of carbon dioxide (or its equivalent in other greenhouse gases) into the atmosphere. It is used by governments in cost–benefit analyses of climate policies and regulations.

Calculation

The SCC is computed with integrated assessment models (such as DICE, PAGE and FUND) that link emissions to temperature change, physical impacts (sea-level rise, agriculture, health, extreme weather) and monetary damages over centuries. Estimates are extremely sensitive to the discount rate chosen, to assumptions about future damages and adaptation, and to whether catastrophic and non-market impacts are included.

Policy use

Under President Barack Obama, a U.S. government Interagency Working Group set a central SCC value of roughly $50 per tonne (in later dollars) for use in federal rulemaking. The Trump administration sharply lowered the official figure by restricting counted damages to the domestic United States and using higher discount rates; the Biden administration restored the earlier approach. Academic estimates vary widely, from tens of dollars to several hundred dollars per tonne of CO2.

The SCC provides an economic rationale for a carbon price: in theory, an optimal carbon tax or cap-and-trade price equals the social cost of carbon.
