Regicide

Regicide
DefinitionThe killing of a monarch
Famous instancesCharles I (1649), Louis XVI (1793), Nicholas II (1918)
RelatedTyrannicide, assassination, coup d'état

Regicide is the killing of a king or queen — the word derives from the Latin rex (king) and the suffix -cide (killing). The term can denote both the act and, historically, the person who commits it: the "regicides" of English history are the 59 commissioners who signed the death warrant of Charles I in 1649. Because monarchs were widely held to rule by divine right, regicide was long treated not merely as murder but as sacrilege and the gravest form of treason.

Historical instances

Judicially sanctioned regicides stand out for their constitutional significance. Charles I of England was tried by a High Court of Justice and beheaded on 30 January 1649 — the first time a reigning European monarch was publicly executed after a trial by his own subjects. After the Restoration of 1660, surviving regicides were hunted down: ten were hanged, drawn, and quartered, and the corpses of Cromwell, Ireton, and Bradshaw were exhumed and posthumously executed. In revolutionary France, Louis XVI was guillotined on 21 January 1793 after trial by the National Convention, and Marie Antoinette followed in October. In Russia, Nicholas II and his family were shot by the Bolsheviks in July 1918 without trial.

Ideas about regicide

Debates over the legitimacy of killing rulers run from John Milton's defence of the English regicide in The Tenure of Kings and Magistrates through monarchomach treatises of the French Wars of Religion to modern discussions of tyrannicide. In English law the memory of 1649 shaped the Treason Act 1661 and the careful constitutional settlement of 1689, which replaced a king by act of Parliament rather than by execution.
