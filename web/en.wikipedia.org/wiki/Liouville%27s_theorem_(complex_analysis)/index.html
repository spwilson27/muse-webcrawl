Liouville's theorem (complex analysis)

In complex analysis, Liouville's theorem, named after Joseph Liouville, states that every bounded entire function must be constant: if f is holomorphic on the whole complex plane and there is a constant M with |f(z)| ≤ M for all z, then f is constant.

Proof

The standard proof uses Cauchy's integral formula for the derivative. For a circle of radius R around any point a, |f′(a)| ≤ M/R. Letting R → ∞ gives f′(a) = 0 for all a, so f is constant. The argument extends to show that an entire function whose growth is bounded by a polynomial of degree n is itself a polynomial of degree at most n.

Corollaries

Liouville's theorem gives a short proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra: if a non-constant polynomial had no root, its reciprocal would be a bounded entire function. It also implies that the image of a non-constant entire function is dense in the complex plane; the stronger Picard theorems sharpen this considerably.

History

The result was first proved by Cauchy in 1844 in a weaker form; Liouville presented it in lectures in 1847 and published related work on doubly periodic functions, from which the theorem took his name.
