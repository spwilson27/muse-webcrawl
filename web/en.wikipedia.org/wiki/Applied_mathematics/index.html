Applied mathematics

Applied mathematics is the application of mathematical methods by different fields such as physics, engineering, medicine, biology, finance, business, computer science, and industry. It combines mathematical science with specialised knowledge to model, analyse, and solve practical problems. Historically, applied mathematics consisted principally of applied analysis — especially differential equations — approximation theory, and applied probability; the rise of computers added numerical analysis, optimisation, and computational mathematics as central branches.

Computational fluid dynamics: differential equations and numerical methods applied to engineering design.

Areas

Core areas include differential equations and dynamical systems (modelling mechanics, weather, epidemics, and ecosystems), fluid and solid mechanics, numerical analysis and scientific computing, statistics and probability (including stochastic processes), optimisation and operations research, mathematical physics, control theory, information theory and cryptography, mathematical finance, and discrete mathematics applied to computer science. Applied mathematicians build mathematical models — simplified representations that capture the essential variables of a system — derive solutions analytically or numerically, and interpret the results back in the domain of application.

Relation to pure mathematics

The boundary between applied and "pure" mathematics is porous and historically shifting: Fourier analysis arose from heat conduction and became a pillar of pure analysis; number theory, long celebrated as useless, now underpins cryptography. Institutions such as the Society for Industrial and Applied Mathematics (SIAM, founded 1951) and university departments of applied mathematics reflect the field's professional identity, while many results migrate freely between the two camps. Mathematicians debate whether applied work is defined by its problems, its methods, or its audience; in practice it is marked by an orientation toward problems originating outside mathematics itself.

History

Ancient mathematics was largely applied — geometry for land measurement, arithmetic for commerce and astronomy. The scientific revolution fused mathematics with physics: Newton's calculus was invented alongside mechanics, and figures such as Euler, Lagrange, and Laplace advanced analysis through celestial and terrestrial mechanics. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries added thermodynamics, electromagnetism, and statistics; the Second World War spurred operations research, game theory, and computing, and the computer era made large-scale simulation routine across science and industry.
