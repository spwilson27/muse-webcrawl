Mixed language

A mixed language is a language that arises among a bilingual group combining aspects of two or more languages, through processes distinct from ordinary borrowing, code-switching or pidginisation. Mixed languages are stable, fully developed languages spoken as a mother tongue, but they cannot be classified as belonging to a single language family, because different major components — for example grammar versus vocabulary — derive systematically from different sources.

Formation

Linguists such as Peter Bakker and Yaron Matras argue that mixed languages form relatively abruptly (within a generation or two) in communities that are thoroughly bilingual and wish to signal a new, distinct ethnic identity — often the descendants of mixed marriages or métis groups. This distinguishes them from pidgins and creoles, which arise where groups lack a common language, and from heavy borrowing, which leaves the basic grammar–lexicon structure of one language intact.

Examples

  • Michif (Canada): French noun phrases combined with Cree verb phrases, spoken by the Métis people.
  • Media Lengua (Ecuador): Spanish vocabulary relexified onto Quechua grammar.
  • Mednyj Aleut (Russia): Aleut lexicon with Russian finite-verb morphology.
  • Ma'a (Mbugu) (Tanzania): Cushitic core vocabulary with Bantu grammar.
  • Gurindji Kriol and Light Warlpiri (Australia): recent mixtures of Aboriginal languages with Kriol/English.
