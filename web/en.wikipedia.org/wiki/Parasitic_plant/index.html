A parasitic plant is a plant that gains all or part of its nutrition from another living plant — its host — to which it attaches by a specialised organ called the haustorium, a modified root (or stem structure) that penetrates host tissues and taps the xylem, the phloem, or both. Parasitism has evolved independently at least a dozen times among the flowering plants, producing some 4,750 known species. Depending on their degree of dependence, parasitic plants are classified as hemiparasites, which retain chlorophyll and photosynthesise (e.g. mistletoes), or holoparasites, which have lost photosynthesis entirely and depend wholly on the host (e.g. dodder, broomrapes, Rafflesia).

Biology

Parasites may attach to host stems (mistletoes, dodder) or roots (Striga, Orobanche, sandalwood). Host location is sophisticated: seeds of witchweed germinate only in response to strigolactones exuded by host roots, and dodder seedlings grow toward volatile chemicals emitted by suitable hosts. Many holoparasites have drastically reduced bodies, genomes and plastids — Rafflesia is reduced to fungus-like threads inside its vine host, emerging only to produce the world's largest single flower. Parasitic plants also exchange mRNAs, proteins and even genes with their hosts; horizontal gene transfer is unusually common in the group.

Ecological and economic impact

Parasites are keystone species in many ecosystems — mistletoes feed birds and increase woodland diversity — but several are devastating crop pests. Striga (witchweed) infests cereal fields across sub-Saharan Africa, causing billions of dollars in losses; broomrapes attack sunflower, legume and solanaceous crops in Eurasia; dodder contaminates forage seed. Control combines resistant varieties, trap crops, herbicides and quarantine, and remains difficult because parasite seeds persist in soil for years.

Related forms

Parasitic plants are distinct from myco-heterotrophs (which parasitise fungi), carnivorous plants, and epiphytes, though the boundaries blur. Santalum album (sandalwood), a prized hemiparasitic tree, illustrates the spectrum: it photosynthesises yet robs neighbouring roots of water and nutrients throughout its life.
