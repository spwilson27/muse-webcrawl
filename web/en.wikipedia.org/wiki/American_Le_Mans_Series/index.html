The American Le Mans Series (ALMS) was a sports car racing series based in North America, sanctioned by IMSA and run under rules adapted from the 24 Hours of Le Mans by an agreement with the Automobile Club de l'Ouest (ACO). Founded by entrepreneur Don Panoz, it held its first season in 1999.

Format

The series featured multi-class endurance racing: Le Mans Prototypes (LMP1, LMP2, and later LMPC) competed on track simultaneously with grand touring classes (GT, and later GTC). Its calendar was anchored by the 12 Hours of Sebring, which it shared with European entrants, and the season-ending Petit Le Mans at Road Atlanta, a 1,000-mile/10-hour race created by Panoz in 1998. Winners earned automatic invitations to the 24 Hours of Le Mans.

History and merger

Audi dominated the prototype ranks for much of the 2000s, while manufacturers including Porsche, Acura, Chevrolet, BMW, and Ferrari contested the GT classes. The series also pioneered "green racing" initiatives with alternative fuels. In September 2012 the ALMS announced a merger with the rival Grand-Am Rolex Sports Car Series; the combined United SportsCar Championship (now the IMSA SportsCar Championship) debuted in 2014, ending the ALMS after fifteen seasons.
