Animal spirits (Keynes)

Coined byJohn Maynard Keynes
SourceThe General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936)
FieldMacroeconomics, behavioural economics

Animal spirits is a term used by John Maynard Keynes in his 1936 book The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money to describe the instincts and emotions that influence human behaviour, and which can be measured in terms of consumer confidence.

Keynes's usage

Keynes argued that most economic decisions with long-term consequences "can only be taken as a result of animal spirits — of a spontaneous urge to action rather than inaction, and not as the outcome of a weighted average of quantitative benefits multiplied by quantitative probabilities." Fluctuations in these spirits, he held, drive swings in investment and hence business cycles.

Earlier usage

The phrase derives from the Latin spiritus animalis of Galenic medicine and was used by philosophers including Descartes and Hume for the motivating forces of human action.

Modern usage

Economists George Akerlof and Robert Shiller revived the concept in their 2009 book Animal Spirits, arguing that confidence, fairness, corruption, money illusion, and stories are essential to explaining macroeconomic events.
