Head direction cell

Head direction (HD) cells are neurons found in a number of brain regions that fire when an animal's head is pointing in a particular direction within the local environment. Like the place cells of the hippocampus and the grid cells of the entorhinal cortex, head direction cells are thought to form part of a neural system for spatial navigation — an internal "compass" that helps the animal orient itself.

Properties

Each head direction cell has a preferred firing direction: it fires maximally when the head points in that direction and progressively less as the head turns away, becoming silent for directions roughly opposite to the preferred one. The population of HD cells collectively covers all azimuthal directions, and the direction of the population activity provides a continuous readout of heading. Importantly, HD cell firing depends on the direction of the head in the horizontal plane, not on the animal's location or on the direction of its movement.

Head direction cells were first described by James B. Ranck Jr. in the rat postsubiculum in 1984, and were characterized in detail by Jeffrey Taube and colleagues in the early 1990s.

Brain regions

HD cells have been recorded in the postsubiculum (dorsal presubiculum), the anterodorsal thalamic nucleus, the lateral mammillary nucleus, the dorsal tegmental nucleus, the lateral dorsal thalamic nucleus, the retrosplenial cortex and the entorhinal cortex, among other areas. The circuit is thought to integrate angular head velocity signals — derived from the vestibular system and from motor efference copy — to update the heading representation, while visual landmarks anchor the representation to the external environment.

Function

The head direction signal is considered a key component of the brain's navigation system. It provides input to place cells and grid cells, and its disruption impairs spatial learning. Because the HD signal persists in darkness and is updated by self-motion cues, it is a classic example of path integration.
