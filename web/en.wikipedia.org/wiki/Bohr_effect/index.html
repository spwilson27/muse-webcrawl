Bohr effect

The Bohr effect is a physiological phenomenon first described in 1904 by the Danish physiologist Christian Bohr. It states that hemoglobin's oxygen binding affinity is inversely related to acidity and to the concentration of carbon dioxide. That is, an increase in blood CO2 concentration or a decrease in blood pH causes hemoglobin to release oxygen (a rightward shift of the oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation curve), while the opposite conditions favor oxygen uptake.

The oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation curve shifts right with falling pH (the Bohr effect)

Mechanism

When CO2 enters the blood, it is converted (largely in red blood cells, via carbonic anhydrase) to carbonic acid, which dissociates into bicarbonate and hydrogen ions, lowering pH. Protons bind preferentially to deoxyhemoglobin (the T state), stabilizing it at residues such as the C-terminal histidines of the β chains and the N-terminal amino groups of the α chains, promoting salt-bridge formation and reducing oxygen affinity. CO2 also binds directly to hemoglobin's N-terminal groups to form carbaminohemoglobin, which likewise stabilizes the deoxy form. In the lungs, where CO2 is expelled and pH rises, the reverse occurs: affinity increases and oxygen loading is favored.

Physiological significance

The Bohr effect matches oxygen delivery to metabolic demand. Actively respiring tissues produce CO2 and acid, locally lowering pH; the effect causes hemoglobin to unload more oxygen precisely in these tissues. In the placenta, the double Bohr effect — combined movements in maternal and fetal blood — helps transfer oxygen from mother to fetus, complementing fetal hemoglobin's intrinsically higher affinity. The size of the Bohr effect varies among species and hemoglobin types.

Related phenomena

The Bohr effect is intimately related to the Haldane effect, its converse: oxygenation of hemoglobin promotes the release of CO2 and protons. In aquatic animals, an exaggerated form called the Root effect allows fish to secrete oxygen into the swim bladder and eye against large concentration gradients, since at low pH hemoglobin cannot remain fully saturated even at high oxygen tensions. Organic phosphates such as 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG) act alongside the Bohr effect by binding deoxyhemoglobin and, like protons, lowering oxygen affinity in tissues.

History

Christian Bohr, father of physicist Niels Bohr and footballer Harald Bohr, published the effect in 1904 ("Über einen in biologischer Beziehung wichtigen Einfluss, den die Kohlensäurespannung des Blutes auf dessen Sauerstoffbindung übt"), based on experiments with his collaborators Karl Hasselbalch and August Krogh. The mechanistic basis in the stereochemistry of hemoglobin was worked out decades later with Max Perutz's structural studies of hemoglobin.
