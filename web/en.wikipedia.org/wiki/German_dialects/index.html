German dialects

German dialects are the traditional local varieties of the German language spoken across Germany, Austria, Switzerland, and neighbouring regions. They form a dialect continuum in which neighbouring varieties are mutually intelligible, while distant ones may not be. Most German dialects belong to the West Germanic continuum and are conventionally grouped into Low German in the north and High German in the centre and south, divided by the effects of the High German consonant shift.

Classification

The traditional subdivision, based largely on isoglosses associated with the consonant shift (the Benrath line separating maken from machen, and the Uerdingen line separating ik from ich), distinguishes:

  • Low German (Plattdeutsch): Westphalian, Eastphalian, North Low Saxon, Mecklenburgish, Pomeranian and Low Prussian (largely extinct), which did not undergo the consonant shift.
  • Central German: Ripuarian (including Kölsch), Moselle Franconian, Hessian, Thuringian, Upper Saxon and Silesian (now mostly extinct in Poland), which underwent a partial shift.
  • Upper German: Alemannic (including Swiss German and Swabian), Bavarian (Austro-Bavarian) and East Franconian, which underwent the fullest shift.

Relationship to Standard German

Standard German (Hochdeutsch) developed from written chancery languages, chiefly of the East Central German region, and was spread by printing, schooling and broadcasting. In much of northern Germany the local dialects were displaced by a colloquial standard, whereas in the south, Austria and German-speaking Switzerland dialect remains vigorous in everyday speech and coexists with the standard in diglossia.

Status and decline

Since the mid-twentieth century, mobility, mass media and standard-language schooling have eroded traditional dialects, especially among younger speakers, producing regional colloquial varieties (Regiolekte) that blend standard grammar with local pronunciation and vocabulary. Low German is recognised as a regional language under the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages, and efforts to teach and document it continue in northern Germany. German dialects also survive overseas, for example Pennsylvania Dutch, Texas German, Hutterite German and the Hunsrik of southern Brazil.
