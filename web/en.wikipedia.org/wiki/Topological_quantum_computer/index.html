A topological quantum computer is a theoretical quantum computer that employs two-dimensional quasiparticles called anyons, whose world lines form braids in three-dimensional spacetime. The quantum state of the system depends only on the topology of these braids — not on their exact geometry — so quantum information is stored nonlocally and is inherently protected against small local perturbations and many sources of decoherence. The idea was introduced by Alexei Kitaev in 1997.

Principle of operation

Anyons are excitations of certain two-dimensional quantum systems, such as fractional quantum Hall states, that are neither bosons nor fermions: exchanging two of them multiplies the wavefunction by a phase (Abelian anyons) or rotates it within a degenerate subspace (non-Abelian anyons). Computation proceeds in three steps: creating anyon pairs from the vacuum, braiding their trajectories around one another to perform unitary gates, and fusing pairs and measuring the outcome. Because braiding outcomes depend on topology alone, small errors in the paths do not change the computation, giving the scheme a built-in level of fault tolerance far above that of ordinary qubits.

Candidate systems

The fractional quantum Hall state at filling factor ν = 5/2 is a leading candidate to host non-Abelian anyons. Another approach seeks Majorana zero modes in hybrid semiconductor–superconductor nanowires and related topological superconductors; Microsoft's quantum programme has long pursued this route toward topological qubits. Fibonacci anyons, if realised, would allow universal quantum computation by braiding alone. As of the mid-2020s, experimental demonstrations of the elementary ingredients — anyonic statistics, Majorana modes — remain active research topics, and no large-scale topological quantum computer has been built.
