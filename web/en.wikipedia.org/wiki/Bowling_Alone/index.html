Bowling Alone: The Collapse and Revival of American Community is a 2000 nonfiction book by political scientist Robert D. Putnam. It argues that the United States has undergone an unprecedented collapse in civic, social and political life — a decline in social capital — since the 1960s, with serious negative consequences for democracy and quality of life.

Argument

Putnam surveys hundreds of measures: membership of clubs and PTAs, church attendance, volunteering, voting, card games, dinner parties and league bowling all fell sharply in the late 20th century — even as total bowling increased, league bowling (its social form) declined, giving the book its title. He distinguishes "bonding" social capital (within groups) from "bridging" capital (across groups) and links high social capital to better education, health, safety and economic outcomes.

Causes

Putnam attributes the decline mainly to generational change (the passing of the civic-minded "long civic generation"), with contributions from television and electronic entertainment, suburbanisation and sprawl, and time and money pressures including two-career families.

Reception

The book became a landmark of public sociology, sparking debate about whether new forms of association (internet communities, informal networks) were being missed and whether the trend could reverse. A 2020 updated edition reflected on the following two decades. See also Putnam's later Our Kids (2015) and Tocqueville's classic account of American associations.
