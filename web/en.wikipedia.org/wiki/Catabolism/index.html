Catabolism

Catabolism (from Greek κάτω kata, "downward", and βάλλειν ballein, "to throw") is the set of metabolic pathways that breaks down large molecules into smaller units, releasing energy in the process. It is the destructive, energy-yielding half of metabolism, complementing anabolism, the constructive half that builds complex molecules. The energy released by catabolic reactions is typically captured in the high-energy phosphate bonds of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) or in reduced coenzymes such as NADH and FADH2.

Major pathways

Catabolism proceeds in stages. In digestion, large food molecules — proteins, polysaccharides, and lipids — are hydrolysed into amino acids, simple sugars, and fatty acids. These smaller molecules are then further oxidised inside cells: glycolysis breaks glucose down to pyruvate, fatty acids undergo beta-oxidation, and amino acids are deaminated. The resulting acetyl-CoA enters the citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle), whose electrons feed the electron transport chain, where oxidative phosphorylation produces the bulk of the cell's ATP. Enzymes catalyse each step, and hormones such as glucagon, adrenaline, and cortisol promote catabolic processes, while insulin opposes them.

Significance

Catabolism supplies the energy for all cellular work and provides the carbon skeletons and intermediates used in biosynthesis. During starvation or prolonged exercise, the body increases the catabolism of stored glycogen, fat, and eventually muscle protein. Excessive or dysregulated catabolism contributes to conditions such as cachexia in chronic disease and the muscle wasting seen in uncontrolled diabetes.
