Oseltamivir, sold under the brand name Tamiflu, is an antiviral medication used to treat and prevent influenza A and influenza B, the viruses that cause the flu. Many medical organizations recommend it in people who have complications or are at high risk of complications within 48 hours of first symptoms of infection. It is taken by mouth as a pill or liquid.

Oseltamivir is a prodrug whose active metabolite, oseltamivir carboxylate, selectively inhibits the influenza virus neuraminidase enzyme, which is necessary for the release of new viral particles from infected cells and for viral penetration of respiratory mucus. By blocking neuraminidase, the drug limits the spread of virus within the respiratory tract.

History and production

The drug was developed by Gilead Sciences from shikimic acid chemistry — early production depended on extraction of shikimic acid from star anise — and licensed to Roche, which brought it to market in 1999. Governments stockpiled oseltamivir on a large scale during the bird flu (H5N1) fears of 2005 and the 2009 swine flu pandemic. Total syntheses not requiring shikimic acid were later developed by several groups, including E. J. Corey's.

Evidence and controversy

Recommendations for oseltamivir are controversial. A long-running dispute between Cochrane Collaboration researchers and Roche over access to full clinical trial data culminated in the release of complete study reports in 2013; subsequent analyses concluded that oseltamivir shortens the duration of symptoms in adults by roughly 17 hours and reduces some complications such as pneumonia requiring antibiotics, but found little evidence that it reduces hospital admissions, and documented increased nausea and vomiting. The episode became a landmark case for clinical data transparency and influenced the policies of regulators and journals. Resistance to oseltamivir (notably the H275Y mutation in seasonal H1N1, which became widespread in 2008–09) is monitored globally.

Side effects

Common side effects include nausea, vomiting, headache and abdominal pain; post-marketing reports from Japan described rare neuropsychiatric events in adolescents, whose causal relationship to the drug remains debated.
