Mesoamerican writing systems

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The writing systems of Mesoamerica are the scripts developed independently in pre-Columbian Mesoamerica, one of the few regions of the world where writing arose without outside influence. By the time of the Spanish conquest in the 16th century, many Mesoamerican cultures had writing systems, of which the Maya script is the most completely deciphered and the only one generally agreed to be a full writing system capable of recording any spoken utterance.

Major systems

  • Olmec / Cascajal: The Cascajal block (c. 900 BCE), found in Veracruz, bears a sequence of signs that may be the earliest writing in the Americas, associated with the Olmec civilisation.
  • Zapotec: Inscriptions from Monte Albán, Oaxaca, from around 500 BCE, are among the earliest certain Mesoamerican texts, largely calendrical and onomastic.
  • Isthmian (Epi-Olmec): Used on stelae such as La Mojarra Stela 1 (2nd century CE) with long calendar dates; a logosyllabic script whose decipherment remains debated.
  • Maya: A logosyllabic system of hundreds of glyphs combining logograms and syllabic signs, used in carved inscriptions, painted ceramics and bark-paper codices; deciphered progressively from the 1950s through the work of Yuri Knorozov and others.
  • Aztec and Mixtec: Primarily pictorial-logographic systems used in codices and lienzo documents, recording names, places, tribute and dynastic history, with some phonetic elements.

Fate after the conquest

Spanish authorities suppressed indigenous books as idolatrous — most notoriously Bishop Diego de Landa's burning of Maya codices at Maní in 1562 — so only a handful of pre-conquest screenfolds survive (the Maya Dresden, Madrid and Paris codices, and Mixtec and Aztec manuscripts such as the Codex Zouche-Nuttall and Codex Mendoza, the latter colonial-era). Indigenous scribes continued producing documents in adapted forms throughout the colonial period.
