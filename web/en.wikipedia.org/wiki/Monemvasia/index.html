Monemvasia

Monemvasia is a town and municipality in Laconia, Peloponnese, Greece. The town is located on a small island off the east coast of the Peloponnese, connected to the mainland by a short causeway. The island is a steep rock about 300 metres high and a kilometre long, separated from the mainland by an earthquake in 375 AD. Its name derives from the Greek mone and emvasia, "single entrance", referring to the one causeway by which the fortress town is reached.

History

Monemvasia was founded in the 6th century AD by inhabitants of ancient Laconia fleeing Slavic invasions, and it grew into one of the great commercial ports of the Byzantine Empire, exporting the sweet wine known in western Europe as Malmsey. Captured by the Franks in 1248 after a long siege, it returned to Byzantine rule in 1262 and in its final Byzantine century was the seat of an imperial governor and an intellectual centre. It was one of the last Byzantine possessions to fall, passing to the Papacy and then Venice before surrendering to the Ottoman Empire in 1540. During the Greek War of Independence it was liberated in 1821 after a siege marked by severe suffering.

The town today

The medieval lower town, with its Byzantine churches — including the 13th-century church of Hagia Sophia on the plateau above — has been carefully restored, and many of its houses are now hotels and homes. Monemvasia is a popular tourist destination and the birthplace of the poet Yiannis Ritsos.
