Lattice truss bridge

A lattice truss bridge is a form of truss bridge that uses many closely spaced diagonal elements forming a lattice as the load-bearing superstructure. The design was patented in 1820 by architect Ithiel Town as Town's lattice truss, originally built of wooden planks pinned together, which made it cheap and easy to construct with unskilled labour.

Unlike other trusses, which use relatively few large members, the lattice distributes loads across a dense web of slender diagonals crossing at two angles. The design was widely used for covered bridges in the United States, including the Eagleville Covered Bridge and the Balmville Bridge.

Town lattice truss

Town's lattice uses two or more layers of planks, typically 2 to 3 inches thick, crossing at angles of 45 to 60 degrees, pinned at every intersection with hardwood treenails. Top and bottom chords of larger timbers complete the truss. Because it uses small, easily obtained timbers, it became one of the most common covered bridge designs of the 19th century.

Iron and steel lattice girders

In metal construction, a lattice girder uses diagonal flat bars or angle sections riveted or welded at intersections. The Brunel-designed Royal Albert Bridge and many railway viaducts employ lenticular or lattice girders. Modern examples are less common, having been superseded by other truss types and by box girders.
