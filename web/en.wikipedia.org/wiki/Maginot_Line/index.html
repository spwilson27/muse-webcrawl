The Maginot Line (French: Ligne Maginot), named after the French Minister of War André Maginot, is a line of concrete fortifications, obstacles, and weapon installations built by France in the 1930s to deter invasion by Nazi Germany and to force any German offensive into a slower, more containable advance. The line ran along France's eastern border, from Switzerland to Luxembourg, with extensions along the Italian border (the Alpine Line). Conceived in the aftermath of the First World War, it reflected France's determination to avoid another war of attrition on its own soil and to compensate for its demographic inferiority to Germany.

Construction

Construction began in 1929 under the direction of the Commission for the Organisation of Fortified Regions (CORF). The system comprised large underground fortresses called ouvrages — some housing more than a thousand men with their own power plants, barracks, kitchens, and narrow-gauge railways — interspersed with smaller casemates, observation posts, anti-tank obstacles, and minefields. Steel turrets, some retractable, mounted artillery and machine guns, and the strongest sections of the line faced Germany across Lorraine and Alsace.

The Second World War

In May 1940, Germany invaded France not through the strongest part of the line but through the Ardennes forest to the north, where the fortifications were thin because the terrain was considered impassable to armour, and through Belgium, which the line did not fully cover. The main ouvrages were largely bypassed; few were attacked directly, and those that were generally held out. When France signed an armistice in June 1940, most Maginot garrisons were still in place and surrendered only under orders from their own government.

Assessment

Historians debate the line's legacy. It was not breached where it stood, and it arguably channelled the German attack as its designers intended; but France's strategy assumed the main battle would be fought in Belgium, and the fortifications consumed resources and fostered a defensive mentality. "Maginot Line" has since become a byword for expensive preparations that fail because they are directed at the wrong threat.
