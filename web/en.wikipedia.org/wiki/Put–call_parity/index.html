Put–call parity

In financial mathematics, put–call parity defines a relationship between the price of a European call option and a European put option, both with the same strike price and expiry. The parity follows from the observation that a portfolio long a call and short a put has the same payoff at expiry as a forward contract on the underlying asset.

C − P = S − K / (1 + r)T

where C and P are the call and put prices, S is the spot price of the underlying, K is the strike, r the risk-free interest rate, and T the time to expiry. Equivalently, C + Ke−rT = P + S: a call plus cash equals a put plus the underlying (a protective put).

Arbitrage

If parity is violated, an arbitrageur can construct a risk-free profit by buying the underpriced side and selling the overpriced side, locking in the difference. Because such opportunities are quickly exploited, parity holds closely in liquid markets and is used by market makers to quote options and to construct synthetic positions — synthetic stock, synthetic calls, and synthetic puts.

History

The relationship was exploited in practice by arbitrageurs in the early twentieth century and was stated formally by Hans Stoll in a 1969 paper on the relationship between put and call option prices.
