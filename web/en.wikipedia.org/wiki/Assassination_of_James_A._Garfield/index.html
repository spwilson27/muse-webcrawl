The assassination of James A. Garfield, the 20th president of the United States, occurred on July 2, 1881, when Charles J. Guiteau shot him at the Baltimore and Potomac Railroad Station in Washington, D.C. Garfield lingered for 79 days before dying of infections on September 19, 1881, at Elberon, New Jersey. He was the second American president to be assassinated, following Abraham Lincoln in 1865.

Background

Garfield, a Republican, had taken office on March 4, 1881, after a narrow election victory. His administration was immediately consumed by the patronage struggles between the Stalwart faction of Senator Roscoe Conkling and the reform-minded Half-Breeds. Guiteau, a marginally employed lawyer and itinerant preacher who considered himself a Stalwart, had delivered a rambling pro-Grant speech in the 1880 campaign and convinced himself that he had earned an appointment as United States consul in Paris. Rebuffed repeatedly at the White House and State Department, and eventually barred from the White House waiting room, Guiteau concluded that God had commanded him to "remove" the president, and that Garfield's removal would unite the Republican Party under Vice President Chester A. Arthur, a Conkling ally.

Shooting

Guiteau purchased a .44 caliber British Bull Dog revolver — reportedly choosing one with ivory grips because he thought it would look better as a museum exhibit — and shadowed Garfield for weeks. On the morning of Saturday, July 2, 1881, Garfield arrived at the Sixth Street station to board a train for a commencement address at Williams College, his alma mater, accompanied by Secretary of State James G. Blaine. Guiteau fired twice from behind. One bullet grazed Garfield's arm; the second entered his back, lodging behind his pancreas. Guiteau was seized immediately, declaring, "I am a Stalwart of the Stalwarts! I did it and I want to be arrested! Arthur is president now!"

Medical treatment and death

Garfield's physician, Dr. Doctor Willard Bliss, took control of his care and repeatedly probed the wound with unsterilized fingers and instruments in an effort to locate the bullet, contrary to the antiseptic methods Joseph Lister had pioneered. Alexander Graham Bell devised an induction-balance metal detector to find the bullet, but Bliss restricted the search to the right side of the body, where he wrongly insisted the bullet lay; the device also may have been confused by the bed's steel springs. The bullet was in fact on the left side. Garfield wasted away, losing over 100 pounds, fed rectally, and suffering massive infection. On September 6 he was moved by a specially built rail line to Francklyn Cottage at Elberon on the New Jersey shore, where he died on September 19, 1881. The autopsy showed the cause of death was infection and internal hemorrhage following the rupturing of a splenic artery aneurysm. Many later medical assessments have concluded that Garfield would likely have survived his wound with sterile technique, and that his doctors' interventions caused his death.

Trial and aftermath

Guiteau's trial, beginning in November 1881, was a media sensation; he pleaded insanity, behaved erratically, and attempted to argue that Garfield had been killed by medical malpractice rather than by the bullet — "The doctors killed Garfield, I just shot him." The jury convicted him of murder on January 25, 1882, and he was hanged on June 30, 1882. Vice President Arthur succeeded Garfield on September 20, 1881.

The assassination intensified public revulsion at the spoils system. In January 1883 Arthur signed the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act, which began replacing political patronage with merit-based appointments for federal jobs — a reform long championed by the slain president.
