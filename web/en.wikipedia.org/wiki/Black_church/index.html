Black church

The Black church (sometimes called Black Christianity or African American churches) is a collective term for Christian churches and denominations in the United States that minister predominantly to African American congregations. The tradition emerged from the religious gatherings of enslaved Africans in the 18th century and from independent Black congregations founded by free Black Christians before the Civil War.

History

Independent Black churches such as the African Methodist Episcopal Church (founded 1816 by Richard Allen in Philadelphia) and the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church grew out of protests against segregation in white congregations. During slavery, clandestine "hush harbor" meetings sustained a faith that blended African traditions with evangelical Christianity. After emancipation the church became the central institution of Black communities — at once school, social centre, political forum and economic cooperative.

Role in civil rights

Black churches were the organisational backbone of the civil rights movement: leaders such as Martin Luther King Jr. were Baptist preachers, and churches hosted mass meetings, voter-registration drives and boycotts. Major historically Black denominations include the National Baptist Convention, the Church of God in Christ, and the AME and AME Zion churches. Distinctive features of the tradition include call-and-response preaching, gospel music, and a theology emphasising liberation and social justice.
