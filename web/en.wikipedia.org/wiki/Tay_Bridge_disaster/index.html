Tay Bridge disaster

1879 railway bridge collapse in Scotland

The Tay Bridge disaster occurred on the night of Sunday 28 December 1879 during a violent storm, when the central "high girders" of the first Tay Rail Bridge across the Firth of Tay collapsed as a train crossed from Wormit towards Dundee. The train, with all 75 passengers and crew aboard, plunged into the icy waters of the Tay; there were no survivors. It remains one of the most famous structural engineering failures of the Victorian age.

The bridge and the collapse

Designed by Sir Thomas Bouch and opened to great acclaim in 1878 — Queen Victoria crossed it and knighted Bouch — the bridge was then the longest in the world at about two miles. Its central navigation spans were carried high above the firth on slender cast-iron piers. On the night of the disaster, winds reached storm force; witnesses in Dundee saw sparks and a flash from the bridge, after which a long section was simply gone.

Inquiry and findings

The Court of Inquiry found the collapse resulted from fundamental defects: the design made inadequate allowance for wind pressure, the cast-iron piers and their lugs were badly cast and brittle, workmanship and inspection were poor, and maintenance had allowed loosened bracing to go uncorrected. Bouch's reputation was destroyed, and he died within a year. The locomotive, remarkably, was recovered and returned to service for decades.

Legacy

The disaster led to far stricter British bridge-design standards, including explicit wind-loading requirements; the later Forth Bridge was conspicuously over-engineered in response. The stumps of the original piers still stand beside the replacement Tay Bridge (opened 1887), visible from V&A Dundee and the River Tay waterfront. William McGonagall's famously awful poem "The Tay Bridge Disaster" (1880) immortalised the event in verse.
