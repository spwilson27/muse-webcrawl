Vendor-managed inventory (VMI) is an inventory management practice in which a supplier (vendor) of goods is responsible for optimizing the inventory held by a distributor or retailer. The supplier receives data on the customer's sales and stock levels — typically via electronic data interchange (EDI) — and decides when and how much to replenish, within mutually agreed bounds.

Process

In a traditional system the buyer monitors stock and places orders; under VMI the buyer instead shares inventory and point-of-sale data, and the supplier generates replenishment orders itself. The buyer sets parameters such as minimum and maximum stock levels and service-level targets, and retains the right to approve or override orders. VMI is frequently combined with consignment stock, in which ownership of goods passes to the buyer only when they are sold or used.

History

VMI became prominent in the late 1980s through the partnership between Wal-Mart and Procter & Gamble, in which P&G managed the retailer's stock of its products, reducing stockouts and inventory across the chain. The practice spread through retail, groceries, industrial distribution and healthcare supply chains.

Benefits and criticism

Proponents cite lower inventory, fewer stockouts, smoother production planning for the supplier and a reduction of the bullwhip effect. Critics note the risks of sharing commercially sensitive data, dependence on the supplier's competence, and the transfer of inventory-holding costs upstream.
