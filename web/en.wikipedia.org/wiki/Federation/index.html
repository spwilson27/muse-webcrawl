Federation

A federation (also known as a federal state) is a political entity characterised by a union of partially self-governing provinces, states or other regions under a central federal government. In a federation, the division of powers between the central government and the constituent units is entrenched in a written constitution and cannot be changed unilaterally by either level — the defining contrast with a unitary state, where regional governments exercise only powers delegated to them.

Features

Federal systems typically allocate exclusive competences to each level (for example defence and foreign affairs to the centre; education and policing to the states), with residual powers assigned by the constitution. Federations usually have a bicameral legislature in which one chamber represents the constituent units, and a constitutional court to arbitrate disputes between levels of government.

Examples

About forty per cent of the world's population lives in federations. The oldest modern federation is the United States (1789), whose model influenced many successors. Other major federations include Germany, Canada, Australia, India, Brazil, Mexico, Russia, Switzerland, Austria, Belgium, Nigeria, Ethiopia and Malaysia. Federations may form by previously independent units "coming together" for common purposes, or by a unitary state "holding together" through devolution of power to regions, as in Belgium and Spain's quasi-federal system.

Federation versus confederation

A confederation, by contrast, is a looser union in which the central authority derives its power from the member states and typically cannot act directly on citizens; the European Union exhibits some confederal features. Political scientists place states on a spectrum from unitary through federal to confederal organisation.
