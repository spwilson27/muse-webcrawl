Nuclear winter

Nuclear winter
MechanismSoot from burning cities injected into the stratosphere, blocking sunlight
First modelled1983 (TTAPS study)
Predicted effectsGlobal cooling, shortened growing seasons, ozone loss, widespread famine

Nuclear winter is a severe and prolonged global climatic cooling effect hypothesised to follow a large-scale nuclear war. Firestorms ignited in burning cities would loft millions of tonnes of soot into the upper troposphere and stratosphere, where — above the weather that could wash it out — the smoke would spread globally, absorb sunlight, and chill the Earth's surface for years to more than a decade. The resulting collapse of growing seasons is predicted to cause crop failures and mass famine far exceeding the direct death toll of the war itself.

History of the concept

The hypothesis emerged in the early 1980s from studies of the climatic effects of asteroid impacts and volcanic eruptions. In 1983 the TTAPS study — by Richard Turco, Owen Toon, Thomas Ackerman, James Pollack, and Carl Sagan — published in Science modelled a baseline exchange of 5,000 megatons and found continental temperature drops of 15–25 °C. Sagan popularised the idea in print and television, and the scenario influenced superpower arms-control debates. Critics argued the models were oversimplified; a 1986 reassessment proposed the milder phrase "nuclear autumn".

Modern modelling

Twenty-first-century climate models with full ocean and stratosphere dynamics have broadly reaffirmed strong cooling. Studies by Alan Robock, Brian Toon and colleagues find that a war using 100 small (15 kt) weapons on cities — about 0.03% of the explosive power modelled in 1983 — would inject about 5 Tg of soot, cool the planet by more than 1 °C for years, cut global precipitation, and deplete ozone by 20–50%, increasing ultraviolet radiation. A full-scale United States–Russia exchange injecting roughly 150 Tg of soot is modelled to lower global average temperatures by about 8–9 °C, with cooling persisting over a decade and food-energy production in major breadbaskets falling by over 90% in the worst years; 2022 modelling estimated more than five billion people could starve.

Related phenomena

The mechanism is analogous to the impact winter believed to have contributed to the extinction of the dinosaurs 66 million years ago, and to the "year without a summer" of 1816 that followed the eruption of Mount Tambora. The possibility of a self-induced nuclear winter also informs debates about nuclear deterrence, arsenals' size, and unilateral restraint.
