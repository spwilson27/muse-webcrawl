Candid photography

Image placeholder — an unposed street scene

Candid photography captures people in spontaneous, unposed moments, usually without the subject's knowledge or direction. Unlike traditional portraiture, candid images aim to record genuine expressions and everyday scenes as they happen, and the style is closely related to street photography and photojournalism.

History

Candid photography became practical with small, fast cameras. The Leica rangefinder cameras of the 1920s, and photographers such as Erich Salomon — who photographed conferences and courtrooms with concealed cameras — established the genre. Henri Cartier-Bresson's idea of the "decisive moment" further shaped candid practice in documentary work.

Technique

Candid photographers typically use available light, fast lenses and unobtrusive equipment, shooting from the hip or at a distance so subjects remain unaware. Modern practice relies on small mirrorless cameras and smartphones; long telephoto lenses are associated with paparazzi photography, a commercially driven variant.

Ethics and law

photographing strangers in public is generally lawful in many jurisdictions, but candid photography raises questions of privacy, consent and harassment, and rules differ widely by country. Publication of candid images of private individuals can be restricted by privacy and data-protection law, while images of public figures in public places are more widely tolerated.
