Campanian Ignimbrite eruption
Volcano: Phlegraean Fields, Italy
Date: about 39,000 years ago
VEI: 7
Volume: roughly 250–300 km³ of magma

The Campanian Ignimbrite eruption was a massive eruption of the Phlegraean Fields caldera west of Naples, Italy, about 39,000 years ago. Rated VEI 7, it is the largest volcanic eruption in Europe of the last 200,000 years, depositing the widespread grey tuff (ignimbrite) that underlies the Campanian plain and dispersing ash (the "Y-5" tephra layer) as far as the Russian plain.

Effects

Pyroclastic flows covered some 30,000 km², and the release of sulfur and the ash veil are thought to have caused a "volcanic winter" with several degrees of global cooling. The eruption has been hypothesised to have contributed to the decline of Neanderthals and environmental stress on early modern humans in eastern Europe, though the size of that effect remains debated among researchers.

Geological significance

The ignimbrite is a key stratigraphic marker across the Mediterranean and eastern Europe, used to date archaeological sites of the Middle-to-Upper Palaeolithic transition. The Phlegraean Fields remain an active, closely monitored caldera.
