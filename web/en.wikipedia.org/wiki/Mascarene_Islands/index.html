Mascarene Islands

The Mascarene Islands (English: /ˌmæskəˈriːn/, French: Mascareignes) are a group of islands in the Indian Ocean east of Madagascar and approximately 2,000 kilometres (1,200 mi) off the southeast coast of Africa. The principal islands are Mauritius, Réunion, and Rodrigues, along with a number of smaller islands and archipelagos. Politically, Mauritius and Rodrigues form part of the Republic of Mauritius, while Réunion is an overseas department and region of France.

History

The archipelago is named after the Portuguese navigator Pedro Mascarenhas, who visited the region in the early 16th century, although Arab and Malay sailors may have known of the islands earlier. The Portuguese did not settle the islands, and they became waypoints — and later contested possessions — for the Dutch, French, and British. The Dutch colonised Mauritius in 1638, introducing sugar cane but also contributing to the extinction of the dodo. France took control of Mauritius (renamed Isle de France) and Réunion (Île Bourbon) in the 18th century; Britain captured Mauritius and Rodrigues in 1810 and retained them under the Treaty of Paris (1814), while Réunion was returned to France.

Geology

The islands are volcanic in origin, formed by the Réunion hotspot, a mantle plume whose activity also produced the Deccan Traps in India tens of millions of years ago. Réunion's Piton de la Fournaise remains one of the world's most active volcanoes. The islands sit on the Mascarene Plateau, a largely submarine plateau associated with the breakup of the ancient supercontinent Gondwana and the microcontinent of Mauritia.

Ecology

Isolated for millions of years, the Mascarenes developed a highly distinctive flora and fauna with many endemic species. The most famous inhabitant was the dodo, a flightless bird endemic to Mauritius that became extinct in the late 17th century, within decades of human settlement — now an emblem of human-caused extinction. Other notable endemics included the Rodrigues solitaire, giant tortoises, and a rich endemic flora. Conservation efforts today protect remnants of native forest and offshore islets, and captive-breeding programmes have recovered species such as the Mauritius kestrel and the pink pigeon from the brink of extinction.
