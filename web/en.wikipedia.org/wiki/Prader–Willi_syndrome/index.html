Prader–Willi syndrome

Prader–Willi syndrome (PWS) is a genetic disorder caused by the loss of function of genes on the paternal copy of chromosome 15 (region 15q11–13). It is characterised by severe hypotonia and feeding difficulties in infancy, followed in early childhood by excessive eating (hyperphagia) and the gradual development of obesity — the leading cause of death in the syndrome if uncontrolled. Other features include short stature, small hands and feet, hypogonadism, mild-to-moderate intellectual disability and distinctive behavioural problems.

Genetics

PWS is the classic example of genomic imprinting in humans. In about 70% of cases a segment of the paternal chromosome 15 is deleted; in about 25% the child inherits two maternal copies of chromosome 15 (maternal uniparental disomy) and no paternal copy; the remainder have defects of the imprinting centre or rearrangements. Loss of the same region on the maternal chromosome instead causes the quite different Angelman syndrome. The small nucleolar RNA gene cluster SNORD116 is thought to be central to the PWS phenotype, and hypothalamic dysfunction underlies the appetite, endocrine and temperature-regulation abnormalities.

Course and management

Affected infants are floppy and feed poorly, often requiring tube feeding. Between about ages two and eight, appetite becomes insatiable because satiety signalling fails; without strict external control of food access, life-threatening obesity follows. Management combines a supervised low-calorie diet and locked food environments, growth hormone therapy (which improves height, muscle mass and body composition), sex-hormone replacement, and behavioural support. With modern care, life expectancy has improved markedly, though complications of obesity remain the main risk.
