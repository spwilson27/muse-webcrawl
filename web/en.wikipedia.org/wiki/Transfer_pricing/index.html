Transfer pricing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transfer pricing refers to the rules and methods for pricing transactions internal to, and between, enterprises under common ownership or control — for example, sales of goods, provision of services, loans, and licences of intellectual property between subsidiaries of a multinational corporation located in different tax jurisdictions.

Arm's length principle

Most tax systems require that related-party transactions be priced as if between independent parties — the arm's length principle, embodied in Article 9 of the OECD Model Tax Convention. Because a multinational can shift profit toward low-tax jurisdictions by manipulating internal prices (overcharging from a tax-haven subsidiary, or undercharging it), transfer pricing is one of the most heavily scrutinised areas of international tax.

Methods

Accepted methods for testing arm's length pricing include the comparable uncontrolled price (CUP) method, the resale price method, the cost plus method, the transactional net margin method (TNMM), and the profit split method. The OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines, first published in 1995 and regularly updated, provide the framework applied by most countries.

Enforcement and disputes

Tax authorities may adjust transfer prices, imposing additional tax, interest and penalties; taxpayers can often seek relief from double taxation through mutual agreement procedures or advance pricing agreements (APAs) negotiated in advance. High-profile disputes involving technology and pharmaceutical groups have produced assessments in the billions of dollars, and the OECD/G20 Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) project of 2013–2015 substantially tightened documentation and country-by-country reporting requirements.
