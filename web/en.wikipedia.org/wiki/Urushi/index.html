Urushi

Urushi (Japanese: 漆) is a traditional Japanese lacquer obtained from the sap of the lacquer tree, Toxicodendron vernicifluum, known in Japanese as urushi no ki. The sap contains the resinous compound urushiol, an irritant that causes severe contact dermatitis in many people, which polymerises in the presence of moisture to form a hard, durable, glossy finish.

Production

The sap is collected by making shallow incisions in the bark of trees that are around ten years old, during the summer months. The milky sap that oozes out is collected in containers, filtered to remove debris, and gently heated and stirred to remove excess water and homogenise it. A single tree yields only a small quantity of sap in a season, and the tapping process often kills the tree, which makes high-quality raw urushi expensive.

Properties and use

Unlike most coatings, urushi does not dry by evaporation; it cures through an enzymatic reaction of urushiol (catalysed by the enzyme laccase) that requires warm, humid conditions, typically in a curing cabinet called a muro. The cured film is exceptionally hard, resistant to water, acids, alkalis, alcohol, and heat, and has been used for thousands of years to coat wood, bamboo, leather, metal, and even paper and ceramics. Japanese lacquerware (shikki) is produced by applying many thin coats of urushi, each cured and polished, sometimes inlaid with gold and silver in techniques such as maki-e.

History

The use of urushi in Japan dates back to the Jōmon period; lacquered artefacts some 9,000 years old have been excavated. Lacquer techniques spread throughout East and Southeast Asia, and Japanese urushi ware became a prized export to Europe from the 16th century onward.

Health hazards

Uncured urushi contains urushiol, the same class of compounds found in poison ivy, and contact can cause an intensely itchy rash. Lacquer workers often develop a tolerance over time. Once fully cured, urushi is generally considered safe and food-safe.
