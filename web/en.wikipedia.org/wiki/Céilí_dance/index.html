Céilí dance

Céilí dances (Irish: rincí céilí) are a traditional form of Irish group folk dancing. A céilí — from the Scottish Gaelic cèilidh, meaning a visit or gathering — is a social event with music and dancing, and the term is applied both to the gathering and to the dances performed at it. Céilí dances are performed by couples arranged in formations such as lines, circles and squares of four or eight dancers, and are danced socially as well as competitively.

History

Group dancing in Ireland drew on native traditions and on the quadrilles and set dances introduced from continental Europe in the 18th and 19th centuries. In the late 19th century the Gaelic League promoted céilí dancing as a distinctly Irish alternative to foreign dances. In the 20th century the Irish Dancing Commission (An Coimisiún le Rincí Gaelacha, CLRG) codified a canon of thirty céilí dances in its handbook Ár Rincí Céilí, which are taught and competed worldwide.

Dance style

Céilí dancing uses the basic steps of Irish dancing, including the "seven" (side step), promenade step and threes, performed with an upright carriage, arms held at the sides except when joined with other dancers, and precise timing to the music. Dances are structured in figures — such as the advance and retire, chains, arches and "houses" — separated by a repeated "body" section. Well-known céilí dances include The Walls of Limerick, The Siege of Ennis, The Haymakers' Jig and The Fairy Reel.

Social and competitive céilís

Social céilís, accompanied by a live céilí band playing reels, jigs and marches, remain a feature of Irish cultural life, at festivals, weddings and community halls, and are a staple of events during Seachtain na Gaeilge. Competitively, teams of eight perform céilí dances at feiseanna up to world championship level (Oireachtas Rince na Cruinne), judged on timing, formation and uniformity.
