Hendecasyllable

The hendecasyllable is a poetic metre of eleven syllables, best known as the principal line of Italian poetry, where it is called the endecasillabo, and widely used in Spanish, Catalan, and Portuguese verse. The name is also given to a quantitative Latin metre used by Catullus and Martial.

Italian endecasillabo

In Italian, the hendecasyllable requires an obligatory stress on the tenth syllable and one further main stress, either on the fourth syllable (a minore) or on the sixth (a maiore). It became the canonical line of Italian literature: Dante used it in terza rima in the Divine Comedy, Petrarch in the sonnets of the Canzoniere, and it remained the standard metre for epic and lyric for centuries. Synalepha — the merging of adjacent vowels across word boundaries — helps the line keep its eleven-syllable count.

In other traditions

The Spanish endecasílabo was introduced in the 16th century by Juan Boscán and Garcilaso de la Vega, adapting Italian models, and became fundamental to Golden Age poetry. In English, eleven-syllable lines appear occasionally as a variant of iambic pentameter with a feminine ending. The classical Latin Phalaecian hendecasyllable, used by Catullus in many of his poems, follows a fixed quantitative pattern and is named after the Greek poet Phalaecus.
