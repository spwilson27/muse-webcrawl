Metaethics is the branch of ethics that seeks to understand the nature, scope, and meaning of moral judgment. It is one of the three branches of ethics generally recognized by philosophers, the others being normative ethics and applied ethics. Where normative ethics asks what people ought to do and which character traits are good, metaethics asks more fundamental questions: what does it mean to say that something is right or good? Are moral judgments true or false, and if so, what makes them true? Do moral facts exist, and how could human beings know them?

The landscape of metaethical theories

History

Questions in metaethics were discussed by ancient philosophers — Plato's Euthyphro dialogue, for example, asks whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or pious because it is loved — but the field crystallized as a distinct discipline in the early twentieth century. G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica (1903) is usually taken as its founding text. Moore argued that "good" names a simple, indefinable, non-natural property, and that any attempt to define it in natural terms (such as pleasure or evolutionary fitness) commits the naturalistic fallacy. His open question argument held that for any natural property N, the question "X is N, but is X good?" remains intelligibly open, which suggests goodness is not identical with any natural property.

The logical positivists of the 1930s, especially A. J. Ayer in Language, Truth and Logic (1936), concluded from the unverifiability of moral claims that they are not genuine statements at all but expressions of emotion — a view developed more subtly by Charles Stevenson as emotivism. R. M. Hare's prescriptivism treated moral judgments as universalizable prescriptions. In the later twentieth century, J. L. Mackie's Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong (1977) defended an error theory — moral judgments are truth-apt but all systematically false, because they presuppose objective values that do not exist — while philosophers such as Peter Railton, Richard Boyd, and David Brink developed new forms of moral realism, and Simon Blackburn and Allan Gibbard refined non-cognitivist expressivism.

Central questions

Moral semantics

What is the function of moral language? Cognitivists hold that moral sentences express beliefs and are capable of being true or false. Non-cognitivists hold that their primary function is not to describe the world but to express an attitude, emotion, or prescription, in the way that "Hurrah for kindness!" or "Be kind!" do. Expressivists face the Frege–Geach problem: explaining how moral sentences behave in unasserted contexts, such as the antecedent of a conditional ("If stealing is wrong, then getting your brother to steal is wrong"), where no attitude is obviously expressed.

Moral ontology

Do moral properties exist, and if so, of what kind? Moral realists maintain that there are objective moral facts, independent of what anyone thinks about them; naturalist realists identify moral properties with natural properties, while non-naturalists (following Moore) treat them as sui generis. Anti-realists include error theorists, who accept that moral thought purports to refer to objective values but deny that any exist, and constructivists and relativists, who ground moral truth in idealized standpoints, cultures, or individual commitments.

Moral epistemology and motivation

How, if at all, can moral truths be known? Intuitionists appeal to a faculty of moral intuition; coherentists to reflective equilibrium; naturalists to the ordinary empirical methods that might track natural moral properties. A further question concerns the link between judgment and motivation: motivational internalists hold that sincerely judging an action right necessarily involves some motivation to perform it, a constraint that fits naturally with non-cognitivism and awkwardly with forms of realism that picture moral judgment as purely descriptive belief.

Key concepts

  • Supervenience: the widely accepted claim that the moral properties of a thing cannot differ without some difference in its non-moral properties.
  • The is–ought problem: David Hume's observation that statements about what ought to be cannot be straightforwardly deduced from statements about what is.
  • Moral twin earth: a thought experiment by Terence Horgan and Mark Timmons challenging naturalist attempts to fix the reference of moral terms.
  • Companions in guilt: arguments that worries about the "queerness" of moral facts apply equally to epistemic facts, such as reasons for belief.
  • Quasi-realism: Blackburn's project of earning, from an expressivist starting point, the right to speak in realist-sounding terms of moral truth and knowledge.
