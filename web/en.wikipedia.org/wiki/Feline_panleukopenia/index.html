Feline panleukopenia

Feline panleukopenia (FP), also called feline distemper or feline parvovirus infection, is a highly contagious and often fatal viral disease of cats caused by feline parvovirus (FPV), a close relative of canine parvovirus. The name refers to the sharp drop in circulating white blood cells (panleukopenia) seen in infected animals.

Transmission and symptoms

The virus is extremely hardy in the environment and spreads through contact with infected cats, their secretions, and contaminated objects such as bedding, bowls and clothing. It attacks rapidly dividing cells — the bone marrow, intestinal lining and, in unborn or newborn kittens, the developing cerebellum. Signs include fever, loss of appetite, vomiting, severe diarrhoea, dehydration and collapse of the white-cell count, leaving the cat open to secondary infections. Mortality is highest in young kittens, in which the disease can kill the majority of an unvaccinated litter; infection of a pregnant queen can produce kittens with cerebellar hypoplasia, which causes lifelong incoordination.

Prevention and treatment

There is no cure specific to the virus; treatment is supportive, with fluids, anti-nausea medication, nutrition and antibiotics against secondary bacterial infection. Vaccination is highly effective and is a core vaccine for all cats: kittens receive a series of doses beginning at around six to eight weeks of age, with boosters thereafter. Because the virus persists in the environment, thorough disinfection with bleach-based products is recommended after an outbreak.
