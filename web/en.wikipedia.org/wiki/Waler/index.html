Waler

The Waler is an Australian breed of riding and light cavalry horse developed in the colony of New South Wales from horses imported in the early decades of European settlement. The name derives from "New South Wales". Walers were renowned for their hardiness, stamina, and ability to thrive on sparse feed, and tens of thousands were exported in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, first to India for the British and Indian armies and later for service in both world wars.

Origins

The Waler was not a formal breed with a closed studbook but a recognised type produced by crossing imported stock — including Thoroughbreds, Arabs, Timor ponies, and draught horses such as Clydesdales, Suffolks and Cleveland Bays — under harsh Australian conditions. Natural selection on vast outback stations culled the weak, yielding a tough, sure-footed animal standing roughly 15 to 16.1 hands, valued as a remount and stock horse.

Military service

From the 1830s, Walers were shipped to India in large numbers to mount British and Indian cavalry regiments. They served in the Boer War and throughout the First World War. Their most famous role was with the Australian Light Horse in the Sinai and Palestine campaigns, including the charge at Beersheba on 31 October 1917, often cited as the last successful mounted charge in military history. Because of quarantine regulations, almost all surviving Walers were destroyed or transferred to other armies at the war's end rather than returned to Australia; the fate of the horses, commemorated in verse and memorials, became a poignant part of the Anzac legend.

Modern Walers

The Waler largely disappeared as a distinct remount type after mechanisation, but in the 1980s enthusiasts established the Waler Horse Society of Australia and began rebuilding the population from brumby (feral horse) stock and old bloodlines matching the historical type. The modern Waler is used for endurance riding, stock work, and general riding.
