Needle exchange programme

A needle and syringe programme (NSP), also known as needle exchange programme (NEP) or syringe services program (SSP), is a social service that allows people who inject drugs to obtain clean, unused needles and syringes and dispose of used ones, usually without charge and often anonymously. It is a core component of harm reduction policy.

History

Informal exchanges began in Amsterdam in 1984 in response to a hepatitis B outbreak, and official programmes followed in the United Kingdom from 1986–87 as the HIV/AIDS epidemic spread among people who inject drugs. Programmes subsequently spread across Europe, Australia and parts of North America and Asia, often against legal and political opposition rooted in drug prohibition laws and fears that providing equipment would encourage drug use.

Operation

Services operate from fixed sites, pharmacies, mobile vans and outreach teams. Besides sterile needles and syringes, many provide sharps containers, condoms, sterile water and other injecting equipment, as well as HIV and hepatitis testing, vaccinations, wound care, naloxone for opioid overdose reversal, and referrals to addiction treatment and social services.

Evidence and debate

A large body of research reviewed by bodies such as the World Health Organization and national academies finds that needle and syringe programmes reduce HIV transmission and are cost-effective, and do not increase drug use, drug injecting frequency or neighbourhood crime. Nonetheless, programmes remain illegal or restricted in some jurisdictions, and debates continue over one-for-one exchange rules versus needs-based distribution.
