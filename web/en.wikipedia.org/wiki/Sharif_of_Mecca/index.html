Sharif of Mecca

Sharif of Mecca (Arabic: شريف مكة, Sharīf Makkah), also known as the Sharif of the Hijaz, was the title of the hereditary ruler of Mecca and, by extension, much of the Hejaz region of western Arabia, from the late 10th century until 1924. The office was held by Hashemite descendants of the Islamic prophet Muhammad through his grandson Hasan ibn Ali — the word sharīf meaning "noble" or "highborn" — who governed the holy city under the nominal suzerainty of successive caliphates and empires, most lastingly the Ottoman Empire.

Hero image: Mecca, seat of the Sharifs for nearly a thousand years.

History

The Sharifate began around 967–968 CE, when the Hashemite Ja'far ibn Muhammad took control of Mecca. Thereafter the city was ruled by a succession of sharifian dynasties — the Ja'farids, Sulaymanids, Hawashim and, from the 13th century, the Qatadids — while acknowledging the sovereignty of the Fatimid, Ayyubid, Mamluk and finally Ottoman caliphs in turn. The sharif's authority rested on his control of the Hajj pilgrimage, his custodianship of the holy sanctuaries, and the subsidies sent by Muslim rulers. Ottoman rule from 1517 confirmed the sharifs in exchange for loyalty, and the arrangement endured for four centuries.

Hussein bin Ali and the Arab Revolt

The last and most famous Sharif, Hussein ibn Ali al-Hashimi, was appointed in 1908. During the First World War, following the Hussein–McMahon Correspondence with Britain, he proclaimed the Arab Revolt in June 1916, raised the Arab flag over Mecca, and declared himself King of the Arab countries; the Allies recognised him as King of the Hejaz. After the war his sons became kings of Iraq and Transjordan. In 1924 Hussein proclaimed himself Caliph following the Turkish abolition of the caliphate, but that same year the Saudi forces of Ibn Saud captured Mecca; Hussein abdicated in favour of his son Ali, who was deposed in 1925, ending the Sharifate after roughly 958 years. Ali's descendants through the Hashemite line continue to reign in Jordan.

Role and prerogatives

The Sharif administered Mecca and usually Jeddah and Taif, commanded a small armed retinue, collected pilgrimage-related revenues and gifts from across the Muslim world, and mediated among the Bedouin tribes of the Hejaz. His religious prestige as a descendant of the Prophet and guardian of Islam's holiest city gave the office influence far beyond its modest military resources.
