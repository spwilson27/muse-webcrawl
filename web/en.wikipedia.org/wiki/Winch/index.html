Winch

A winch is a mechanical device that is used to pull in (wind up) or let out (wind out) or otherwise adjust the tension of a rope or wire rope, which is wound around a horizontal rotating drum turned by a crank, engine, electric motor or hydraulic drive. In its simplest form, it consists of a spool (or drum) attached to a hand crank. Winches are the basis of machines such as tow trucks, steam shovels and elevators, and are used in applications from launching gliders and mooring ships to recovering stuck vehicles.

History

The earliest literary reference to a winch can be found in the account of Herodotus of Halicarnassus on the Persian Wars, describing wooden winches used to tighten the iron cables for a pontoon bridge across the Hellespont in 480 BC. By the 4th century BC, winch and pulley hoists were in use in Greek theatre machinery. On ships, large winches called windlasses handle anchors and mooring lines, while the related vertical-axis device is the capstan.

Design and operation

A winch's pulling power is rated by the maximum load it can pull with a single layer of cable on the drum; capacity falls as more layers wind on. Most powered winches use a gear reduction — commonly worm, spur or planetary gears — between the motor and the drum, and incorporate a brake to prevent the load from unwinding the drum. Wire rope and synthetic fibre rope are the usual winding media; synthetic rope is lighter and safer in the event of breakage but less resistant to heat and abrasion.

Applications

  • Vehicle recovery: off-road vehicles, tow trucks and military vehicles carry winches for self-recovery and extracting other vehicles.
  • Marine: anchor winches, mooring winches and sailboat winches for sheets and halyards.
  • Aviation: winch launching of gliders, in which a powerful stationary winch reels in a long cable to launch a glider like a kite.
  • Industry and entertainment: construction hoists, logging, theatre rigging and stunt work.
  • Trailers and boats: small hand winches pull boats onto trailers.
