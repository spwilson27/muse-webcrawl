Glass–Steagall Act

The Glass–Steagall Act is a common name for several pieces of United States banking legislation. It most often refers to four provisions of the Banking Act of 1933 that separated commercial banking from investment banking, prohibiting banks that took deposits from underwriting or dealing in securities, and barring securities firms from taking deposits. The name derives from the Act's sponsors, Senator Carter Glass of Virginia and Representative Henry B. Steagall of Alabama. An earlier law, the Glass–Steagall Act of 1932, had liberalised the collateral rules for Federal Reserve lending.

Provisions of the 1933 Act

Sections 16, 20, 21 and 32 of the Banking Act of 1933 separated commercial and investment banking: national banks' securities powers were limited; member banks could not be affiliated with firms "engaged principally" in securities underwriting; firms engaged in the securities business could not accept deposits; and interlocking directorates between member banks and securities firms were prohibited. The same Act created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) to insure bank deposits. The separation was administered for decades through regulation and court decisions, including the Supreme Court's ruling in Investment Company Institute v. Camp (1971).

Erosion and repeal

From the 1970s onward, regulators and courts progressively loosened the separation, notably the Federal Reserve's Section 20 orders beginning in 1987 that allowed bank holding companies limited securities underwriting through subsidiaries. The Gramm–Leach–Bliley Act of 1999 repealed Sections 20 and 32, formally permitting affiliations between commercial banks and securities firms and giving rise to universal banks such as Citigroup, which had already been formed by merger in anticipation of repeal.

Debate

The repeal has been debated in connection with the 2007–2008 financial crisis: critics argue it encouraged excessive risk-taking by combining deposit banking with securities activities, while defenders note that the institutions at the centre of the crisis were mostly pure investment banks or non-bank lenders. The Dodd–Frank Act of 2010 did not restore Glass–Steagall, though its Volcker Rule restricted proprietary trading by deposit-taking banks. For the earlier statute, see Glass–Steagall Act of 1932.
