Gin

Gin is a distilled alcoholic drink whose predominant flavour comes from juniper berries (Juniperus communis). It evolved from the Dutch juniper-flavoured spirit jenever and became enormously popular in Britain — notoriously during the "Gin Craze" of the early 18th century — before developing into today's styles, of which London dry gin is the best known.

History

Jenever was produced in the Low Countries from at least the 16th century and was credited (apocryphally) to the physician Franciscus Sylvius as a medicine. English soldiers encountered it during the wars in the Netherlands — "Dutch courage" — and after William of Orange took the English throne in 1688, domestic distilling was encouraged while French brandy imports were restricted. Cheap gin flooded London, provoking the moral panic immortalised in Hogarth's Gin Lane (1751) and a series of Gin Acts culminating in the restrictive Act of 1751, which curbed the craze. The column still later enabled the cleaner "dry" style, and gin became central to colonial life — combined with quinine tonic water as the gin and tonic — and to the cocktail culture of the 19th and 20th centuries: the martini, the Gibson, the gimlet, the Tom Collins and the negroni.

Production and styles

Gin begins as a neutral spirit, usually from grain, which is flavoured with botanicals — always including juniper, and commonly coriander seed, citrus peel, angelica, orris root, liquorice, cassia and cardamom. In distilled gin the botanicals are redistilled with the spirit, by pot still or vapour infusion; "compound gin" is flavoured without redistillation. Styles include:

  • London dry gin — a dry, juniper-forward style (despite the name, it need not be made in London); all flavour must come from distillation and nothing but water and minimal sugar may be added after.
  • Plymouth gin — historically a protected style from Plymouth, England.
  • Old Tom gin — a slightly sweetened style popular in the 18th–19th centuries, revived by craft distillers.
  • Jenever (genever) — the maltier Dutch/Belgian original, in oude and jonge forms.
  • Sloe gin — a liqueur made by steeping sloe berries in gin.
  • Contemporary / new western gins — emphasising botanicals other than juniper.

Under EU law gin must be at least 37.5% ABV (40% minimum in the United States) with juniper as the predominant flavour. A craft-gin revival from the 2000s onward has produced thousands of small brands worldwide.
