Ceilidh

OriginScotland and Ireland
FromScottish Gaelic cèilidh, "visit"
ElementsTraditional music, set dancing, storytelling, song
Typical instrumentsFiddle, accordion, flute, tin whistle, bodhrán

A ceilidh or céilí is a traditional Scottish and Irish social gathering. In its most basic form, it simply means a social visit. In contemporary usage, a ceilidh usually involves playing Gaelic folk music and dancing, either at a house party or a larger concert at a social hall or community gathering place.

Etymology and history

The word derives from the Scottish Gaelic cèilidh and the Irish céilí (older spelling céilidhe), meaning "visit" or "companion". Originally, a ceilidh was a gathering of neighbours in a house, where stories, poems, ballads, proverbs and news were shared and riddles posed; dancing was not necessarily a feature. In Scotland's Highlands and Islands, these house ceilidhs were a central institution of Gaelic oral culture well into the twentieth century.

Modern ceilidhs

The modern ceilidh centres on participatory social dancing to a live band, typically led by a "caller" who explains each dance. Popular dances include the Gay Gordons, the Dashing White Sergeant, Strip the Willow, the Military Two-Step and the Canadian Barn Dance. Dances are energetic, inclusive of beginners, and often performed at weddings, school functions, fundraisers and festivals. Ceilidh dancing has spread well beyond Scotland and Ireland, with regular events in England, North America, Australia and elsewhere in the Scottish and Irish diasporas.

Music

Ceilidh bands usually feature fiddle, accordion (piano or button), flute or tin whistle, guitar, and drums or bodhrán, playing reels, jigs, hornpipes, polkas, marches and waltzes in sets matched to each dance. Gaelic song and step dancing may feature in Irish céilithe, which, through the Gaelic League and later Comhaltas Ceoltóirí Éireann, developed a somewhat more formalised dance repertoire.
