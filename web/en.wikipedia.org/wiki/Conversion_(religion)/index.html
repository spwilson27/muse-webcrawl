Conversion (religion)

Religious conversion is the adoption of a set of beliefs identified with one particular religious doctrine to the exclusion of others. Conversion involves a change of religious identity and is often marked by rituals such as baptism, confirmation, or a public declaration of faith. People convert for many reasons, including a genuine change of belief, marriage, community belonging, or a transformative spiritual experience; historically, conversion has also occurred under social or political pressure, a practice widely criticized as forced conversion.

Within traditions

In Christianity, conversion is often understood as turning from sin toward God, classically exemplified by the conversion of Paul on the road to Damascus; many denominations distinguish an initial conversion experience from a lifelong process of sanctification. In Islam, conversion is effected by sincere recitation of the shahada, the declaration of faith. Judaism accepts converts through a formal process supervised by a rabbinical court, including study, ritual immersion, and (for men) circumcision. In Buddhism, taking refuge in the Three Jewels — Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha — marks entry into the tradition.

Study of conversion

Sociologists and psychologists of religion study conversion as a process rather than only an event, examining the roles of crisis, social networks, and gradual affiliation. Legal frameworks in many countries protect the freedom to change religion as part of freedom of religion, while some jurisdictions restrict proselytism or regulate conversion, producing continuing debate.

A person who has converted is called a convert; one who actively seeks converts engages in proselytism. Leaving a religion altogether is termed apostasy in traditions that use that category.
