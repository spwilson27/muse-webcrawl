Green's theorem

In vector calculus, Green's theorem relates a line integral around a simple closed curve C to a double integral over the plane region D bounded by C. It is the two-dimensional special case of the more general Stokes' theorem, and is named after the British mathematician George Green.

Statement

Let C be a positively oriented, piecewise smooth, simple closed curve in the plane, and let D be the region bounded by C. If L and M are functions of (x, y) with continuous partial derivatives on an open region containing D, then

∮C (L dx + M dy) = ∬D (∂M/∂x − ∂L/∂y) dA.

The positive orientation means that C is traversed counter-clockwise, so that the region D always lies to the left as the curve is followed.

Relationship to other theorems

Green's theorem is a special case of both the Kelvin–Stokes theorem (in which the surface is flat and lies in the xy-plane) and the divergence theorem. In complex analysis it is closely connected with the Cauchy integral theorem: if a complex function is holomorphic in a region, its contour integral around the boundary vanishes, which Green's theorem recovers from the Cauchy–Riemann equations.

Applications

The theorem is used to compute areas of plane regions from boundary integrals — the principle behind the planimeter — and appears throughout fluid mechanics and electromagnetism, where circulation around a loop is equated to the flux of curl through the enclosed area. In particular, choosing L and M appropriately gives the formulas Area(D) = ∮ x dy = −∮ y dx = ½ ∮ (x dy − y dx).

History

Green published the result in his 1828 Essay on the Application of Mathematical Analysis to the Theories of Electricity and Magnetism. Augustin-Louis Cauchy and others later gave proofs for wider classes of regions.
