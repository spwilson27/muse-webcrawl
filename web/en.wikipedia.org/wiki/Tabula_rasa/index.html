Tabula rasa

Tabula rasa
FieldEpistemology / Philosophy of mind

Tabula rasa (Latin for "blank slate" or "scraped tablet") is the theory that individuals are born without built-in mental content and that knowledge comes largely or entirely from experience and perception. The idea is central to Empiricism and to debates about nature versus nurture in psychology and philosophy of mind.

History

An early version appears in Aristotle's De Anima, which compares the intellect to a writing tablet on which nothing has yet been written. The Stoics and later the medieval scholastics developed related accounts; Thomas Aquinas held that the intellect begins as a potentiality actualised by sensory experience, while Ibn Sina (Avicenna) offered a famous "floating man" thought experiment on self-awareness.

The phrase is most closely associated with John Locke and his Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1689), which argued against innate ideas and compared the mind at birth to "white paper, void of all characters". Locke's account does not deny innate faculties or tendencies, but holds that the content of ideas derives from sensation and reflection.

Criticism and later use

Rationalists such as Leibniz objected that the mind has innate dispositions. In the 20th century the idea was taken up in behaviourism, and linguist Noam Chomsky argued that the rapid acquisition of language by children ("poverty of the stimulus") points to innate structures — a position developed in modern cognitive science and evolutionary psychology, where few defend a completely blank slate. The term survives in developmental psychology, education theory and debates about artificial intelligence.
