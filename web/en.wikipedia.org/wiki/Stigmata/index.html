Stigmata (singular stigma) are bodily wounds, scars and pain appearing in locations corresponding to the crucifixion wounds of Jesus Christ: the hands or wrists, feet, side, and sometimes the head (from the crown of thorns) or back (from scourging and carrying the cross). The term originates from Saint Paul's words in Galatians 6:17, "I bear on my body the marks (stigmata) of Jesus".

History and notable cases

The first recorded case is that of Saint Francis of Assisi, who in 1224, during a retreat on Mount La Verna, was said to have received the wounds after a vision of a seraph. Hundreds of cases have since been reported, disproportionately among women and among members of religious orders; Saint Catherine of Siena claimed invisible stigmata. In the 20th century the Capuchin friar Padre Pio of Pietrelcina bore visible wounds for some fifty years and drew worldwide devotion; his case was examined by physicians and remains debated. Therese Neumann of Konnersreuth and Gemma Galgani are other well-known cases.

Interpretations

The Roman Catholic Church does not require belief in any particular case and has canonised some stigmatics while treating claims cautiously. Medical and sceptical explanations proposed for various cases include self-infliction (conscious or unconscious, including in states of religious ecstasy), psychosomatic bleeding through intact skin, factitious disorders, and skin conditions or bruising disorders. Historians of religion study stigmata in the context of late medieval and early modern devotion to Christ's suffering, in which bodily imitation of the Passion (imitatio Christi) was a central spiritual ideal.
