Cashew

The cashew nut hangs below the fleshy cashew apple.

The cashew (Anacardium occidentale) is a tropical evergreen tree in the family Anacardiaceae, native to northeastern Brazil. It is cultivated for its kidney-shaped seed, the cashew nut, and for the fleshy cashew apple to which the nut is attached. Portuguese colonists spread the tree to Africa and Asia in the 16th century; major producers now include Vietnam, India, Côte d’Ivoire, and Brazil.

The nut and apple

The true fruit is the hard-shelled nut, whose shell contains caustic phenolic oils related to poison ivy’s irritants; kernels must be carefully processed with heat before eating, so cashews sold as “raw” have been steamed or roasted. The swollen stalk, the cashew apple, is juicy and astringent and is eaten fresh or made into juice, preserves, and fermented drinks in producing regions.

Cultivation and trade

Cashew trees tolerate poor, sandy soils and seasonal drought. Much of the world’s harvest is shelled and processed in India and Vietnam, and hand processing—needed to avoid breaking the kernel—has raised labour and safety concerns because of the shell’s caustic oil.

Uses

Kernels are eaten as snacks and used in confectionery, curries, and dairy-free creams and cheeses. Cashew nutshell liquid is an industrial raw material for resins, friction materials, and coatings.
