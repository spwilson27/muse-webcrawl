Huayna Capac (Quechua: Wayna Qhapaq, "splendid youth"; c. 1464/1468 – c. 1525/1527) was the third Sapa Inca of the Inca Empire, succeeding his father Topa Inca Yupanqui in 1493. He extended the empire to its greatest extent, annexing territory in present-day Ecuador and pushing its frontiers to modern Colombia in the north and the Maule River region of Chile in the south. His death — probably from a European epidemic, most often identified as smallpox, sweeping ahead of the conquistadors — left no unambiguous heir, and the succession struggle between his sons Huáscar and Atahualpa tore the empire apart just as Francisco Pizarro's expedition arrived.

Reign and conquests

Huayna Capac spent much of his long reign in the empire's north, campaigning against the chiefdoms of present-day Ecuador — the Cañari, the Cara (Quitu) and the peoples of the coast and highlands around Quito — wars that were long and costly; Spanish chroniclers recount a catastrophic defeat at Yahuarcocha ("Lake of Blood") before final victory. He founded the great northern capital of Tumebamba (modern Cuenca) and lavished attention on Quito, where he kept a second court, and he continued consolidation in the south along the Chilean frontier and in Bolivia. Under him the empire, Tawantinsuyu, reached over 4,000 km from north to south, administered through the Qhapaq Ñan road network, storehouses (qollqa) and the mit'a labour system.

Death and succession crisis

Around 1525–1527, while in the north, Huayna Capac died, together with his designated heir Ninan Cuyochi, in an epidemic — generally believed to have been smallpox or another Old World disease spreading south from the Spanish Caribbean and Panama ahead of direct contact; the dates and the diagnosis remain debated. His mummy was carried in procession back to Cusco. The dead emperor's many sons by different mothers opened a succession contest between Huáscar, crowned in Cusco and backed by the traditional priesthood and nobility, and Atahualpa, based in Quito with the loyalty of his father's veteran northern armies. The ensuing Inca civil war (c. 1529–1532) devastated the empire's north, ended with Atahualpa's victory and Huáscar's capture — and left the victor at Cajamarca, celebrating his triumph, when Pizarro's 168 men arrived in November 1532.
