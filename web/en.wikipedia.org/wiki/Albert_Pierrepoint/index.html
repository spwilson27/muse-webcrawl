Albert Pierrepoint (30 March 1905 – 10 July 1992) was an English executioner who carried out more executions than any other British hangman of the twentieth century. Between 1932 and 1956 he hanged over 400 people, following his father Henry and his uncle Thomas into the family occupation.

Career

Pierrepoint qualified as an assistant executioner in 1932 and became a chief executioner in 1941. After the Second World War he was sent to Hamelin in Germany, where he hanged 202 Nazi war criminals, including staff of Bergen-Belsen concentration camp, in groups over successive days. At home he executed some of Britain's most notorious murderers, among them John Haigh (the "Acid Bath Murderer"), John Christie of Rillington Place, and Derek Bentley, whose conviction was posthumously overturned. In 1955 he hanged Ruth Ellis, the last woman to be executed in Britain, and he also executed the traitors William Joyce ("Lord Haw-Haw") and John Amery.

Later life and views

Pierrepoint resigned in 1956 after a dispute over fees and ran pubs in Lancashire. His 1974 autobiography, Executioner: Pierrepoint, publicly opposed capital punishment, concluding that executions achieved nothing but revenge — a striking position from the state's principal hangman. Capital punishment for murder was suspended in Britain in 1965 and abolished permanently in 1969. His life was dramatised in the 2005 film Pierrepoint, starring Timothy Spall.
