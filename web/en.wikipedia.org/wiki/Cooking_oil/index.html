Cooking oils are plant, animal, or synthetic fats used in frying, baking, and other types of cooking. Most cooking oils are extracted from the seeds, fruits, or nuts of plants — including olives, sunflowers, soybeans, rapeseed (canola), peanuts, palm fruit, and coconuts — while some are rendered from animal fat. Oils serve as a heat-transfer medium, add flavour and texture, and prevent food from sticking.

Properties

The most important property for high-heat cooking is the smoke point, the temperature at which an oil begins to produce visible smoke and degrade. Refined oils have higher smoke points — refined avocado oil about 270 °C (520 °F), refined safflower and light olive oil around 240 °C — whereas unrefined and extra-virgin oils smoke at lower temperatures and are better suited to dressings and gentle cooking. Oils differ in fatty-acid composition: coconut and palm oils are rich in saturated fat; olive and canola oils in monounsaturated fat; sunflower, soybean, and grapeseed oils in polyunsaturated fat.

Production

Vegetable oils are obtained by mechanical pressing (expeller or cold pressing) or by solvent extraction, usually with hexane, followed by refining, bleaching, and deodorizing for neutral-flavoured commodity oils. Cold-pressed extra-virgin olive oil is produced purely mechanically and retains the most flavour and antioxidants.

Storage and disposal

Oils oxidize and become rancid with exposure to heat, light, and air; they keep best in cool, dark storage. Repeated reheating of frying oil accumulates polar compounds and is regulated in some countries. Used cooking oil can be recycled into biodiesel and other products rather than poured down drains.

Health

Nutrition guidance generally favours oils high in unsaturated fats over saturated fats for cardiovascular health, and industrial trans fats produced by partial hydrogenation have been banned or restricted in many jurisdictions because of their strong association with heart disease.
