The Treaty of London of 1839, often called the Treaty of the XXIV Articles, was signed on 19 April 1839 between the United Kingdom of the Netherlands and the Kingdom of Belgium, together with the great powers of Europe — the United Kingdom, France, Austria, Prussia, Russia and others. It recognized Belgium as an independent and perpetually neutral state.

Background

Belgium had declared independence from the Netherlands during the Belgian Revolution of 1830. The great powers had recognized Belgian independence at the London Conference of 1830–31, but King William I of the Netherlands refused to accept the settlement for nearly a decade, holding out for better terms and even attempting a military reconquest in the Ten Days' Campaign of 1831.

Terms

The treaty fixed Belgium's borders. Belgium ceded the eastern part of Limburg and the German-speaking part of Luxembourg back to the Netherlands; the remaining western part of Luxembourg became Belgian. Belgium assumed a share of the Dutch national debt and guaranteed free navigation of the Scheldt. Crucially, Article VII declared Belgium an independent and perpetually neutral state, and the signatory powers guaranteed that neutrality.

Significance

The guarantee of Belgian neutrality became a central element of European diplomacy. Germany's violation of Belgian neutrality in August 1914, when the Schlieffen Plan required marching through Belgium, was denounced by Chancellor Theobald von Bethmann Hollweg as treating the treaty as a "scrap of paper," and it contributed to Britain's decision to declare war on Germany. A separate Treaty of London concluded the same day also settled matters between the Netherlands and the German Confederation over Luxembourg.
