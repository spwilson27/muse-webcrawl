Intaglio (printmaking)

Intaglio (from Italian intagliare, "to incise") is the family of printing and printmaking techniques in which an image is cut into a surface — the matrix, usually a metal plate — and the incised lines or areas hold the ink. It is the direct opposite of a relief print, in which the raised surface carries the ink. The main intaglio techniques are engraving, etching, drypoint, aquatint and mezzotint.

Process

Ink is worked into the recesses of the plate and the surface is wiped clean, leaving ink only below the surface. Dampened paper is then laid over the plate and both are passed through a high-pressure rolling press, which forces the paper into the incised lines and draws the ink out. Because the printed lines sit slightly raised on the paper, an intaglio print can often be identified by the platemark impressed around the image and by the slight relief of the ink.

Techniques

  • Engraving: lines are cut directly into the plate with a burin, producing clean, swelling-and-tapering lines; the oldest intaglio method, developed in the 15th century.
  • Etching: the plate is coated with an acid-resistant ground, drawn through with a needle, and bitten with acid, allowing freer, more sketch-like lines.
  • Drypoint: the image is scratched with a sharp point, throwing up a burr that holds ink and gives a rich, velvety line, though the burr wears quickly.
  • Aquatint: powdered resin is fused to the plate and bitten to create tonal areas rather than lines.
  • Mezzotint: the whole plate is roughened with a rocker so it prints black, and the image is created by burnishing areas smooth to produce tones.

History

Intaglio printing developed in Germany in the mid-15th century, around the same time as printing with movable type, and became the dominant means of reproducing images for centuries — for book illustration, maps, portraits and banknotes. Masters of the medium include Martin Schongauer, Albrecht Dürer, Rembrandt van Rijn (whose etchings remain touchstones), and Francisco Goya. Intaglio plates are still used for security printing, since the raised ink and fine detail are hard to counterfeit.
