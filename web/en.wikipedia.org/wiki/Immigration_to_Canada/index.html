Immigration to Canada

Immigration to Canada is the process by which people migrate to Canada to live and work there. The majority move of their own volition, although a significant number of people have fled to Canada from countries experiencing war or other conflicts. As of the 2021 census, immigrants comprised 23 per cent of the total population of Canada—the highest proportion among G7 countries—and Canada has one of the highest per-capita immigration rates in the world.

Canadian immigration policy is administered by Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) and governed principally by the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act (2002). Canada targets high levels of permanent immigration—over 400,000 new permanent residents annually in recent years—with stated goals of economic growth, family reunification, and humanitarian commitments.

Categories

Economic immigration

The largest category. Since 2015, most economic immigrants are selected through Express Entry, a points-based system ranking candidates by age, education, language proficiency in English or French, and skilled work experience. The Comprehensive Ranking System governs invitations under the Federal Skilled Worker Program, the Federal Skilled Trades Program, and the Canadian Experience Class. The Provincial Nominee Program lets provinces select immigrants for their labour markets.

Family reunification

Canadian citizens and permanent residents may sponsor spouses, partners, children, parents and grandparents. Family-class immigration is the second-largest category.

Refugees and humanitarian

Canada resettles refugees through government-assisted and privately sponsored programs; private sponsorship, introduced in 1979, is distinctive to Canada. Asylum seekers may also claim protection after arrival. Canada has resettled large numbers of Indochinese, Syrian, Afghan, and Ukrainian displaced people over the decades.

History

After Confederation in 1867, immigration was promoted to settle the West, with Clifford Sifton's campaigns recruiting farmers from Europe and the United States. Early policy was openly discriminatory: a head tax (1885) and then the Chinese Exclusion Act (1923) targeted Chinese migrants, and the Continuous Journey Regulation (1908) excluded most South Asian migrants. Reforms after the Second World War gradually removed racial criteria; a points system introduced in 1967 selected immigrants by skills regardless of origin, transforming the source countries of immigration toward Asia, Africa, and Latin America.

Settlement and citizenship

Permanent residents may apply for Canadian citizenship after living in Canada for at least three of the previous five years, passing a citizenship test, and demonstrating language ability. Canada officially adopted multiculturalism as policy in 1971, and the Canadian Multiculturalism Act of 1988 entrenched it in law.
