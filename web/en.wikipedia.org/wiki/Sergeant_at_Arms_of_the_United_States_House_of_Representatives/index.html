Sergeant at Arms of the United States House of Representatives

The Sergeant at Arms of the United States House of Representatives is an officer of the House, elected by the members at the start of each Congress, who serves as the chamber's chief law enforcement and protocol officer. The office was established on April 14, 1789, when the First Congress created the post; Joseph Wheaton was the first to hold it.

Duties

The Sergeant at Arms maintains order in the House chamber under the direction of the Speaker, and may be directed to compel the attendance of absent members — including by arrest — to establish a quorum. The officer is responsible for the security of members and the Capitol complex in coordination with the United States Capitol Police, serving as a member of the Capitol Police Board, and oversees House garages, parking, and identification services. Protocol duties include escorting the President and foreign dignitaries on formal occasions.

The Mace

The ceremonial symbol of the office is the Mace of the United States House of Representatives, a bundle of ebony rods bound with silver and topped by a globe and eagle. The Sergeant at Arms, or a deputy, places the Mace on its pedestal beside the Speaker's rostrum when the House is in session, and carries it before the Speaker in procession. In disorderly moments, presenting the Mace before unruly members traditionally signals them to return to order.

History

The office was modelled on the sergeants-at-arms of British parliamentary tradition. In 1858 the Sergeant at Arms intervened in a brawl on the House floor by presenting the Mace. Following the January 6, 2021 Capitol attack, the acting Sergeant at Arms was replaced, and the office's security role received renewed attention.
