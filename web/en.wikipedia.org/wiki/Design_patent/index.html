In United States patent law, a design patent protects the ornamental design — the visual appearance — of an article of manufacture, as distinct from a utility patent, which protects how an invention works. Design patents are defined by their drawings rather than by written claims, and are denoted by a "D" prefix in the patent number.

Scope and requirements

To be patentable, a design must be new, original, ornamental and non-obvious, and must be embodied in or applied to a manufactured article. Pure surface ornamentation divorced from an article, and designs dictated solely by function, are excluded. A design patent gives its owner the right to exclude others from making, using, selling or importing the design for 15 years from the date of grant, with no maintenance fees. Infringement is judged by the "ordinary observer" test: whether an ordinary observer would be deceived into buying the accused product believing it to be the patented design.

Notable cases

Design patents featured prominently in the smartphone wars: in Apple v. Samsung, a jury found Samsung infringed Apple's iPhone design patents, and the US Supreme Court's 2016 decision clarified that damages could be based on the relevant "article of manufacture" rather than the whole product. Other countries protect equivalent rights as registered designs; in the EU, a registered Community design lasts up to 25 years. Design rights complement copyright, such as the moral rights protected under the Visual Artists Rights Act.
