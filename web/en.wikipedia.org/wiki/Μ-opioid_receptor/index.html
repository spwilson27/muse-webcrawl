Μ-opioid receptor

GeneOPRM1
ClassG protein-coupled receptor
Endogenous ligandsβ-endorphin, endomorphins, enkephalins
Key agonistsMorphine, fentanyl, methadone
Key antagonistsNaloxone, naltrexone

The μ-opioid receptor (MOR) is a protein that in humans is encoded by the OPRM1 gene. It is the primary site of action of most opioid analgesics, including morphine, and the main receptor through which opioids produce analgesia, euphoria, physical dependence, and respiratory depression.

Structure and signalling

Like the other opioid receptors (δ, κ, and the nociceptin receptor), MOR is a G protein-coupled receptor with seven transmembrane helices. Activation couples to inhibitory Gi/o proteins, reducing cyclic AMP production, opening inwardly rectifying potassium channels, and closing voltage-gated calcium channels, thereby suppressing neuronal excitability and transmitter release. Crystal and cryo-EM structures of the receptor have informed the design of new analgesics.

Endogenous ligands

The body produces its own MOR agonists: β-endorphin and the endomorphins (endomorphin-1 and -2) bind with high affinity and selectivity, while the enkephalins act mainly at δ- and μ-receptors. These peptides modulate pain, reward, stress, and feeding.

Pharmacology

Exogenous agonists include morphine, codeine, oxycodone, fentanyl and its analogues, methadone, and tramadol. Heroin crosses into the brain and is converted to 6-monoacetylmorphine and morphine. The antagonists naloxone and naltrexone competitively block MOR and are used to reverse opioid overdose and to treat opioid and alcohol dependence. Partial agonists such as buprenorphine are used in substitution therapy.

Subtypes and biased agonism

Pharmacological studies long suggested μ1, μ2, and μ3 subtypes, now attributed largely to splice variants of a single gene. Research into "biased" agonists — compounds that preferentially activate G-protein signalling over β-arrestin recruitment, such as oliceridine — seeks analgesia with fewer adverse effects.
