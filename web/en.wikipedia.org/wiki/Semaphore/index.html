Semaphore

Semaphore (from Greek sēma, "sign", and -phoros, "bearer") is the use of an apparatus to create a visual signal transmitted over distance. The term covers several related signalling systems: the optical telegraph networks of the 1790s–1850s, flag signalling by sailors, and railway signalling arms. The word is also used in computing for a synchronisation primitive.

Optical telegraph

Claude Chappe built the first successful optical telegraph network in France from 1792: lines of towers with pivoting wooden arms whose positions encoded letters and symbols. The Paris–Lille line (1794) carried the first messages, and France eventually operated more than 500 stations. Similar systems were adopted in Sweden, Britain and elsewhere until electric telegraphy displaced them in the mid-19th century.

Flag semaphore

Flag semaphore uses two hand-held flags, extended at various angles to represent letters and numerals. Developed in the 19th century from earlier naval flag systems, it remains in use by navies and in life-saving contexts where radio silence or simplicity is required.

Railway semaphore signals

Railway semaphore signals use a pivoting arm whose angle indicates stop, caution or proceed. Widely installed from the 1840s, they have largely been replaced by colour-light signals on main lines but survive on heritage and some secondary routes.

In computing

In concurrent programming, a semaphore is a variable or abstract data type used to control access by multiple processes to a common resource in a parallel programming environment, proposed by Edsger W. Dijkstra in the 1960s with operations traditionally named P and V.
