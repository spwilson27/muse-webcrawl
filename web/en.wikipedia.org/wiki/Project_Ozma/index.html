Project Ozma was the first modern experiment in the search for extraterrestrial intelligence (SETI). Conducted by astronomer Frank Drake at the National Radio Astronomy Observatory in Green Bank, West Virginia, in 1960, it listened for artificial radio signals from two nearby Sun-like stars. Although it detected no alien transmissions, Ozma established the template for all subsequent SETI searches and helped launch the scientific discipline.

Background

In 1959, physicists Giuseppe Cocconi and Philip Morrison published a paper in Nature arguing that radio telescopes had become sensitive enough to detect transmissions from civilisations around other stars, and suggesting the 1420 MHz emission line of neutral hydrogen — the "21-centimetre line" — as a natural, universally obvious frequency at which to listen. Independently, Drake had reached the same conclusion and was already preparing such a search. He named the project after Princess Ozma, ruler of the fictional Land of Oz in L. Frank Baum's books, which he described as "a place very far away, difficult to reach, and populated by strange and exotic beings".

The experiment

Drake used the observatory's new 85-foot (26-metre) Howard E. Tatel telescope, fitted with a single-channel receiver of 100 Hz bandwidth that he scanned slowly around 1420 MHz. He chose two target stars, Tau Ceti and Epsilon Eridani, each about 11 light-years away and similar to the Sun. Observations began on 8 April 1960 and accumulated roughly 150 hours over about four months, alternating between the two stars. The output was recorded on chart paper and monitored for regular, pulsed signals unlike natural sources.

Early in the run a strong, regular signal caused brief excitement, but it was traced to a high-flying military aircraft — a U-2 spy plane. No other candidate signals were found.

Legacy

Despite its null result, Project Ozma demonstrated that a dedicated search for interstellar signals was feasible and attracted lasting scientific and public interest. Drake went on to organise the first SETI conference at Green Bank in 1961, for which he devised the Drake equation estimating the number of communicative civilisations in the Milky Way. A follow-up, Ozma II, was conducted at Green Bank in the 1970s by Patrick Palmer and Benjamin Zuckerman, who examined some 650 nearby stars. The fictional name also inspired the later "Project Ozma" references in SETI literature and the naming of the SETI Institute's Ozma-related initiatives.
