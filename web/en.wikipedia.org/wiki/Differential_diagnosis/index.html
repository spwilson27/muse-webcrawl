Differential diagnosis

In healthcare, a differential diagnosis (DDx) is a method of analysis that distinguishes a particular disease or condition from others that present with similar clinical features. Differential diagnostic procedures are used by clinicians to diagnose the specific disease in a patient, or, in many cases, to eliminate potentially life-threatening conditions. It involves distinguishing a particular disease or condition from others with similar clinical features, drawing on the history, physical examination, and investigations.

Method

A common approach is to generate a list of possible diagnoses — the "differential" — then gather information to support or refute each candidate. Clinicians often organise candidate causes by anatomical system, by mechanism (for example with mnemonics such as VINDICATE), or by probability combined with seriousness: conditions that are common, and conditions that are dangerous, are considered first. Investigations are then chosen to discriminate between the remaining candidates, with Bayes' theorem providing a formal basis for updating probabilities as results arrive.

Probabilistic reasoning

The process is inherently probabilistic. Pre-test probability, based on prevalence and the patient's presentation, is modified by the sensitivity and specificity of each symptom, sign, or test to give a post-test probability. A diagnosis may be accepted when its probability passes a treatment threshold, or excluded when it falls below a testing threshold.

Computer-aided diagnosis

Computer systems such as DXplain, Isabel, and more recent large-language-model tools can generate differential diagnoses from entered findings. Studies suggest such systems can broaden the differential and reduce premature closure, a common cognitive cause of diagnostic error — the tendency to settle on a diagnosis too early and stop considering alternatives.

Usage outside medicine

The term is also used more generally for processes of discriminating between alternative explanations — for example in fault diagnosis in engineering, in plant pathology, and, metaphorically, in literary and historical analysis.
