Information hiding

In computer science, information hiding is the principle of segregating the design decisions in a computer program that are most likely to change, thus protecting other parts of the program from extensive modification if the design decision is changed. The protection involves providing a stable interface which shields the remainder of the program from the implementation — the details that are most likely to change.

Origins

The concept was articulated by David Parnas in his influential 1972 paper "On the Criteria to Be Used in Decomposing Systems into Modules", which argued that modules should be defined around difficult design decisions rather than around steps of processing. Information hiding underlies the later ideas of encapsulation in object-oriented programming, abstract data types, and the distinction between interface and implementation in software engineering.

Benefits

By hiding internal representations — for example, how a data structure is stored — a module can be rewritten without affecting its clients, localising change, reducing coupling and making programs easier to understand, test and maintain. Most modern programming languages provide access-control mechanisms (such as private and public members) to enforce information hiding. The term "information hiding" is also used in security contexts for techniques such as steganography, which conceal the very existence of a message; that sense is distinct from the software-design principle.
