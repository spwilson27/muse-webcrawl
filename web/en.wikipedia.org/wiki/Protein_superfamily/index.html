Protein superfamily

Field: Molecular biology, bioinformatics

Classification resources: SCOP, CATH, SUPERFAMILY, Pfam

A protein superfamily is the largest grouping (clade) of proteins for which common ancestry can be inferred (see homology). Usually this common ancestry is inferred from structural alignment and mechanistic similarity, even though no sequence similarity is evident. Sequence homology can then be deduced even if not readily apparent, because of the short evolutionary timescales involved relative to the rate of structural change.

Definition and identification

Superfamilies typically contain several protein families that show sequence similarity within each family. The term "clan" is commonly used for protease superfamilies in the MEROPS classification. Superfamily membership is usually established by hidden Markov models, profile comparisons, and structural classification in databases such as SCOP and CATH; the SUPERFAMILY database assigns genomes to proteins of known structure using such models.

Evolutionary significance

Superfamilies demonstrate that protein structure is far more conserved than sequence: members may share less than 15 percent sequence identity while retaining the same fold and key functional residues. They often encompass proteins with differing or related functions descended from a common ancestor, so superfamily analysis informs predictions of protein function and the reconstruction of early protein evolution. Examples include the immunoglobulin superfamily, the globin superfamily, and the TIM barrel superfamilies.
