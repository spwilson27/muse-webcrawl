Mycoplasma pneumoniae

Scientific classification

Domain: Bacteria

Phylum: Mycoplasmatota

Class: Mollicutes

Genus: Mycoplasma

Species: M. pneumoniae

Mycoplasma pneumoniae cells attached to respiratory epithelium.

Mycoplasma pneumoniae is a very small bacterium of the class Mollicutes that causes the respiratory illness often called "walking pneumonia" — a common form of atypical community-acquired pneumonia — as well as tracheobronchitis and pharyngitis. It is unusual among bacteria in lacking a cell wall, which makes it intrinsically resistant to beta-lactam antibiotics such as penicillins, and gives its cells a flexible, pear-like shape with a specialised attachment tip.

Biology

With a genome of only about 816 kilobase pairs and some 690 genes, M. pneumoniae was among the first organisms to have its genome fully sequenced (1996). Because it has lost many biosynthetic pathways, it depends on its host for nutrients, including sterols: its cell membrane uniquely contains cholesterol taken up from the environment. The bacterium attaches to ciliated respiratory epithelial cells via the P1 adhesin at its tip organelle and produces hydrogen peroxide and the CARDS toxin, damaging the airway lining.

Disease and treatment

Infections occur worldwide, with epidemics roughly every 3–7 years, and are most common in school-age children and young adults in close-contact settings such as dormitories and military barracks. Illness typically develops slowly with fever, headache, malaise and a persistent dry cough; most cases are mild enough that patients remain ambulant ("walking pneumonia"), though severe pneumonia and extrapulmonary complications — rash, haemolytic anaemia, encephalitis — can occur. Diagnosis is by PCR of respiratory samples or serology. Treatment uses macrolides (such as azithromycin), tetracyclines or respiratory fluoroquinolones; rising macrolide resistance, especially in East Asia, is a concern. See also atypical pneumonia.
