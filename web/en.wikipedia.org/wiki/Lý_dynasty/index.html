The Lý dynasty (Vietnamese: nhà Lý; 1009–1225) was a Vietnamese dynasty that ruled the kingdom of Đại Việt for over two centuries. It is regarded as one of the most prosperous and formative periods in Vietnamese history, consolidating independence from China and laying down enduring institutions of government, Buddhism and education.

Founding

The dynasty was founded by Lý Công Uẩn, a palace guard commander of peasant origin, who ascended the throne in 1009 as Emperor Lý Thái Tổ after the death of the last Lê ruler. In 1010 he issued the Edict on the Transfer of the Capital (Chiếu dời đô) and moved the capital from Hoa Lư to Đại La, renamed Thăng Long ("Ascending Dragon") — modern Hanoi.

Government and culture

The Lý emperors organised a centralised bureaucracy modelled partly on Chinese practice while preserving a distinct Vietnamese identity. In 1070 the Temple of Literature (Văn Miếu) was built in Thăng Long; the first imperial examinations were held from 1075, and the Quốc Tử Giám academy (1076) is considered Vietnam's first university. Mahayana Buddhism flourished under royal patronage, producing landmarks such as the One Pillar Pagoda (1049) and the Báo Thiên tower.

Foreign relations

Đại Việt under the Lý successfully defended itself against the Song dynasty: in 1075–1077 General Lý Thường Kiệt first struck into Song territory and then defeated the Song invasion at the Như Nguyệt River (Cầu River), a victory celebrated in the poem "Nam quốc sơn hà," often called Vietnam's first declaration of independence. The dynasty also fought Champa to the south and expanded gradually southward.

Decline and fall

From the late 12th century the court weakened through child emperors and clan factionalism, with the Trần family rising as kingmakers. In 1225 the last ruler, the child empress Lý Chiêu Hoàng, was made to abdicate in favour of her husband Trần Cảnh, founding the Trần dynasty, which continued many Lý institutions.
