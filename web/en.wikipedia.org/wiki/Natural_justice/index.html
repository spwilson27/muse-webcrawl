Natural justice

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Natural justice is the rule of law doctrine that decision-makers must act fairly, comprising two classical principles: audi alteram partem (hear the other side — no one should be condemned unheard) and nemo judex in causa sua (no one should be a judge in their own cause — the rule against bias). In common-law systems it governs administrative and disciplinary decisions affecting rights or legitimate expectations, and underlies the modern duty to act fairly and give reasons in appropriate cases.

The doctrine was revitalised in England by Ridge v Baldwin (1964), which restored natural justice as a general check on administrative power after a period of narrow readings. Its content is flexible — what fairness requires depends on context, from a full oral hearing for dismissal from office to written representations for lesser decisions. Apparent bias, not only actual bias, disqualifies: justice must be seen to be done. In the United States the corresponding protections are constitutionalised in the due-process clauses, and in European law, in the right to good administration and the European Convention's fair-trial guarantees.
