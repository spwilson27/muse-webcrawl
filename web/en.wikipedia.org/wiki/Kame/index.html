Kame

A kame, or knob, is a glacial landform: an irregularly shaped hill or mound composed of sand, gravel and till that accumulates in a depression on a retreating glacier and is then deposited on the land surface as the ice melts. Kames are a characteristic feature of deglaciated terrain, often occurring alongside eskers, kettle lakes, and drumlins in a "kame and kettle" topography.

Formation

As a glacier thins and stagnates, meltwater collects sediment in cavities on or within the ice, in supraglacial ponds, or in crevasses and moulins filled with debris. When the supporting ice melts away, the sediment slumps down into a steep-sided mound whose original bedding is commonly distorted by collapse. Kames may also form as alluvial fans or deltas built by meltwater streams into ice-dammed lakes at the glacier margin; such kame deltas preserve flat tops at the former lake level.

Characteristics

Kames range from a few metres to several tens of metres high. Their stratified, sorted sediments distinguish them from drumlins and chaotic glacial till of moraines, and reflect their meltwater origin. Because they consist of loose granular material, kames are commonly quarried for sand and gravel, which threatens the survival of many examples.

Examples

Notable kames include the Fonthill Kame in Ontario, Canada, the kames of the Minooka area in Illinois, and numerous examples in Scotland — the word itself derives from the Scots kaim, meaning a comb or steep ridge. See also esker and kettle.
