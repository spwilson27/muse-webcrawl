Orientalism

Orientalism is a term with several interrelated meanings. In art history it denotes the depiction of subjects from the Middle East, North Africa, and Asia in 19th-century European art. In scholarship it historically referred to the academic study of Eastern languages and cultures. Since the publication of Edward Said's Orientalism (1978), the term has most commonly referred to the Western tradition of prejudiced, outsider interpretations of the Eastern world — Asia, North Africa, and the Middle East — which Said argued served and justified European and American political and cultural dominance.

Said's Orientalism (1978)

In Orientalism, the Palestinian-American scholar Edward Said argued that Western scholarship, literature, and art produced a largely fabricated image of "the Orient" as irrational, exotic, backward, and feminine, in contrast to a rational, progressive, masculine West. Drawing on Michel Foucault's concept of discourse, Said contended that this body of knowledge was inseparable from imperial power: to know the Orient was to exercise authority over it. The book became a founding text of postcolonial studies.

Orientalism in art

Orientalist painting flourished especially in 19th-century France and Britain. Artists such as Jean-Léon Gérôme, Eugène Delacroix, and John Frederick Lewis depicted harems, slave markets, desert landscapes, and scenes of prayer, often with meticulous ethnographic detail, though many painters never visited the regions they depicted. Related movements appeared in architecture, music, and literature.

Academic oriental studies

Before Said, "Orientalism" neutrally described the scholarly discipline of studying Eastern languages, literatures, and religions, institutionalised in bodies such as the Asiatic Society (founded 1784) and university chairs of Oriental studies. Scholars including William Jones made foundational contributions to comparative linguistics and translation.

Criticism

Said's thesis has been widely debated. Critics such as Bernard Lewis accused him of selective evidence and of ignoring German and Russian oriental scholarship that lacked an imperial project in the region; others faulted the book for homogenising "the West". Supporters regard it as a landmark critique of the politics of knowledge.
