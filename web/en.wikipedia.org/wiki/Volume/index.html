Volume is a measure of the amount of three-dimensional space occupied by an object or enclosed within a container. It is quantified numerically using SI derived units, principally the cubic metre (m³). The volume of a container is generally understood to be its capacity — the amount of fluid (gas or liquid) it can hold — rather than the amount of space the container's material itself displaces.

Units

One litre is defined as the volume of a cube 10 centimetres on a side (1 dm³ = 1000 cm³ = 0.001 m³). Imperial and US customary units include the fluid ounce, pint, quart, and gallon. Traditional units such as the bushel, peck, and hogshead were historically used for dry goods and liquids. In cooking, volume is often measured with cups and spoons.

Formulas

Standard formulas include: cube, V = a³; cuboid, V = lwh; sphere, V = (4/3)πr³; cylinder, V = πr²h; cone, V = (1/3)πr²h. For irregular solids, volume can be measured by fluid displacement — the principle behind Archimedes' famous "Eureka" insight — or, in calculus, by integrating cross-sectional area along an axis: V = ∫ A(x) dx. In three-dimensional calculus, volume is the triple integral of the constant function 1 over a region.

Measurement

Laboratory glassware — graduated cylinders, burettes, volumetric flasks, and pipettes — measures liquid volumes with varying precision. Gas volumes depend on temperature and pressure and are typically reported at standard conditions. Flow meters integrate flow rate over time to measure cumulative volume.

Volume in thermodynamics and geometry

In thermodynamics, volume is a fundamental state variable of a system, conjugate to pressure. In differential geometry, a volume form generalizes the concept to curved manifolds, enabling integration over spaces without a natural Euclidean structure.
