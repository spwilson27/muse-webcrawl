Oromo migrations

The Oromo migrations were a series of expansions in the 16th and 17th centuries by the Oromo people, a Cushitic ethnic group of the Horn of Africa, from their historical homeland around the lakes of southern Ethiopia (particularly around Lake Abaya and the Bale region) northward and eastward into much of what is now Ethiopia and northern Kenya. The migrations were among the most consequential demographic movements in the region's history and reshaped the ethnic and political map of the Horn.

Background

The Oromo practised a pastoralist way of life organised through the gadaa system, an age-set system in which male age-grades advanced through ten-year periods and elected leaders (luba) periodically. Historians debate the triggers of the expansion: population pressure, drought, the search for grazing land, and the weakening of both the Ethiopian Empire and the Adal Sultanate after the devastating Ethiopian–Adal War (1529–1543) all played roles.

The expansion

Beginning in earnest around the 1520s–1540s, Oromo groups moved in waves into the Shawa plateau, Hararghe, Wollo, Gojjam and the Rift Valley lakes region, as well as south into northern Kenya. The movement was not a single invasion but a generations-long process of migration, warfare, settlement and assimilation, in which Oromo groups absorbed—and were absorbed by—neighbouring peoples. By the 18th century, Oromo dynasties such as the Yejju ruled much of northern Ethiopia, and during the Zemene Mesafint (Era of the Princes, 1769–1855) Oromo warlords dominated imperial politics.

Sources

The principal contemporary written sources are Ethiopian royal chronicles and the account of the monk Abba Bahrey, whose Zenahu la Galla (1593) described the migrations with alarm; modern scholarship has balanced these hostile sources with Oromo oral traditions and linguistics.

Legacy

The Oromo are today the largest ethnic group in Ethiopia, and Oromo language and culture extend across Oromia and into Kenya. The migrations remain a subject of scholarly debate and political significance in modern Ethiopia.
