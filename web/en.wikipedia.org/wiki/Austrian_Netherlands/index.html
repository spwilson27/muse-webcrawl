The Austrian Netherlands was the territory of the Southern Netherlands ruled by the Austrian Habsburgs from the end of the War of the Spanish Succession until the French Revolutionary Wars. It comprised most of modern Belgium and Luxembourg, with its capital at Brussels.

Acquisition

Under the Treaty of Rastatt (1714) and the Barrier Treaties, the former Spanish Netherlands passed to Emperor Charles VI. Dutch garrisons were maintained in a chain of barrier fortresses against France, a humiliation resented locally. Austrian rule was exercised through a governor-general — most notably Prince Charles Alexander of Lorraine — and a minister plenipotentiary in Brussels, subject to the Supreme Council of the Netherlands in Vienna.

Reform and revolt

The later eighteenth century saw a period of economic revival and administrative reform. Emperor Joseph II's sweeping programme of religious and judicial reform after 1781 — including edicts of toleration and the abolition of contemplative religious orders — provoked fierce conservative resistance. Opposition coalesced in the Brabant Revolution of 1789, which drove out the Austrians and proclaimed the short-lived United Belgian States in January 1790. Imperial authority was restored later that year, but Joseph's successor Leopold II had to promise respect for provincial privileges.

French conquest

French revolutionary armies first invaded in 1792, were repelled in 1793, and returned decisively after the Battle of Fleurus in June 1794. France formally annexed the territory in 1795, ending the Austrian Netherlands and reorganising it into French departments — a settlement confirmed by the Treaty of Campo-Formio (1797).
