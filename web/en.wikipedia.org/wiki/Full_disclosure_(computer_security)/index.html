Full disclosure (computer security)

In computer security, full disclosure is the practice of making the details of a software vulnerability public — often including proof-of-concept exploit code — rather than keeping them private or disclosing them only to the vendor. The philosophy is that public information allows users to protect themselves and pressures vendors to fix flaws quickly.

History

The movement grew out of early hacker mailing lists, most famously Bugtraq (founded 1993), and later the Full Disclosure mailing list (2002), which gave the practice its name. Full disclosure arose partly as a reaction against "security through obscurity" and against vendors perceived to ignore or delay fixing reported bugs.

Debate

Supporters argue that secrecy benefits attackers, who may already know the flaw, more than defenders, and that openness equalises knowledge. Critics counter that publishing exploit code before a patch exists exposes users to attack, and favour coordinated (responsible) disclosure, in which the finder privately notifies the vendor and agrees an embargo period before publication. In practice, much of the industry has settled on coordinated disclosure with time limits — after which full disclosure is threatened or carried out — as a compromise between the two camps.
