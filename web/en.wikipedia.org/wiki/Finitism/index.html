Finitism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Finitism accepts only mathematical objects that can be constructed in finitely many steps.

Finitism is a philosophy of mathematics that accepts the existence only of finite mathematical objects. It is best understood in comparison to the mainstream philosophy of mathematics, in which infinite mathematical objects (such as infinite sets) are accepted as legitimate objects that exist.

Main idea

The main idea behind finitism is that infinity does not exist as a completed totality. A mathematical statement is meaningful only if it can, in principle, be verified by a finite construction or computation. Leopold Kronecker was an early advocate, famously holding that the natural numbers were given directly while other mathematical objects must be built from them in finitely many steps.

Hilbert's program

David Hilbert adopted a finitist standpoint as the secure foundation for his program to justify classical (infinitary) mathematics: infinitary methods were to be treated as a useful formal game whose consistency would be proved by finitary means. Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems of 1931 showed that this goal, as originally conceived, cannot be achieved.

Ultrafinitism

A stricter variant, ultrafinitism (or ultraintuitionism), rejects even some finite objects whose construction would require more steps than are physically feasible, such as very large numbers that cannot actually be written down.
