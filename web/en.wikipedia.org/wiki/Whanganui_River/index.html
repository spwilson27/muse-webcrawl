The Whanganui River is a major river in the North Island of New Zealand. It is the country's longest navigable river and the third-longest overall, running 290 kilometres (180 mi) from the northern slopes of Mount Tongariro to the city of Whanganui on the Tasman Sea coast, much of it through the isolated forested country of Whanganui National Park.

History and significance

The river is of immense spiritual and cultural importance to Māori, particularly the iwi of Whanganui, who regard it as a living ancestor. The relationship is captured in the saying "Ko au te awa, ko te awa ko au" — "I am the river and the river is me." In the 19th century, paddle steamers carried passengers and trade upriver, and the road to Pipiriki was a major tourist route.

Legal personhood

In March 2017, New Zealand passed the Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River Claims Settlement) Act, granting the river legal personhood — it became one of the first natural features in the world to be recognized in law as a legal person with rights and interests of its own, represented by two guardians, one appointed by the Crown and one by iwi. The settlement resolved the longest-running litigation in New Zealand's history, dating to claims first brought in the 1870s.
