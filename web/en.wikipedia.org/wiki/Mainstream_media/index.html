Mainstream media

Mainstream media
Related termsMass media, legacy media, MSM
Contrasted withAlternative media, independent media

Mainstream media (MSM) is a term used to refer to the various large mass news media that influence many people and both reflect and shape prevailing currents of thought. The term is used to contrast with alternative media. Large news conglomerates, including newspapers and broadcast media, which underwent successive mergers in many countries during the late 20th and early 21st centuries, are often grouped under the label.

Usage and criticism

In the United States the term is commonly applied to outlets such as major television networks, national newspapers and wire services. The phrase is sometimes used in a pejorative sense by critics who allege that mainstream outlets share an ideological bias or serve corporate and government interests. Media scholar Noam Chomsky, with Edward S. Herman, developed the propaganda model in Manufacturing Consent (1988), arguing that mass media operate as a system of propaganda shaped by ownership, advertising, sourcing, flak and ideology. Defenders of mainstream journalism respond that professional norms, editorial standards and competition provide checks against bias.

Decline and fragmentation

Since the 2000s, audiences have fragmented with the rise of cable news, online media and social media platforms, reducing the dominance once held by a small number of broadcasters and broadsheet newspapers. Scholars debate whether this fragmentation has produced greater pluralism or increased political polarization and misinformation.
