Hyperpolarization (biology)

Hyperpolarization is a change in a cell's membrane potential that makes it more negative. It is the opposite of depolarization: it inhibits action potentials by increasing the stimulus required to move the membrane potential to the action potential threshold.

Hyperpolarization (c) follows the repolarization phase of an action potential.

Mechanism

Hyperpolarization is often caused by efflux of K+ (potassium ions) through potassium channels, or influx of Cl− (chloride ions) through chloride channels. Conversely, inhibition of cation influx, or increased efflux of Na+, can also hyperpolarize the membrane. During the action potential, the opening of voltage-gated potassium channels causes a potassium efflux that drives the membrane potential below its resting value — the afterhyperpolarization — contributing to the refractory period.

Role in signalling

Inhibitory postsynaptic potentials (IPSPs) hyperpolarize postsynaptic neurons, for example through GABAA receptors opening chloride channels, making the neuron less likely to fire. In photoreceptor cells of the retina, light causes hyperpolarization (closing of cyclic-nucleotide-gated channels), which is the signal transmitted to downstream retinal neurons — an unusual arrangement in which the sensory receptor is depolarized in the dark and hyperpolarizes in response to its stimulus.

Experimental techniques

Hyperpolarization can be induced experimentally by current injection through a patch-clamp electrode, and hyperpolarization-activated currents (Ih, carried by HCN channels) produce the characteristic "sag" seen in many neurons and cardiac pacemaker cells.
