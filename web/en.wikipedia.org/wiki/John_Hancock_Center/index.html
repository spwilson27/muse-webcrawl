The John Hancock Center, officially renamed 875 North Michigan Avenue in 2018, is a 100-story, 1,128-foot (344 m) supertall skyscraper located in the Magnificent Mile district of Chicago, Illinois. Completed in 1970, it was the second-tallest building in the world at the time, after the Empire State Building. The tower is famous for its distinctive X-braced exterior structure, which made its tapered, trussed-tube design one of the most celebrated achievements of structural engineering.

Design and construction

The building was designed by architect Bruce Graham and structural engineer Fazlur Rahman Khan of Skidmore, Owings & Merrill. Its enormous diagonal cross-braces are not merely decorative: they tie the exterior columns together into a rigid tube, allowing the tower to resist wind loads with far less steel than a conventional frame. The black anodized aluminum cladding and bronze-tinted windows give the building its characteristic dark, monolithic appearance. Construction began in 1965 under developer Jerry Wolman, whose financial difficulties forced a takeover by the John Hancock Mutual Life Insurance Company, from which the building took its name.

Uses

The tower is a mixed-use building. Its lower floors hold offices, retail, and parking, while the upper floors contain roughly 700 condominiums — making it one of the highest residential buildings in the world. The 94th floor houses the 360 Chicago observation deck, which features the TILT attraction, a moving platform that tilts visitors outward over Michigan Avenue. The 95th and 96th floors formerly hosted the Signature Room restaurant, which closed in 2023. The building's antennas reach 1,500 feet (457 m), raising its total height well above the roofline.

Name change

In February 2018, the John Hancock insurance company asked that its name and signage be removed, and the building was officially renamed 875 North Michigan Avenue, though Chicagoans persist in calling it the "Hancock" or "Big John."

In popular culture

The building appears in films including Poltergeist III (1988) and is a regular feature of Chicago skyline photography. Its silhouette is often used as a symbol of the city alongside the Willis Tower.
