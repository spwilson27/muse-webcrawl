Childcare, also known as day care, is the care and supervision of one or more children at a time, ranging in age from a few weeks old to adolescence. Childcare is provided by parents and family members, informal caregivers such as relatives and neighbours, and by paid professionals including daycare centres, nurseries, preschools, registered childminders, and nannies. Access to affordable, high-quality childcare is a major public policy issue in most developed countries because it supports parental (particularly maternal) employment and is linked to children's early development.

Types of childcare

Centre-based care (daycare centres, crèches, kindergartens) looks after groups of children in dedicated facilities, usually organised by age, and frequently integrates early childhood education. Home-based care includes care by nannies and au pairs in the child's own home and family day care or childminding in the caregiver's home. Informal care by grandparents and other relatives accounts for a large share of childcare worldwide. After-school care serves older children outside school hours.

Quality and effects

Research, including the large US NICHD Study of Early Child Care, indicates that high-quality childcare — characterised by low child-to-staff ratios, trained and stable caregivers, and stimulating interactions — is associated with modest benefits in language, cognitive, and school-readiness outcomes, with larger benefits for disadvantaged children. Policy interest focuses on affordability: childcare costs can rival housing costs for families, and many governments subsidise provision, as in the extensive public systems of the Nordic countries.

Regulation

Most jurisdictions license providers, setting maximum group sizes and ratios, staff qualifications, safety and hygiene standards, and inspection arrangements (for example by Ofsted in England). Requirements and public funding levels vary greatly between countries.
