Flak tower

Flak towers (German: Flaktürme) were eight complexes of large, above-ground, anti-aircraft gun blockhouses constructed by Nazi Germany in Berlin (three), Hamburg (two) and Vienna (three) from 1940 onwards during the Second World War. The towers, built in pairs — a gun tower (G-Turm) and a smaller fire-control tower (L-Turm) equipped with radar and rangefinders — mounted heavy anti-aircraft artillery, most notably twin 12.8 cm FlaK 40 guns, supported by smaller 2 cm and 3.7 cm weapons on lower platforms.

Design and use

Built of reinforced concrete with walls up to 3.5 metres thick, the towers were designed to be self-sufficient, with their own water and electricity supplies, a hospital, and storage. In addition to their anti-aircraft role, they served as air-raid shelters for tens of thousands of civilians and protected cultural treasures in storage rooms. During the Battle of Berlin in 1945, the towers served as strongpoints; the Zoo tower, for example, provided covering fire and sheltered thousands of soldiers and civilians, and proved so resistant that Soviet forces largely bypassed it until the city's surrender.

Post-war fate

The towers were so massive that demolition was extremely difficult: the British blew up the Zoo and Friedrichshain towers in Berlin (with only partial success in one case), and the Humboldthain tower was partly buried under rubble to form a hill in a park. The Hamburg towers were converted to other uses, one becoming an energy bunker. In Vienna all three pairs survive essentially intact; one gun tower houses an aquarium (the Haus des Meeres) and another is used as a contemporary art depot and climbing wall.
