Confessional

A confessional is a small, enclosed booth used for the sacrament of penance (confession) in the Roman Catholic Church, as well as in some Lutheran and Anglican churches. The penitent and the priest occupy separate compartments, communicating through a grille, which preserves the penitent's anonymity.

A traditional three-part wooden confessional; the priest sits in the centre, penitents kneel at either side.

Design and history

The modern confessional box is traditionally credited to St Charles Borromeo, Archbishop of Milan, who in the later sixteenth century prescribed enclosed confessionals with a fixed grille to safeguard both propriety and privacy, in keeping with the reforms of the Council of Trent. A standard confessional has a central compartment for the priest, entered through a door, flanked by two compartments for penitents, each entered through a curtain or half-door and furnished with a kneeler; a perforated screen separates priest from penitent. A small light or indicator may signal when the confessional is occupied.

Modern practice

Since the Second Vatican Council, many churches have added reconciliation rooms that allow face-to-face confession as an alternative to the anonymous grille, which canon law requires to remain available. Confessionals remain characteristic furnishings of Catholic churches worldwide, and the word "confessional" has given rise to wider usages, such as confessional poetry and confessional media.
