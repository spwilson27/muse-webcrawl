The llama (Lama glama) is a domesticated South American camelid, widely used as a meat and pack animal by Andean cultures since the pre-Columbian era. Llamas are social animals and live with others as a herd. Their wool is very soft, lanolin-free, and produced in a range of natural colours. They were domesticated from the wild guanaco in the central Andes roughly 4,000–5,000 years ago, and were central to the economy and religion of the Inca Empire.

A llama at Machu Picchu, Peru

Characteristics

A full-grown llama stands about 1.09–1.19 m at the shoulder and weighs 130–200 kg. Like other camelids, llamas have a three-chambered stomach, padded two-toed feet suited to rocky ground, and efficient metabolism adapted to high-altitude grazing. They communicate by humming, and use spitting — regurgitated stomach contents — mainly in disputes with other llamas over food or rank.

History and use

In Inca society, llamas provided transport across mountain terrain (a llama can carry 25–30% of its body weight over long distances), meat, wool, leather, and sacrificial offerings. The Spanish conquest drastically reduced their numbers, but they remained essential in Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, and Argentina. Today llamas are also raised in North America, Europe, and Australia for fibre, as pack animals for trekking, and as guard animals protecting sheep and alpacas from coyotes and dogs.

Related species

The llama's closest relatives are the alpaca (domesticated from the vicuña), the wild guanaco, and the vicuña. Hybrids with alpacas (huarizo) and camels (cama) have been produced.
