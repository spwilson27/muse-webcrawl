Gallic acid (also known as 3,4,5-trihydroxybenzoic acid) is a trihydroxybenzoic acid with the formula C6H2(OH)3CO2H. It is classified as a phenolic acid. It is found in gallnuts, sumac, witch hazel, tea leaves, oak bark, and other plants. It is a white solid, although samples are typically brown owing to partial oxidation. Salts and esters of gallic acid are termed "gallates".

History and occurrence

Gallic acid takes its name from oak galls, from which it was historically obtained; Carl Wilhelm Scheele isolated it in 1786 by fermenting gall extracts. It occurs free and as part of hydrolysable tannins (gallotannins); plants store it largely as esters such as theogallin and galloyl glucoses in tea, grapes, berries and pomegranate.

Uses

Gallic acid was a key component of iron gall ink and is used in the pharmaceutical and food industries as an antioxidant precursor — propyl gallate is a permitted food additive. It forms the building block of many derivatives under study for antimicrobial and anticancer activity, and is a standard reagent (the Folin–Ciocalteu assay) for measuring phenolic content. Pyrogallol is made by its decarboxylation.
