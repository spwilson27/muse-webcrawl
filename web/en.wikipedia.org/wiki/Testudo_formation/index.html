Testudo formation

The testudo formation (Latin: testūdō, "tortoise") was a type of shield wall formation commonly used by the Roman legions during battles, particularly sieges. In the testudo formation, the men would align their shields to form a front wall and a ceiling — a packed formation covered with shields on all sides and from above, resembling the shell of a tortoise.

Description

The soldiers in the first rank held their scuta (large curved shields) upright, edge to edge, to form a continuous front wall. The ranks behind held their shields above their heads, overlapping like roof tiles, protecting the formation from missiles thrown or shot from above. The men at the sides and rear likewise formed walls, so the whole unit was enclosed. The formation was very slow-moving but offered excellent protection against arrows, sling stones and other projectiles.

Front-line soldiers placed the lower edges of their shields on the ground and overlapped them with the shields of their neighbours, while the men in the middle raised their pila or shields overhead. Because the formation was so compact, it was primarily defensive; it was used to approach fortifications under missile fire during sieges, to cross open ground, and occasionally as a mobile ramp.

History and variants

Ancient authors including Livy, Plutarch, Cassius Dio and Vegetius describe the testudo. Mark Antony's legions used the formation during the Parthian campaign of 36 BC, when, according to Plutarch, soldiers knelt in the testudo to deflect arrows. A related device, the testudo arietaria, covered battering rams. Modern riot police and military units use similar overlapping-shield formations for crowd control, a direct descendant of the Roman tactic.
