Fecundity

Fecundity is defined in two ways: in human demography, it is the potential for reproduction of a recorded population as opposed to a sole organism, whereas in population ecology it is the actual reproductive rate of an organism or population — how many offspring are produced. In biology more generally it is the ability to produce an abundance of offspring or new growth.

In demography

Demographers distinguish fecundity — the physiological capability of a woman (or couple) to bear children — from fertility, the actual number of live births. A woman who can conceive but chooses not to, or uses contraception, is fecund but has low fertility. The distinction matters in studies of population growth, contraceptive effectiveness and reproductive health.

In population ecology

In ecology, fecundity is a key component of an organism's life history. Species range from those producing very few, well-provisioned offspring (low fecundity, as in elephants and humans) to those producing enormous numbers with little parental investment (high fecundity, as in many fish, plants and invertebrates). Fecundity schedules are used in life tables and matrix population models to project population change, and fecundity generally trades off against survival in theories of life-history evolution.
