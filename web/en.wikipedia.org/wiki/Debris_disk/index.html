Debris disk

A debris disk (American English), or debris disc (Commonwealth English), is a circumstellar disk of dust and debris in orbit around a star. Sometimes these disks contain prominent rings, as seen in images of Fomalhaut. Debris disks are found around stars with mature planetary systems, including at least one debris disk in orbit around an evolved neutron star. Debris disks can also be produced and maintained as the remnants of collisions between planetesimals.

By 2001, more than 900 candidate stars had been found to possess a debris disk. They are usually discovered by examining the star system in infrared light and looking for an excess of radiation beyond that emitted by the star. This excess is inferred to be radiation from the star that has been absorbed by the dust in the disk and re-radiated as infrared energy.

History of observation

The first debris disk was discovered in 1984 around the star Vega using the IRAS satellite, detected as an infrared excess. Initially believed to be a protoplanetary disk, it is now thought to be a debris disk because of the lack of gas in the disk. Subsequently, infrared excesses were found around the stars Fomalhaut and Beta Pictoris. In 1998 a debris disk was imaged around the nearby star Epsilon Eridani, containing a structure suggestive of a planet. The disk around Beta Pictoris was among the first imaged directly, revealing warps later attributed to a giant planet, Beta Pictoris b.

Composition and dynamics

Debris disks typically contain small dust grains (micrometre-sized) generated by collisions among larger bodies such as asteroids and comets, analogous to the Solar System's Kuiper belt and zodiacal dust. Because radiation pressure and Poynting–Robertson drag remove small grains quickly, the dust must be continuously replenished by ongoing collisions — the hallmark that distinguishes debris disks from gas-rich protoplanetary disks.

Known systems

Well-studied debris disks surround Vega, Fomalhaut, Beta Pictoris, Epsilon Eridani, Tau Ceti, and HR 8799, the latter also hosting directly imaged planets. Surveys by the Spitzer and Herschel space telescopes suggest that a substantial fraction of Sun-like stars host detectable debris.
