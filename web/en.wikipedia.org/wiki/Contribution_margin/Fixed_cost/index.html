Fixed cost

In economics, fixed costs, indirect costs or overheads are business expenses that are not dependent on the level of goods or services produced by the business. They tend to be recurring, such as interest or rents paid each month. These costs also tend to be capital related, such as the cost of the factory or machinery that produces the goods. This is in contrast to variable costs, which are volume-related (and are paid per quantity produced) and unknown at the beginning of the accounting year.

Characteristics

Examples of fixed costs include rent, insurance, property taxes, depreciation of equipment, and salaries of permanent staff. Fixed costs remain constant in total over a given period and within a relevant range of activity, so the fixed cost per unit falls as production increases — a spreading effect that gives rise to economies of scale. Over a long enough time horizon, however, nearly all costs become variable, since leases expire, staff contracts change and capacity can be expanded or reduced; costs that jump with capacity changes are called step costs or semi-fixed costs.

Role in analysis

The distinction between fixed and variable costs is central to cost accounting. It underlies break-even analysis, contribution margin calculations and decisions about pricing, outsourcing and shutdown points. A firm with a high proportion of fixed costs has high operating leverage: profits rise rapidly once sales exceed the break-even point, but losses also mount quickly when sales fall short.
