Break-even analysis

Break-even analysis in economics, business, and specifically cost accounting is a technique for determining the point at which total cost and total revenue are equal — the break-even point (BEP). At this point there is no net loss or gain, though opportunity costs have been paid and capital has received the risk-adjusted expected return. Businesses use the analysis to determine what level of sales is required to cover all costs, and to evaluate how changes in price, volume and cost structure affect profitability.

Calculation

The break-even point in units is calculated by dividing total fixed costs by the contribution margin per unit (selling price per unit minus variable cost per unit):

BEP (units) = Fixed costs ÷ (Price per unit − Variable cost per unit)

The break-even point in sales revenue can be found by dividing fixed costs by the contribution margin ratio. The margin of safety is the amount by which actual or expected sales exceed break-even sales, indicating how much sales can fall before the business begins to make a loss.

Assumptions and limitations

The basic analysis assumes that selling prices, fixed costs and variable costs per unit remain constant across the relevant range of output, and that everything produced is sold. In multi-product firms, an assumed constant sales mix is required. Because real costs are often semi-variable and prices change with volume, break-even analysis is regarded as a useful but simplified planning tool, often supplemented by sensitivity analysis.
