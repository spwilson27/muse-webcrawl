Operating leverage

Operating leverage is a measure of how revenue growth translates into growth in operating income. It is a measure of leverage, and of how risky, or volatile, a company's operating income is. A firm with high operating leverage has a relatively large proportion of fixed costs in its cost structure, so a small change in sales volume produces a large change in operating income.

Measurement

The degree of operating leverage (DOL) at a given level of sales is commonly defined as the percentage change in operating income divided by the percentage change in sales. Equivalently, it can be computed from the contribution margin:

DOL = Contribution margin ÷ Operating income

Because operating income equals the contribution margin minus fixed costs, the DOL rises as production approaches the break-even point and falls as sales grow beyond it.

Implications

High operating leverage magnifies both gains and losses: above break-even, profits grow faster than sales; below it, losses accumulate quickly. Industries with large fixed investments — airlines, hotels, utilities and capital-intensive manufacturing — typically display high operating leverage, while labour-variable businesses such as professional services display low operating leverage. Operating leverage is analysed alongside financial leverage to give the total leverage effect on earnings per share.
