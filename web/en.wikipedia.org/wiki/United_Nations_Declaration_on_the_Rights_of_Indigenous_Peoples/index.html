United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples

The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) is a declaration adopted by the United Nations General Assembly on 13 September 2007. It sets out individual and collective rights of Indigenous peoples, including rights to culture, identity, language, education, health, employment, lands and resources, and self-determination.

Adoption

The declaration resulted from more than two decades of negotiation among states and Indigenous representatives. It was adopted by 143 votes in favour, four against and eleven abstentions. Australia, Canada, New Zealand and the United States initially voted against, citing concerns about lands, resources, redress and free, prior and informed consent; all four later announced support for the declaration.

Contents

UNDRIP affirms that Indigenous peoples are equal to all other peoples and have the right to self-determination, autonomy in internal affairs and participation in decisions affecting them. It states that they should not be forcibly removed from their lands and that states should obtain free, prior and informed consent before adopting measures or projects affecting Indigenous lands and resources. Other provisions address conservation of traditional knowledge, spiritual traditions, education in Indigenous languages, media, development and protection from forced assimilation.

Status and influence

As a General Assembly declaration, UNDRIP is not by itself a binding treaty, but it has substantial moral, political and legal influence. It is used by courts, legislatures, treaty bodies and Indigenous organizations in interpreting human rights obligations. Some states have enacted implementation legislation or used it to shape consultation and resource-development policies. Debate continues over the practical meaning of consent, land redress and consistency with existing constitutional arrangements.
