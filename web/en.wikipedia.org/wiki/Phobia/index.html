Phobia

Phobia
SpecialtyPsychiatry, clinical psychology
SymptomsIntense, irrational fear of a specific object or situation; avoidance; panic symptoms
Usual onsetOften childhood or adolescence
TreatmentExposure therapy, cognitive behavioural therapy, medication

A phobia is an anxiety disorder defined by a persistent and excessive fear of an object or situation. Phobias typically result in a rapid onset of fear and persist for more than six months. Those affected go to great lengths to avoid the perceived danger, to a degree greater than the actual danger posed, and suffer significant distress or impairment as a result.

Classification

The DSM-5 classifies phobias into three broad groups. Specific phobias are fears of particular objects or situations, such as animals (e.g. arachnophobia, fear of spiders), heights (acrophobia), blood and injections, or flying. Social anxiety disorder (formerly social phobia) involves fear of social scrutiny and embarrassment. Agoraphobia is fear of situations from which escape might be difficult, such as crowds or open spaces. Specific phobias affect roughly 7–9% of people in a given year and are among the most common anxiety disorders.

Causes

Phobias arise from a combination of genetic vulnerability, temperament and experience. Many are learned through direct traumatic conditioning, observing others' fear, or receiving alarming information. Evolutionary preparedness may explain why fears of spiders, snakes, blood and heights are so much more common than fears of more dangerous modern objects.

Treatment

The treatment of choice is exposure therapy, particularly systematic desensitisation and in-vivo exposure, in which the person gradually and repeatedly confronts the feared stimulus until the fear response extinguishes. Cognitive behavioural therapy addresses the catastrophic thoughts that maintain avoidance. Medications such as SSRIs and short-term benzodiazepines are sometimes used, especially for social anxiety and agoraphobia.
