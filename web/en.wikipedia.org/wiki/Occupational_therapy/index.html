Occupational therapy (OT) is a healthcare profession concerned with enabling people of all ages to participate in the meaningful activities of daily life — their "occupations" — including self-care, work, education, play, and leisure. Occupational therapists work with individuals whose ability to participate is affected by injury, illness, disability, developmental conditions, ageing, or mental health problems. Interventions use purposeful activity, environmental adaptation, assistive technology, and skills training to promote health, independence, and quality of life.

An occupational therapist helping a patient relearn everyday tasks.

History

The roots of the profession lie in the moral treatment movement of the late 18th and 19th centuries, which used structured occupation in the care of people with mental illness. Occupational therapy emerged as a distinct profession in the early 20th century; the (US) National Society for the Promotion of Occupational Therapy was founded in 1917, and the profession expanded rapidly rehabilitating soldiers wounded in the First World War. It has since broadened from hospitals into schools, community services, workplaces, and social care.

Practice areas

  • Physical rehabilitation — after stroke, brain injury, surgery, or hand injury: restoring function, splinting, and retraining in activities of daily living (ADLs).
  • Paediatrics — supporting children's development, play, school participation, and sensory processing.
  • Mental health — building routines, coping strategies, and social participation.
  • Older people — falls prevention, home adaptations, dementia care, and maintaining independence.
  • Work and vocational rehabilitation — ergonomic assessment and return-to-work planning.

Process

Practice typically involves assessment of the person, their occupations, and their environment; collaborative goal-setting; intervention (activity-based therapy, adaptation of tasks or surroundings, provision of equipment such as wheelchairs and adaptive aids); and evaluation of outcomes using standardised measures. Therapists practise in multidisciplinary teams alongside physical therapists, speech-language pathologists, physicians, nurses, and social workers.

Education and regulation

Occupational therapists usually qualify with a bachelor's or master's degree in occupational therapy and must be licensed or registered in most jurisdictions. The World Federation of Occupational Therapists (founded 1952) sets international education standards.
