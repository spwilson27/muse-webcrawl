The Banu Qurayza (Arabic: بنو قريظة) were a Jewish tribe living in the south-eastern oasis settlements of Medina, allied in local politics with the Arab tribe of Aws. They were wealthy date farmers and possessed strong fortresses. They were the last of Medina's three major Jewish tribes to come into conflict with Muhammad after the expulsion of the Banu Qaynuqa (624) and the Banu Nadir (625).

Battle of the Trench and surrender

During the siege of Medina by a Meccan-led confederation in 627 (the Battle of the Trench), the Qurayza, after initial loyalty to their pact with Muhammad, entered negotiations with the besiegers — encouraged by the exiled Nadir leader Huyayy ibn Akhtab — though their actual military contribution remains debated in the sources. When the confederates withdrew, Muslim forces besieged the Qurayza for some 25 days until they surrendered.

Judgement and execution

The Qurayza agreed to accept the judgement of Sa'd ibn Mu'adh, a chief of their former Aws allies, who had been mortally wounded in the battle. Sa'd ruled, in the traditional account by the law of the Torah for a besieged enemy, that the fighting men be executed, the women and children enslaved, and the property divided. Traditional Muslim sources report between 600 and 900 men beheaded in the market of Medina; a few individuals were spared for conversion or intercession. The event is described in the Qur'an (33:26–27) and in the early biographies of Muhammad. Historians have long debated the scale and details: W. M. Watt accepted the broad outline, while others, such as M. J. Kister, defended the traditional account against revisionist arguments (notably Walid Arafat's) that the massacre was exaggerated or fabricated.
