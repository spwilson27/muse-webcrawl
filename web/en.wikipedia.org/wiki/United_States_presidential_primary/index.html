United States presidential primary

A United States presidential primary is the process by which political parties select their nominees for President. Voters choose delegates to national conventions through state primaries and caucuses held from winter to early summer of the election year.

History

Early nominees were chosen by congressional caucuses and later by conventions dominated by party leaders. Progressive-era reforms introduced direct primaries. After the chaotic 1968 Democratic convention, the McGovern-Fraser Commission shifted power to primary voters, a model Republicans also broadly adopted.

Process

States choose primary elections or caucuses, open, closed or semi-closed eligibility, and proportional or winner-take-all delegate allocation within party rules. Iowa caucuses and the New Hampshire primary traditionally open the calendar, followed by events such as Super Tuesday.

Invisible primary

Before voting begins, candidates compete for endorsements, donors and media attention in the so-called invisible primary.
