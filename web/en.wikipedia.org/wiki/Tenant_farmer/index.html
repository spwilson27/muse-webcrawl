Tenant farmer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tenant farmer works land owned by a landlord in return for rent or a share of the crop.

A tenant farmer is a person who resides on land owned by a landlord and farms that land in return for some form of payment. Tenant farming is an agricultural system in which farmers cultivate land that they do not own. In some arrangements the tenant pays a fixed cash rent; in others, known as sharecropping, the tenant pays a share of the crop produced.

Forms of tenancy

Tenancy arrangements vary widely. Cash tenants pay a fixed rent in money; share tenants pay a proportion of the harvest; and some tenants supply labour services to the landlord. Tenancies may be held for a fixed term, from year to year, or at will, and the security of tenure afforded to the tenant has historically been a major political question in many countries.

History

Tenant farming has existed since ancient times, including in the Roman Empire, where coloni worked estates owned by wealthy landowners. In medieval Europe, much agricultural land was worked by tenants owing rents and services to a lord. In Ireland, insecure and often exploitative tenancies provoked the Land War of the late 19th century and a series of Land Acts that eventually transferred ownership to the occupying tenants. In the post-Civil War American South, sharecropping became the dominant arrangement for both Black and white farmers, frequently trapping tenants in cycles of debt to furnishing merchants under the crop lien system.

Around the world

Tenant farming remains common in many parts of the world, including South Asia, where tenants may rent small plots from larger landowners, and in developed countries, where a substantial share of farmland is rented — in the United States roughly 40 percent of farmland is leased, often by large operating farmers expanding their acreage rather than by impoverished smallholders.
