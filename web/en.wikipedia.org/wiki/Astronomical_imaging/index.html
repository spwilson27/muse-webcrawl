Astronomical imaging

Long-exposure CCD images reveal objects far too faint for the eye.

Astronomical imaging is the recording of images of astronomical objects and phenomena, from the first daguerreotypes of the Moon in the 1840s to today's giant mosaic detectors on space telescopes. It transformed astronomy by permanently recording light, integrating faint signals over long exposures, and extending vision beyond the visible spectrum.

History

John William Draper photographed the Moon in 1840; his son Henry Draper obtained the first photograph of a nebula (Orion) in 1880. Photographic plates dominated for a century — enabling surveys such as the Palomar Observatory Sky Survey — before charge-coupled devices (CCDs), invented in 1969, replaced them in the 1980s–90s with far higher quantum efficiency and linear response.

Technique

Scientific imaging requires calibration frames (bias, dark and flat-field) to remove instrumental signatures, and data are stored in the FITS format. Ground-based imaging contends with atmospheric seeing, mitigated by adaptive optics and by "lucky imaging" — selecting the sharpest fraction of many short exposures. Narrowband filters isolate emission lines of hydrogen, oxygen and sulfur, famously combined in the Hubble palette. Amateurs practise astrophotography with cooled CMOS cameras and image stacking software.
