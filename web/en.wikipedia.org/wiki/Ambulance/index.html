Ambulance

An ambulance is a medically equipped vehicle which transports patients to treatment facilities, such as hospitals. Typically, out-of-hospital medical care is provided to the patient during transport. Ambulances are used to respond to medical emergencies by emergency medical services (EMS), and can rapidly transport paramedics and other first responders, carry equipment for administering emergency care, and transport patients to hospital or other definitive care. Most ambulances use a design based on vans or pickup trucks, though motorcycles, buses, aircraft, and boats also serve as ambulances.

History

The term ambulance comes from the Latin ambulare, "to walk or move about", a reference to early field hospitals that moved with armies. Horse-drawn wagons were used to carry the wounded from battlefields from the late 15th century, and organised ambulance services were developed during the Napoleonic Wars by Dominique Jean Larrey, whose "flying ambulances" treated and evacuated casualties during battle. Civilian hospital-based ambulance services began in the 1860s in cities such as Cincinnati and London. Motor ambulances appeared in the early 20th century, and the modern EMS model — bringing treatment to the patient as well as transporting them — developed after the 1960s.

Types and equipment

Road ambulances are commonly classed by capability: basic life support (BLS) units staffed by emergency medical technicians, and advanced life support (ALS) units staffed by paramedics who can administer drugs, interpret electrocardiograms, and manage airways. Equipment typically includes a stretcher, oxygen, defibrillator and cardiac monitor, suction unit, splints, medications, and communications equipment. Many vehicles display the Star of Life, a six-barred blue cross associated with emergency medical services.

Operation

Ambulance services may be run by fire departments, hospitals, municipal agencies, private companies, or charities. Crews navigate to calls dispatched by emergency telephone numbers (such as 911 or 112), provide stabilising care on scene, and transport patients to an appropriate emergency department, communicating with the receiving hospital en route. Lights and sirens are used to navigate traffic in time-critical responses, with protocols balancing speed against safety.
