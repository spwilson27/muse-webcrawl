Gaussian curvature

FieldDifferential geometry
Introduced byCarl Friedrich Gauss (1827)

In differential geometry, the Gaussian curvature or Gauss curvature κ of a smooth surface in three-dimensional space at a point is the product of the principal curvatures, κ1 and κ2, at that point:

κ = κ1 κ2

For example, a sphere of radius r has Gaussian curvature 1/r2 everywhere, while a flat plane and a cylinder both have Gaussian curvature zero at every point. A hyperboloid or the surface of a saddle has negative Gaussian curvature.

Informal definition

At any point of a surface one can draw two mutually perpendicular normal planes; the curves where these planes cut the surface have curvatures, and the maximum and minimum of these are the principal curvatures. Their product is the Gaussian curvature. The sign of the curvature distinguishes elliptic points (κ > 0, like a sphere), hyperbolic points (κ < 0, like a saddle), and parabolic or flat points (κ = 0).

Theorem egregium

Gauss's celebrated theorema egregium, published in his 1827 Disquisitiones generales circa superficies curvas, states that Gaussian curvature is an intrinsic invariant: it can be determined entirely from measurements of distance made within the surface itself, without reference to how the surface sits in ambient space. A consequence is that no map of a sphere onto a plane can preserve all distances.

Total curvature

The integral of the Gaussian curvature over a whole closed surface is related to its topology by the Gauss–Bonnet theorem: for a closed orientable surface of genus g, the total curvature equals 2π(2 − 2g), so a sphere has total curvature 4π and a torus has total curvature 0.
