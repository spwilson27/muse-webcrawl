Secondary dominant

A V/V chord resolving to V in C major

In music theory, a secondary dominant (also applied dominant) is a major triad or dominant seventh chord borrowed from a key other than the prevailing one, used to tonicise — that is, to temporarily treat as a tonic — a chord within the home key. The chord being tonicised is usually a major or minor triad, most often the supertonic (ii), mediant (iii), subdominant (IV or iv), submediant (vi) or dominant (V) of the main key.

Notation and terminology

Secondary dominants are written in Roman numeral analysis as "V of x", conventionally compressed to the form V/x — for example V/V ("five of five") denotes the dominant of the dominant. The target chord must be a major or minor triad; diminished and augmented chords cannot be tonicised in this way because they cannot function even temporarily as tonics.

Examples

In C major, the chord D major (D–F♯–A) is V/V: the F♯ is foreign to the key signature but turns the following G major chord into a temporary tonic. Secondary dominants are a hallmark of Baroque and Classical harmony, of ragtime and early jazz progressions (where chains such as VI–II–V–I are common), and of popular songwriting, where V/IV and V/vi appear frequently.

Secondary leading-tone chords

Closely related are secondary leading-tone chords — diminished triads and half- or fully diminished seventh chords that function as vii° of a target chord — which are analysed similarly, for example vii°/V.
