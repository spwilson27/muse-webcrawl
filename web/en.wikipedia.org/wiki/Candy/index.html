Candy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: An assortment of colourful wrapped candies in a glass dish]

Candy (American English; sweets in British English) is a confection made mainly of sugar or other sweeteners, often flavoured and coloured, and eaten as a snack or treat rather than as part of a meal. Candy ranges from hard boiled sweets and caramels to gummies, liquorice, marshmallow, candy floss (cotton candy) and chocolate-based bars. Its history tracks the history of sugar itself: a rare luxury for most of human existence, candy became a cheap everyday pleasure only with industrial sugar production in the 19th century.

History

Ancient peoples candied fruits, nuts and flowers in honey; sugar candy followed sugarcane's spread from India. In Europe, sugar was a costly medicine and spice before it was a food, sold by apothecaries as lozenges and comfits. Steam-powered machinery, cheap beet sugar and new techniques (the candy press, starch mogul moulding) industrialised sweets in the 1800s, creating brands and products — boiled sweets, caramels, jelly beans, candy canes — that persist today.

Types

Confectioners divide candy into crystalline (fudge, fondant — containing sugar crystals) and non-crystalline (hard candy, caramels, taffy, gummies). Hard candies are sugar cooked to the "hard crack" stage (about 150 °C) and flavoured; gummies and jellies are set with gelatin, pectin or starch; aerated candies are whipped with air; sour candies are coated in citric or malic acid.

Manufacture

Candy-making is applied sugar chemistry: syrup concentration, measured by temperature stages from "soft ball" to "hard crack", determines texture. Colourings and flavourings are added near the end. Health debates over sugar, dental decay and marketing to children have led to taxes, labelling rules and reformulation in many countries, while artisan and nostalgic candy enjoy revivals.
