Carbonic acid

Formula: H₂CO₃
Molar mass: 62.03 g/mol
pKₐ₁: ~3.6 (true, for H₂CO₃ itself)
Conjugate bases: Bicarbonate, carbonate
Appearance: Exists only in solution (and transiently in pure form)

Carbonic acid is a chemical compound with the formula H2CO3. It is formed in small amounts when carbon dioxide dissolves in water: CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3. The equilibrium lies far to the left — only about 0.2% of dissolved CO₂ is converted to carbonic acid at room temperature — yet the resulting weak acid is of great importance in chemistry, geology and physiology. Pure carbonic acid is unstable under ordinary conditions and was long thought impossible to isolate, though it has been prepared at low temperatures and detected in the gas phase and in outer space.

Acid–base chemistry

Carbonic acid is diprotic: it loses one proton to form the bicarbonate (hydrogen carbonate) ion, HCO3−, and a second to form the carbonate ion, CO32−. The apparent first pKₐ of about 6.35 commonly quoted for "carbonic acid" combines the CO₂/H₂CO₃ equilibrium with the acid dissociation; the true pKₐ of H₂CO₃ itself is around 3.6, making it a moderately strong weak acid comparable to formic acid. Its salts — bicarbonates and carbonates such as sodium bicarbonate and calcium carbonate — are widespread and industrially important.

Roles in nature and the body

The carbonic acid–bicarbonate buffer system is the principal means by which blood pH is maintained near 7.4 and by which CO₂ is transported from tissues to the lungs, a process catalysed by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase. In the oceans, dissolved CO₂ and carbonic acid buffer seawater; increasing atmospheric CO₂ is lowering ocean pH, a phenomenon known as ocean acidification.

Carbonic acid formed in rainwater slowly dissolves limestone, producing karst landscapes, caves and stalactites, and it gives carbonated drinks their mild acidity and tang.
