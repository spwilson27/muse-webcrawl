Gloster Meteor

The Gloster Meteor was the first British jet fighter and the Allies' only operational jet aircraft to engage in combat during the Second World War. Designed by George Carter at the Gloster Aircraft Company around Frank Whittle's pioneering turbojet engines, the Meteor entered service with No. 616 Squadron RAF in July 1944 and remained in widespread use, in many variants, through the 1950s and beyond.

Design and development

Development began in 1940 under Air Ministry specification F.9/40. Because the early centrifugal-flow jet engines produced modest thrust, the design adopted a twin-engine layout with the nacelles mounted mid-wing. The prototype flew on 5 March 1943 powered by Halford H.1 (de Havilland Goblin) engines, after ground runs in a modified airframe known as the Pioneer. Production aircraft used Whittle-derived Rolls-Royce Welland engines.

Operational history

Meteor F.1s were first used against V-1 flying bombs in the summer of 1944; pilots sometimes tipped the bombs over with their wingtips when their cannon jammed. The improved F.3 deployed to Belgium in early 1945 but was forbidden to cross enemy lines for fear of capture, so the Meteor never met the German Messerschmitt Me 262 in combat.

The definitive post-war variant, the F.8, served as the RAF's main day fighter until replaced by the Hawker Hunter and Supermarine Swift. Australian Meteors fought in the Korean War with No. 77 Squadron RAAF, where they were outclassed by the MiG-15 in air combat but proved effective in ground attack. Other variants included the T.7 two-seat trainer, the FR.9 reconnaissance fighter, and the NF.11–14 series of Armstrong Whitworth-built night fighters. Meteors were exported to many nations and also licence-built; ejector-seat manufacturer Martin-Baker long used a Meteor as a test aircraft.

Records

In November 1945 a Meteor F.4 flown by Group Captain H. J. Wilson set a world air speed record of 606 mph (975 km/h), and a further record of 616 mph followed in 1946.
