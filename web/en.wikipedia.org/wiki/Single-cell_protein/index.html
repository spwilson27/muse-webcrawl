Single-cell protein

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Single-cell protein Type: Protein source / food and feed ingredient
Sources: Bacteria, yeasts, fungi, algae
Typical protein content: 30–80% of dry weight
Main substrates: Methanol, methane, molasses, whey, lignocellulose
First commercialised: 1960s–1970s

Single-cell protein (SCP) refers to the dried cells of microorganisms — such as bacteria, yeasts, filamentous fungi and algae — that are grown in large-scale culture systems and harvested for use as a protein supplement in human food or animal feed. The term was coined in 1966 by Carroll L. Wilson of MIT to give the field a more neutral name than "microbial protein" or "petroprotein".

Because microorganisms grow extremely rapidly, doubling in minutes to hours rather than the months or years required for crops and livestock, SCP production can yield far more protein per unit of land and water than conventional agriculture. Well-managed fermenters can convert inexpensive carbon substrates — including industrial by-products and waste streams — into a biomass containing 50 percent or more protein by dry weight, together with vitamins, minerals and, in some organisms, useful lipids.

History

Interest in microbial protein grew after the Second World War, when population growth raised fears of a global "protein gap". The German scientist Max Knorr and others had already explored feeding yeast to animals during wartime shortages. In the 1960s and 1970s, oil companies including BP and ICI invested heavily in SCP plants that grew microorganisms on petroleum-derived substrates such as n-paraffins and methanol. ICI's Pruteen plant at Billingham, England, used the methanol-consuming bacterium Methylophilus methylotrophus and was one of the largest continuous fermenters ever built, with a capacity of about 50,000 tonnes per year.

Rising oil prices in the 1980s made petroleum-based SCP uncompetitive with soya and fishmeal, and most of the early plants closed. A second wave of interest has since focused on waste substrates, gas fermentation using methane or hydrogen, and premium products such as mycoprotein for human consumption.

Production organisms and substrates

Production organisms are selected for rapid growth, high protein content, a favourable amino-acid profile, low nucleic-acid content where possible, and safety. Commonly used organisms include:

  • Yeasts such as Candida utilis (torula yeast), grown on molasses, sulfite waste liquor or ethanol.
  • Bacteria such as Methylophilus species on methanol and methane-oxidising bacteria on natural gas.
  • Filamentous fungi, notably Fusarium venenatum, the source of the mycoprotein sold as Quorn.
  • Microalgae such as Spirulina (Arthrospira) and Chlorella, grown photosynthetically in ponds or photobioreactors.

Typical crude protein contents range from about 30–50 percent for algae, 45–65 percent for yeasts and fungi, and up to 80 percent for some bacteria.

Processing and safety

After fermentation, the biomass is harvested by centrifugation or filtration, washed, and dried. A major technical problem is the high content of nucleic acids (RNA) in fast-growing microorganisms: excessive dietary nucleic acid raises uric acid levels in humans and can cause gout, so food-grade SCP is usually treated — for example by heat shock or enzymatic treatment — to reduce RNA to below about 2 percent. Cell walls of some organisms must also be disrupted to improve digestibility. Products intended for human food are subjected to extensive toxicological testing.

Applications

SCP is used principally as an animal-feed ingredient, replacing fishmeal and soybean meal in poultry, pig and aquaculture diets. Mycoprotein and spirulina are marketed directly as human foods. More recently, companies have developed SCP from hydrogen-oxidising bacteria fed with carbon dioxide and electrolytic hydrogen — a process sometimes called "food from air" — and from methane fermentation for salmon feed.

Advantages and limitations

Advantages include independence from arable land and season, very rapid production, the ability to use waste or gaseous substrates, and high protein concentration. Limitations include production cost, the need for sterile large-scale fermentation, nucleic-acid and digestibility issues, consumer acceptance, and — for fossil substrates — carbon emissions.
