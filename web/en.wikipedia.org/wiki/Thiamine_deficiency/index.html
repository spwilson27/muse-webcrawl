Thiamine deficiency

SpecialtyNeurology, nutrition
SymptomsFatigue, weight loss, confusion, peripheral neuropathy, heart failure
CausesDiet low in thiamine, alcohol use disorder, malabsorption
TreatmentThiamine supplementation

Thiamine deficiency is a medical condition of low levels of thiamine (vitamin B1). Thiamine is an essential nutrient required for carbohydrate metabolism and normal function of the nervous and cardiovascular systems; the body stores only small amounts, so deficiency can develop within weeks of inadequate intake.

Manifestations

A severe and chronic form is known as beriberi, historically common where polished white rice was the staple food. "Wet beriberi" predominantly affects the cardiovascular system, causing oedema and high-output heart failure, while "dry beriberi" affects the nervous system, causing peripheral neuropathy with weakness and loss of reflexes. In people with alcohol use disorder, deficiency classically produces the Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome: Wernicke encephalopathy (confusion, ophthalmoplegia and ataxia) which, untreated, may progress to Korsakoff syndrome with profound memory impairment and confabulation.

Causes, diagnosis and treatment

Causes include diets low in thiamine, chronic alcohol use, hyperemesis gravidarum, bariatric surgery, dialysis and malabsorption. Diagnosis is based on symptoms, dietary history and response to supplementation, supported by blood assays of transketolase activity. Treatment is prompt thiamine replacement, given intravenously when Wernicke encephalopathy is suspected — an emergency, since glucose given before thiamine can precipitate or worsen the condition. Prevention relies on a balanced diet and on thiamine fortification of staple foods in at-risk populations.
