Steelmaking

Steelmaking is the process of producing steel from iron ore and scrap. In steelmaking, impurities such as nitrogen, silicon, phosphorus, sulfur, and excess carbon are removed from the iron, and alloying elements such as manganese, nickel, chromium, and vanadium are added to produce different grades of steel.

Primary steelmaking

The dominant route is integrated steelmaking: a blast furnace reduces iron ore with coke to pig iron, which is then refined in a basic oxygen furnace (BOF), where pure oxygen blown through the molten metal burns off carbon and impurities in minutes. The older Bessemer converter and open-hearth furnace were the principal earlier processes.

Secondary and electric routes

The electric arc furnace (EAF) melts scrap steel (and sometimes direct-reduced iron) using high-current electric arcs and now accounts for a large and growing share of production, especially in the United States. Secondary metallurgy — ladle refining, vacuum degassing, and desulfurisation — adjusts composition and cleanliness before continuous casting solidifies the steel into slabs, blooms, or billets.

History and industry

Steel was produced for millennia in small quantities (crucible and blister steels). The Bessemer process (1856) and Siemens–Martin open hearth made mass production possible. Global crude steel production is roughly 1.9 billion tonnes per year, with China producing more than half. Steelmaking is energy- and carbon-intensive — about 7–9% of global CO2 emissions — driving research into hydrogen-based direct reduction and other "green steel" methods.
