Medium spiny neuron

Medium spiny neurons (MSNs) are a special type of GABAergic inhibitory cell representing about 95% of neurons within the human striatum, the main input structure of the basal ganglia. They receive convergent input from the cerebral cortex, thalamus, and dopaminergic neurons of the midbrain.

Structure

MSNs are named for their medium-sized cell bodies (roughly 15–25 µm) and for the dense spines covering their dendrites, which are the sites of excitatory glutamatergic synapses from cortical and thalamic inputs. Their axons are the sole output of the striatum, projecting to the globus pallidus and substantia nigra pars reticulata.

Two pathways

Striatal MSNs are classically divided into two populations by their projections and dopamine receptor expression:

  • Direct pathway (D1) MSNs express D1-type dopamine receptors and project directly to the output nuclei of the basal ganglia, facilitating movement.
  • Indirect pathway (D2) MSNs express D2-type dopamine receptors (and adenosine A2A receptors) and project to the external globus pallidus, ultimately suppressing movement.

Dopamine modulates the balance between the pathways, exciting D1 and inhibiting D2 neurons. In the dorsal striatum these populations are intermingled; in the ventral striatum (nucleus accumbens) they contribute to reward and motivation circuitry.

Clinical significance

MSNs are central to several disorders. In Huntington's disease they are the earliest and most severely lost striatal neurons. Their dysfunction is implicated in Parkinson's disease (via dopamine loss), dystonia, addiction, schizophrenia, and obsessive–compulsive disorder.
