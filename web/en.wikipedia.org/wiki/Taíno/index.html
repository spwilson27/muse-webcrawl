Taíno

The Taíno are the Indigenous peoples of the Greater Antilles and parts of the Bahamas and Lesser Antilles. Their ancestors migrated from the Orinoco basin of South America through the Lesser Antilles into the Caribbean, reaching the Bahamas and Greater Antilles by roughly the late first millennium CE. At the time of European contact in 1492, Taíno societies — most notably the chiefdoms (cacicazgos) of Hispaniola, Puerto Rico, Jamaica, Cuba, and the Bahamas — numbered, by most modern estimates, in the hundreds of thousands, though some scholars argue for several million on Hispaniola alone.

The Taíno spoke an Arawakan language and developed a ranked, matrilineal chiefdom society organised around the yucayeque (village), ruled by a cacique (chief) and advised by behiques (priests/healers) and nitaínos (nobles). Their culture is noted for three-pointed stone zemís (spiritual objects), carved wooden duhos (ceremonial seats), large central plazas and ball courts (batey) where the ceremonial ball game batú was played, and intensive conuco agriculture centred on cassava (yuca).

Within decades of Columbus's arrival, Taíno populations collapsed catastrophically through enslavement, violence, famine, and Old World epidemic diseases to which they had no immunity. Despite long being described as "extinct," Taíno people survived through intermarriage, and Taíno ancestry, language, and cultural practices persist in the modern Caribbean; today, organised Taíno identity movements exist in Puerto Rico, the Dominican Republic, Cuba, and the diaspora.

Etymology

According to early Spanish accounts, when Columbus's men first encountered the inhabitants of the Bahamas and Hispaniola, the people greeted them with the word taíno, understood to mean "good" or "noble" — reportedly to distinguish themselves from the more feared Kalinago (Caribs) of the Lesser Antilles. The term has since been adopted as the general ethnonym for the Arawak-speaking peoples of the Greater Antilles.

Origins and settlement

Archaeological and linguistic evidence traces Taíno ancestors to Arawak-speaking horticulturalists of the Orinoco Delta in present-day Venezuela. Successive waves of migration — identified in the archaeological record with Saladoid and later Ostionoid ceramic traditions — moved north through the Lesser Antilles over many centuries. By about 600–1200 CE, the distinctive Taíno culture had crystallised in the Greater Antilles, with regional variants: Classic Taíno in Hispaniola and Puerto Rico, Western Taíno in Jamaica, Cuba, and the Bahamas (the Lucayan Taíno), and Eastern Taíno in the northern Lesser Antilles.

Society and culture

Political organisation

Taíno chiefdoms were among the most complex societies of the pre-Columbian Caribbean. Hispaniola was divided into five major cacicazgos — Marién, Maguá, Maguana, Jaraguá, and Higüey — each ruled by a principal cacique, with subordinate chiefs governing districts and villages. Succession was matrilineal: a cacique's heir was typically the eldest son of his eldest sister. Society was stratified into nitaínos (nobles), behiques (priest-healers), and naborias (commoners/labourers).

Religion

Taíno religion centred on yocahú, the supreme spirit associated with fertility and the growth of yuca, and Atabey, goddess of fresh water and fertility. Spiritual forces called yocahú or zemís were embodied in carved objects of stone, wood, shell, bone, and cotton — most famously the three-pointed stone zemís buried in conucos. Behiques conducted healing and divination rituals, including the cohoba rite, in which a hallucinogenic snuff made from Anadenanthera peregrina seeds was inhaled through a Y-shaped tube to communicate with the zemís. Ancestor veneration was important; the skulls of deceased caciques were sometimes kept in zemí figures.

Agriculture and food

The staple crop was bitter cassava (yuca), grown in earthen mounds called conucos that improved drainage and soil fertility. Poisonous juice was pressed from the grated root with a woven sleeve press (tipi), and the pulp baked into flat casabe bread. Other crops included sweet potatoes (batata), maize, beans, squash, peanuts, pineapple, papaya, and tobacco. The Taíno also fished extensively, hunted hutias and iguanas, and kept a small domestic dog (the alco).

Ball game

Villages were built around a central plaza, often a rectangular stone-lined court (batey), used for ceremonies and the ball game batú. Teams kept a solid rubber ball in play using hips, knees, and shoulders; the game had ceremonial and possibly conflict-resolution functions and echoes the Mesoamerican ballgame.

Language and legacy words

The Taíno language, an Arawakan tongue, died out as a community language within about a century of contact, but it left a large legacy in the world's languages. Words of Taíno origin include hammock (hamaca), hurricane (huracán, from the storm deity Juracán), barbecue (barbacoa), canoe (canoa), tobacco (tabaco), maize (mahís), savanna (sabana), iguana, guava, and cacique. Many Caribbean place names are also Taíno, including Jamaica (Xaymaca, "land of wood and water"), Haiti (Ayiti, "land of high mountains"), and Borikén (Puerto Rico).

European contact and demographic collapse

Columbus landed in the Bahamas (Guanahani) on 12 October 1492 and established the short-lived settlement of La Navidad on Hispaniola. From 1493, Spanish colonisation brought forced labour under the encomienda system, warfare, famine, and disease. Taíno resistance — including the rebellions of Caonabo, Cotubanamá, and the cacica Anacaona (executed in 1503), and later Hatuey in Cuba — was crushed. Smallpox (1518), measles, and influenza epidemics devastated communities. A 1514 census counted roughly 26,000 Taíno remaining on Hispaniola; by the mid-16th century, chroniclers such as Bartolomé de las Casas (whose A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies denounced Spanish atrocities) described the population as nearly annihilated. Modern genetic studies, however, show substantial Indigenous ancestry in contemporary Puerto Ricans, Dominicans, and Cubans, demonstrating biological and cultural survival through mestizaje.

Contemporary Taíno identity

Since the late 20th century, a Taíno resurgence movement has reclaimed Indigenous identity in the Caribbean and its diaspora, reviving language vocabulary, ceremonies (such as the areíto), craft traditions, and political advocacy. Organisations such as the United Confederation of Taíno People represent Taíno communities internationally. The extent and definition of Taíno continuity remain debated among scholars, but archaeological, documentary, and genetic evidence all confirm that Taíno heritage endures in the modern Caribbean.
