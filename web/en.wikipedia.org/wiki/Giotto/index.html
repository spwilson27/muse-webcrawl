Giotto di Bondone (c. 1267 – January 8, 1337), known mononymously as Giotto and Latinised as Giottus, was an Italian painter and architect from Florence during the Late Middle Ages who worked during the Gothic and Proto-Renaissance period. Giotto's contemporary, the banker and chronicler Giovanni Villani, wrote that Giotto was "the most sovereign master of painting in his time, who drew all his figures and their postures according to nature".

Life

Tradition holds that Giotto was born near Florence, the son of a farmer, and was discovered drawing sheep on a rock by the painter Cimabue, who took him as an apprentice. Giotto worked in Assisi, Rome, Padua, Florence and Naples, and was appointed master mason and architect of Florence Cathedral in 1334, designing its famous campanile (bell tower).

Works

Giotto's masterpiece is the cycle of frescoes in the Scrovegni Chapel in Padua, completed around 1305, depicting the lives of the Virgin Mary and Christ; its naturalistic figures, emotional depth and spatial coherence marked a decisive break from Byzantine conventions. He is also credited with the Life of St Francis cycle in the Upper Church of Assisi (attribution debated), the Ognissanti Madonna, and frescoes in the Bardi and Peruzzi chapels of Santa Croce, Florence.

Legacy

Dante praised Giotto in the Divine Comedy, and later writers from Boccaccio to Vasari celebrated him as the founder of modern Western painting. The ESA space probe that flew by Halley's Comet in 1986 was named Giotto in his honour, after his depiction of the comet as the Star of Bethlehem in the Scrovegni Chapel.
