The Cthulhu Mythos is a shared fictional universe rooted in the work of the American horror writer H. P. Lovecraft (1890–1937). It takes its name from Cthulhu, the vast tentacled entity introduced in Lovecraft's story "The Call of Cthulhu" (1928). The Mythos consists of the stories of Lovecraft and his correspondents and successors, linked by a common pantheon of alien gods and beings (the "Great Old Ones" and "Outer Gods"), forbidden books such as the Necronomicon, recurring places (Arkham, Innsmouth, Dunwich, and the sunken city of R'lyeh), and a philosophy of "cosmicism" — the insignificance of humanity in an indifferent universe.

Cthulhu, "dead and dreaming" in the sunken city of R'lyeh, is the emblematic figure of the Mythos.

Development

Lovecraft wrote within a circle of pulp-magazine authors — Robert E. Howard, Clark Ashton Smith, Robert Bloch, Frank Belknap Long and others — who borrowed each other's inventions freely, creating the impression of a shared mythology; Lovecraft himself called it, jokingly, "Yog-Sothothery". After Lovecraft's death, his friend August Derleth founded Arkham House to publish his work, coined the term "Cthulhu Mythos", and systematised the mythos into a moral struggle between good Elder Gods and evil Great Old Ones — an interpretation many later scholars (such as S. T. Joshi) regard as contrary to Lovecraft's amoral cosmicism. Later writers, from Ramsey Campbell and Brian Lumley to modern authors, have continued to expand the mythos.

Elements

Major entities include Cthulhu, Yog-Sothoth, Azathoth, Nyarlathotep and Shub-Niggurath; creatures such as the Deep Ones, Mi-go and Elder Things; and grimoires including the Necronomicon, the Book of Eibon and Unaussprechlichen Kulten. Typical plots involve scholars or antiquarians uncovering blasphemous knowledge, followed by madness, degeneration or death — knowledge itself is the horror.

In other media

The Mythos has generated role-playing games (Chaosium's Call of Cthulhu, 1981, one of the most influential horror RPGs), board and video games, films, music and a large body of fan and professional fiction; "Lovecraftian horror" is now a recognised subgenre term, and Cthulhu has become a widespread popular-culture figure, from plush toys to mock presidential campaigns ("Cthulhu for President — Why vote for the lesser evil?").
