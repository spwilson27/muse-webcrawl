Nabataeans

The Nabataeans (Nabataean Aramaic: Nabāṭū) were an ancient Arab people who built a wealthy trading kingdom in northwestern Arabia and the southern Levant between the fourth century BCE and the second century CE. From their capital at Petra, in present-day Jordan, they controlled the caravan routes carrying frankincense, myrrh, spices and silk between southern Arabia, Damascus and the Mediterranean.

History

Originally nomadic herders and traders, the Nabataeans appear in Hellenistic sources by 312 BCE, when they repelled an expedition of Antigonus I. By the first century BCE their kingdom stretched from Damascus to the Hejaz. Kings such as Aretas III, Obodas III and Aretas IV — under whom Petra's greatest monuments were built — maintained independence between Rome, the Hasmoneans and the Herodians, supplying troops and trade to each in turn. In 106 CE the emperor Trajan annexed the kingdom as the Roman province of Arabia Petraea, with remarkably little recorded resistance.

Culture

The Nabataeans wrote in Aramaic using a distinctive cursive script that is a direct ancestor of the Arabic alphabet, while speaking an early form of Arabic. Their religion worshipped deities including Dushara, the chief god, and the goddess al-Uzza. In the deserts they became celebrated hydraulic engineers: dams, cisterns, channels and ceramic pipelines captured flash-flood water, letting them farm and water caravans in regions with under 100 mm of annual rain.

Petra and other sites

Petra's rock-cut façades — the Treasury (Al-Khazneh), the Monastery (Ad Deir), the royal tombs and a Roman-style theatre — were carved into rose-red sandstone cliffs and are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Other Nabataean cities include Hegra (Mada'in Salih) in Saudi Arabia, with its inscribed tomb façades; Avdat, Haluza and Shivta in the Negev; and Bostra in Syria, which became the Roman provincial capital.

After annexation, Nabataean identity faded as trade routes shifted to the sea and to Palmyra, but their water technology sustained Negev farming into the Byzantine period.
