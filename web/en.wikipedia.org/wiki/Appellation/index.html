Appellation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An appellation is a legally defined and protected geographical indication used to identify where the grapes for a wine were grown, and by extension a system for regulating the production of wine and other agricultural products associated with a place. The term comes from the French appellation, "name" or "designation". The best-known system is France's Appellation d'origine contrôlée (AOC), literally "controlled designation of origin", established in the 1930s and administered today by the Institut national de l'origine et de la qualité (INAO).

Concept

An appellation system rests on the idea of terroir: that the combination of soil, climate, topography, and local tradition of a delimited area gives its products distinctive characteristics that merit protection. Appellation rules typically specify the geographic boundaries of the area, the permitted grape varieties (or other ingredients), maximum yields, minimum alcohol levels, and authorised production methods. Only products meeting the rules may bear the appellation name on their label.

France

The French AOC system grew out of fraud-prevention measures in the early twentieth century, including the 1905 and 1919 laws on the protection of designations of origin, and was formalised in 1935 with the creation of the national committee that became the INAO. Famous appellations include Bordeaux, Burgundy, Champagne, and Châteauneuf-du-Pape — whose 1923 rules, drawn up by Baron Le Roy, became a model for the national system. The strictest French rules require not only origin but adherence to traditional grape varieties and methods; wines that fall outside the rules are sold under looser categories such as Vin de France or the intermediate Indication géographique protégée (IGP).

Other systems

Similar systems operate worldwide, now generally framed as geographical indications (GIs) under international trade law, notably the European Union's Protected Designation of Origin (PDO) scheme. Italy has its DOCG and DOC categories, Spain its Denominación de Origen, and Portugal its DOC, all modelled partly on the French example. The United States uses American Viticultural Areas (AVAs), such as Napa Valley, which regulate geographic origin but generally not grape varieties or production methods. Appellation protection extends beyond wine to cheeses (such as Roquefort and Parmigiano-Reggiano), spirits (Cognac, Tequila), and other products.

Criticism

Supporters credit appellations with preserving quality, rural livelihoods, and regional identity. Critics argue that rigid rules can stifle innovation — a debate dramatised by the "Super Tuscan" wines of Italy, which were initially excluded from Chianti's appellation for using non-traditional grapes — and that appellation names are sometimes claimed as generic terms in other countries, a recurring source of trade disputes.
