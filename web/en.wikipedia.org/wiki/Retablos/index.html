Retablos

Retablos are a form of folk art originating in the Andean highlands of Peru, especially the Ayacucho region. They consist of portable wooden boxes with doors that open to reveal one or more tiers of small, brightly painted figures carved or modelled from wood, plaster, or a paste of boiled potatoes and gypsum. The scenes depicted range from religious subjects — Nativity and Passion scenes, saints' lives — to scenes of everyday life, festivals, markets, bullfights and political events.

Origins

The retablo developed from portable Spanish colonial altar boxes (cajones) carried by muleteers and used as travelling shrines of patron saints. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries the boxes, known as cajones de San Marcos, protected cattle and households. Ayacucho artisans — celebrated families such as the López Antay family, whose patriarch Joaquín López Antay (1897–1981) won Peru's National Culture Prize in 1975 — gradually expanded the repertoire from sacred scenes to social commentary.

Modern retablos

During the internal conflict in Peru in the 1980s, retablos became a vehicle for recording violence and social protest, a testimonial function recognised internationally. Today retablos are sold worldwide as decorative art while continuing in devotional use. In Mexico the term retablo also refers to small votive paintings on tin (ex-votos) offered in thanks for divine intervention, and in Spain it denotes the large altarpiece (reredos) behind a church altar.
