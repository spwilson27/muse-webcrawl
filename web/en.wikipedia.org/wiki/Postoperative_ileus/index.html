Postoperative ileus

Postoperative ileus is a temporary impairment of gastrointestinal motility that occurs after abdominal (and sometimes other) surgery. It is a form of ileus — a functional, non-mechanical obstruction — and is one of the most common reasons for prolonged hospital stay after major abdominal operations.

Mechanism

Surgical handling of the bowel, anaesthetic agents, opioid painkillers, inflammation of the intestinal wall, and electrolyte disturbances all suppress coordinated peristalsis. The stomach and colon are typically the last segments to recover, with normal colonic motility returning after about 48–72 hours in uncomplicated cases.

Signs and symptoms

Features include abdominal distension, nausea and vomiting, intolerance of oral intake, absence of flatus or stool, and diminished or absent bowel sounds. Prolonged ileus must be distinguished from mechanical obstruction and intra-abdominal complications such as leaks or collections.

Prevention and treatment

Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols reduce ileus through minimally invasive surgery, early oral feeding, early mobilization, gum chewing, opioid-sparing analgesia, and avoiding routine nasogastric tubes. The peripherally acting μ-opioid receptor antagonist alvimopan is approved in some countries to accelerate gastrointestinal recovery after bowel resection. Management of established ileus is mainly supportive: nil by mouth, intravenous fluids, correction of electrolytes, and nasogastric decompression if vomiting is severe.
