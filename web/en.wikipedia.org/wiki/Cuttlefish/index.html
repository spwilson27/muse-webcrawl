Cuttlefish are marine molluscs of the order Sepiida. They belong to the class Cephalopoda, which also includes squid, octopuses, and nautiluses. Cuttlefish have a unique internal shell, the cuttlebone, which is used for control of buoyancy. They have large, W-shaped pupils, eight arms, and two tentacles furnished with suckers, with which they secure their prey.

Anatomy

Despite their name, cuttlefish are not fish but molluscs. Their most distinctive feature is the cuttlebone — a gas-filled, chambered internal shell of aragonite that the animal floods or empties to adjust buoyancy. Cuttlefish eyes are among the most developed in the animal kingdom, though the animals are colour-blind. Their blood contains the copper-based protein haemocyanin, giving it a green-blue colour, and they have three hearts.

Camouflage and intelligence

Cuttlefish are masters of rapid colour change, using neurally controlled chromatophores, iridophores, and skin papillae to match their surroundings or produce dazzling displays used in courtship and communication — despite being unable to see colour themselves. They are also highly intelligent invertebrates with large brain-to-body size ratios, capable of learning and of passing delayed-gratification tests.

Range and diet

Cuttlefish inhabit shallow tropical and temperate waters worldwide except off the Americas. They feed on small molluscs, crabs, shrimp, fish, and other cuttlefish, and are themselves preyed upon by dolphins, sharks, seals, and seabirds. Most species live only one to two years.
