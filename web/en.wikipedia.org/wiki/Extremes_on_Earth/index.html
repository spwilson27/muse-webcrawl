Extremes on Earth

This article describes extreme locations on Earth and record-setting geographical and meteorological values: the highest and lowest points, the most remote places, and the extremes of temperature, precipitation, and wind that have been reliably measured.

Temperature

The highest air temperature reliably recorded is 56.7 °C (134 °F) at Furnace Creek Ranch, Death Valley, California, on 10 July 1913, according to the World Meteorological Organization. The lowest natural temperature recorded at ground level is −89.2 °C (−128.6 °F) at Vostok Station, Antarctica, on 21 July 1983; satellite remote sensing has suggested lower surface temperatures near −93 °C on the East Antarctic Plateau, though these are not directly measured air temperatures.

Precipitation

The greatest average annual rainfall is recorded at Mawsynram, India, with about 11,872 mm (467 inches) per year; nearby Cherrapunji holds records for the most rain in a single year (26,461 mm in 1860–61) and in a single month. The driest inhabited place is commonly cited as Arica, Chile, and the Atacama Desert and the McMurdo Dry Valleys of Antarctica are among the driest regions overall.

Remoteness

The oceanic pole of inaccessibility, "Point Nemo" in the South Pacific, is the point in the ocean farthest from any land, about 2,688 km from the nearest coast. The Eurasian pole of inaccessibility in northwestern China is the land point farthest from any ocean. Tristan da Cunha is the most remote inhabited archipelago, lying about 2,400 km from South Africa.

Wind and pressure

The highest surface wind speed ever directly measured was 408 km/h (253 mph) during Tropical Cyclone Olivia on Barrow Island, Australia, in 1996, recognized by the WMO. The highest sea-level pressure recorded was 1,085.7 hPa in Mongolia (2001, adjusted), and the lowest in a tropical cyclone was 870 hPa in Typhoon Tip (1979).
