Base load

FieldElectric power systems
DefinitionMinimum level of demand on an electrical grid over a period of time
Typical sourcesNuclear, coal, geothermal, run-of-river hydro, biomass
Related termsPeak load, load following, load duration curve, capacity factor

A load duration curve: the area beneath the minimum demand level is the base load.

The base load (also baseload) is the minimum level of demand on an electrical grid over a span of time, for example one week. Electricity demand fluctuates over the day and across seasons, but it rarely falls below a floor set by continuously running processes, street lighting, refrigeration, data centres and other always-on consumption. Grid operators traditionally meet this minimum demand with base load power plants — large generators designed to run constantly at high utilisation, with demand above the base level met by intermediate and peaking power plants.

Characteristics of base load plants

Base load plants are characterised by high capital costs but low fuel and operating costs per unit of electricity, making them economical only when run nearly all the time. They typically have high capacity factors and slow start-up and shut-down times: a large coal or nuclear unit may take hours or days to reach full power and cannot respond quickly to changes in demand. Nuclear power stations, large coal plants, geothermal plants, run-of-river hydroelectric stations and some biomass plants have historically filled this role in most power systems.

Grid operation

In traditional vertically integrated systems, dispatch followed a "merit order": base load plants ran continuously, load-following plants (often combined-cycle gas turbines or reservoir hydro) adjusted output through the day, and peaking plants (open-cycle gas turbines, pumped storage) covered the highest-demand hours. The shape of the load is summarised by the load duration curve, which orders demand from highest to lowest over a period.

Changing role

The growth of variable renewable energy has complicated the base load concept. Solar and wind output is inexpensive but not controllable, and on sunny, windy days renewables may supply more than the traditional base load level, forcing thermal plants to ramp down, export power, or curtail output. Some analysts argue that "base load" as a category of inflexible generation is becoming less relevant, with grids instead valuing flexibility, energy storage, interconnection and demand response; others maintain that firm, dispatchable low-carbon capacity remains essential for reliability. The debate has featured prominently in energy policy discussions in countries such as Australia and Germany.
