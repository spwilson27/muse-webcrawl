Mercury-vapor lamp

Mercury lamps produce a characteristic blue-green light.

A mercury-vapor lamp is a gas-discharge lamp that uses an electric arc through vaporized mercury to produce light. Developed commercially in the first years of the twentieth century — Peter Cooper Hewitt's low-pressure lamp dates to 1901, and high-pressure versions followed in the 1930s — it was for decades the standard light source for street lighting, factories, gymnasiums and large outdoor areas.

Construction and operation

The lamp consists of a small fused-quartz arc tube containing mercury and a starting gas (usually argon), mounted inside a larger glass bulb that may be coated with phosphor and filled with nitrogen. When power is applied, an arc strikes between a main and an auxiliary electrode, heating the tube until the mercury vaporizes and the main arc takes over — a warm-up of several minutes. A ballast limits the current. Because mercury is highly toxic, broken lamps and disposal are environmental hazards.

Light quality

A clear mercury lamp emits a stark blue-green light dominated by mercury's spectral lines (notably at 436, 546 and 579 nm) with poor colour rendering. Phosphor-coated bulbs ("deluxe" lamps) convert some ultraviolet emission to red light, improving colour rendering substantially. Efficacy ranges from roughly 35 to 65 lumens per watt — far better than incandescent lamps but well below modern sources.

Phase-out

Mercury lamps have been progressively displaced by metal-halide lamps, high-pressure sodium lamps and, increasingly, LEDs. The US Energy Policy Act of 2005 banned certain mercury ballasts (effective 2008), and the European Union phased out most mercury lamps under its Ecodesign and RoHS rules, with the last exemptions expiring in the 2020s.
