Galaxy formation and evolution

The study of galaxy formation and evolution concerns how galaxies formed from the nearly uniform matter distribution of the early universe, how they changed over cosmic time, and the processes that produced the variety of structures observed today. It is one of the most active fields of research in astrophysics.

Deep-field images reveal galaxies at many stages of evolution.

Formation

In the prevailing Lambda-CDM model, small density fluctuations present after the Big Bang grew under gravity. Gas fell into dark matter halos, cooled, and formed the first stars and protogalaxies within the first billion years. Two broad pictures were historically debated: the "top-down" monolithic collapse model of Eggen, Lynden-Bell, and Sandage (1962), and the "bottom-up" hierarchical model of Searle and Zinn (1978), in which large galaxies assemble from smaller fragments. Modern evidence favours hierarchical assembly within dark matter cosmology.

Evolution

Galaxies evolve through mergers, accretion of gas, star formation, and feedback from supernovae and active galactic nuclei. Collisions between spiral galaxies can transform them into ellipticals; the Milky Way will eventually merge with the Andromeda Galaxy. Star formation in most massive galaxies declined after "cosmic noon", around 10 billion years ago, and many galaxies are now "quenched" — a process linked to environment, mass, and black hole feedback.

Observations

Observations of the Hubble Deep Field and Ultra Deep Field, and now of very early galaxies by the James Webb Space Telescope, allow astronomers to watch galaxy populations evolve directly across most of cosmic history, testing simulations such as Illustris and EAGLE.
