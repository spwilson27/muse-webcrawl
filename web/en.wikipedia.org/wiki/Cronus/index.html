In Greek mythology, Cronus (Ancient Greek: Κρόνος, Krónos) was the leader of the first generation of Titans, the divine descendants of the primordial Gaia (Earth) and Uranus (Sky). He overthrew his father Uranus and ruled during the mythological Golden Age, until he was in turn overthrown by his own son Zeus and imprisoned in Tartarus. Cronus was usually depicted with a sickle or scythe, the weapon with which he castrated and deposed Uranus. His Roman equivalent is Saturn.

Mythology

In Hesiod's Theogony, Gaia, angered that Uranus hid her children within her, fashioned a great flint sickle and persuaded Cronus, the youngest and "most terrible" of the Titans, to ambush his father. From the blood of Uranus sprang the Erinyes, the Giants and the Meliae; from his severed genitals cast into the sea arose Aphrodite.

Warned by a prophecy that he would be overthrown by his own child, Cronus swallowed each of his children by his sister-wife Rhea at birth: Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hades and Poseidon. When Zeus was born, Rhea hid him in Crete and gave Cronus a stone wrapped in swaddling clothes, which he swallowed instead. Grown to manhood, Zeus — with the help of Metis, whose draught made Cronus disgorge the swallowed children and the stone — waged the ten-year war called the Titanomachy. The Titans were defeated and cast into Tartarus, guarded by the Hecatoncheires. The stone Cronus had swallowed was set up at Delphi as the Omphalos.

Golden Age and festivals

Cronus's reign was remembered as a Golden Age in which the earth gave abundance without toil. At Athens the festival of Kronia honoured him as a god of the harvest; its Roman counterpart, Saturnalia, was a season of feasting and role reversal.
