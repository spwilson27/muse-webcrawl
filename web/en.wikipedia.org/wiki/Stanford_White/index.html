Stanford White

Born: 9 November 1853, Union Square, New York City
Died: 25 June 1906 (aged 52), New York City
Occupation: Architect
Firm: McKim, Mead & White

Stanford White (November 9, 1853 – June 25, 1906) was an American architect and a partner in the firm McKim, Mead & White, one of the most significant American architectural firms of the Gilded Age. He designed houses for the wealthy — including the Newport "cottages" of the Vanderbilts — as well as clubs, universities, and public monuments.

Works

Among White's best-known designs are the second Madison Square Garden (1890), crowned by a tower modelled on the Giralda of Seville and a statue of Diana by Augustus Saint-Gaudens; the Washington Square Arch in New York; the New York Herald Building; the Boston Public Library (with his partners); the campus of the University of Virginia, which he largely rebuilt after fire; Columbia University's Low Memorial Library; and numerous country houses such as Rosecliff in Newport, Rhode Island. White was also a collector and designer of interiors and decorative arts, and a central figure in New York social and artistic life.

Murder

White was shot and killed on 25 June 1906 by the wealthy eccentric Harry Kendall Thaw, on the rooftop theatre of Madison Square Garden during a performance of the musical Mam'zelle Champagne. Thaw's wife, the model and actress Evelyn Nesbit, had been romantically involved with White as a teenager, and Thaw claimed to be avenging her. The resulting trials — the first ending in a hung jury, the second finding Thaw not guilty by reason of insanity — were sensationalised as the "Trial of the Century."
