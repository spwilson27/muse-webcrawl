Peter Ward (paleontologist)

Born: Peter Douglas Ward, 1949, United States

Field: Paleontology, astrobiology

Institution: University of Washington

Known for: Mass extinction research; Rare Earth; Medea hypothesis

Peter Douglas Ward (born 1949) is an American paleontologist and professor of biology and of Earth and space sciences at the University of Washington, specialising in mass extinctions, ammonites and astrobiology.

Work

Ward's field research has focused on the Cretaceous–Paleogene and Permian–Triassic extinction events, including work on the Hell Creek and Karoo sequences. With astronomer Donald Brownlee he co-authored Rare Earth: Why Complex Life Is Uncommon in the Universe (2000), which argues that microbial life may be widespread in the universe but complex animal life is rare — the Rare Earth hypothesis. Their follow-up The Life and Death of Planet Earth (2003) considered Earth's far future.

In The Medea Hypothesis (2009) Ward proposed a counterpoint to the Gaia hypothesis: that Earth's biosphere, left to itself, tends toward self-destructive outcomes — life as "Medea" rather than a nurturing mother. His earlier book Under a Green Sky (2007) argued that most past mass extinctions were driven by greenhouse warming and ocean anoxia, with implications for present climate change. Ward has written numerous popular books, including Rivers in Time and a biography of the coelacanth search, and he has worked extensively on living nautiluses.
