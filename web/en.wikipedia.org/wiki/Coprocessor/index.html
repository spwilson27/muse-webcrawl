Coprocessor

A coprocessor is a computer processor used to supplement the functions of the primary processor (the CPU). Operations performed by the coprocessor may be floating-point arithmetic, graphics, signal processing, string processing, cryptography or I/O interfacing with peripheral devices. By offloading processor-intensive tasks from the main CPU, coprocessors can accelerate system performance, and they can sometimes be used by themselves to do what the CPU cannot.

History

Mainframe computers used channel controllers to offload I/O as early as the 1950s. In microcomputers, the Intel 8087 floating-point coprocessor for the 8086/8088 family became famous in the 1980s, greatly speeding up floating-point computation on PCs; subsequent Intel processors integrated the FPU on-chip from the 80486DX onward. Graphics processing units began as graphics coprocessors and accelerators before becoming programmable parallel processors in their own right.

Types

Examples of coprocessors include floating-point units, graphics processing units, digital signal processors, network processors, cryptographic accelerators, and Apple's motion and neural coprocessors in mobile devices. Modern systems-on-a-chip integrate many specialized coprocessors managed by the main CPU.

Programming

Coprocessors typically expose special instruction extensions, memory-mapped interfaces, or driver APIs. Heterogeneous computing frameworks such as CUDA and OpenCL allow general-purpose computation on graphics coprocessors.
