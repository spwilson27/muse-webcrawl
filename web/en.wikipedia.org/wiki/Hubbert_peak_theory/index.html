Hubbert peak theory

Hubbert peak theory
ProponentM. King Hubbert
Introduced1956
Key conceptPeak oil

The Hubbert peak theory says that for any given geographical area, from an individual oil-producing region to the planet as a whole, the rate of petroleum production tends to follow a bell-shaped curve. It is one of the primary theories on peak oil.

Choosing a particular curve determines a point of maximum production based on discovery rates, production rates and cumulative production. Early in the curve (pre-peak), the production rate increases due to an increase in the discovery rate and the addition of infrastructure. Late in the curve (post-peak), production declines because of resource depletion.

Origin

American geophysicist M. King Hubbert, working for Shell, first presented the model in his 1956 paper "Nuclear Energy and the Fossil Fuels", delivered to the American Petroleum Institute. Hubbert used a logistic model of production and correctly forecast that United States oil production would peak between 1965 and 1971; US conventional crude output in fact peaked in 1970.

The Hubbert curve

The Hubbert curve is the derivative of a logistic function fitted to cumulative production. Empirically, production from a field or region tends to lag discovery by several decades, and cumulative discoveries also follow a logistic path, so the production curve roughly mirrors the discovery curve with a time offset. Hubbert linearisation — plotting the ratio of annual to cumulative production against cumulative production — gives a straight line whose intercept estimates the ultimately recoverable resource.

Applications and debate

The method has been applied to individual fields, countries, global conventional oil, natural gas, coal, and other non-renewable resources, and has been extended metaphorically to resources such as whale oil and to renewable harvesting. Critics note that prices, technology (such as hydraulic fracturing, which drove US output to new highs in the 2010s), demand changes and unconventional reserves can shift or multiply peaks, so many modern forecasts model plateaus or multiple peaks rather than a single symmetric bell.
