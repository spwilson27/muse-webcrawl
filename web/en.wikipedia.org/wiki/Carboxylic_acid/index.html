Carboxylic acid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A carboxylic acid is an organic acid that contains a carboxyl group (–C(=O)–OH) attached to an R-group. The general formula is R–COOH or R–CO2H. Carboxylic acids occur widely in nature and industry: from formic acid in ant venom and acetic acid in vinegar to the fatty acids of lipids and the amino acids that build proteins.

Properties

Carboxylic acids are polar, hydrogen-bonding molecules that usually exist as dimers in the liquid and solid states, giving them higher boiling points than comparable alcohols or aldehydes. They are weak acids (pKa typically 4–5) that dissociate in water to give carboxylate anions; the anion is resonance-stabilised, which accounts for acidity far greater than that of alcohols. Short-chain acids are miscible with water and have sharp, often rancid odours — butyric acid is the smell of rancid butter — while longer-chain fatty acids are waxy, poorly soluble solids.

Nomenclature

IUPAC names replace the -e of the parent alkane with -oic acid: methanoic (formic) acid, ethanoic (acetic) acid, benzoic acid for the simplest aromatic member. Molecules with two carboxyl groups are dicarboxylic acids (e.g. oxalic, succinic); citric acid is a tricarboxylic acid central to the citric acid cycle.

Reactions and uses

Carboxylic acids form salts with bases (soaps are sodium salts of fatty acids), esters with alcohols (Fischer esterification), amides with amines, and acid chlorides with reagents such as thionyl chloride; reduction gives primary alcohols, and decarboxylation removes the group as CO2. Salts and esters of carboxylic acids are widely used as food preservatives, buffers, polymers (polyesters, polyamides) and pharmaceuticals — aspirin is the acetyl ester of salicylic acid.
