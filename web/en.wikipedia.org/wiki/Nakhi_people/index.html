Nakhi people

The Nakhi (also spelled Naxi; Chinese: 纳西族; pinyin: Nàxīzú) are an ethnic group native to the foothills of the Himalayas in the northwestern part of Yunnan Province, as well as the southwestern part of Sichuan Province, in China. They form one of the 56 ethnic groups officially recognised by the People's Republic of China, with a population of roughly 300,000–330,000.

History

The Nakhi are believed to descend from the nomadic Qiang tribes who migrated south from northwestern China. Their historical centre is Lijiang, whose old town — a UNESCO World Heritage Site — was ruled for centuries by the Mu family of Nakhi chieftains under the Ming and Qing dynasties. The Nakhi maintained a written culture unusually rich for a group of their size, and Lijiang became a flourishing trading post on the Tea Horse Road.

Culture

Nakhi society is traditionally patrilineal around Lijiang, in contrast to their close relatives the Mosuo of the Lugu Lake region, who are famously matrilineal. Nakhi music, including the ancient Baisha and Dongjing orchestras preserving Tang- and Song-dynasty forms, is celebrated; the Austrian-American botanist and explorer Joseph Rock lived among the Nakhi for decades in the early 20th century and documented their culture extensively.

Dongba script and religion

The Nakhi are renowned for the Dongba script, a pictographic writing system of roughly 1,400 glyphs used by Dongba priests to record religious texts — often described as the only pictographic script still in use in the world. A syllabic script, Geba, supplements it. The Dongba religion blends Bon, shamanism, and Tibetan Buddhist influences, centred on nature worship and the relationship between humans and the natural world, personified by the god Shu. The Dongba manuscripts were inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 2003.
