English literature

English literature is literature written in the English language from the English-speaking world. It begins with the heroic poetry of Anglo-Saxon England — most famously Beowulf — and encompasses Geoffrey Chaucer, William Shakespeare, John Milton, the rise of the novel in the eighteenth century, the Romantic poets, the great Victorian novelists, and the modernist and postcolonial writing of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. As English spread worldwide, the literature came to include major traditions from Ireland, the United States, India, Africa, the Caribbean and beyond, though those are often treated under their national headings as well.

From Beowulf to the modern novel, English literature spans more than a thousand years.

Old and Middle English

Old English literature (c. 450–1066) includes the epic Beowulf, the elegies of the Exeter Book ("The Wanderer", "The Seafarer"), the religious verse of Cædmon and Cynewulf, and the prose of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, Ælfric and Wulfstan. After the Norman Conquest, Middle English literature flowered in the fourteenth century with Geoffrey Chaucer's The Canterbury Tales, the alliterative Pearl and Sir Gawain and the Green Knight, William Langland's Piers Plowman, the mystic writings of Julian of Norwich, and, at the period's end, Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur (1485).

Renaissance and seventeenth century

The English Renaissance produced the sonnets and plays of William Shakespeare (1564–1616), the central figure of the canon, alongside Marlowe, Jonson, Webster, Edmund Spenser's The Faerie Queene, Philip Sidney's Arcadia, and the metaphysical poetry of John Donne and George Herbert. The King James Bible (1611) shaped English prose for centuries. John Milton's epic Paradise Lost (1667) and John Bunyan's allegory The Pilgrim's Progress (1678) crowned the century's religious writing, while Restoration comedy (Congreve, Wycherley) revived the theatre after 1660.

The eighteenth century and Romanticism

The novel emerged as the dominant form with Defoe's Robinson Crusoe (1719), Richardson's Pamela (1740), Fielding, Sterne and Smollett; satire flourished in Swift's Gulliver's Travels (1726) and Pope's verse; Samuel Johnson's Dictionary (1755) and his criticism set standards of taste. Romanticism (c. 1785–1832) — Wordsworth and Coleridge's Lyrical Ballads (1798), Byron, Shelley, Keats, Blake, and the novels of Jane Austen and Walter Scott — redefined poetry and the self across Europe.

Victorian to contemporary

The Victorian age made the novel supreme: Dickens, the Brontës, George Eliot, Thackeray, Trollope, Hardy and Henry James, with Tennyson and Browning in verse. Modernism after 1900 — Virginia Woolf, James Joyce (Irish), T. S. Eliot, D. H. Lawrence — revolutionised form; post-war and contemporary writing ranges from the theatre of Pinter and Stoppard to the global English novel of Rushdie, Achebe (Nigerian), Walcott (Caribbean) and Ishiguro, reflecting English's worldwide reach and its postcolonial transformations.
