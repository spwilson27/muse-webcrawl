Daylight saving time (DST), also called summer time, is the practice of advancing clocks during warmer months so that darkness falls at a later clock time, and setting them back again in autumn. Typically, clocks are set forward by one hour in spring ("spring forward") and back by one hour in autumn ("fall back"), transferring an hour of daylight from the morning to the evening.

History

The idea is often traced to Benjamin Franklin's satirical 1784 essay suggesting Parisians rise earlier to save candles, but the modern proposal came from New Zealand entomologist George Vernon Hudson (1895), who wanted evening light for insect collecting, and from English builder William Willett, whose 1907 pamphlet The Waste of Daylight campaigned tirelessly until his death. The first national implementation came during the First World War: Germany and Austria-Hungary adopted DST in April 1916 to save coal, Britain and other belligerents followed within weeks, and the United States in 1918.

After both world wars the practice was patchy and controversial — imposed during emergencies, repealed in peacetime, and varying chaotically between US localities — until the US Uniform Time Act of 1966 and European harmonisation from the 1980s standardised dates. The energy crises of the 1970s revived DST as a fuel-saving measure.

Rationale and effects

The original justifications — saving fuel and giving workers evening daylight — have mixed empirical support. Modern studies find energy savings near zero in many contexts; some find increases from air conditioning. Research documents other costs and benefits: sleep disruption and small spikes in heart attacks, strokes and road accidents after the spring change; reductions in evening crime and road deaths during DST months; and benefits to retail, sport and tourism. Farmers, the canonical supposed beneficiaries, have historically often opposed it.

Current status and debates

Roughly 70 countries observe DST at some point, though fewer than 40% of the world's countries do so and only a minority of the world population experiences a clock change. Several countries — including Russia (2014), Turkey (2016) and others — have abandoned seasonal changes, most opting for permanent "summer" time. The European Union voted in 2019 to end mandatory seasonal changes but implementation stalled; in the United States the Senate passed the Sunshine Protection Act in 2022 to make DST permanent, though it was not enacted. Health bodies generally favour permanent standard time as better aligned with human circadian rhythms, while many businesses prefer permanent daylight time.
