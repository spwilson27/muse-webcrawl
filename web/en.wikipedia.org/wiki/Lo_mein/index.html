Lo mein

Lo mein (traditional Chinese: 撈麵; simplified Chinese: 捞面; pinyin: lāo miàn; Cantonese Yale: louh mihn) is a Chinese dish with soft noodles that are tossed with a flavorful sauce and ingredients such as vegetables and meat or seafood. The name comes from the Cantonese louh mihn, meaning "stirred noodles". Lo mein is a popular dish in Chinese-American cuisine and in Cantonese cooking.

Etymology

The term lo mein derives from the Cantonese 撈麵 (louh mihn), literally "stirred" or "tossed" noodles. In Cantonese, louh refers to the act of mixing or scooping up, describing how the noodles are tossed with sauce after boiling.

Preparation

Lo mein noodles are typically fresh egg noodles made from wheat flour. The noodles are boiled briefly until just tender, drained, and then tossed — rather than stir-fried — with a sauce commonly made from soy sauce, oyster sauce, sesame oil, sugar, and sometimes dark soy sauce for color. Common additions include bok choy, cabbage, carrots, bean sprouts, mushrooms, scallions, and proteins such as beef, pork (often char siu), chicken, shrimp, or wontons.

Lo mein versus chow mein

Lo mein is often contrasted with chow mein. In chow mein ("fried noodles"), the noodles are stir-fried in a wok, sometimes until crisp, whereas lo mein noodles are fully boiled and merely tossed with hot sauce and briefly cooked ingredients. In Cantonese usage, a premium version is served with the sauce and wontons on the side ("dry" lo mein), with soup served separately.

Regional variations

In Hong Kong, ginger and scallion lo mein (geung chung louh mihn) is a classic accompaniment to wonton noodle soup. Beef lo mein and shrimp lo mein are staples of Chinese-American take-out menus. In northern China, similar tossed noodle dishes are made with different wheat noodles and heavier use of vinegar and garlic.
