Palais des Papes

The Palais des Papes ("Palace of the Popes") is a fortified palace in Avignon, in the south of France, and one of the largest and most important medieval Gothic buildings in Europe. Built principally between 1335 and 1352 under Popes Benedict XII and Clement VI, it was the seat of Western Christianity during the Avignon Papacy, when seven successive popes resided in Avignon rather than Rome. With the neighbouring cathedral and the Pont Saint-Bénézet, it forms part of a UNESCO World Heritage site.

History

The papal court settled in Avignon from 1309. Benedict XII, a former Cistercian monk, replaced the old episcopal palace with the austerely fortified Palais Vieux (Old Palace), complete with cloister and the great Tour de la Campane. His successor Clement VI, a lavish patron, added the Palais Neuf (New Palace) with the Grande Chapelle and the audience hall, and commissioned frescoes from Matteo Giovannetti and the Italian masters of the Avignon school — including the famous naturalistic hunting and fishing scenes of the Chambre du Cerf.

Gregory XI returned the papacy to Rome in 1377, but during the Western Schism the antipopes Clement VII and Benedict XIII held Avignon, and the palace withstood sieges before passing to papal legates. After the French Revolution the palace became a barracks and prison; its military use, ironically, preserved much of the fabric. Restoration began in the early twentieth century, and since 1947 the palace has hosted exhibitions and performances of the Festival d'Avignon, whose main stage is the Cour d'Honneur.

Architecture

Rising on the Rocher des Doms above the Rhône, the palace is a fortress as much as a residence: walls up to several metres thick, machicolated towers and a single well-defended approach. Inside, the vast vaulted halls — among them the Grand Tinel banqueting hall, 41 m long — the papal apartments, treasury and library (then the largest in Europe) testify to the scale of the fourteenth-century papal court, which employed hundreds of officials and consumed a large share of Christendom's revenues.
