The 1931 China floods, or the 1931 Yangtze–Huai River floods, were a series of devastating floods that occurred in the Republic of China in 1931. They are considered among the deadliest floods — and among the deadliest natural disasters — in recorded history. The Yangtze, Yellow River and Huai River basins flooded during an exceptionally wet summer following a period of drought, and the city of Wuhan stood under water for months. Estimates of the total death toll, including deaths from disease and famine in the aftermath, range from about 145,000 to as many as 3.7 million or more.

Causes

From 1928 to 1930 China suffered a severe drought. The winter of 1930–31 brought heavy snow, followed by intense spring thaw and an abnormally active rainy season: nine cyclones struck the Yangtze basin in July 1931 alone, compared with an average of two per year. River levels rose far above normal; at Hankou the Yangtze reached 53 feet (16 m) above its average level. Deforestation, silted channels and neglected dikes — consequences of years of civil war — worsened the catastrophe.

Course and impact

Flooding peaked in July and August 1931. On 25 August the Grand Canal's Gaoyou Lake dike burst, drowning thousands of people in their sleep in a single night. Wuhan's tri-cities were submerged for up to four metres and remained flooded from August into October. An area of about 180,000 km² was inundated across several provinces, displacing tens of millions; roughly 52 million people were affected in some accounts. Crop failure brought famine to the flooded regions even into 1932, and cholera, typhoid and other diseases spread through the refugee camps.

Response

The Nationalist government of Chiang Kai-shek established the National Flood Relief Commission, directed relief works under figures such as the engineer Li Yiqing, and sought loans and wheat from the United States. Relief work — including major dike reconstruction — was one of the young Republic's largest administrative undertakings. Even so, resources were diverted by the campaigns against the Communists and, from September 1931, by the Japanese invasion of Manchuria following the Mukden Incident, which overshadowed the flood in international attention.
