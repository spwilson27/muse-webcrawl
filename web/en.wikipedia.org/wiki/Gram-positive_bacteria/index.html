Gram-positive bacteria

Gram-positive bacteria are bacteria classified by their response to the Gram stain: they retain the crystal violet stain and appear purple under a microscope. The defining feature of the group is a thick cell wall composed largely of peptidoglycan, which retains the dye after an alcohol wash.

Characteristics

The Gram-positive cell wall consists of many layers of peptidoglycan, threaded with teichoic acids, which give the wall a negative charge and play roles in cell growth and ion regulation. Because there is no outer membrane, Gram-positive bacteria are generally more susceptible than Gram-negative bacteria to antibiotics such as penicillin that target peptidoglycan synthesis, though resistance has become widespread in several species.

Classification

Historically the Gram-positive bacteria were grouped in the phylum Firmicutes (now Bacillota), which includes well-known genera such as Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Bacillus and Clostridium, along with the high-GC Actinobacteria (Actinomycetota), which includes Mycobacterium and Streptomyces.

Medical significance

Many important human pathogens are Gram-positive, including Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and the spore-forming Clostridioides difficile. Others are beneficial or industrially important, such as the lactic acid bacteria used in food fermentation.
