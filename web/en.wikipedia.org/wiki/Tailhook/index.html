Tailhook

A tailhook, also known as an arrestor hook, is a device attached to the empennage (tail) of an aircraft, primarily naval aircraft designed to operate from aircraft carriers. The hook is used to achieve rapid deceleration during an arrested landing: as the aircraft touches down, the hook snags one of the arresting wires stretched across the flight deck, and the gear absorbs the aircraft's kinetic energy, stopping it in a short distance.

Design and operation

The tailhook is a sturdy metal bar with a hook-shaped fitting at the end, mounted on a pivot at the rear of the airframe and extended by hydraulic or pneumatic pressure for landing, pressing the hook against the deck so that it skips along the surface until it catches a wire. Because the hook must engage on essentially every landing, carrier landings are flown as controlled arrivals at full or near-full power, allowing the pilot to go around ("bolter") if no wire is caught. Hooks are inspected and replaced frequently because of the extreme loads and abrasion involved.

Other uses

Some land-based military aircraft also carry tailhooks for emergency use with runway arresting systems — for example, to stop an aircraft after a brake failure, engine problem on takeoff, or other emergency — engaging a cable rigged across the runway. Such hooks are generally lighter than naval tailhooks and are not stressed for repeated carrier use. Aircraft equipped with tailhooks include many U.S. Air Force fighters.

Tailhook Association and the 1991 scandal

The device lends its name to the Tailhook Association, a naval aviation fraternity whose 1991 convention in Las Vegas became the centre of a major scandal after numerous attendees, mostly women, reported being sexually assaulted by naval and Marine officers. The resulting investigations and political fallout affected careers at the highest levels of the U.S. Navy and accelerated policy changes on women in combat aviation.
