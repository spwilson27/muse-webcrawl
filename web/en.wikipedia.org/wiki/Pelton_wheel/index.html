Pelton wheel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Pelton wheel or Pelton turbine is an impulse-type water turbine invented by the American engineer Lester Allan Pelton in the 1870s. The Pelton wheel extracts energy from the impulse of moving water, as opposed to water's dead weight like the traditional overshot water wheel. Many earlier variations of impulse turbines existed, but they were less efficient than Pelton's design.

Design

Water from a high-head source is delivered through a penstock to one or more nozzles, which convert pressure into a high-velocity free jet. The jet strikes a series of split, double-ellipsoidal buckets mounted around the rim of a runner. A central ridge — the splitter — divides the jet into two streams that are turned through almost 180 degrees, extracting nearly all of the water's kinetic energy and exiting to the sides at low velocity. Because the runner operates in air at atmospheric pressure, the Pelton design suits high head, low flow sites, with heads commonly from 60 to more than 1,000 metres.

Efficiency and use

Well-designed Pelton turbines reach efficiencies above 90%. Power is regulated by a spear valve varying the jet size and by deflectors that divert the jet when load falls. Pelton turbines are used at hydroelectric stations such as Bieudron in Switzerland, which uses three 423 MW Pelton units under a head of about 1,869 m — among the most powerful water turbines ever built. Small Pelton units are also common in micro-hydro installations in mountainous regions.
