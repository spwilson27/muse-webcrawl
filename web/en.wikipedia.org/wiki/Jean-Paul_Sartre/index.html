Jean-Paul Charles Aymard Sartre (1905–1980) was a French philosopher, playwright, novelist and political activist, the leading figure of existentialism. His slogan 'existence precedes essence' holds that human beings, unlike tools, have no pre-given nature: we are 'condemned to be free', making ourselves through choices for which we bear total responsibility — a burden Sartre calls anguish, and evasion of it 'bad faith'.

Life and work

Educated at the École Normale Supérieure, where he met Simone de Beauvoir, his lifelong partner, Sartre taught philosophy, served in the war, and was a prisoner of war in 1940–41. Being and Nothingness (1943) and the novel Nausea made his name; after the war his public lecture Existentialism Is a Humanism made him an international celebrity. He founded the journal Les Temps Modernes, supported (and quarrelled with) communism, backed Algerian independence, and in Critique of Dialectical Reason (1960) tried to fuse existentialism with Marxism. He refused the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1964. His funeral in 1980 drew tens of thousands onto the streets of Paris.
