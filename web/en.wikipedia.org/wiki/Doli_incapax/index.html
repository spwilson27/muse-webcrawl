The doctrine concerns children's criminal capacity

Doli incapax

Doli incapax is a Latin legal term meaning "incapable of wrong". In common law jurisdictions it describes the presumption that a child of a certain age cannot be held legally responsible for criminal acts because they lack the mental capacity to form criminal intent (mens rea).

Operation

Historically in England and Wales, children under seven were conclusively presumed incapable; between seven and fourteen the presumption applied but could be rebutted by the prosecution proving the child knew the act was seriously wrong. The rebuttable presumption for 10-to-13-year-olds was abolished in England and Wales by the Crime and Disorder Act 1998, leaving an absolute minimum age of criminal responsibility of 10. Debate about the doctrine intensified after the 1993 murder of James Bulger by two ten-year-olds.

Other jurisdictions

The doctrine survives in several Commonwealth jurisdictions, including Australia, where the High Court in RP v The Queen (2016) reaffirmed that the prosecution must prove beyond reasonable doubt that a child aged 10 to 13 understood the moral wrongness of the act, and in Hong Kong and parts of Canada in modified forms. Minimum ages of criminal responsibility vary worldwide, and the UN Committee on the Rights of the Child recommends a minimum age of at least 14.
