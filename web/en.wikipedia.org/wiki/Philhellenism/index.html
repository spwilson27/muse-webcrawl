Philhellenism

Philhellenism ("the love of Greek culture") is an intellectual movement and sympathy for Greek culture, history, and people that arose in Europe and the Americas, reaching its height in the early 19th century. It played an important role in generating support for Greek independence from the Ottoman Empire during the Greek War of Independence (1821–1829).

Origins

European admiration for classical Greece grew with the Enlightenment and the neoclassical movement. Writers such as Johann Joachim Winckelmann idealised Greek art and antiquity, while travellers, archaeologists, and the classical education of European elites fostered a sense of cultural debt to Greece. When the Greeks rose against Ottoman rule in 1821, philhellene committees formed in London, Paris, and German and American cities to raise money, recruit volunteers, and lobby governments.

The Greek War of Independence

Hundreds of foreign volunteers travelled to Greece to fight. The most famous philhellene was the poet Lord Byron, whose death at Missolonghi in 1824 became a symbol of the cause and galvanised public opinion. Other notable philhellenes included the French painter Eugène Delacroix, whose Massacre at Chios publicised Ottoman atrocities, and Dr Samuel Gridley Howe in America. Philhellenic pressure contributed to the eventual intervention of Britain, France, and Russia, sealed by the victory at the Battle of Navarino (1827) and the establishment of an independent Greek kingdom in 1830–1832.

Legacy

Philhellenism shaped European classical scholarship, museum collections, and architecture, and it remains a reference point in Greek foreign relations and cultural diplomacy.
