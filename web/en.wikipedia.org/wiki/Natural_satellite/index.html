Natural satellite

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia · Redirected from "Moon (satellite)"

A natural satellite is, in the most common usage, an astronomical body that orbits a planet, dwarf planet, or small Solar System body (or sometimes another natural satellite). Natural satellites are colloquially referred to as moons, a derivation from the Moon of Earth. In the Solar System, there are more than 890 known natural satellites orbiting planets and dwarf planets, with hundreds more orbiting minor planets.

Origin and characteristics

Natural satellites are thought to form either from the accretion disc that surrounded a young planet (regular satellites), by capture of an object passing nearby (irregular satellites), or from debris produced by a giant impact — the leading explanation for Earth's Moon. Moons range in size from tiny bodies a few kilometres across to Ganymede, a satellite of Jupiter that is larger than the planet Mercury.

Notable satellites

Besides Earth's Moon, famous satellites include the four Galilean moons of Jupiter — Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto, discovered by Galileo in 1610 — Saturn's large moon Titan, which has a thick nitrogen atmosphere and liquid methane lakes; Neptune's captured moon Triton; Saturn's Enceladus, which vents water plumes from a subsurface ocean; and Pluto's large companion Charon. Mercury and Venus have no natural satellites.

Tidal locking

Many satellites, including Earth's Moon, are tidally locked, rotating at the same rate at which they orbit so that the same face always points toward the parent body. Tidal interactions gradually transfer angular momentum; Earth's Moon is receding from Earth by about 3.8 cm per year.
