Diction

Diction (Latin: dictionem, "a saying, expression, word"), in its original meaning, is a writer's or speaker's distinctive vocabulary choices and style of expression in a poem or story. In its more common and narrower meaning today, diction also refers to clear and precise enunciation in spoken language.

In literature

Diction analysis examines the words an author selects — their formality, connotation, imagery and abstractness — to understand tone and meaning. The level of diction can be described as formal, informal, colloquial or slang, and particular choices of words (for example, Latinate versus Germanic vocabulary in English) shape a text's register and effect. Aristotle discussed the appropriate diction for rhetoric and poetry in the Rhetoric and Poetics, and classical rhetoric treated word choice (Latin elocutio) as a major division of the art.

In speech

In the spoken sense, good diction involves clear articulation of consonants and vowels so that speech is intelligible; it is trained in singing, acting and public speaking. Poor diction — mumbling or slurring — can impede communication even where word choice is apt.
