Kerma

The Western Deffufa at Kerma

Kerma was the capital city of the Kerma culture, one of the earliest complex societies in Africa, which flourished from around 2500 BC to 1500 BC in ancient Nubia, in present-day Sudan. The city was located on the eastern bank of the Nile, south of the third cataract, in the fertile Dongola Reach.

At its height, Kerma controlled a large kingdom that rivalled ancient Egypt, trading in gold, ivory, ebony, cattle, and other goods. The city is marked by the massive mudbrick temple known as the Western Deffufa, one of the oldest and largest mudbrick structures in sub-Saharan Africa, and by royal tumulus burials accompanied by hundreds of sacrificed retainers and rich grave goods, including fine pottery, bronze work, and faience.

Kerma culture periods

Archaeologists divide the culture into Early Kerma (c. 2500–2050 BC), Middle Kerma (c. 2050–1750 BC), and Classic Kerma (c. 1750–1500 BC), the period of greatest power, when Kerma allied at times with the Hyksos against Egypt. Around 1500 BC, the Egyptian pharaoh Thutmose I conquered Kerma, bringing Nubia under New Kingdom rule.

Excavations

The site was excavated by George Andrew Reisner in 1913–1916, who initially misattributed it as an Egyptian outpost, and later by Swiss teams under Charles Bonnet, whose work established Kerma as the centre of an indigenous African kingdom.
