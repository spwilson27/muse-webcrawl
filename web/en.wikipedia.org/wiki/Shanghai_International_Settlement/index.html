The Shanghai International Settlement (Chinese: 上海公共租界) was a foreign-controlled enclave in Shanghai that existed from 1863 to 1943. It arose from the merger of the British and American settlements established after the Treaty of Nanking (1842) opened Shanghai as a treaty port, and it was administered by the Shanghai Municipal Council, elected by foreign ratepayers, under the system of extraterritoriality. Although geographically part of China, it functioned for eighty years as a largely self-governing, multi-national city-state within Shanghai, distinct from the neighbouring French Concession.

History

Origins

Following the First Opium War, the 1845 Land Regulations set aside land north of the Chinese city for British residence. An American settlement formed across Suzhou Creek in Hongkou. During the Small Swords and Taiping upheavals of the 1850s, large numbers of Chinese refugees moved into the settlements, permanently changing their character. In 1863 the British and American settlements formally merged as the International Settlement.

Growth and heyday

Governed by the Municipal Council through the Land Regulations, the Settlement built roads, policing (the Shanghai Municipal Police), a volunteer defence force, and utilities. It became China's principal port and financial centre, lined along the Bund with banks, trading houses and hotels. Its population exceeded one million by the 1930s, overwhelmingly Chinese, though political power remained with a small foreign electorate; Chinese councillors were admitted only from 1928.

Conflict and end

The Settlement was the scene of the May Thirtieth Movement of 1925, sparked when Municipal Police fired on Chinese demonstrators. Japanese forces occupied the Settlement in December 1941 after Pearl Harbor. In 1943, by treaties with the collaborationist Reorganized National Government, Britain and the United States formally relinquished the Settlement, ending a century of extraterritorial privilege in Shanghai.

Administration

The Shanghai Municipal Council, elected annually by ratepayers, ran departments for police, public works, health and education, funded by land and wharfage taxes. Legal jurisdiction was fragmented: foreigners answered to their own consular courts under extraterritoriality, while Chinese residents fell under a Mixed Court until 1927.

Legacy

The Settlement's built heritage — the Bund's neoclassical skyline, garden suburbs, and institutions such as the North-China Daily News — shaped modern Shanghai, and its history remains a touchstone in Chinese narratives of the "century of humiliation" as well as in nostalgic accounts of cosmopolitan "Old Shanghai".
