The Upper Middle Rhine Valley (German: Oberes Mittelrheintal), also known as the Rhine Gorge, is a 65-kilometre stretch of the Middle Rhine between Bingen and Rüdesheim in the south and Koblenz in the north, in Germany. In 2002 it was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site as a cultural landscape of outstanding beauty and historical significance.

Geography

The Rhine here cuts a deep, narrow gorge through the Rhenish Slate Mountains. Steep slopes, terraced with vineyards, are crowned by more than 40 castles and fortresses built along the river over a period of about a thousand years. Principal towns include Bingen, Bacharach, Oberwesel, St. Goar, Boppard and Koblenz, where the Moselle joins the Rhine at the Deutsches Eck.

History

The valley has been a major transport artery between northern and southern Europe since prehistoric times. In the Middle Ages its princes and robber barons grew rich on river tolls, building the dense chain of castles for which the gorge is famous. The Romantic movement of the late 18th and 19th centuries made the valley an emblem of German landscape sentiment, popularised by poets and painters and by the legend of the Lorelei, the siren said to lure boatmen onto the rock of that name near St. Goarshausen.

Economy and culture

Viticulture, especially Riesling on the steep slate slopes, and tourism dominate the local economy, alongside continued heavy use of the river for freight. Railway lines run along both banks, and there are no bridges across the gorge between Mainz/Wiesbaden and Koblenz — traffic crosses by ferry.
