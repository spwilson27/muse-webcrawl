Solvay Conference

Participants of the fifth Solvay Conference, 1927

The Solvay Conferences (French: Congrès Solvay) are a series of scientific conferences in physics and chemistry held in Brussels, Belgium, sponsored by the Belgian industrialist Ernest Solvay. The early conferences, especially the first (1911) and fifth (1927), were pivotal events in the development of quantum mechanics and are among the most famous gatherings in the history of science.

History

Following a suggestion by Walther Nernst, Solvay funded an invitation-only conference on "Radiation and the Quanta," held at the Hôtel Métropole in Brussels from 30 October to 3 November 1911, chaired by Hendrik Lorentz. Attendees included Marie Curie, Max Planck, Albert Einstein, Ernest Rutherford, and Henri Poincaré. The success led Solvay to establish the International Solvay Institutes and a recurring series, interrupted by the two world wars.

The 1927 conference

The fifth Solvay Conference on Physics (October 1927), on "Electrons and Photons," is the most famous: of its 29 participants, 17 had won or would win Nobel Prizes. It was the setting for the Bohr–Einstein debates, in which Einstein challenged the completeness and consistency of quantum mechanics with thought experiments (summed up in his remark that God "does not play dice") and Niels Bohr defended the emerging Copenhagen interpretation. The group photograph of the attendees — including Einstein, Bohr, Curie, Planck, Heisenberg, Schrödinger, Dirac, Born, and de Broglie — is an iconic image in science.

Later conferences

Physics conferences have continued roughly every three years, covering topics from elementary particles to astrophysics and condensed matter; chemistry conferences, begun in 1922, run in parallel series. The proceedings remain a record of the state of fundamental science at each era.
