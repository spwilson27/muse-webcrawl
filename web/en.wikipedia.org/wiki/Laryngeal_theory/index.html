Laryngeal theory

The laryngeal theory is a hypothesis in Indo-European linguistics proposing that Proto-Indo-European (PIE) possessed a set of consonantal sounds — conventionally three, written *h₁, *h₂, and *h₃ — which disappeared in nearly all Indo-European languages but left detectable traces: they lengthened preceding vowels when lost, coloured adjacent vowels (*h₂ turned *e into *a; *h₃ turned *e into *o), and vocalised to vowels between consonants. The theory, first sketched by Ferdinand de Saussure in 1879 using purely internal reasoning, was dramatically confirmed when Jerzy Kuryłowicz showed in 1927 that Hittite preserved one of these sounds as the consonant ḫ.

Saussure's proposal

In his Mémoire sur le système primitif des voyelles dans les langues indo-européennes (1879), written at age 21, Saussure argued that the irregular alternations of long and short vowels in Indo-European roots (ablaut) could be systematised if roots underlying long vowels contained a following "sonantic coefficient" — an abstract sound that had since vanished. Hermann Möller later connected these coefficients with laryngeal sounds of Semitic languages, giving the theory its name, though the actual phonetic nature of the PIE laryngeals remains uncertain (they may have been glottal and pharyngeal fricatives or approximants).

Confirmation in Anatolian

When Hittite was deciphered in the early 20th century, it contained a consonant written ḫ in exactly the positions where Saussure's theory predicted a lost coefficient — for instance in ḫanti "front" (compare Latin ante) and the root peḫ- "protect". Kuryłowicz's 1927 demonstration transformed the theory from elegant speculation into the consensus framework of Indo-European reconstruction. Modern reconstructions accordingly posit three laryngeals: *h₁ (colourless, probably [h] or [ʔ]), *h₂ (a-colouring, probably [χ] or [ħ]), and *h₃ (o-colouring, its voiced/labialised counterpart). In Greek, the vocalised laryngeals appear as e, a, o respectively — the "triple reflex" visible in words such as patḗr "father" (*ph₂tḗr).

Consequences

The theory reshaped PIE phonology: it explained apparent vowel alternations as regular ablaut of *e, reduced the reconstructed vowel system, and accounted for the PIE root-structure constraint (roots of shape CeC), the origin of long vowels, the Greek prothetic vowels, and features of Balto-Slavic accentuation (such as the acute register). Debates continue over the exact number of laryngeals (two to four have been defended) and their phonetic values.
