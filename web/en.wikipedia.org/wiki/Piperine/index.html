Piperine, along with its isomer chavicine, is the alkaloid responsible for the pungency of black pepper and long pepper. It has been used in some forms of traditional medicine.

History

Piperine was discovered in 1819 by Hans Christian Ørsted, who isolated it from the fruits of Piper nigrum, the source plant of both the black and white pepper grains. Piperine was also found in Piper longum and Piper officinarum (now called Piper retrofractum), two species called "long pepper".

Chemistry

Piperine is a weak base that forms salts only with strong acids. It is sparingly soluble in water but readily soluble in alcohol, ether, and essential oils. On hydrolysis it yields piperidine and piperic acid. Commercially, piperine is extracted from black pepper, which contains between five and nine percent of the alkaloid.

Pungency

Like capsaicin in chili peppers, piperine produces its burning sensation by activating the heat- and acidity-sensing TRPV1 ion channel on nociceptors. Piperine is, however, roughly one hundred times less pungent than capsaicin; on the Scoville scale it rates about 100,000 units.

Biological effects

Piperine is a known inhibitor of drug-metabolizing enzymes such as CYP3A4 and of the efflux transporter P-glycoprotein, which can markedly increase the bioavailability of other compounds — most famously curcumin, whose absorption it enhances many-fold. It is marketed as a dietary supplement, sometimes under the name BioPerine.
