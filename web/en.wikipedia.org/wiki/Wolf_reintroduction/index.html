Wolf reintroduction

Wolf reintroduction is the deliberate release of wolves into areas from which they were previously extirpated, undertaken to restore their ecological role as apex predators. The best-known case is the reintroduction of grey wolves to Yellowstone National Park and central Idaho in 1995–96, which became a landmark in conservation biology — and a continuing source of political controversy.

Background

Wolves were systematically eliminated from most of the United States and much of Europe by the early 20th century through government-sponsored predator control. By the 1970s, when the grey wolf was listed under the US Endangered Species Act, only a few hundred wolves survived in the contiguous United States, mainly in northern Minnesota. Natural recovery began in the northern Rockies in the 1980s as wolves dispersed south from Canada.

Yellowstone and Idaho

In January 1995, 14 wolves captured in Alberta were released into Yellowstone National Park, with 17 more following in 1996; a further 35 were released in Idaho over the same two years. The population grew rapidly, and by the early 2000s the northern Rockies met federal recovery goals. Wolves were delisted in various states in the following years, and managed hunting seasons were introduced in Montana, Idaho, and Wyoming. Colorado voters approved a ballot measure in 2020 requiring wolf reintroduction west of the Continental Divide, and the first releases took place there in December 2023.

Ecological effects

The Yellowstone reintroduction is widely cited as a trophic cascade: by preying on elk and altering their behaviour, wolves are said to have allowed willow and aspen recovery along streams, benefiting beavers, songbirds, and scavengers. The size of the effect is debated among ecologists, since elk declines also reflected drought, other predators, and hunting. Wolves also kill livestock and hunting dogs, driving compensation schemes and persistent conflict with ranchers and hunters.

Elsewhere

In Europe, wolves have recolonised much of their former range naturally from refuges in Italy, the Balkans, and eastern Europe, reaching France, Germany, the Low Countries, and Denmark. Rewilding proposals for Scotland remain contentious and unimplemented.
