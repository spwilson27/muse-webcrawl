Common-pool resource

In economics, a common-pool resource (CPR) is a type of good consisting of a natural or human-made resource (for example an irrigation system or fishing grounds), the size or characteristics of which makes it costly, but not impossible, to exclude potential users from obtaining benefits from its use. Unlike pure public goods, common-pool resources face problems of congestion or overuse, because they are subtractable (rivalrous).

Characteristics

Common-pool resources are characterized by two attributes: difficulty of exclusion and subtractability of use. Examples include fisheries, groundwater basins, grazing lands, irrigation systems, and the atmosphere as a sink for greenhouse gases. Because one user's extraction reduces what is available to others, unmanaged CPRs are vulnerable to overexploitation — the situation Garrett Hardin popularized as the "tragedy of the commons" in 1968.

Governance

Elinor Ostrom's influential work Governing the Commons (1990) showed that many communities successfully manage common-pool resources through locally devised institutions, without either state control or privatization. Ostrom identified design principles found in long-enduring CPR institutions, including clearly defined boundaries, monitoring, graduated sanctions, and conflict-resolution mechanisms. She shared the 2009 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for this work.
