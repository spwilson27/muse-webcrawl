Great Northern route

The Great Northern route was the northernmost transcontinental railroad route in the United States, built by the Great Northern Railway under James J. Hill between Saint Paul, Minnesota, and Seattle, Washington. Completed in 1893, it was the fifth transcontinental line and the northernmost of them, and it was notable for having been built largely without federal land grants.

Construction

James J. Hill, a Canadian-American railroad magnate known as "The Empire Builder," assembled the route by acquiring and extending the St. Paul, Minneapolis and Manitoba Railway westward across the northern Great Plains and the Rocky Mountains. His crews crossed Marias Pass in Montana in 1890—a pass Hill's scouts had sought for years—which gave the line the lowest crossing of the Continental Divide of any northern transcontinental route, at 5,213 feet (1,589 m). The main line reached Seattle in 1893, crossing the Cascade Range initially by switchbacks and later by the Cascade Tunnel (1900; replaced by the longer New Cascade Tunnel in 1929, at 7.8 miles the longest railroad tunnel in the United States at the time).

Characteristics

Hill promoted settlement and trade along the line, encouraging farming, mining, and timber development in the northern tier states, and pursued Asian trade through Pacific ports. Unlike other transcontinentals, the Great Northern remained financially sound through the panics of the 1890s. The route's profile—low grades and the favorable Marias Pass crossing—gave it operating advantages for freight traffic.

Legacy

The Great Northern merged in 1970 with the Northern Pacific, Chicago, Burlington and Quincy, and Spokane, Portland and Seattle railways to form Burlington Northern, a predecessor of today's BNSF Railway, which still operates over much of the route. Amtrak's Empire Builder passenger train follows the Great Northern route between Chicago and Seattle/Portland, named in honor of Hill.
