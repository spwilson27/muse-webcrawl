Submarine canyon

A submarine canyon is a steep-sided valley cut into the seabed of the continental slope, sometimes extending well onto the continental shelf, having nearly vertical walls, and occasionally having relief of up to 5 km (3.1 mi) from canyon floor to rim. Just as river canyons are carved on land, submarine canyons are eroded into the margin sediments and rock, and they serve as major conduits transporting sediment from the continents to the deep sea.

Formation

More than one mechanism shapes submarine canyons. During periods of low sea level, rivers extended across the exposed continental shelf and could cut directly into the slope. The dominant process today is erosion by turbidity currents — sediment-laden flows that rush down the slope, sometimes triggered by earthquakes, storms, or the collapse of accumulated sediment at a river mouth. Mass wasting, slumping, and internal waves also enlarge and maintain canyons.

Notable examples

  • Monterey Canyon, off California, comparable in scale to the Grand Canyon, beginning close to shore in Monterey Bay.
  • Hudson Canyon, off the coast of New York and New Jersey, aligned with the Hudson River.
  • Zhemchug Canyon in the Bering Sea, among the largest submarine canyons by volume.
  • Congo Canyon, extending from the mouth of the Congo River, notable for powerful, long-lasting turbidity currents recorded there.
  • Kaikōura Canyon, off New Zealand, known for its exceptional biological productivity.

Ecological importance

Canyons funnel nutrients and organic matter to the deep ocean and generate localized upwelling, supporting rich ecosystems including cold-water coral gardens, sponge grounds, and foraging grounds for whales and commercially important fish. Their heads can approach the coast closely enough to influence local wave patterns and hazard risks from submarine landslides and tsunamis.
