Morse theory

In mathematics, specifically in differential topology, Morse theory enables one to analyse the topology of a manifold by studying differentiable functions on that manifold. According to the basic insights of Marston Morse, a generic differentiable function on a manifold will reflect the topology quite directly. Morse theory allows one to find CW structures and handle decompositions on manifolds and to obtain substantial information about their homology.

Basic concepts

Given a smooth function f : M → ℝ on a manifold, points where the differential of f vanishes are called critical points. A function is a Morse function if all its critical points are nondegenerate, meaning the Hessian matrix of second derivatives is invertible at each critical point. The index of a critical point is the number of negative eigenvalues of the Hessian — informally, the number of independent directions in which the function decreases.

Morse inequalities

The central results relate the counts of critical points of each index to the topology of the manifold. If mk denotes the number of critical points of index k and bk the k-th Betti number, then mk ≥ bk, and the alternating sum of the mk equals the Euler characteristic of the manifold. A classic example is the height function on a torus standing on end: it has four critical points (a minimum, two saddles, and a maximum), matching the torus's Betti numbers 1, 2, 1.

History

Idea precursors appear in the work of Arthur Cayley and James Clerk Maxwell on mountain landscapes in the 19th century. Marston Morse developed the theory in the 1920s and 1930s, notably in his 1934 book The Calculus of Variations in the Large, applying it to problems such as the existence of infinitely many geodesics. Later developments include Bott periodicity, Smale's h-cobordism theorem, the Morse–Bott generalisation to functions with critical manifolds, and Andreas Floer's infinite-dimensional analogue, Floer homology.
