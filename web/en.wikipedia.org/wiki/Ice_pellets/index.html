Ice pellets

Ice pellets are a form of precipitation consisting of small, translucent balls of ice, usually no larger than 5 mm (0.2 in) in diameter. In the United States they are also known as sleet — a term that in the United Kingdom and Commonwealth usage instead refers to a mixture of rain and snow. Ice pellets are reported in METAR weather observations with the code PL.

Formation

Ice pellets form when snowflakes fall through a layer of warm air above freezing, partially or completely melting, and then pass into a deep subfreezing layer near the surface, where the droplets refreeze into solid pellets before reaching the ground. This temperature profile — a warm layer aloft sandwiched over colder air at the surface — is typical of the cold side of a winter storm's warm front. The pellets bounce on striking hard surfaces and can accumulate like snow, though they are denser and do not drift as readily.

Distinctions

Ice pellets differ from freezing rain, in which the melted droplets do not refreeze in the air and instead freeze on contact with surfaces, forming a glaze of ice. They differ from hail, which forms in thunderstorm updrafts during warm seasons and reaches much larger sizes, and from graupel (soft hail or snow pellets), which consists of opaque, soft, conical particles formed by riming of snowflakes.

Effects

Heavy accumulations of ice pellets can make roads treacherous, though generally less destructively than freezing rain, which brings down trees and power lines. Ice pellets are also a recognized aviation icing hazard.
