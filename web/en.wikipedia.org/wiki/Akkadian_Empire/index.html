Akkadian Empire

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Victory stele of Naram-Sin, showing the king ascending a mountain over defeated enemies]

The Akkadian Empire (c. 2334–2154 BC) was the first ancient empire of Mesopotamia, founded by Sargon of Akkad after he conquered the Sumerian city-states. From its capital, Akkad (not yet certainly located), Sargon's armies campaigned from the Mediterranean coast to the Persian Gulf, and his dynasty ruled for some 180 years. The empire made Akkad the centre of a Semitic-speaking realm whose language became the lingua franca of the ancient Near East.

Sargon and his successors

Sargon (r. c. 2334–2279 BC) rose, legend says, from cupbearer to the king of Kish. He defeated Lugal-zage-si of Uruk, unified Sumer and Akkad, appointed his daughter Enheduanna high priestess at Ur — she is the earliest author known by name — and installed Akkadian officials and garrisons across the realm. His grandson Naram-Sin (r. c. 2254–2218 BC) expanded the empire further and was the first Mesopotamian king to be deified in his lifetime.

Collapse and legacy

Under Shar-Kali-Sharri the empire fragmented amid revolts, Gutian invasions from the Zagros mountains and possibly severe drought; by about 2154 BC it had dissolved. Later Mesopotamian literature remembered Sargon and Naram-Sin as the twin models of the successful and the hubristic king, and Akkadian remained the diplomatic language of the Near East for over a thousand years.
