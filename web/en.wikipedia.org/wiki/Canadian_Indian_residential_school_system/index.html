The Canadian Indian residential school system was a network of boarding schools for Indigenous peoples in Canada. The network was funded by the Canadian government's Department of Indian Affairs and administered by Christian churches. The school system was created to isolate Indigenous children from the influence of their own culture and religion in order to assimilate them into the dominant Euro-Canadian culture, under the pretence of helping them adapt to life in their new country.

Over the system's hundred-year history, more than 150,000 First Nations, Métis and Inuit children were placed in residential schools nationally, typically by force. The schools were characterised by poor living conditions, harsh discipline, widespread physical, emotional and sexual abuse, and the suppression of Indigenous languages and cultures. At least 3,200 children, and likely far more, died while attending the schools, from disease, neglect, accidents and other causes. The last federally run school closed in 1997.

History

The system's roots lay in colonial mission schools of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. In the 19th century, the Canadian government adopted a policy of aggressive assimilation, articulated by officials such as Duncan Campbell Scott, who declared the object was "to continue until there is not a single Indian in Canada that has not been absorbed into the body politic." The 1876 Indian Act provided the legal framework; amendments in 1894 and 1920 made attendance compulsory for status Indian children. At the system's peak in 1931 there were 80 schools operating across Canada.

Children were usually taken from their families by police or Indian agents and transported far from home. They were issued uniforms, their hair was cut, they were given European names or numbers, and they were forbidden to speak their own languages. Half the day was typically spent in classroom instruction and half in manual labour — farming, laundry, sewing — that kept the chronically underfunded schools running. Parents who resisted faced fines and imprisonment.

Conditions and abuse

Survivors have testified to overcrowded and poorly built dormitories, inadequate and rotten food, insufficient clothing, and brutal punishments for speaking Indigenous languages or for ordinary misbehaviour. Medical care was poor, and tuberculosis and influenza killed children at rates far above those in the general population; some schools reported death rates of 30 to 60 percent of students in their early years. Sexual abuse by staff was widespread and seldom punished. The trauma produced intergenerational effects in Indigenous communities, including loss of language and parenting skills, addiction and elevated suicide rates.

Apologies and settlements

Beginning in the 1990s, survivors brought class-action lawsuits and churches began to apologise for their roles. In 2006 the Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement — the largest class-action settlement in Canadian history — provided compensation to survivors, established a healing fund, and created the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada (TRC). On 11 June 2008, Prime Minister Stephen Harper issued a formal apology to former students in the House of Commons. Pope Francis apologised for the Catholic Church's role during a visit to Canada in July 2022.

Truth and Reconciliation Commission

The TRC (2008–2015), chaired by Justice Murray Sinclair, gathered testimony from some 7,000 witnesses. Its final report concluded that the residential school system amounted to cultural genocide, documented at least 3,200 confirmed deaths of children (with the register later growing above 4,000 names), and issued 94 Calls to Action covering child welfare, education, language, health, justice and commemoration. The National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation in Winnipeg maintains the student memorial register.

Unmarked graves

In May 2021, the Tk'emlúps te Secwépemc First Nation announced that ground-penetrating radar had detected some 200 potential burial sites at the former Kamloops Indian Residential School. Announcements of hundreds more potential unmarked graves followed at other former school sites across the country, prompting national mourning, the lowering of flags, the creation of the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation (30 September), and renewed searches and investigations that continue.

Legacy

The residential school system is now recognised as one of the darkest chapters in Canadian history. Its history is taught in Canadian schools, memorials stand across the country, and 30 September — Orange Shirt Day, originating in survivor Phyllis Webstad's account of having her new orange shirt taken away on her first day at residential school — is a federal statutory holiday dedicated to reconciliation.
