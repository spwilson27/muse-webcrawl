Salivary gland

The salivary glands in many vertebrates, including mammals, are exocrine glands that produce saliva through a system of ducts. Humans have three paired major salivary glands (parotid, submandibular, and sublingual), as well as hundreds of minor salivary glands scattered through the mouth, lips, cheeks and palate. Saliva moistens food, begins the digestion of starches via the enzyme amylase, lubricates the mouth for speech and swallowing, and helps protect the teeth and oral mucosa.

Major glands

The parotid glands are the largest, lying in front of and below each ear; they secrete a watery, enzyme-rich saliva through the parotid duct into the cheek. The submandibular glands, beneath the floor of the mouth, produce a mixed serous and mucous secretion and account for the majority of resting saliva. The sublingual glands, under the tongue, secrete mainly mucous saliva. The enzyme content and flow of each gland are controlled largely by the autonomic nervous system: parasympathetic stimulation produces copious watery saliva, while sympathetic stimulation produces a smaller volume of thicker secretion.

Structure

Salivary glands are composed of secretory acini arranged in lobules, drained by a branching system of intercalated, striated and excretory ducts. Serous acinar cells produce a protein-rich fluid containing amylase; mucous cells produce viscous mucin. Myoepithelial cells around the acini contract to expel saliva into the duct system.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of a salivary gland is called sialadenitis; the parotid gland is classically swollen in mumps. Stones (sialoliths) can obstruct ducts, most often in the submandibular gland. Sjögren's syndrome is an autoimmune condition that destroys salivary and tear glands, causing dry mouth. Tumours of the salivary glands are most common in the parotid and are usually benign pleomorphic adenomas, though malignant tumours also occur.
