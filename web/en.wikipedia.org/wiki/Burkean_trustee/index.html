Burkean trustee

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Burkean trustee is a model of political representation drawn from Edmund Burke's Speech to the Electors of Bristol (3 November 1774). Against the claim that a representative is a mere ambassador bound by constituents' instructions, Burke argued that a member of Parliament owes voters not only his industry but his judgement, and betrays them if he sacrifices it to their opinion: “Your representative owes you, not his industry only, but his judgment; and he betrays, instead of serving you, if he sacrifices it to your opinion.” Parliament, he held, is a deliberative assembly of one nation, not a congress of ambassadors from hostile localities.

The trustee model contrasts with the delegate model and with imperative mandates (binding instructions with recall), which most constitutions prohibit. Modern practice blends them: representatives weigh constituency opinion, party programme and conscience in varying proportions, and defend departures from constituents' wishes by appeal to the national interest or superior information. Political scientists measure the trustee–delegate question through studies of roll-call voting and constituency influence.
