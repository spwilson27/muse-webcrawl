Shellac

Shellac is a resin secreted by the female lac bug (Kerria lacca) on trees in the forests of India and Thailand. It is processed and sold as dry flakes (pictured) and dissolved in alcohol to make liquid shellac, which is used as a brush-on colourant and wood finish. Shellac was once used in electrical applications — it was a good insulator and was used to manufacture 78 rpm gramophone records until they were replaced by vinyl records in the 1950s. In commerce and in food and pharmaceutical regulation, the raw resin is called lac.

Production

Lac insects colonise host trees, feeding on sap and secreting a resinous encrustation ("sticklac") over the twigs. The harvested sticklac is crushed, sieved, washed to remove insect remains and dye (seedlac), then melted, strained, and stretched into thin sheets that are broken into flakes. Bleached ("white") shellac is made by dissolving the resin in alkaline solution and decolourising it. The chief constituents are polyhydroxy polycarboxylic esters, principally aleuritic and jalaric acids.

Uses

As a wood finish, shellac is the basis of French polish and remains prized for fine furniture and musical instruments because it dries quickly, is non-toxic once cured, and is easily repaired. It is used as a food glaze (confectioner's glaze, E904) on candies and pills, in cosmetics and nail products, as a primer and knot sealer in paints, in printing inks and adhesives, and historically as an electrical insulating varnish and binder in grinding wheels. From the 1890s to the 1940s, shellac compounds were the standard material for pressing gramophone records.

Properties

Shellac is soluble in alcohol and alkaline solutions but not in water or hydrocarbons; it is a poor conductor of electricity, resists ultraviolet degradation better than many finishes, but is sensitive to heat, water, and alcohol spills, and has a limited shelf life in solution ("blushing" and eventual failure to harden).
