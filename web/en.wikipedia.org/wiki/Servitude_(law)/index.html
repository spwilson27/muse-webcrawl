Servitude (law)

OriginRoman law (servitutes)

In civil law jurisdictions, a servitude is a limited real right over someone else's property, entitling its holder to a specific use or benefit of that property. The concept descends from Roman law, which recognized servitutes such as rights of way, rights to draw water, and rights to light. The land burdened by the servitude is called the servient estate, and the land (or person) benefited is the dominant estate.

Types

Praedial (real) servitudes benefit a particular piece of land — for example, a right of passage across a neighbour's field, or a prohibition on building above a certain height. Personal servitudes benefit a specific person rather than land; the classical examples are usufruct (the right to use property and take its fruits), usus (limited use), and habitatio (the right to dwell in a house). Personal servitudes normally end with the holder's death, whereas praedial servitudes "run with the land" and bind successors in title.

Creation and extinction

Servitudes may be created by agreement, will, prescription (long use), or operation of law. They are extinguished by merger of the two estates, release by the holder, expiry of their term, or prolonged non-use. In common-law systems, the praedial servitude corresponds approximately to the easement, while personal servitudes have no exact equivalent, the closest devices being the life estate and the licence.
