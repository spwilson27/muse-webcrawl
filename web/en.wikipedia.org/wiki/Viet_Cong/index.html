The Viet Cong was the epithet for the National Liberation Front of South Vietnam — a mass political organisation and its guerrilla army, the People's Liberation Armed Forces — which fought against the government of South Vietnam and its American allies during the Vietnam War. Founded on 20 December 1960 at Tân Lập in Tây Ninh province, it united communists and non-communist opponents of Ngo Dinh Diem's regime. The name "Viet Cong" — shorthand for Việt Nam Cộng sản ("Vietnamese communists") — was coined by the Saigon government and popularised by the Americans; the Front called itself the Giải phóng ("Liberation") forces. Directed by Hanoi through the Central Office for South Vietnam (COSVN) and sustained via the Ho Chi Minh trail, the Viet Cong combined guerrilla warfare, political organisation in the villages, and conventional operations in coordination with North Vietnamese regulars.

Origins

After the 1954 Geneva Accords, tens of thousands of southern Viet Minh cadres regrouped to the North while many others remained in the South in arms caches and political networks. Diem's repression — especially the 1959 Law 10/59 authorising executions — drove survivors to resume armed struggle; in 1959 Hanoi authorised "armed propaganda" and began infiltrating regrouped southerners back south. The NLF's founding congress presented a ten-point programme of independence, neutralism and reunification, broadening its appeal beyond communists.

Organisation and strategy

The Viet Cong operated as a "shadow government" across much of the countryside, taxing, recruiting, running schools and clinics, and assassinating government officials — tens of thousands of village chiefs, teachers and others were killed. Its forces were tiered into village self-defence militia, local guerrillas, and full-time regional and main-force units indistinguishable from North Vietnamese regulars. Strategy followed the Maoist doctrine of protracted war: political struggle (dau tranh) combined with military action, avoiding decisive battle until conditions favoured the 1968 and 1975 general offensives. The tunnel systems of Cu Chi and the Iron Triangle epitomised its underground infrastructure.

Tet and after

The Viet Cong spearheaded the Tet Offensive of January–February 1968, attacking more than 100 cities and towns including Saigon and Hue. Militarily the offensive devastated the Viet Cong's southern infrastructure — its main-force units suffered losses from which they never fully recovered, and North Vietnamese regulars increasingly took over the fighting — but it shattered American confidence and proved a decisive political turning point. The US–South Vietnamese Phoenix Program (1968–1972) targeted the Viet Cong's political apparatus with arrests and assassinations. In 1969 the Front proclaimed a Provisional Revolutionary Government of South Vietnam, which signed the 1973 Paris Peace Accords and administered "liberated" zones until 1976.

Dissolution

After the fall of Saigon on 30 April 1975, the Provisional Revolutionary Government nominally governed the South until reunification on 2 July 1976, when the NLF and its armed forces were absorbed into the institutions of the Socialist Republic of Vietnam. Many southern cadres felt marginalised by northern officials in the unified state, a grievance voiced in later memoirs such as Trương Như Tảng's A Vietcong Memoir.
