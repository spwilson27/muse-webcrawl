American Renaissance (literature)

Period: c. 1830s–1860s
Key figures: Emerson, Thoreau, Hawthorne, Melville, Whitman, Dickinson
Coined by: F. O. Matthiessen (1941)

The American Renaissance is the period in American literature, roughly from the 1830s to the Civil War, in which the young United States produced a body of writing recognised as its first great literary flowering. The term was popularised by F. O. Matthiessen's influential 1941 study American Renaissance: Art and Expression in the Age of Emerson and Whitman, which placed Ralph Waldo Emerson, Henry David Thoreau, Nathaniel Hawthorne, Herman Melville and Walt Whitman at the centre of the canon.

Themes

The writers of the period grappled with defining a distinctly American voice independent of European models, while engaging democracy, individualism, nature, the frontier, and the moral contradictions of slavery and expansion. Transcendentalism, centred in Concord, Massachusetts around Emerson and Thoreau, preached self-reliance and the spiritual significance of nature; darker contemporaries such as Hawthorne, Melville and Edgar Allan Poe explored guilt, evil and the unconscious.

Major works

The period produced Nature (1836) and Emerson's essays, Walden (1854), The Scarlet Letter (1850), Moby-Dick (1851), Whitman's Leaves of Grass (1855) and, written in the same decades though published later, Emily Dickinson's poetry. Harriet Beecher Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852) was the era's best-selling novel. Later scholarship has broadened Matthiessen's all-male canon to include women writers and African American authors such as Frederick Douglass, whose 1845 Narrative is now central to the period.
