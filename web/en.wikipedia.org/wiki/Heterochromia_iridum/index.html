Heterochromia iridum

Heterochromia is a difference in coloration, usually of the iris but also of hair or skin. Heterochromia iridum refers specifically to the eyes: one iris is a different colour from the other (complete heterochromia), or part of one iris differs from the rest (partial or sectoral heterochromia). A third form, central heterochromia, shows a ring of contrasting colour around the pupil.

Causes

The condition results from excess or deficiency of melanin in the iris. Most cases are congenital and benign, often inherited as an autosomal dominant trait; congenital heterochromia can also accompany syndromes such as Waardenburg syndrome, Sturge–Weber syndrome and congenital Horner's syndrome. Acquired heterochromia may follow injury, inflammation (such as Fuchs heterochromic iridocyclitis), tumours, or certain glaucoma medications, and warrants medical evaluation.

Prevalence and in animals

Complete heterochromia is uncommon in humans, affecting well under one percent of the population. It is considerably more common in some animals: Siberian Huskies and Australian Shepherds frequently display it, as do white cats — notably the Turkish Van and Turkish Angora — in which it is called "odd-eyed".

In culture

Heterochromia has been noted in historical figures including Alexander the Great (according to later tradition) and in modern celebrities and fictional characters, where it is often treated as a striking and memorable trait.
