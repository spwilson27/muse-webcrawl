Right to strike

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The right to strike is the recognised right of workers to withdraw their labour collectively as a means of defending their economic and social interests. It has no express text in the ILO's freedom-of-association Convention (No. 87), but the ILO's supervisory bodies have long read it as an intrinsic corollary of freedom of association — an interpretation employers' groups at the ILO have contested since 2012, making the right's international-law basis an open institutional quarrel; it is expressly protected in the European Social Charter (Article 6(4)) and, as interpreted, in the ECHR's Article 11 (Demir and Baykara, Enerji Yapı-Yol Sen).

Domestic variation

States regulate strikes within wide margins: procedural conditions (secret ballots, notice, cooling-off, peace obligations during agreements), sectoral bans (military, police; essential-services minimum-service regimes), sympathy- and political-strike prohibitions (UK law among the most restrictive in Western Europe; US federal employees barred outright — the PATCO precedent of 1981), and, at the other pole, constitutional entrenchment (France's 1946 preamble right, Italy's Article 40). Economic analysis treats the strike as a costly signal in bargaining breakdowns (the Hicks paradox: rational parties should settle without it — strikes as information failures or commitment devices), while strike statistics track the business cycle and political eras: the post-pandemic inflation wave (2022–24) produced the first broad strike revival in a generation in Britain, France, Germany and the United States.
