The Charlottetown Accord was a package of proposed amendments to the Constitution of Canada, agreed by federal, provincial, territorial, and Indigenous leaders on 28 August 1992. Put to a national referendum on 26 October 1992, it was rejected by a majority of voters nationally and in six provinces, ending a decade of Canadian constitutional negotiation.

Background

The Accord grew out of the failure of the Meech Lake Accord in 1990, which had sought Quebec's assent to the Constitution Act, 1982. After extensive public consultations — including the Spicer Commission and parliamentary committees — Prime Minister Brian Mulroney convened first ministers' meetings in the summer of 1992, with representatives of the Assembly of First Nations, Inuit, and Métis organizations participating for the first time in constitutional talks.

Contents

The package included a "Canada clause" interpreting the Constitution, recognition of Quebec as a distinct society, recognition of the inherent right of Indigenous peoples to self-government, reform of the Senate into an elected body with equal provincial representation, changes to the division of powers over culture, forestry, mining, and other fields, a social and economic union commitment, and guarantees of Quebec's share of seats in the House of Commons.

Referendum and defeat

Although supported by the federal government, all provincial premiers, and most major political parties and media, the Accord faced opposition from Pierre Trudeau, Preston Manning's Reform Party, Quebec nationalists who found it insufficient, and many women's and Indigenous groups. In the referendum, 54.3% of voters — and 56.7% in Quebec — rejected it. The defeat contributed to the collapse of Mulroney's Progressive Conservative government in 1993 and to the Quebec referendum of 1995, in which sovereignty was narrowly defeated.
