Vertep

A traditional Ukrainian vertep puppet theatre.

A vertep (Ukrainian: вертеп; Russian: вертеп) is a portable puppet theatre and dramatic form that originated in Ukraine in the late 16th to early 17th century and spread widely through the Russian Empire. The vertep presents folk theatrical performances, principally the Nativity story, staged inside a two-storey wooden box carried by travelling performers, often students of theological academies or seminaries.

Structure

The vertep box is traditionally divided into two levels. The upper storey, representing heaven or the sacred world, hosts the religious part of the performance — typically the story of the birth of Christ, Herod's massacre of the innocents, and related biblical episodes. The lower storey, representing earth, hosts a secular, often comic part featuring folk characters such as a Cossack, a Jew, a Gypsy, a peasant, Death, and the Devil, whose antics satirised contemporary society.

History

The form is thought to derive from Western European mystery plays and puppet traditions transmitted through Poland. Vertep performances became a fixture of Christmas festivities in Ukraine, Belarus, and Russia, performed in villages and towns by itinerant troupes. In some traditions, particularly in Belarus and parts of Ukraine, vertep performances were also connected with carol singing (koliada) during the Christmas season.

Modern revivals

After decades of suppression in the Soviet period, when religious content was discouraged, the vertep tradition was revived following Ukrainian independence in 1991. Contemporary vertep performances sometimes incorporate modern political satire; during the Euromaidan protests of 2013–14 and after the Russian invasion of Ukraine in 2022, vertep troupes performed topical versions featuring caricatures of political figures.
