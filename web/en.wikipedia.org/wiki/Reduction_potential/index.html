Reduction potential

The reduction potential of a chemical species is a measure of its tendency to acquire electrons and thereby be reduced. It is measured in volts (V), or millivolts (mV), relative to a reference electrode. The more positive the reduction potential, the greater the species' tendency to be reduced (i.e., its oxidising strength); the more negative, the greater its tendency to be oxidised when combined with a standard partner.

Standard electrode potential

The standard reduction potential (E°) is measured under standard conditions: 25 °C, 1 M concentration for solutes, 1 atm pressure for gases, with the reaction written as a reduction. All values are referenced to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), arbitrarily assigned a potential of exactly 0.00 V for the half-reaction 2H+ + 2e− → H2.

Applications

Reduction potentials predict the direction of redox reactions, the voltage of galvanic cells (E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode), metal reactivity and corrosion behaviour, and the feasibility of electrolysis. Together with the Nernst equation, which adjusts potentials for non-standard concentrations and temperatures, they form the quantitative basis of electrochemistry. In biochemistry, reduction potentials order the electron carriers of respiratory chains, from NADH (about −0.32 V) to oxygen (+0.82 V).
